Model written answer

(a) EU ETS and FuelEU Maritime - two different approaches

1. The EU ETS - a price on emissions

The EU Emissions Trading System (Directive 2003/87/EC as amended by Directive (EU) 2023/959) has covered shipping since 1 January 2024. It is cap-and-trade: the shipping company must surrender one allowance per tonne of CO2-equivalent emitted, and buys allowances at the market price.

  • Scope: ships of 5,000 GT and above carrying cargo or passengers commercially; 100% of emissions on voyages between EU ports and at berth in them, 50% on voyages into or out of the EU; emissions monitored under the EU MRV Regulation.
  • Phase-in, distinguishing the years: 40% of emissions in 2024, 70% of 2025 emissions and 100% from 2026 emissions, each surrendered by 30 September of the following year. At this sitting the September 2026 surrender, for 70% of 2025 emissions, has just fallen due; 2026 emissions will be surrendered in full in 2027.
  • Gases: CO2 to 2025; methane and nitrous oxide from 2026 emissions, which matters for LNG-fuelled ships because of methane slip.
  • Liability and enforcement: the shipping company - the owner or the ISM company that has assumed responsibility; a shortfall attracts an excess-emissions penalty per tonne and the missing allowances must still be surrendered.

2. FuelEU Maritime - a standard on the fuel

FuelEU Maritime (Regulation (EU) 2023/1805), applying from 1 January 2025, does not price emissions at all. It sets a maximum GHG intensity for the energy a ship uses, measured well-to-wake in grams of CO2-equivalent per megajoule, so that the production of the fuel counts as well as its combustion.

  • The limit tightens: against a reference value of 91.16 gCO2e/MJ - 2% lower from 2025, 6% from 2030, 14.5% from 2035, 31% from 2040, 62% from 2045 and 80% from 2050.
  • Same voyage scope as the ETS (100% intra-EU and at berth, 50% external), for ships above 5,000 GT.
  • Shore power: container and passenger ships at berth in the major EU ports must connect from 2030.
  • Flexibility: a surplus can be banked, a limited deficit borrowed against the next year, and ships can be pooled so that a low-intensity ship covers others.
  • Enforcement: a financial penalty on any remaining deficit, and a FuelEU document of compliance that the ship must carry.

3. The primary difference, stated

The EU ETS puts a price on the quantity of greenhouse gas emitted; FuelEU limits the greenhouse-gas intensity of the energy used. The first is a market-based measure - it leaves the choice of abatement to the company and lets the carbon price decide; the second is a performance standard - it obliges the fleet, over time, to use lower-carbon fuels and shore power whatever the price. The ETS counts emissions on board (tank-to-wake); FuelEU counts the whole life of the fuel (well-to-wake), so a fossil-derived 'alternative' fuel gains little under FuelEU. Together they make fossil fuel more expensive and compel the move away from it.

(b) Green shipping corridors

4. Green shipping corridors - what they are and what they do

A green shipping corridor is a specific trade route between two or more ports on which zero-emission shipping is demonstrated and supported - the ships, the fuel supply, the bunkering and port infrastructure, and the regulatory and commercial arrangements all aligned on that route. The idea was launched by the Clydebank Declaration at COP26 in November 2021, a voluntary undertaking by signatory States to support the establishment of such corridors.

Primary function: to break the 'chicken and egg' problem - no one builds zero-emission ships without fuel, and no one produces fuel without ships - by concentrating first-mover action on one route, where the parties can commit together, prove the technology and the logistics at commercial scale, and de-risk investment so that the solutions can be copied elsewhere.

(c) Why the cost of transition is high

5. EEXI and CII - the cost of complying with IMO rules

  • EEXI has applied since the first annual, intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2023. Most existing ships met it by limiting engine or shaft power, which reduces their reserve of speed and their earning capacity; others needed energy-saving retrofits and dry-dock time.
  • CII rates each ship A to E every year, against a required value that tightens annually. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E once, must carry an approved corrective action plan in its SEEMP. The operational answer - slower steaming, better routing, hull cleaning - costs time, and slower ships mean more ships are needed to carry the same cargo.
  • The market adds its own penalty: charterers, cargo owners and lenders increasingly prefer well-rated ships, so a poor rating lowers hire rates and asset value.

6. The EU ETS and FuelEU - regional costs on top

  • EU ETS: every tonne emitted on EU-related voyages now carries an allowance cost at a volatile market price, rising to full coverage from 2026 emissions, and from 2026 methane slip is charged too.
  • FuelEU: a ship that keeps burning conventional fuel faces a penalty on its deficit unless the company buys lower-carbon fuel or pays to join a pool.
  • Administration: monitoring, reporting and verification, registry accounts and documents of compliance add people and systems.
  • Allocation: who bears the cost - owner, operator or time charterer - has to be settled in contracts (for example by BIMCO emissions clauses), and disputes follow.
  • Ships trading to Europe therefore comply with two regimes at once, the IMO's and the EU's.

7. Fuel, capital and uncertainty - why the bill is so high

  • Fuel: the zero- and low-carbon fuels that would cut emissions - green methanol, ammonia, bio-fuels - cost several times as much as conventional fuel and are available only in small quantities.
  • Capital: dual-fuel newbuildings cost more than conventional ships, and retrofits cost both money and off-hire time.
  • Asset life: ships trade for twenty years or more, so a ship ordered now must remain compliant under rules that will be far tighter; the risk is a stranded asset.
  • Uncertainty: The IMO Net-Zero Framework - the draft amendments to MARPOL Annex VI approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025, including a global fuel standard and a GHG pricing mechanism - was not adopted at this sitting: the extraordinary session in October 2025 voted to adjourn for one year, and adoption was still pending in October 2026; MEPC 84 set the resumed extraordinary session for December 2026. It is a proposal, not law. Owners must invest without knowing which global fuel standard and carbon price will apply, while regional rules already bite.
  • Split incentive: the owner pays for efficiency, but under a time charter the charterer saves the fuel.

Common mistakes

  • Saying FuelEU is 'another carbon tax'. It is an intensity standard; its penalty applies only to a deficit.
  • Stating that 100% of emissions were surrendered in 2025. 2025 surrendered 40% of 2024 emissions; 100% applies from 2026 emissions, surrendered in 2027.

  • Confusing EEXI (one-off technical) with CII (annual operational).

Model written answer

1. The aim, and the treatment train

A ballast water management system (BWMS) exists to make the water discharged meet the D-2 performance standard of the Ballast Water Management Convention: fewer than 10 viable organisms per cubic metre of 50 micrometres and above, fewer than 10 per millilitre between 10 and 50 micrometres, and indicator-microbe limits: toxicogenic Vibrio cholerae under 1 cfu per 100 mL, E. coli under 250 cfu per 100 mL and intestinal enterococci under 100 cfu per 100 mL.

Almost every system shares the same train: the ballast pump delivers sea water through a filter - usually an automatic back-flushing screen filter, sometimes a hydrocyclone - which removes larger organisms and sediment and returns them overboard at the uptake location; then through the treatment unit; then to the tanks. On discharge the water either passes a second treatment (UV systems) or is neutralised (systems using an active substance).

2. Control, monitoring and approval

  • A control and monitoring unit sets the treatment to the flow, monitors the treatment parameter - UV intensity, or residual oxidant concentration - raises alarms, and records operation, including any bypass.
  • The records support the Ballast Water Record Book and port State inspection.
  • A system installed on or after 28 October 2020 needs flag type approval under the BWMS Code, which replaced the G8 Guidelines; earlier installations may hold G8 approval.
  • A system that uses or generates an Active Substance - a chemical that acts on organisms - also needs Organization approval under Procedure (G9). That distinction runs through the four methods below.

(a) UV treatment method

3. UV treatment

After filtration the water flows through a reactor in which medium- or low-pressure UV-C lamps in quartz sleeves irradiate it. UV light at germicidal wavelengths damages the DNA and RNA of organisms so that they cannot reproduce - they are rendered non-viable rather than necessarily killed.

The dose depends on lamp intensity, exposure time and the water's UV transmittance; turbid, sediment-laden water absorbs UV, which is why filtration comes first. Most systems treat on uptake and again on discharge to deal with regrowth in the tanks. Because nothing is added, there is no neutralisation and no Active Substance approval. Disadvantages are reduced performance in turbid water, fouling of the sleeves (removed by automatic wipers), and a high electrical load.

(b) Gas treatment method

4. Gas treatment

'Gas treatment' covers two quite different families:

  • Ozone. Ozone is generated on board from air or oxygen by electrical discharge and injected into the ballast stream, where it acts as a strong oxidant. In sea water it forms secondary oxidants and by-products such as bromate, so the system uses an Active Substance, needs G9 approval, and must neutralise the residual oxidant before discharge. Ozone is toxic and corrosive, so the generator space needs gas detection and ventilation.
  • Deoxygenation (inert gas). A low-oxygen gas, typically from an inert gas generator, is mixed into the water so that dissolved oxygen falls to a level at which organisms die of hypoxia; some systems also lower the pH with the carbon dioxide in the gas. It needs a holding time of some days in closed, well-sealed tanks, which suits long voyages, and reduces corrosion in the tanks.

(c) Electrolysis method

5. Electrolysis (electrochlorination)

Sea water is passed through an electrolytic cell with coated titanium electrodes. Electrolysis of the chloride in the water produces sodium hypochlorite and hypochlorous acid - active chlorine - which disinfects the ballast. The cell works either on the full flow or on a side stream whose product is injected into the main line, dosed to a target total residual oxidant measured by sensors.

Hydrogen is produced at the cathode and must be separated and vented safely. Output falls in low-salinity or cold water, so river ports may need stored brine or heating. The oxidant is an Active Substance requiring G9 approval, and at discharge the residual oxidant is neutralised - typically with a sulphite or thiosulphate dose - to within the system's approved limit.

(d) Magnetic separation method

6. Magnetic separation

A coagulant and a fine magnetic powder are dosed into the incoming water. Plankton, bacteria and suspended particles flocculate around the magnetic particles, forming flocs that a magnetic separator - a rotating magnetised drum or discs - pulls out of the flow. A filter may follow for polishing.

Its advantage is that it removes sediment as well as organisms and does not depend on water clarity. Its disadvantages are the chemical consumables, the sludge that has to be stored and landed, and the bulk of the equipment. Because a coagulant is added, such a system has been taken through the Active Substance (G9) approval procedure as well as type approval; it is not a purely physical method.

Understand this first

A ship is aground and there is no time. Somebody with tugs and pumps and people who know what they are doing has to decide, now, whether to sail towards her - and the owner has to decide, now, whether to let them. Neither side can possibly agree a price at that moment: nobody knows how long it will take, what it will cost, whether it will work, or what the ship and her cargo will turn out to be worth when she is safe. So the law does something unusual. It says: go and do the work, and if you succeed you will be paid, and the amount will be worked out afterwards by someone applying settled criteria. If you fail, you get nothing. That single arrangement - reward for success, nothing for failure - is what makes a professional salvage industry possible, because it turns an act of rescue into a business that can own expensive equipment and keep it waiting. The rest follows from it. Because the reward is fixed afterwards, there has to be a list of things the person fixing it must weigh - how dangerous it was, how skilful, how quick, what was risked, and what was saved. Because a rescuer who is paid only for saving property has no reason to care about oil on a beach, the list was expanded to reward protecting the coastline too, and duties to look after the environment were placed on both sides - the salvor and the shipowner. And because none of this can be negotiated line by line on a lee shore, the industry uses a short standard form that the master can sign in minutes, which settles who decides the amount, under what law, and where - while leaving the amount itself to be worked out later on the settled criteria. So the two documents in this question are not rivals. One says what a rescue is worth; the other is how you get started before anyone has time to argue about it.

Model written answer

1. The Convention, and where the stem's facts have got to

The governing instrument is the International Convention on Salvage, 1989, which entered into force in 1996 and replaced the Brussels Convention of 1910. On the stem's facts a professional salvor has refloated a grounded ship and towed her to a place of safety. A useful result has therefore been achieved, which under article 12 is the condition of any payment at all - no cure, no pay, retained by the 1989 Convention rather than abolished by it. The entitlement has accrued, and the three limbs ask how it is quantified, what duties the parties owed one another while it was being earned, and what happens when the parties have also signed a contract.

MIW holds no licensed text of the Convention and no copy of LOF 2000 or SCOPIC. Articles and forms below are identified and described by effect; nothing is quoted, and no clause number is cited.

(a) The criteria under Article 13 for determining the amount of the award (6 marks)

2. The scheme Article 13 sits inside

Two articles work together and it is worth separating them in one line. Article 12 creates the entitlement: salvage operations which have had a useful result give right to a reward, and no payment is due if they have had no useful result. Article 13 then fixes the amount - and it does so, in the Convention's own words of purpose, with a view to encouraging salvage operations.

That phrase governs everything else in the limb. The reward is not a reimbursement of the salvor's costs, and it is not a quantum meruit for hours worked. It is deliberately set at a level intended to make it worth someone's while to own tugs, pumps and trained crews and keep them idle against the day they are needed. A candidate who explains that has explained why criterion (10) - the salvor's state of readiness and the value of his equipment - is in the list at all.

3. The Article 13 criteria

The Convention lists ten. Give them all, and in this order, because the examiner has named the article:

  1. The salved value of the vessel and other property.
  2. The skill and efforts of the salvors in preventing or minimising damage to the environment.
  3. The measure of success obtained by the salvor.
  4. The nature and degree of the danger.
  5. The skill and efforts of the salvors in salving the vessel, other property and life.
  6. The time used and the expenses and losses incurred by the salvors.
  7. The risk of liability and other risks run by the salvors or their equipment.
  8. The promptness of the services rendered.
  9. The availability and use of vessels or other equipment intended for salvage operations.
  10. The state of readiness and efficiency of the salvor's equipment and the value thereof.

Note where the environmental criterion sits. It is second, immediately after salved value - not appended at the end. That placement is the Convention's most important single reform: under the 1910 regime a salvor who spent days and money keeping oil off a coastline but saved nothing of value earned nothing for it, and the 1989 Convention rewards environmental protection inside the ordinary reward, before ever reaching the separate special compensation provisions.

4. How the criteria are applied

  • They are not in order of importance. The Convention says so expressly. All ten are weighed together in the round, and the relative weight depends on the casualty - danger and skill will dominate one case, promptness and readiness another.
  • The reward is capped. It shall not exceed the salved value of the vessel and other property. The salvor can never recover more than he saved, however heroic the operation or however great his expenditure.
  • The cap is why more was needed. Where the property saved is of little value but the environmental exposure is enormous - a tanker in ballast, or a ship of low salved value, aground and threatening a coastline - the cap can leave a salvor with a reward far below his costs. That is the gap the Convention's separate special compensation provisions were written to fill. Those are not the subject of this stem and are not developed here; they are mentioned so that the shape of the scheme is visible.
  • The criteria are applied by whoever fixes the award - by agreement between the parties, or failing that by the arbitrator or tribunal to whom the question has been referred. This is the point at which limb (a) hands over to limb (c).

5. Who pays, and what can reduce or extinguish the award

Payment. The reward is payable by all of the vessel and other property interests in proportion to their respective salved values. Ship and cargo therefore contribute separately and proportionately - a point worth making, because it explains why a salvor takes security from cargo interests as well as from the owner before releasing the property, and why cargo underwriters are as interested in a salvage award as hull underwriters.

What reduces it. The scheme has teeth in both directions:

  • Article 18 - a salvor may be deprived of the whole or part of the payment due to the extent that the salvage operations have become necessary or more difficult because of his own fault or neglect, or if he has been guilty of fraud or other dishonest conduct.
  • Article 19 - services rendered notwithstanding the express and reasonable prohibition of the owner or master of the vessel give rise to no payment under the Convention. A salvor cannot force his services on an unwilling ship and then present a bill.
  • And within article 13 itself, a poor measure of success, a lack of promptness or unimpressive skill and efforts simply produce a smaller award. The criteria cut both ways.

(b) The duties of the salvor and of the shipowner and master (4 marks)

6. The salvor's duties

Article 8 is the duties article and it is expressed as a reciprocal scheme. The salvor owes to the owner of the vessel or other property in danger a duty:

  1. to carry out the salvage operations with due care;
  2. in performing that duty, to exercise due care to prevent or minimise damage to the environment;
  3. whenever circumstances reasonably require, to seek assistance from other salvors; and
  4. to accept the intervention of other salvors when reasonably requested to do so by the owner or master of the vessel - though the amount of his reward is not prejudiced should it be found that such a request was unreasonable.

Duties (3) and (4) are the ones candidates omit, and they are doing real work. A professional salvor who has a casualty under contract has an obvious commercial incentive to keep the job to himself; the Convention removes that incentive where the ship's interests require more resources than he has. The salvor may not hold a casualty hostage to his own capacity - and the closing qualification protects him from being displaced on an unreasonable demand.

7. The duties of the owner and the master

The same article imposes duties the other way. The owner and master of the vessel or the owner of other property in danger owe the salvor a duty:

  1. to co-operate fully with him during the course of the salvage operations;
  2. in doing so, to exercise due care to prevent or minimise damage to the environment; and
  3. when the vessel or other property has been brought to a place of safety, to accept redelivery when reasonably requested by the salvor to do so.

The third duty fits the stem's facts exactly. The examiner has told you the ship has been towed by the salvor to a place of safety - which is precisely the trigger for the owner's obligation to take her back when reasonably asked. It exists because the alternative is a salvor left holding a damaged ship he cannot dispose of and cannot leave, accruing cost, while the owner delays redelivery to improve his negotiating position on security.

8. The mutual character of the duties, the master's authority, and breach

  • The environmental duty is imposed on both sides, and that is the Convention's genuine innovation. The 1910 regime asked only whether property had been saved. The 1989 Convention makes the prevention of environmental damage a duty of the owner and master as well as of the salvor, rewards it in the article 13 criteria, and backs it with separate compensation provisions. An answer that gives the environmental duty only to the salvor has given half of it.
  • The master's authority to contract. Under article 6 the master has authority to conclude contracts for salvage operations on behalf of the owner of the vessel, and the master or the owner has authority to conclude such contracts on behalf of the owner of the property on board. This is the provision that makes limb (c) possible at all: it is why a form can be signed at three in the morning by the only person present with authority, without waiting for owners, charterers, cargo interests and underwriters to be assembled.
  • Breach. The Convention does not create a tariff of penalties for breach of article 8. Its consequences run through the rest of the scheme: a salvor whose want of due care made the operation necessary or more difficult may be deprived of all or part of his reward under article 18; an owner or master who fails to co-operate, or who refuses reasonable redelivery, exposes himself to liability for the loss that follows and weakens his position on the assessment; and failure by either side to exercise due care for the environment bears directly on the article 13 assessment and on the special compensation provisions.

(c) LOF 2000 - when the Convention applies, and when the LOF (6 marks)

9. What LOF 2000 is, and what it is not

Lloyd's Open Form is a standard-form salvage contract. It is commercial contract, not an international instrument, and the single most common error on this limb is to treat it as though it competed with the Convention on the same plane. Its defining characteristics are:

  • It is 'open' - the remuneration is deliberately not fixed at the time of agreement. That is the whole point of the form: at a casualty nobody can price the job, so the parties agree that it will be done and that the price will be settled afterwards.
  • It is no cure, no pay, mirroring the Convention's own condition of payment.
  • It is short and can be agreed in minutes, often verbally and confirmed later, and typically signed by the master under his article 6 authority - which is why work can begin while the ship is still in danger.
  • It fixes the machinery, not the amount - who will determine the sum, under what law, where, and how the property will be secured in the meantime.

SCOPIC is a separate contractual supplement which the parties may incorporate, providing an alternative, tariff-based method of remunerating a salvor in circumstances where the ordinary reward may be inadequate. It too is industry contract, outside the Convention. No clause of either the LOF or SCOPIC is quoted or cited here.

10. When the Convention applies

The Convention applies as the general law of salvage in any State that has given it effect, and it governs by default. Its own article 6 sets out the relationship with contracts:

  • The Convention applies to any salvage operation save to the extent that a contract otherwise provides, expressly or by implication. Parties are therefore free to vary its terms by agreement, and an LOF is exactly such an agreement.
  • But the freedom is not unlimited. By article 6(3) nothing in that article affects article 7 or the duties to prevent or minimise damage to the environment - those cannot be contracted out of. The other article 8 duties (to seek or accept other salvors, to accept redelivery) can be varied by contract.
  • And the Convention supervises the bargain. A contract or any of its terms may be annulled or modified where the contract has been entered into under undue influence or the influence of danger and its terms are inequitable, or where the payment under the contract is in an excessive degree too large or too small for the services actually rendered.

That supervisory power is the answer to the obvious objection to signing anything in an emergency. A master agreeing terms with a ship aground and making water is, by definition, not negotiating freely, and the Convention accordingly reserves the right to reopen a bargain struck in those conditions.

11. When the LOF applies

Where the parties have agreed an LOF, its terms govern the relationship between them to the extent that they provide for the matter in hand. In practice the form governs:

  • the fact and scope of the engagement - that the salvor will use best endeavours to salve the property and take it to an agreed or a place of safety;
  • the forum and the law - that the amount of any award, and any dispute, will be referred to arbitration in London under the terms of the form and the law it specifies, rather than to a court in whatever jurisdiction the casualty happens to lie;
  • security - the obligation on the property interests to provide security for the salvor's claim, and the salvor's corresponding obligation to release the property once it is given; and
  • the procedure - the appointment of the arbitrator, the appeal structure within the form, and the administration of the reference.

What the LOF does not do is create the entitlement or supply the criteria. It records that a reward will be payable if the operation succeeds and provides the machinery for fixing it; the substantive principles the arbitrator applies are the Convention's.

12. How a dispute is actually resolved under an LOF

  1. The ship is salved and delivered to a place of safety. Security is provided by the ship and cargo interests, on the form's terms, and the property is released - which is what allows the ship to trade and the cargo to move while the money is argued about.
  2. The salvor's claim is referred to arbitration in London in accordance with the form. This is the practical significance of the LOF and it is where most of the marks in limb (c) are: the form has removed the question of forum from the dispute entirely. There is no argument about which country's courts have jurisdiction, no race to arrest, and no forum-shopping - a real gain when a casualty may involve a ship of one flag, a salvor of another, cargo owned in a third and a coastline belonging to a fourth.
  3. The arbitrator fixes the amount by applying the Article 13 criteria - salved value, environmental efforts, measure of success, danger, skill, time and expense, risks run, promptness, availability and readiness - and observes the cap at salved value, apportioning the liability between ship and cargo in proportion to their salved values.
  4. An appeal lies within the structure the form itself provides, and the award is then enforceable against the security already held.

14. Why the arrangement exists at all

Close by explaining why two documents are needed rather than one, because it makes the whole answer cohere.

A casualty demands a decision in minutes and a valuation that takes months. Nobody at the scene knows how long the work will take, what it will cost, whether it will succeed, or what the property will be worth when it is safe. So the two functions are split. The form lets the work start at once, on terms brief enough for a master to accept while his ship is still aground, and settles in advance the questions that would otherwise be fought over afterwards - who decides, under what law, where, and against what security. The Convention then supplies the principles on which the sum is fixed, guarantees the duties neither side may bargain away, protects the party who agreed terms under the influence of danger, and rewards the salvor for protecting a coastline whose owner is not a party to anything.

Between them they answer the question that makes professional salvage possible: why would anyone sail towards a ship that nobody has yet agreed to pay for? Because success is rewarded on principled criteria that recognise readiness and risk as well as effort; because failure genuinely earns nothing, which keeps the industry honest; and because the terms on which all of that will be decided were settled before anyone had time to argue.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed marks are 6 / 5 / 5 and the limbs are three different tasks. Limb (a) says list, so it must be a numbered list - the examiner has named Article 13, which means partial recall of the criteria will be visible as partial recall. Give all ten. Limb (b) is two-sided and the second side is where the marks hide. Limb (c) is the limb most candidates lose, and it is worth as much as limb (b).

Spend your time in proportion. A common failure is a beautiful limb (a) followed by two thin paragraphs - because the criteria are memorable and the duties and the contract are not. If you are short of time, a bare list of the ten criteria takes ninety seconds and then limb (c) deserves the rest.

In limb (c), the sentence that earns the most is the one that says what each document actually does: the contract fixes forum, law, security and machinery; the Convention supplies the criteria the arbitrator applies. Everything else in that limb is elaboration of that sentence.

The idea underneath salvage law

Salvage exists because ordinary contract law cannot do the job. Normally you agree a price and then do the work. At a casualty that is impossible in both directions: the salvor cannot price a job whose duration, difficulty and outcome are unknown, and the owner cannot negotiate freely with a ship aground and making water. If the law required a bargain, either nothing would happen or everything would be extortion.

So the law substitutes a different arrangement: do the work, and if it succeeds you will be paid an amount fixed afterwards on settled criteria. That single move solves both problems. The salvor does not need a price to start. The owner cannot be held to ransom, because the amount will be assessed rather than demanded. And because the assessment expressly aims to encourage salvage operations, it can be generous enough to sustain an industry that must keep expensive equipment idle most of the time - which is why readiness and the value of the salvor's equipment are among the criteria, and why 'you only worked for two days' is not an answer to a large award.

The 1989 Convention then adds the piece the 1910 regime lacked. Under the old law a salvor was rewarded for saving property, so a tanker that could not be saved but whose oil could be kept off a coastline earned him precisely nothing - which meant the law was paying salvors to make exactly the wrong choice about where to put their effort. The 1989 Convention rewards environmental protection inside the ordinary reward criteria, imposes environmental duties on both parties, and provides separately for the case where the property saved is too small to fund the effort. Understand that one reform and most of the Convention's shape follows.

They fall into four groups, and grouped they are much easier to reproduce under pressure.

It rewards the salvor for things that happened before the casualty - for having bought the tugs, maintained them and had them where they were needed. That is the law deliberately paying for standing capacity, and it is the clearest evidence that the award is not a fee for hours worked.

LOF and the Convention - the mistake to avoid

The commonest error in limb (c) is to write as though the question were 'which of these two applies?', as if they were rival sources of law competing for the same territory. They are not. One is a treaty and one is a contract, and they operate on different questions.

Think of it as a division of labour. The Convention answers: is a reward payable at all? on what criteria? capped at what? paid by whom? what duties did each party owe? and can this bargain be reopened? The LOF answers: are we agreed that you will start work? who will decide the amount? under what law? where? and how is the property secured in the meantime?

Once you see that, the exceptions make sense too. The Convention lets the parties vary its terms by contract - because most of it is default machinery. But it will not let them contract out of the article 8 duties, and it reserves power to annul or modify a contract made under the influence of danger on inequitable terms, or one where the payment is in an excessive degree too large or too small. Those are the protective provisions, and they are the answer to 'what if the master signs something disastrous?'

Why arbitration in London matters more than it sounds

It is easy to read 'arbitration in London under English law' as a piece of parochial detail. It is not. Consider the alternative on a real casualty: a ship of one flag, salvors of another nationality, cargo owned in several countries, insurers in more, and a grounding on the coast of a State with its own interest in the outcome. Without an agreed forum, the first dispute is about where the dispute will be heard, and the salvor's practical remedy is to arrest the property wherever he can reach it.

The form removes all of that in advance, before anyone knows whether there will be an argument. The greatest practical value of the LOF is not what it says about money; it is that it settles the questions that would otherwise be fought over first. That, plus the security provisions that let the ship trade and the cargo move while the amount is assessed, is what makes the form worth signing.

(a), six lines. The criteria are: salved value of the vessel and other property; skill and efforts in preventing or minimising damage to the environment; measure of success; nature and degree of the danger; skill and efforts in salving the vessel, other property and life; time used and expenses and losses incurred; risk of liability and other risks run; promptness of the services; availability and use of vessels or other equipment intended for salvage; and the state of readiness, efficiency and value of the salvor's equipment. (b), four lines. Article 8 imposes duties on both sides. The salvor must carry out the operations with due care; in doing so exercise due care to prevent or minimise damage to the environment; seek assistance from other salvors whenever circumstances reasonably require; and accept their intervention when reasonably requested by the owner or master, without prejudice to his reward if the request proves unreasonable. (c), five lines. LOF 2000 is Lloyd's Open Form - a standard-form salvage contract, not an instrument: no cure no pay, remuneration deliberately left open, signable in minutes by the master. The Convention applies as the general law and governs by default; article 6 provides that it applies to any salvage operation save to the extent a contract otherwise provides, but by article 6(3) the environmental duties cannot be contracted out of, and under article 7 a contract made under the influence of danger on inequitable terms, or grossly mispriced, may be annulled or modified. The LOF applies between the parties who agreed it and fixes the machinery: the engagement, security for the claim and release of the property, and reference of the amount and of any dispute to arbitration in London under the form's terms and law, with its own appeal structure. In a dispute, therefore, the LOF determines where, under what law and by whom the question is decided, while the arbitrator applies the Convention's Article 13 criteria to fix the sum. The rule: the contract governs procedure and terms, the Convention governs substance, and where the contract would defeat the Convention's protective provisions the Convention prevails.

Understand this first

Every IMO convention creates reporting duties - tell the Organization about your casualties, your recognized organizations, your reception facilities, your fuel supply. For decades those reports arrived as paper, in whatever format the sending State chose, and once filed they were almost useless: nobody could search them, aggregate them or see a pattern in them. GISIS exists to fix that, and it is best understood not as a website but as the Organization's memory. That framing answers limb (a) - the modules are simply one drawer per reporting duty, filled by the States themselves and administered rather than authored by the Secretariat. It also answers limb (b), which is really a question about why anyone would want this data. The answer is that MARPOL works by prohibiting things on board - do not burn high-sulphur fuel, do not discharge waste at sea - and each of those prohibitions is only workable if the shore side provides an alternative. So each is paired with a duty on States, to promote compliant fuel and to provide adequate reception facilities. GISIS is where a ship reports that the shore side failed, and where enough such reports become evidence that a port has a real problem.

Model written answer

1. a) What GISIS is, and the problem it solves

The Global Integrated Shipping Information System is the IMO Secretariat's web-based platform for the information that Member States are required or invited to report under IMO instruments. Before it, that information arrived as paper circulars and correspondence: it was slow, inconsistently formatted, effectively unsearchable, and once filed it was of little use to anyone. Conventions imposed reporting duties without creating anywhere useful for the reports to go.

GISIS turns those duties into a single, structured, electronic repository. Its purpose is threefold - to collect what States must report, to disseminate it to those who need it, and to make it analysable so that the Organization can see patterns rather than incidents.

2. a) The components - the module structure

GISIS is organised into modules, each corresponding to a reporting obligation or an information need. The principal ones include:

  • Marine casualties and incidents - casualty reports and investigation reports;
  • Piracy and armed robbery against ships;
  • Port reception facilities - what exists at each port, and reports of alleged inadequacy;
  • Contact points - the national focal points a State must designate;
  • Ship and company particulars, built on the IMO identification number schemes;
  • Recognized organizations - which ROs each flag has authorized, and for what;
  • Status of multilateral conventions - who is party to what, and from when;
  • Port State control, and survey and certification;
  • Marine environment and MARPOL reporting, including fuel oil availability;
  • Maritime security and the condition assessment scheme.

The list is not exhaustive, and it should not be memorised as though it were fixed: modules are added and reorganised administratively as reporting needs change, without any instrument being amended.

3. a) Access, and how the data gets in

Content is divided between public and restricted. Much is openly accessible after free registration - convention status, ship particulars, recognized organizations, reception facility details - while operationally or commercially sensitive material is confined to Member State users. Data is entered and maintained by each State's designated focal points, with the Secretariat administering the system rather than authoring the content.

That division of labour is the system's strength and its weakness together: the data is authoritative because the reporting State owns it, and it is incomplete wherever a State does not report.

4. a) How it facilitates data sharing among Member States

  • One repository, one format. A State reports once, electronically, in a defined structure, instead of circulating documents bilaterally.
  • Immediacy. What is entered is visible at once, rather than after a publication cycle.
  • Searchable and analysable. Data can be filtered, aggregated and exported, which is what allows the Organization's own sub-committees to analyse casualties and identify systemic causes rather than reading individual reports.
  • It feeds regulatory work. Casualty analysis informs amendments; port State control data supports targeting; convention status underpins every enforcement decision a port State takes.
  • Transparency and accountability. Because the record is visible, a State's failure to report is visible too - which is itself a compliance mechanism, and is used in the Organization's member State audit work.
  • It directs assistance. Gaps revealed by the data show the Organization where technical cooperation and capacity building are actually needed.

5. b) Reporting on compliant fuel oil

MARPOL Annex VI regulation 18, 'Fuel oil availability and quality', is the hook. It sits behind the sulphur limits - 0.50% m/m globally since 1 January 2020 and 0.10% m/m in emission control areas (which since 1 May 2025 include the Mediterranean Sea) - and it does two things that reach the Organization.

  • Parties must promote the availability of compliant fuel oil and inform the Organization about it, including where it is and is not obtainable. That is what makes a global limit administrable.
  • Where a ship cannot obtain compliant fuel oil despite best efforts, it records the fact in a fuel oil non-availability report, notifying its Administration and the competent authority of the port of destination, and the information reaches the Organization.

GISIS is where those two streams are collected and made visible. The value is that an individual non-availability report is an excuse, but many reports naming the same port is evidence of a supply failure - and only a central register can convert one into the other. It also protects the honest operator: a port State officer can see whether non-availability at that port is corroborated, and the shipowner is not left arguing an unverifiable case.

6. b) Reporting inadequacy of port reception facilities

MARPOL Annex VI regulation 17, 'Reception facilities', and its counterparts in the other Annexes, require Parties to ensure the provision of adequate reception facilities. The port reception facilities module serves that obligation from both ends.

  • It holds a database of the facilities available at each port - waste types accepted, capacities and contacts - so that a ship can plan its discharges before arrival rather than discovering the position alongside.
  • It carries the alleged inadequacy reporting mechanism. Where a facility is unavailable, insufficient, unreasonably delayed or effectively inaccessible, the master reports the alleged inadequacy; the report goes to the flag Administration, which forwards it to the port State and to the Organization; and the port State is expected to investigate and respond, with the exchange recorded.

The word alleged is deliberate and is worth noticing: the system records a claim and a response, not a finding. It is a structured complaint channel, not an adjudication.

7. b) What the reported data is actually useful for

Both limbs of the printed question turn on one idea. A prohibition addressed to the ship is only enforceable, and only fair, if the shore side delivers. A ship may be required to burn compliant fuel only if compliant fuel can be bought; a ship may be prohibited from discharging waste at sea only if there is somewhere ashore to land it. In each case the obligation on the shipowner is matched by an obligation on States - to promote fuel availability, and to provide adequate reception facilities - and GISIS is the feedback channel that shows whether those State-side obligations are being met.

So the usefulness runs at four levels. For the ship, it is operational: plan the waste discharge and the bunkering with knowledge of what is actually there. For the company, it is evidential: a documented report is the defence against a deficiency, and it is what turns a commercial dispute into a regulatory record. For the port and flag States, it creates an obligation that can be seen to be discharged or not. And for the Organization, aggregation is the whole point - it converts scattered individual complaints into evidence of a systemic shortage at a named port or in a named region, which is what justifies technical cooperation, guidance or, in the end, an amendment. Its limitation follows from the same design: GISIS reports only what is reported, so under-reporting appears in the data as compliance.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) has two demands - the key functions and components, and how it facilitates data sharing. Give the modules as a list, because a list of modules is counted, and then give the data-sharing benefits as a second list. Two lists, eight marks, no prose padding.

Step 7 is the discussion, and it is what lifts the limb above description.

The printed question has a looseness worth handling deliberately

Limb (b) says 'this module', in the singular, and then asks about two different things - compliant fuel oil, and inadequacy of port reception facilities. These are handled in different parts of GISIS: fuel oil availability sits with the MARPOL and marine environment reporting, and reception facilities have their own module. Do not try to force them into one. Answer both, and note in a clause that they are separate reporting streams serving the same purpose. Recognising the looseness and dealing with it cleanly reads as competence, not pedantry.

The one idea that unifies limb (b)

Every obligation MARPOL places on the ship has a matching obligation on the State, and GISIS is how you find out whether the State kept its side.

  • The ship must burn compliant fuel - so Parties must promote its availability, and non-availability must be reportable.
  • The ship must not discharge waste at sea - so Parties must provide adequate reception facilities, and inadequacy must be reportable.

State that once and the whole of limb (b) becomes an argument rather than two descriptions. It is also the honest answer to 'why does the Organization bother collecting this?'

Common mistakes

  • Describing GISIS as a ship-tracking or AIS-type system. It is a reporting and information system, not an operational traffic picture.
  • Saying it is entirely public. It is mixed - some modules open after free registration, others restricted to Member State users.
  • Saying IMO writes the content. Member States enter it through designated focal points; the Secretariat administers the platform.
  • Reciting a memorised list of modules as though it were fixed and complete. The set changes administratively, and saying so is safer and more accurate than a confident wrong list.
  • Answering limb (b) without naming a single regulation. Regulation 18 for fuel oil availability and quality, regulation 17 for reception facilities - both MARPOL Annex VI, chapter 3.
  • Treating a non-availability report as a waiver or exemption. It is a report of fact made after best efforts; it does not authorise burning non-compliant fuel as a matter of course, and it invites scrutiny rather than ending it.
  • Forgetting that a reception facility report is of an ALLEGED inadequacy, and that the port State is expected to respond.

Examiner traps

  • Who enters the data? The Member State's designated focal point - not IMO, and not the ship.
  • What is the sulphur limit that makes fuel availability an issue? 0.50% m/m globally from 1 January 2020, and 0.10% m/m in emission control areas.
  • Where does a non-availability report go? To the ship's Administration and to the competent authority of the port of destination - and from there onward to the Organization. Not to IMO directly from the ship.
  • Who reports an inadequate reception facility, and to whom? The master, through the flag Administration, which forwards to the port State and the Organization.
  • Does GISIS decide anything? No. It records claims, responses and reported facts. It is not an adjudicating body.
  • What is its principal weakness? It reflects only what States report, so a gap in the data can mean either that there is no problem or that nobody reported one - and those are not the same thing.

The regulatory hooks are corpus-backed: MARPOL Annex VI regulations 17 and 18 were resolved this session through the MIW consumer adapter, which returns their identity, chapter placement and title from the governed corpus object. Model written answer

(a) Immediate actions after the blackout

1. Secure the ship first - bridge, emergency power, steering

At full ahead a blackout stops every electrically driven auxiliary at once - lubricating oil, cooling water and fuel booster pumps - so the main engine trips or must be stopped, and the ship is left with way on and no propulsion. The first duty is the ship's safety, not the engine.

  • Inform the bridge immediately that propulsion is lost and that steering is on its emergency supply. The master decides on the navigational response - not-under-command lights or shapes, a warning to traffic and the VTS, and anchoring if the depth and sea room allow.
  • Confirm the emergency source has taken the load. SOLAS chapter II-1 requires the emergency generator of a cargo ship to start and connect automatically, and to supply its services within 45 seconds; on a passenger ship the emergency source must likewise supply within 45 seconds, and a transitional source of emergency power is also mandatory. Check the emergency switchboard is live; start the generator by hand if it has not.
  • Check the essential emergency loads: emergency lighting, steering gear, emergency fire pump, navigation lights and communications.
  • Muster the engine-room team and allocate tasks; the Chief Engineer goes to the control room.

2. Find the cause before restoring power

Restoring power onto a fault turns one blackout into two. Before any breaker is closed:

  • Read the alarm and event log of the power management system and switchboard to see what tripped first.
  • Decide whether a generator tripped (fuel starvation, governor fault, high temperature, low lubricating oil pressure, overspeed) or whether a fault on the switchboard or a feeder took the bus down (short circuit, earth fault, reverse power, overload).
  • If a feeder or consumer is at fault, isolate it before re-energising the bus.
  • Check the fuel system in particular - a choked filter, water in the service tank or a fuel changeover in progress is a common cause and will trip the next generator too.

3. Restore main power and essential services in sequence

  • Start a standby generator - most plants do this automatically on blackout; otherwise start it by hand and confirm its own fuel, lubricating oil and cooling.
  • Close it onto the dead bus once voltage and frequency are correct, and confirm the emergency switchboard's supply from the main board is restored as designed.
  • Restart the essential auxiliaries in sequence, one at a time, because several large motors starting together will overload a single generator and black the ship out again: main engine lubricating oil and jacket cooling, sea water cooling, fuel supply and booster pumps, then steering pumps on main supply, then air compressors and the remaining services. Many plants have an automatic sequential restart; check it has run rather than assume it.
  • Bring a second generator on line and share load before any heavy consumer such as a bow thruster or cargo pump is started.

4. Restart propulsion safely, then record and report

  • Restart the main engine only when lubricating oil pressure, cooling water and fuel conditions are normal; a slow-speed engine on heavy fuel needs the fuel at its correct temperature and viscosity. Follow the maker's restart instructions after an emergency stop.
  • Report propulsion available to the bridge; the master decides when it is used.
  • Record times, alarms and actions in the logbooks, report to the company, and treat the event as a non-conformity to be investigated under the safety management system.

(b)(i) Making a blackout less likely

5. Remove the common causes

Blackouts are rarely random. Most come from a handful of causes, and each has a systemic answer:

  • Fuel - bunker quality and compatibility checks, settling and service tanks drained of water, filters and purifiers maintained, and a written fuel changeover procedure (for example before entering an emission control area), because changeovers are a well-known cause of generator trips.
  • Generators - governors, automatic voltage regulators and protective devices maintained and their settings verified; load tests after overhaul.
  • Switchboards - breakers serviced and tested, connections checked by thermographic survey, insulation and earth faults monitored.
  • Load discipline - no large motor started without sufficient generating capacity on line.

6. Engineer the plant so one failure is not a blackout

  • Keep the power management system in automatic, with the standby generator selected to start on high load or loss of a running set.
  • Run two generators in parallel during manoeuvring, in restricted waters and on port approaches, so the loss of one is not a blackout.
  • Keep preferential tripping of non-essential loads correctly set and tested, so an overload sheds load instead of tripping the generator.
  • Test the automatic blackout restart and sequential start, and the emergency generator with its automatic start, on a regular schedule.
  • List the generators, switchboards and their controls as critical equipment under the safety management system, with planned maintenance and spares.

(b)(ii) A trained team that recovers fast

7. A written procedure, clear roles and real drills

  • A written blackout recovery procedure and checklist, posted in the control room and at the emergency switchboard, matched to this ship's plant.
  • Named roles - who goes to the switchboard, who to the generators, who to the main engine, who communicates with the bridge.
  • Regular blackout and dead-ship recovery drills, including unannounced ones and drills run with the bridge team, so that the procedure is practised rather than read.
  • Familiarisation for every new joiner with the switchboard, the emergency generator and the restart sequence, as the ISM Code requires for duties relating to safety.

8. Team behaviour, communication and learning

  • Engine room resource management - clear leadership by the Chief Engineer, closed-loop communication, and a shared picture of the plant.
  • One coordinator in the control room; nobody operates a breaker or starts a large consumer without that person knowing.
  • Debrief after every drill and every real event, update the procedure, and report near misses and lessons through the safety management system so other ships in the fleet learn too.

Understand this first

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

General average is the principle that where one interest is deliberately sacrificed to save the whole, the loss is shared rateably by all the interests saved. It is given effect not by statute but by the York-Antwerp Rules, incorporated by contract; the current edition is the 2016 Rules. In India the statutory definition appears in section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

Rule A states the test: there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure. The words when, and only when matter - the conditions are cumulative.

2. The essential features, all of which must be present

  1. Extraordinary - outside the ordinary incidents of the voyage. Fuel burnt on a normal passage, or wear and tear, is not; nor is anything the owner was already bound to do.
  2. Intentional - a deliberate act. This is the heart of the doctrine: accidental loss, however catastrophic, is never general average. Cargo washed overboard is particular average; cargo jettisoned is general average.
  3. Reasonable - judged on the circumstances as they appeared at the time, reinforced by the Rule Paramount: in no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.
  4. For the common safety - for the whole adventure. Action to save only the ship, or only the cargo, is not general average.
  5. A real peril - real and substantial, though it need not be immediate or inevitable.
  6. A common maritime adventure, and a useful result - more than one interest at risk, or there is nobody to contribute, and something must be saved, since contribution is assessed on the values preserved.

3. What is excluded, and who must prove it

  • Rule C allows only the direct consequence of the general average act, and excludes demurrage, loss of market, any loss or expense by reason of delay and any indirect loss whatsoever, and environmental damage or loss consequent on the escape of pollutants.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected though the event was due to the fault of a party, without prejudice to remedies or defences for that fault. Contribution and liability are separate questions.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming; failing particulars within twelve months the adjuster may estimate.

4. b) Testing the casualty against the rules

Taking the Rule A elements against the facts - aground, the main engine worked ahead and astern to move her from the ground, bearings burnt through excessive load:

  • extraordinary - burnt bearings from deliberate overload are outside the ordinary incidents of the voyage, and are not wear and tear;
  • intentional - working the engine hard astern to refloat is a deliberate act of the master;
  • for the common safety - a grounded vessel endangers ship, cargo and freight alike;
  • direct consequence - the damage arose immediately from that act, satisfying Rule C;
  • peril - the one element the question does not settle, and it must be examined rather than assumed.

5. Rule VII, which decides the point

The Rules deal with this precise situation expressly. Rule VII, Damage to Machinery and Boilers: Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.

Three conditions and one absolute bar. The conditions: ashore; in a position of peril; machinery worked with an actual intention to float her for the common safety, at the risk of such damage. The bar: if she is afloat, machinery damage from working the engines is never allowed.

6. Conclusion, and what must be done to sustain it

Yes - the bearing damage can be included in general average, provided the Rule VII conditions are established. The first is satisfied: she was aground. The other two are questions of evidence:

  • Position of peril. Hard aground on a falling tide, on a lee shore or on rock, taking water, or with weather deteriorating - peril is readily shown. Merely touching soft ground in sheltered water, certain to float on the next tide, is not; the act was one of convenience and the claim fails.
  • Actual intention, accepting the risk. The master must have worked the engine knowing it might be damaged and accepted that to free the ship. Engines worked to test the ground, or in routine hope of coming off, do not meet the rule.
  • Evidence. Timed deck and engine log entries, the master's recorded assessment of the danger, engine parameters and alarms, soundings, weather, owner or salvor advice. The onus is on the claimant under Rule E.

Two points complete the answer. The damage must be reasonable in extent under the Rule Paramount. And by Rule D, fault does not destroy the right to contribution, though cargo may refuse to contribute if the casualty was caused by a fault actionable under the contract of carriage - for example unseaworthiness through want of due diligence. Negligent navigation is normally an excepted peril under the Hague-Visby Rules (applied in India by the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025), so it does not on its own give cargo that defence. In practice the owner declares general average, appoints an average adjuster and releases cargo against a general average bond and an average guarantee.

Why the law inverts ordinary intuition

The rule that deliberate damage is recoverable and accidental damage is not looks perverse until you see what it is for. General average is a cost-allocation mechanism, not a compensation scheme. Its purpose is to remove the master's conflict of interest at the moment of crisis: if the shipowner alone bore the cost of sacrificing the ship's machinery, or of jettisoning cargo, the person best placed to save the adventure would have a financial reason to hesitate. By promising that a reasonable, deliberate sacrifice will be shared by everyone who benefits, the doctrine makes the right decision the cheap decision. Accidental loss needs no such encouragement, which is why it stays where it falls.

The afloat proviso, and why it is absolute

Rule VII's second half is unusually emphatic - in any circumstances. The reason is evidential rather than moral. When a ship is afloat, working the propelling machinery is exactly what she is supposed to do, so there is no way to distinguish an extraordinary sacrifice from ordinary use, and a claim would be unanswerable and unverifiable. When she is ashore, the act is visibly abnormal and the intention can be tested. The Rules solve the evidential problem by drawing a bright line.

(b) Yes, allowable - subject to proof, and the governing provision is Rule VII, Damage to Machinery and Boilers: damage to machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, is allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where the ship is afloat, no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers is in any circumstances allowed. So the burnt bearings qualify if the vessel was genuinely in peril and the engine was worked with that intention, accepting the risk - both questions of evidence, proved from the log, engine records and the master's recorded decision.

Model written answer

1. Which text applies, and how the Convention is built

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is an ILO instrument which entered into force on 20 August 2013 and is generally described as the fourth pillar of the international regulatory regime, alongside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW.

Say how it is built, because all four limbs of this question sit inside that structure:

  • Articles - the treaty obligations of the Member;
  • Regulations - the substantive requirements;
  • Code Part A - Standards, which are mandatory;
  • Code Part B - Guidelines, which are guidance to be given due consideration.
  • Five Titles, of which Title 5 is compliance and enforcement and divides into flag State, port State and labour-supplying responsibilities - the same three-way split this question follows.

One temporal point belongs at the top. At this sitting the operative text is the Convention as amended through the 2022 amendments - the amendments approved by the International Labour Conference at its 110th Session in 2022, in force since 23 December 2024 - among them access to social connectivity, free drinking water, appropriately sized personal protective equipment, strengthened food and catering provisions, and the repatriation of the remains of a seafarer who dies on board. The amendments approved by the 113th Session of the International Labour Conference on 6 June 2025 - among them shore leave without a visa or special permit, and recognition of seafarers as key workers (a Guideline) - are adopted but not in force; their expected entry into force is 23 December 2027. Nothing from that set is a present obligation.

(a) Flag State and port State responsibilities

2. Flag State and port State responsibilities

Flag State. The flag State must establish an effective system of inspection and certification of labour conditions, ensuring that ships flying its flag comply.

  • It may authorise recognized organizations to carry out inspection and certification, but it remains fully responsible for the work done on its behalf, must satisfy itself of their competence and independence, and must make the list public.
  • Ships of 500 gross tonnage and over on international voyages, or operating from or between foreign ports, must carry a Maritime Labour Certificate and a Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance; others may be certified on request.
  • The DMLC is in two parts, and the split is the classic examination point. Part I is drawn up by the competent authority and identifies the national requirements; Part II is drawn up by the shipowner and sets out the measures adopted on the ship to ensure ongoing compliance.
  • The certificate is valid for five years, with an intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates; an interim certificate may be issued for six months.
  • The interval between inspections shall in no case exceed three years. Inspectors must be qualified and independent, must report to the authority and the master, and have power to require rectification and to prohibit the ship from sailing until deficiencies are put right.

Port State. Any foreign ship calling in the port of a Member may be inspected to determine compliance.

  • But the certificate and the DMLC must be accepted as prima facie evidence of compliance, so the ordinary inspection is a document review. Without that presumption the certificate would be worth nothing and every port would re-audit every ship.
  • The port State inspection is limited to the Articles, the Regulations and Part A of the Code - the mandatory material. Part B Guidelines are not the measure of compliance.
  • No more favourable treatment. A ship flying the flag of a State that has not ratified the Convention is entitled to no better treatment than one that has - which is what stops non-ratification from becoming a commercial advantage.
  • Where more is needed, the officer proceeds to a more detailed inspection, which is limb (c).

(b) On-board and on-shore complaint procedures

3. The on-board complaint procedure

Every ship must carry on-board procedures for the fair, effective and expeditious handling of seafarer complaints alleging breaches of the Convention.

  • The design preference is resolution at the lowest level - the complaint is raised with the head of department and worked upward.
  • But that preference is expressly without prejudice to the seafarer's right to complain directly to the master, and to complain directly to external authorities - the flag State administration, or an authorized officer in a port of call. A procedure that required him to exhaust the chain of command first would not comply.
  • A copy of the procedures must be provided to every seafarer, together with contact information for the competent authority of the flag State and of the seafarer's country of residence, and the name of a person on board who can advise him impartially and otherwise assist him.
  • The seafarer has the right to be accompanied or represented during the procedure.
  • Victimization for filing a complaint is prohibited and must be penalized. This is the provision that makes everything else usable: without it the machinery exists and nobody dares use it.

4. The onshore complaint-handling procedure

A seafarer may bring a complaint to an authorized officer in the port at which the ship has called. The procedure is an escalation with three rungs:

  • Ship level first. The officer makes an initial investigation, and where the complaint can be resolved at ship level he seeks that resolution - the on-board procedure is used or reinstated, and where the complaint concerns matters within a more detailed inspection he proceeds accordingly.
  • Flag State next. If it cannot be resolved on board, the officer notifies the flag State, seeking advice and a corrective plan of action, with a deadline.
  • The ILO last. Where the matter remains unresolved, the port State sends a copy of the report, with the flag State's response, to the Director-General of the International Labour Office, so that the failure is recorded internationally; the shipowners' and seafarers' organisations in the port State are similarly informed.
  • The source of the complaint is kept confidential at every stage, and the officer must take appropriate steps to safeguard it - including giving no intimation to the master or the owner that an inspection was made on account of a complaint.

(c) Detainable deficiencies under flag State and port State intervention

5. Detainable deficiencies under flag State and port State intervention

Begin with what the Convention does not do. Unlike the regional port State control memoranda, the MLC publishes no list of detainable deficiencies. It states two grounds, and a candidate who recites a memorandum's list has answered from the wrong instrument.

Port State intervention - the two grounds. A foreign ship may be detained where the conditions on board are shown, following a more detailed inspection, to be:

  • clearly hazardous to the safety, health or security of seafarers; or
  • a serious or repeated breach of the requirements of the Convention, including seafarers' rights.

Flag State intervention uses the same two grounds. The flag State's inspectors may require deficiencies to be remedied and, where they have grounds to believe that deficiencies are a serious breach of the Convention, including seafarers' rights, or a significant danger to seafarers' safety, health or security, they may prohibit the ship from leaving port until the necessary action is taken (Standard A5.1.4). The difference is reach: the flag State can stop its own ship in any port; the port State can detain a foreign ship only after a more detailed inspection.

The route to them matters as much as the grounds themselves. Detention is reached only through a more detailed inspection, and that inspection may be carried out only where one of four triggers is present:

  • the required documents are not produced, or are not maintained, or are falsely maintained, or do not contain the information required;
  • there are clear grounds for believing the working and living conditions do not conform;
  • there are reasonable grounds to believe the ship changed flag to avoid compliance;
  • there is a complaint alleging that specific conditions do not conform.

What follows a finding.

  • The deficiencies are to be rectified; or, where rectification cannot be completed in that port, the officer may accept a plan of action with a timetable.
  • The ship is not released until the hazard is removed or the plan accepted.
  • The flag State is notified forthwith, and the shipowners' and seafarers' organisations in the port State are informed.
  • Compensation is payable for any loss or damage suffered where a ship is unduly detained or delayed. The Convention places the burden of proof in each case on the complainant, and that provision is the discipline on the inspector.

(d) Grievance redressal mechanisms for Indian seafarers

6. Grievance redressal for Indian seafarers

Answer this in three layers, because a single list would hide the point.

Layer 1 - the Convention's own route, which every Indian seafarer already has. India is a ratifying Member, so the whole of the machinery in limbs (b) and (c) is available to him: the on-board procedure with its impartial adviser and its protection from victimization; direct complaint to the master; direct complaint to the flag State administration; complaint to an authorized officer in any port of a ratifying Member; and, at the end, the Director-General of the ILO.

Layer 2 - what India can actually reach, and this is the substance of the limb.

  • For an Indian-flag ship, India is the flag State and the full inspection, certification and enforcement apparatus of Title 5 is Indian.
  • But most Indian seafarers serve on foreign-flag ships, where India is neither the flag State nor, usually, the port State. The operative head is then India's labour-supplying responsibility, which is the third limb of Title 5 and is the one that exists precisely for large seafarer-supplying countries.
  • Under it, and under the recruitment and placement provisions, India licenses and regulates crewing agents, requires registers to be kept and employment agreements to be verified, enforces the prohibition on charging recruitment fees to the seafarer - save for the statutory medical certificate, the seafarer's book and a passport - and requires a system of protection to compensate the seafarer for monetary loss arising from the agent's failure to meet its obligations.
  • That is where the practical remedy lies. The foreign owner is beyond India's reach; the agent who put the man on board is not.

Layer 3 - the domestic frame.

  • The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, in force since 15 March 2026 (repealing the 1958 Act), is the statute through which India gives effect to its flag State, port State and labour-supplying obligations.
  • The shipping master is the officer concerned with engagement and discharge of seafarers and with disputes over wages arising from them.
  • The maritime administration is the Directorate General of Maritime Administration (DGMA) - the Directorate General of Shipping as reconstituted under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. Its e-Navik portal carries a round-the-clock grievance redressal mechanism open to seafarers and their families, with a ticketed record of each complaint.
  • Practical actors, but not law: the seafarers' unions, and the ITF inspectorate, which in practice recovers a great deal of unpaid wage.

No circular number, named cell, named office, forum or tribunal is asserted here, and no section of the 2025 Act is cited by number. The machinery above is stated at the level at which it is verifiable.

7. The thread that joins the four limbs

Close by connecting them, because the connection is the understanding the examiner is testing.

The flag State certifies and the port State verifies - and both can be entirely satisfied while a seafarer sits unpaid on a properly certified ship. Certification catches systems; it does not catch individual wrongs. What closes that gap is the complaint - and that is exactly why a complaint is not merely a welfare mechanism but one of the four triggers that entitles an officer to stop treating the certificate as conclusive, carry out a more detailed inspection, and if necessary detain the ship.

Limb (d) is the same thread followed to its Indian end. India cannot inspect a foreign ship in a foreign port; what it can do is regulate the agent who is within its reach, and make that agent answerable for the engagement it arranged.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four limbs at four marks each. The framing section is short and buys structure for the whole answer by establishing the Articles / Regulations / Part A / Part B architecture, which every limb afterwards uses without re-explaining.

Limb (b) is given two numbered sections because the on-board and onshore procedures are separate machinery with separate rules. Running them together is the commonest way to lose half that limb. The closing section is worth writing even under time pressure: it is where the candidate shows he understands why the pieces are arranged as they are, and it takes four lines.

Common mistakes

  • Reciting the Paris MoU list of detainable deficiencies. That is a different instrument. The MLC publishes no list and gives two grounds.
  • Saying Part I of the DMLC is drawn up by the shipowner. Part I is the competent authority's; Part II is the shipowner's.
  • Describing port State inspection as a full audit. The certificate is prima facie evidence; the ordinary inspection is a document review.
  • Treating the on-board procedure as compulsory before any external complaint. The lowest-level preference is expressly without prejudice to direct complaint to the master or to external authorities.
  • Answering limb (d) as though India were the flag State. For most Indian seafarers it is not; the operative head is labour-supplying responsibility.
  • Inventing an Indian forum. No cell, tribunal, circular or section number should appear.
  • Answering from the pre-2024 text. The 2022 amendments have been in force since 23 December 2024 and are law at this sitting.
  • Citing the 2025 amendments as current obligations. Shore leave without a visa (new Standard A2.4) and key-worker recognition (a Guideline) are adopted, with entry into force expected on 23 December 2027.
  • Citing the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. It was repealed on 15 March 2026.

Examiner traps

  • Part B is guidance, not the measure of compliance. A port State inspection is limited to the Articles, the Regulations and Part A.
  • The MLC is amended by the ILO's own tripartite machinery, not by IMO tacit acceptance. Candidates habitually import the IMO procedure.
  • 'Detainable deficiency' is port State control vocabulary. The examiner has used it; answer it with the MLC grounds and say so.
  • Compensation for undue detention is a real provision and is the check on the inspector. It is regularly omitted.
  • Confidentiality of the source runs through the whole complaint machinery, including that the master is not to be told an inspection arose from a complaint.
  • Victimization is prohibited AND penalized. Both words matter - a prohibition without a penalty would be decorative.
  • The administration at this sitting is the DGMA - the Directorate General of Shipping reconstituted under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. Naming 'DG Shipping' is understood, but the current name scores.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Who signs Part II of the DMLC, and what is in it?
  • A seafarer tells you he has not been paid for three months. Walk me through what happens.
  • On what grounds can a port State detain a ship under the MLC, and who pays if you are wrong?

  • What is the difference between the MLC route to detention and the SOLAS route?

Memory framework

Follow the seafarer outward, one rung at a time, and the four limbs come in the printed order:

  • Who certifies, and who checks? - flag State and port State (limb a).
  • How does he complain on board? - lowest level, but direct access preserved, and no victimization (limb b, first half).
  • How does he complain ashore? - authorized officer, then flag State, then the ILO (limb b, second half).
  • When is the ship stopped? - two grounds, four triggers, one more detailed inspection (limb c).
  • What can India reach? - not the foreign owner; the agent (limb d).

(a) The bill of lading and its three legal functions

1. What a bill of lading is

A bill of lading is the document issued by or on behalf of the carrier - signed by the master or the carrier's agent - acknowledging that goods have been received for shipment or shipped on board a named vessel, for carriage to a named destination and delivery there to the person entitled.

Under the Hague-Visby Rules the carrier must, on the shipper's demand, issue a bill showing the leading marks, the number of packages or the quantity or weight, and the apparent order and condition of the goods. India's governing statutes changed in 2025: the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 (Act 18 of 2025) and the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 (Act 19 of 2025), both in force from 10 September 2025, repealed the Indian Bills of Lading Act, 1856 and the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925. The 2025 COGSA's Schedule applies the Hague-Visby Rules by statute.

2. Function 1 - a receipt for the goods

The bill is the carrier's receipt: it records what was shipped and in what apparent order and condition. A clean bill carries no adverse remark; a claused bill notes defects. Under the Hague-Visby Rules the bill is prima facie evidence of receipt as described, and conclusive once it has been transferred to a third party acting in good faith. That is why the master must not sign a clean bill for goods he knows are damaged.

3. Function 2 - evidence of the contract of carriage

The contract of carriage is usually concluded before the bill is issued - on booking, or under a charterparty - so between the original parties the bill is evidence of the contract's terms, not the contract itself. Once it reaches the consignee or endorsee to whom the property in the goods passes, the bill becomes the contract between that person and the carrier, and the rights - and liabilities - of suit pass with it; in India that transfer is governed by the Bills of Lading Act, 2025.

4. Function 3 - a document of title

The bill is a document of title: holding it represents the right to take delivery of the goods, so the goods can be sold or pledged while still at sea by transferring the bill. The carrier, in return, delivers only against presentation of an original bill. This function is what lets banks finance trade under letters of credit, holding the bill as security.

One caveat the examiner may test: the full document-of-title function belongs to a negotiable bill - an order or bearer bill. A straight bill is not negotiable, as part (b) shows.

(b) Straight, order and bearer bills - and how delivery is effected

5. The straight bill

A straight bill names a specific consignee and contains no words such as 'to order'. It is not negotiable: it cannot be transferred to anyone else by endorsement, so it suits sales where the goods will not be resold in transit, or shipments between related companies.

Delivery: to the named consignee only, on proof of identity. Whether the carrier may deliver without the original being presented depends on the governing law and the bill's terms; English law, for example, has held that an original must be presented. The safe practice is to deliver against an original straight bill unless the bill expressly provides otherwise.

6. The order bill

An order bill is made out 'to order', 'to the order of the shipper' or 'to the order of' a named party such as the buyer's bank. It is negotiable: it passes by endorsement and delivery. A special endorsement names the next holder; an endorsement in blank (signature only) turns it into a bearer bill.

Delivery: to the person who presents an original bill bearing an unbroken chain of endorsements down to himself. This is the normal bill for documentary credits and for cargo sold in transit.

7. The bearer bill

A bearer bill is made out 'to bearer', or is an order bill endorsed in blank. It passes by delivery alone - whoever physically holds it is the holder.

Delivery: to whoever presents an original. It is the easiest bill to transfer and the most dangerous: a lost or stolen bearer bill can be used to take the cargo, so it is rarely used deliberately.

Understand this first

Strip the two limbs down and they are one idea seen twice. The first asks what the law of the sea means by pollution and what it therefore requires of the State whose coastline is at risk; the second asks who actually pays when the pollution happens anyway. The definition is deliberately enormous - anything a person puts into the sea, whether a substance or merely energy such as heat or noise, that is likely to do harm, even if no harm has yet occurred. Because it is that wide, the duty it creates has to be split up, and the way the Convention splits it is the real content of the first limb: a State has almost a free hand over what it does on its own land, and almost none over what it demands of a foreign ship, because a ship moves and belongs to somebody else. So the rules for ships are made through the international organisation, and the coastal State's power to enforce them grows as the evidence and the seriousness grow. The second limb follows from the last piece of the first: having imposed a duty, the Convention also requires that victims can actually recover. For oil that recovery is built in layers, and the reason it is layered is that no single shipowner could carry the cost of a bad spill and no insurer would write unlimited cover. So the cost is shared between the ship and the cargo - the owner pays first, strictly but only up to a limit, and beyond that the people who receive the oil pay through funds they finance. Once you see that the whole scheme is ship-then-cargo, the details stop being a list to memorise.

Model written answer

1. The Convention's definition of pollution, and what makes it unusually wide

UNCLOS article 1(1)(4) defines "pollution of the marine environment" as the introduction by man, directly or indirectly, of substances or energy into the marine environment, including estuaries, which results or is likely to result in such deleterious effects as: harm to living resources and marine life; hazards to human health; hindrance to marine activities, including fishing and other legitimate uses of the sea; impairment of quality for use of sea water; and reduction of amenities.

Five features of that wording earn marks, and each should be stated as a point:

  1. "By man" - a natural seep or a volcanic discharge is not pollution under the Convention, however damaging.
  2. "Directly or indirectly" - a river carrying industrial waste to the sea is caught, so the polluting act need not happen at sea at all.
  3. "Substances or energy" - heat, noise and radiation are pollution. This is the element most candidates omit and it is the one that makes the definition modern.
  4. "Results or is likely to result" - no damage need have occurred. Likelihood is enough, which is what allows preventive regulation.
  5. "Reduction of amenities" - purely aesthetic and recreational harm counts. Oil on a beach is pollution even where nothing is killed.

Note finally that the definition is source-neutral. Nothing in it mentions ships. Ships are one source among land-based sources, dumping, seabed activities and the atmosphere, and that is why the coastal State's obligations are structured source by source.

2. The general obligation, and why it is not confined to any zone

Article 192 states the whole of Part XII in one sentence: States have the obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment. It is free-standing and not zone-limited - the rest of the Convention allocates rights zone by zone, but this imposes a duty across all of them, including the high seas.

Article 193 then makes the balance explicit: States have the sovereign right to exploit their natural resources pursuant to their environmental policies and in accordance with their duty to protect and preserve the marine environment. Development is permitted; it is not unconditioned.

Article 194 is the operative obligation:

  • 194(1) - all measures necessary to prevent, reduce and control pollution from any source, using the best practicable means at their disposal and in accordance with their capabilities, endeavouring to harmonize policies. Note that the standard is expressly capability-relative.
  • 194(2) - activities under a State's jurisdiction or control must not cause damage by pollution to other States, and pollution from incidents under its control must not spread beyond its zones.
  • 194(3)(b) - for vessels specifically: preventing accidents, dealing with emergencies, ensuring safety of operations, preventing intentional and unintentional discharges, and regulating design, construction, equipment, operation and manning.
  • 194(5) - measures to protect rare or fragile ecosystems and the habitat of depleted, threatened or endangered species.

Two short articles complete the frame and are easy marks. Article 195 forbids a State, in taking measures, from transferring damage or hazards from one area to another, or transforming one type of pollution into another - so scrubbing a stack emission into the sea is not compliance. Article 196 covers harmful technologies and the introduction of alien or new species, which is the treaty root of ballast water and biofouling control.

Article 211 is the one that matters for ships, and its internal structure is the answer to "key features":

  • 211(1) - rules and standards are established through the competent international organization, that is the IMO, and are re-examined from time to time.
  • 211(2) - a flag State's own laws must have at least the same effect as those international standards. Stricter for your own ships, yes; laxer, no.
  • 211(4) - in the territorial sea a coastal State may legislate for foreign vessels including those in innocent passage, but such laws shall not hamper innocent passage.
  • 211(5) - in the exclusive economic zone it may legislate only conforming to and giving effect to generally accepted international rules. It cannot invent its own standard.
  • 211(6) - the one escape route: for a particular, clearly defined area of its EEZ where the international rules are inadequate, it may submit scientific and technical evidence to the IMO; the IMO determines within 12 months; the measures bind foreign vessels only 15 months after submission; and they may never impose design, construction, manning or equipment standards other than the generally accepted international ones.

That last prohibition is the single most quotable limit in Part XII. It is why a coastal State can impose a discharge or routeing requirement but cannot demand a different hull, and it is the legal basis on which Special Areas and Particularly Sensitive Sea Areas are created through the IMO rather than declared unilaterally.

4. The coastal State's obligations - enforcement, and its limits

Rule-making and enforcement are separately allocated, and confusing them is the commonest error on this question.

  • Article 217 - the flag State. The primary enforcement duty: compliance by ships of its flag wherever the violation occurs.
  • Article 218 - the port State. Where a ship is voluntarily in port, proceedings may be taken for a discharge outside that State's own waters - a jurisdiction that exists nowhere else in the Convention.
  • Article 220 - the coastal State, and its powers are deliberately graded: proceedings where the ship is voluntarily in port for a violation in its territorial sea or EEZ (220(1)); on clear grounds for a territorial-sea violation, physical inspection and, where the evidence warrants, proceedings including detention (220(2)); and for an EEZ violation, first only the power to require information - identity, port of registry, last and next port of call (220(3)) - escalating to inspection and then to detention as the evidence and the gravity of the discharge increase.
  • Article 221 - after a maritime casualty, the right to take and enforce measures beyond the territorial sea, proportionate to the actual or threatened damage, to protect the coastline and related interests including fishing, where major harmful consequences may reasonably be expected. The article carries its own definition of maritime casualty.
  • Article 235 - States are responsible for fulfilling their international obligations and must ensure recourse for prompt and adequate compensation for pollution damage.

The safeguards in section 7 of Part XII then constrain how all of this is exercised. The shape of the coastal State's position is therefore: a duty everywhere, rule-making power that narrows as you move seaward, and enforcement power that is graded by evidence and by seriousness. And article 235 is the bridge to the second limb - the compensation regime exists because UNCLOS requires that recourse.

5. Why the oil pollution compensation regime has three tiers

Before listing the tiers, say why there are tiers. A major tanker spill can cost more than any shipowner or his insurer could reasonably carry. Unlimited liability would have made tankers uninsurable; leaving victims uncompensated was politically and legally unacceptable. The compromise reached was that the shipowner is liable strictly but with a limit, and the balance is placed on the cargo interest - those who receive the oil.

The defining idea of the regime is therefore that the cost of an oil spill is shared between the ship and the cargo. Every feature below follows from it.

Three points here separate a good answer from a list:

  1. The cargo interest pays for tiers two and three - not the shipowner and not governments. The Funds are financed by the oil receivers in the member States. This is the fact most candidates get wrong.
  2. The tiers are cumulative and sequential, each with its own overall ceiling for any one incident, and each tier is reached only when the one below it is exhausted or unavailable.
  3. Tier three is optional. A State must already be a member of the 1992 Fund and must separately accede to the 2003 Protocol. So the compensation available for the same casualty depends on where it happens.

Note the answer to "certification required by ship under each tier" precisely: only tier one produces a certificate carried by the ship. Saying so, and explaining why - the Funds are financed ashore, not by the ship - is worth more than inventing two more certificates.

8. What the three tiers do NOT cover - and the trap

  • The regime is about persistent oil carried in bulk as cargo - in substance, the laden or residue-carrying tanker case. It is not a general marine pollution compensation scheme.
  • Bunker oil spilled from a non-tanker is the Bunkers Convention 2001: strict liability on a wider group of owners, compulsory insurance and a certificate, but a single tier with no fund behind it. A candidate who offers Bunkers as one of the three tiers has misread the question.
  • Hazardous and noxious substances have their own convention, which is not in force at this sitting. It cannot be cited as a tier or as current law.
  • Geography matters. The tiers respond only in States party, and the third only where that State has opted in.
  • In India, domestic effect to the civil liability and fund regimes is given through the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, in force since 15 March 2026 (it repealed the 1958 Act), and rules made under it. India is a Party to CLC 1992 and the Fund Convention 1992 but not to the Supplementary Fund Protocol 2003, so in Indian waters only the first two tiers respond.

Close by joining the two limbs, because the examiner printed them together: UNCLOS article 235 obliges States to ensure recourse for prompt and adequate compensation for pollution damage, and the three tiers are how that obligation is discharged for oil. The first limb states the duty; the second shows the machinery.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs at 8 marks each, and a very wide subject. The risk is not ignorance - most candidates know something about UNCLOS and something about CLC - it is drift: writing a general essay on marine pollution and never answering either printed instruction. The route defends against that by anchoring each limb on the words the examiner actually used.

Limb A prints two demands - "define" and "discuss the key features of obligations of coastal states". Definition is a quotation task: reproduce article 1(1)(4) as closely as you can and then dissect it, because every element of it is a scoring proposition. Obligations is a structure task: the marks are in showing that the Convention allocates rule-making and enforcement separately, and that the coastal State's freedom narrows as it moves seaward.

Limb B prints three demands and they are easy to leave one out: who pays, the source of funds, and the certification required by the ship, in each case. Three demands times three tiers is nine propositions, and they are best written as a table. Step 5 - why there are tiers at all - is what turns the table from a memorised list into an explanation, and step 8 is where the examiner separates candidates by asking, in effect, what the regime is not.

The definition is worth learning word for word

Very few provisions repay verbatim memory as well as article 1(1)(4), because the marks are distributed across its clauses. If you can write "the introduction by man, directly or indirectly, of substances or energy into the marine environment, including estuaries, which results or is likely to result in such deleterious effects as..." you have earned several propositions before you have explained anything.

The five deleterious effects are: harm to living resources and marine life; hazards to human health; hindrance to marine activities including fishing and other legitimate uses of the sea; impairment of quality for use of sea water; and reduction of amenities. Reduction of amenities and or energy are the two most frequently omitted, and both are distinctive enough to be noticed.

Rule-making and enforcement are different questions

Candidates routinely answer "what are the coastal State's obligations" by listing enforcement powers. The Convention separates them, and saying so is itself a scoring point. Rule-making runs 207 land-based, 210 dumping, 211 vessels, 212 atmosphere - and the coastal State's discretion is widest for land-based sources and narrowest for ships. Enforcement runs 217 flag, 218 port, 220 coastal, 221 casualties - and the coastal State's power is graded rather than absolute.

The reason for the asymmetry is worth one sentence: a State may regulate its own territory as it likes, but a ship is a moving object subject to another State's flag, so uniform international standards are the only workable basis. That is why article 211 keeps referring back to the competent international organization.

The 12 and 15 month periods in article 211(6)

If you can carry two numbers into this answer, carry these. Where a coastal State wants special mandatory measures for a clearly defined area of its EEZ, it communicates to the IMO with scientific and technical evidence; the IMO determines within 12 months; and the laws become applicable to foreign vessels only 15 months after the submission. They may never impose design, construction, manning or equipment standards beyond the generally accepted international ones. That paragraph is the legal skeleton of the Special Area and PSSA machinery, and quoting the two periods proves the Convention was opened.

Common mistakes

  • Paraphrasing the definition loosely and losing "or energy", "indirectly", "is likely to result" and "reduction of amenities" - four separate propositions gone.
  • Answering limb A entirely with enforcement powers and never mentioning the rule-making obligations.
  • Treating article 192 as applying only inside national zones. It is not zone-limited.
  • Offering the Bunkers Convention 2001 as one of the three tiers. It is a separate, single-tier regime for bunkers from non-tankers.
  • Offering the HNS Convention as a tier, or describing it as in force. It is not in force at this sitting.
  • Saying the Funds are financed by shipowners or by governments. They are financed by receivers of contributing oil.
  • Inventing a Fund certificate or a Supplementary Fund certificate carried by the ship. Only tier one produces a ship's certificate.
  • Writing that the coastal State may impose its own construction standards on foreign ships in its EEZ. Article 211(6) expressly forbids it.
  • Forgetting that the owner loses the right to limit for his own intentional or reckless conduct with knowledge.

Examiner traps

  • "Is noise pollution?" Yes - the definition says substances or energy. So is waste heat.
  • "Must damage have occurred?" No - 'results or is likely to result'.
  • "Can a coastal State demand a double hull in its EEZ?" No. Article 211(6) permits special measures for a defined area but not design, construction, manning or equipment standards beyond the generally accepted international ones.
  • "Who may be sued under CLC 92?" The registered owner, and liability is channelled to him - not the manager, charterer, crew, pilot or salvor.
  • "Who pays for tiers two and three?" Receivers of contributing oil. Say cargo, not ship.
  • "What does the ship carry for tier two?" Nothing. Only the CLC certificate is a ship's document.
  • "Is the third tier available everywhere?" No - it is optional, and requires 1992 Fund membership first.
  • "What links the two limbs?" Article 235 - the duty to ensure recourse for prompt and adequate compensation.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Define pollution under UNCLOS. Give the article. Article 1(1)(4).
  • Which article states the general obligation? 192. And what qualifies the right to exploit resources? 193.
  • Which article deals with pollution from vessels? 211. What standard must a flag State's law meet? At least the same effect as the generally accepted international rules (211(2)); a coastal State's EEZ laws must conform to and give effect to them (211(5)).
  • What can a coastal State do about a suspected discharge in its EEZ? Require information first; inspect on clear grounds; detain only where the evidence and the gravity warrant it - article 220.
  • What may a State do after a casualty outside its territorial sea? Measures proportionate to the actual or threatened damage - article 221.
  • Name the three tiers. CLC 1992, the 1992 Fund, the Supplementary Fund Protocol 2003.

  • What certificate does the ship carry, and who issues it? A certificate of insurance or other financial security under CLC 92, issued or certified by the State, on the strength of the insurer's blue card.
  • Is the Bunkers Convention one of the tiers? No - separate regime, one tier, no fund.
