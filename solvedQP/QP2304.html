Understand this first

A cargo does not become dangerous because it is reclassified; it was always dangerous, and the classification was wrong. Bauxite sat in the cargo code as a substance that could neither turn fluid nor poison anyone, and the practical effect of that listing was that nobody had to measure how wet it was and no master had a lawful reason to turn it away. A ship carrying it in full compliance with that listing was lost with almost all her crew. What was eventually understood is that the danger is not the whole cargo turning to liquid, but water working its way up through a mass that is settling under the ship's own working, until a soupy layer is lying on top of the solid material - and a ship that was loaded with a firm, trimmed cargo finds herself carrying something that sloshes. Everything the question asks for follows from that: the causes are the reasons the old listing was wrong, and the care and action are what the corrected listing now demands - measure the water, refuse what is too wet, and keep looking at the surface all the way across.

Model written answer

1. What bauxite is, and why it was treated as safe

Bauxite is the principal ore of aluminium and one of the largest dry bulk trades in the world. For most of the life of the IMSBC Code it was listed as a Group C cargo. The Code defines that group precisely: Group C consists of cargoes which are neither liable to liquefy (Group A) nor to possess chemical hazards (Group B). The practical consequence is the important one, and it is what the answer should open on: a Group C cargo has no transportable moisture limit. No TML means no moisture certificate, no moisture limit, and no lawful basis on which a master could refuse the cargo for being wet.

2. The casualty that forced the reassessment

The reassessment was forced by casualty, and principally by one. On 2 January 2015 the 10-year-old Bahamas-flag Supramax bulk carrier Bulk Jupiter was lost with 18 of her 19 crew, carrying bauxite. The cargo had been declared as Group C, and IMO recorded that the loss may have been caused by liquefaction of the cargo. The characteristic of the casualty was its speed - the ship was lost so rapidly that there was almost no opportunity to respond.

That single loss exposed a structural gap rather than a single failure. The cargo had been carried in accordance with its schedule, and the schedule was wrong.

3. The mechanism - dynamic separation

The research that followed established that bauxite's moisture hazard is not classic liquefaction, and that distinction is the heart of this question. The Code now states the two mechanisms side by side in the BAUXITE FINES schedule itself.

  • Classic liquefaction - the cargo mass loses its internal shear strength under vibration, impaction or ship's motion and behaves as a liquid. The whole body flows; water and solids move together. In the resulting viscous fluid state the cargo may flow to one side with a roll and not completely return, so each roll worsens the list.
  • Dynamic separation - the schedule's words are that the cargo may suffer instability due to moisture content resulting in dynamic separation and formation of a liquid slurry (water and fine solids) above the solid material, resulting in a free surface effect which may significantly affect the ship's stability. The cargo compacts from underneath, moisture migrates upward, and the ship acquires a free surface where the stability calculation assumed a solid, trimmed cargo.

Two further Code provisions explain why the danger is hard to see. Section 7.2.3 records that some cargoes are susceptible to moisture migration and may develop a dangerous wet base even where the average moisture content is less than the TML, so that although the surface may appear dry, undetected liquefaction may take place. And because a slurry lies on top and moves out of phase with the ship's roll period, the ship develops an atypical motion - a wobble - which is the one warning sign a watchkeeper can actually detect.

The schedule also gives the discharge condition for the hazard: the cargo is not liable to undergo dynamic separation when it is shipped below its TML. Keeping moisture below the limit is therefore not a precaution among others - it is the control.

4. The regulatory response, in sequence

The sequence matters, because the interim measures have been overtaken and must not be quoted as current requirements.

  • CCC.1/Circ.2, 20 October 2015 - issued within months of the loss as an interim awareness measure, carrying an indicative moisture limit of 10%.
  • The Global Bauxite Working Group then researched the material's behaviour and established that bauxite presents a risk caused by moisture and that some bauxite cargoes should be treated as Group A.
  • CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1, 20 September 2017 - supersedes the 2015 circular. The 10% indicative limit does not survive into Rev.1 and is not a requirement. CCC 4 finalized the TML test procedure for bauxite, the new Group A schedule and the amended Group C schedule.
  • Amendment 05-19, adopted by resolution MSC.462(101) on 13 June 2019, brought all of it into the Code and entered into force on 1 January 2021.

5. What governs at this sitting, and which schedule applies

The IMSBC Code is mandatory under SOLAS chapters VI and VII. The edition that governs carriage today is the Code as amended by amendment 05-19 - mandatory since 1 January 2021. State the amendment position precisely, because there is a second amendment in the system and it is not yet law:

  • Amendment 06-21, resolution MSC.500(105), adopted 28 April 2022. It is deemed accepted on 1 June 2023 and enters into force on 1 December 2023. It is therefore ADOPTED BUT NOT IN FORCE.
  • The same resolution agrees that Contracting Governments may apply it, in whole or in part, on a voluntary basis from 1 January 2023 - so it may lawfully be applied by a loading State, but it cannot yet be demanded as a requirement.
  • The distinction to write down is adopted, accepted, in force. They are three different things, and a compliance answer that treats them as one is wrong.

Bauxite carries two individual schedules, and everything else follows from which one the consignment falls into:

There is one further route worth knowing, because an examiner may press on it. A cargo that meets the fines criterion may nevertheless be carried as Group C under the BAUXITE schedule where the shipper provides the master with a certificate, based on a test approved by the competent authority of the port of loading, stating that the moisture freely drains from the cargo so that the degree of saturation is not liable to reach 70%. That is a documented, authority-approved escape - not a commercial assurance.

The governing principle for the ship should be stated explicitly: the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its individual schedule govern - never the commodity name. "Bauxite" on a fixture note is not a classification. The schedule's own physical properties are a bulk density of 1,100 to 2,000 kg/m3 and a stowage factor of 0.50 to 0.91 m3/t, with the angle of repose recorded as not applicable.

6. Care and action before loading

  • Obtain the shipper's declaration required by SOLAS regulation VI/2 and section 4 of the Code, giving the BCSN, the group and the cargo's properties, and satisfy yourself that the BCSN matches what is actually being offered.
  • If the cargo is BAUXITE FINES, or any Group A cargo, require the certificate of TML and the certificate of moisture content. Under section 4.5.1 the TML test must have been conducted within six months of the date of loading - and again where the composition or characteristics of the cargo are variable. Under section 4.5.2 moisture content must be sampled and tested as near as practicable to the commencement of loading and never more than seven days before it.
  • Under section 4.3.3 the shipper must have established approved procedures for sampling, testing and controlling moisture content so that it remains below the TML while the cargo is on board. Ask for them.
  • Check where and how the cargo has been stored - open stockpiles in a wet season are the best single predictor of trouble - and the loading port's recent and forecast weather.
  • Prepare the holds: clean and dry, bilge wells clean, dry and covered as appropriate to prevent ingress of the cargo, and the bilge system of each loaded space tested to prove it works. That last requirement is in the schedule itself. A bilge system that cannot be sounded or pumped is useless precisely when it matters.
  • Confirm the loading plan and stability, and for a bulk carrier the loading instrument, the SOLAS chapter XII requirements including water level detectors and alarms, and the ESP Code survey status of the ship.

7. Care and action during loading

  • For a Group A cargo the rule is absolute: the schedule requires that the moisture content be kept less than its TML during loading operations and the voyage. If it is not, the cargo must not be loaded.
  • Observe the schedule's weather precautions: the cargo shall not be handled during precipitation, and during handling all non-working hatches of the spaces loaded or to be loaded shall be closed. Handling in precipitation is permitted only under the approved 4.3.3 procedures. Discharge in rain is permitted only where the whole of the cargo in that space is to be discharged in that port.
  • Inspect what is coming aboard. Visibly wet cargo, free water in the grab or on the conveyor, splattering, or material that flattens and glistens on the stow are all grounds to stop.
  • The can test is a complementary shipboard screening check, not a substitute for a TML determination. A cargo that fails it should be refused; a cargo that passes it has proved very little.
  • Where the cargo has been exposed to significant rain or snow between testing and the completion of loading, section 4.5.2 puts the shipper under a duty to establish that the moisture content is still below the TML and to provide the master with evidence of it.
  • The master is not bound by the shipper's declaration. If there is reason to doubt that the cargo is consistent with it, stop loading and require the shipper to verify the cargo's properties, seeking the advice of the competent authority of the port of loading if necessary.
  • Trim the cargo reasonably level under sections 4 and 5, both to limit shifting and to reduce the free surface available to any slurry. Where the stowage factor is 0.56 m3/t or less the schedule warns that the tank top may be overstressed unless the cargo is evenly spread to equalize the weight distribution.

8. Care and action on passage, and if instability develops

  • The schedule requires that the appearance of the surface of the cargo be checked regularly, including at least daily visual inspections where condition permits. Do it from the hatch opening or access without entering the space, and record what is seen.
  • The four things to look for are named in the schedule: free water, a liquid slurry above the cargo, a fluid state of the cargo, and flattening of the cargo.
  • Sound the bilges at regular intervals, pump as necessary and record the quantities. A rising trend is evidence, and the record is what makes it visible.
  • Treat atypical motion - the wobble - as a warning of cargo instability, not as a curiosity of the loaded condition.
  • If any of those signs appears, the schedule requires the master to take appropriate actions to prevent cargo shifting, loss of stability due to free surface effect and potential capsize of the ship: reduce speed and alter course to ease the motion, avoid heavy weather and synchronous rolling, avoid ballast changes that would worsen stability, and inform owners, charterers, the flag Administration and the P&I club.
  • The schedule then says, in terms, that the master shall give consideration to seeking emergency entry into a place of refuge. The Bulk Jupiter lesson is that once a slurry has formed and the ship is wobbling, the time available is very short - so this is an early decision, not a last one.

The examiner's phrase is taken from the IMO circular itself, which asked that extreme care and appropriate action be taken, taking into account the provisions of relevant IMO instruments, when handling and carrying bauxite in bulk. The substance of that care is the sequence above: classify correctly, certify the moisture, refuse what is wet, watch the surface and the bilges, and act on the wobble.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints no mark allocation, and the stem contains two demands - causes and care and action - so give them roughly equal weight. The commonest failure is to spend the whole answer on a general account of liquefaction and never reach the practical care, which forfeits half the question. The second commonest is to give the care as an undifferentiated list of precautions. Answer the care limb in voyage order - before loading, during loading, on passage - because that is how it would be used and it is far easier to mark. Name dynamic separation early: it is the discriminating content, and an answer that never uses the term has not identified what makes bauxite different.

The single idea that carries the answer

Bauxite was reclassified because of a casualty. Everything the question asks for follows from that one sentence: the causes are the reasons the old classification was wrong, and the care and action are the requirements the new classification imposes. Open with that and the answer organises itself.

Dynamic separation is NOT liquefaction - and the distinction earns marks

  • In liquefaction the cargo mass loses shear strength and the whole body flows; water and solids move together. In the viscous fluid state it may flow to one side with a roll and not completely return.
  • In dynamic separation the cargo compacts from underneath, moisture migrates upward, and a slurry forms on top of the solid cargo. The danger is a free surface the stability calculation never allowed for.
  • The behavioural tell follows from the geometry: the slurry sloshes out of phase with the roll, producing the wobble. A liquefying cargo does not do that.
  • Both mechanisms are switched off by the same control - ship below the TML. The schedule says so expressly for dynamic separation.

Why a Group C classification was so dangerous

Not because Group C is a lax standard, but because of what it switches off. A Group C cargo has no TML, so there is no moisture certificate, no moisture limit and no basis on which a master can lawfully refuse cargo for being wet. The cargo aboard the Bulk Jupiter was carried in accordance with its schedule. The schedule was the defect. One sentence on this shows you understand that compliance and safety are not the same thing.

The amendment position at this sitting - state it precisely

This is the temporal discriminator on the question and it is unusually clean, because the sitting falls inside the window between adoption and entry into force of the current amendment.

  • Amendment 05-19, resolution MSC.462(101), adopted 13 June 2019, in force 1 January 2021. This is the mandatory edition. It carries both bauxite schedules.
  • Amendment 06-21, resolution MSC.500(105), adopted 28 April 2022, deemed accepted 1 June 2023, in force 1 December 2023 - and expressly open to voluntary application in whole or in part from 1 January 2023.
  • So at this examination 06-21 is adopted, not yet accepted, not in force, and voluntarily applicable. All four statements are true at once, and being able to say that is what separates a strong answer from a competent one.

Numbers you can state, and numbers you cannot

  • Can state: the particle-size criteria - more than 30% under 1 mm and more than 40% under 2.5 mm for the Group A schedule, and the mirror-image thresholds for Group C; the six month TML interval and the seven day moisture interval; the 70% degree of saturation in the drainage certificate; bulk density 1,100 to 2,000 kg/m3 and stowage factor 0.50 to 0.91 m3/t; the 0.56 m3/t tank-top overstress threshold.
  • Cannot state: a TML value for bauxite. TML is a property of the consignment, determined by test for each shipment. Any general figure is wrong by construction, and offering one suggests you think TML belongs to the commodity.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a generic liquefaction answer. Bauxite's mechanism is dynamic separation, and saying so is the point of the question.
  • Saying bauxite is a Group A cargo. BAUXITE is Group C; BAUXITE FINES is Group A. Which applies turns on particle size distribution, and the declared BCSN governs.
  • Quoting the 10% indicative limit as current. It belongs to the 2015 circular and Rev.1 superseded it in 2017.
  • Offering a TML figure for bauxite.
  • Treating the can test as a substitute for a TML certificate.
  • Forgetting that the master may stop loading and require verification. The declaration is a starting point, not a defence.
  • Omitting the practical shipboard limb - daily surface inspection, bilge sounding and records, and the wobble.
  • Saying the current amendment is 'in force' when it is only adopted. At this sitting 06-21 is not yet law.

Examiner traps

"The shipper hands you a Group C declaration for bauxite. Are you satisfied?" Not necessarily. Ask whether the material meets the Group C schedule's particle size distribution, how and where it has been stockpiled, and what the weather has been. If it is fine material that has been in the open in the wet season, the declaration is questionable and you may stop loading and require verification.

"Your cargo passed the can test. Can you load?" The can test is a complementary screening check. Passing it does not establish that moisture content is below TML - only a certificate based on the proper test procedure does that.

"Fine bauxite, but the shipper offers you a Group C certificate. Is that lawful?" It can be. The BAUXITE FINES schedule permits carriage as Group C where the shipper provides a certificate, based on a test approved by the competent authority of the loading port, that the moisture freely drains so that the degree of saturation is not liable to reach 70%. Check that it is that certificate, from that authority.

"The ship has developed an odd wobble two days out. What do you do?" Treat it as evidence of cargo instability. Reduce speed and ease the motion, inspect the surface where safe, sound and record the bilges, avoid heavy weather, notify owners, charterers, flag and club, and consider a place of refuge early - the schedule requires that consideration in terms.

Not as such. There are two schedules: BAUXITE is Group C, and BAUXITE FINES is Group A, a cargo which may undergo dynamic separation. Which applies turns on particle size distribution, and the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its individual schedule govern - never the commodity name.

Saying 'bauxite is Group A' is as wrong as the old position that it was always Group C.

Understand this first

Think of this as the chemical version of the oil pollution system, with two twists worth remembering. The first twist is how it decides what it covers. Instead of writing out a list of chemicals, which would be out of date the year after it was signed, it points at the other rulebooks the industry already uses for cargoes and says: anything those books treat as dangerous, carried as cargo, is covered here. So the scope maintains itself. The second twist is what counts as harm. An oil convention is about mess - contamination, cleaning up, lost earnings. A chemical cargo, though, kills people directly, by burning, exploding or poisoning, and it can do that without a drop reaching the water. So the first kind of harm the convention recognises is death and injury, and no spill is required for it. The last part of the question is really about boundaries. Each of the three conventions is switched on by a different thing - one by how sticky the oil is, one by what the oil is being used for, one by which category the cargo falls into - and each is drafted to stand back from whatever its neighbour has already claimed. A tanker that carries a different parcel every voyage moves across all three boundaries in the course of ordinary trading, and since the paperwork has to be in hand before she sails, she carries all of it all the time.

Model written answer

1. The Convention, and its status at this sitting

The instrument is the International Convention on Liability and Compensation for Damage in Connection with the Carriage of Hazardous and Noxious Substances by Sea, 1996, as amended by the Protocol of 2010. The Protocol directs that the two are to be read and interpreted together as one single instrument, and the combined text is what is meant by "the HNS Convention".

The examiner's premise must be handled before anything else. The paper states that the Convention "is expected shortly to come into force". At this sitting it is adopted but NOT in force, and the conditions its entry-into-force article sets have not been met. Those conditions are of three kinds: a minimum number of States must have expressed consent to be bound; among them a minimum number must each hold a substantial registered gross tonnage; and the Secretary-General must have been told that receivers in those States took a stated quantity of contributing cargo in the preceding calendar year. The expectation of imminence was widely held; it is recorded here as the examiner's, and no date is offered, because none existed.

One consequence is worth stating early, because it protects the rest of the answer: the substantive provisions were fixed by the 2010 Protocol and do not change while the instrument waits. So the scope, the heads of damage and the certification scheme can all be answered with confidence even though the Convention is not yet law. The scheme itself is the familiar two-tier shape: strict liability on the owner up to a limit, backed by compulsory insurance and a certificate, with a second-tier Fund financed by the receivers of the cargo.

2. (a) The substances covered - the seven categories of article 1.5

The first thing to say about limb (a) is the thing most candidates miss. The Convention does not list chemicals. Article 1.5(a) defines hazardous and noxious substances as anything carried on board a ship as cargo which falls within seven categories, each defined by pointing at another IMO instrument. The practical effect is that the scope updates itself whenever those instruments are amended, and no list in the Convention can go stale.

Note also the two limits built into the definition: the substance must be carried as cargo, so a ship's own stores and bunkers are not HNS, and category (iv) is the only packaged category.

3. (a) Bulk HNS and packaged HNS - why the split matters

  • Article 1.5bis - bulk HNS: categories (i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii), together with the residues in 1.5(b).
  • Article 1.5ter - packaged HNS: category (iv) alone.
  • The split is not a classification for its own sake. It fixes the owner's limit of liability under article 9, and the packaged scale is the higher of the two - packaged chemicals are harder to identify, recover and account for after a casualty.
  • It also matters to the second tier: packaged cargo does not contribute to the Fund in the way bulk cargo does. Where a ship carries both, or where the cargo cannot be determined, the packaged figures apply.

4. (b) The heads of damage - article 1.6

Limb (b) reads as a technical question and is a legal one. "Damage" is defined in article 1.6 and has four heads:

  1. Loss of life or personal injury, on board or outside the ship, caused by the substances. Note what is not required: no pollution at all. This is the widest departure from the oil regime and it is the single best mark in the limb.
  2. Loss of or damage to property outside the ship.
  3. Loss or damage by contamination of the environment - with the standard restriction that compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit from that impairment, is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken.
  4. The costs of preventive measures, and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Because the stem says "types of pollution", name the four ways a chemical cargo does harm alongside the legal heads, and the two readings of the question are both satisfied: fire and explosion, toxicity (acute and chronic, to people and to marine life), contamination of water, seabed, shoreline and air, and reactivity - substances that react violently with water, with air or with each other. Article 1.6 also deals with the practical problem that chemical damage often cannot reasonably be separated from damage caused by other things.

5. (b) What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it

The exclusions in article 4 are what make the three conventions fit together, so they lead directly into limb (c).

  • 4.1 - the Convention applies to claims other than those arising out of a contract for the carriage of goods and passengers. Cargo and passenger claims travel under their own regimes.
  • 4.2 - disapplied to the extent it is incompatible with workers' compensation or social security schemes.
  • 4.3(a) - pollution damage as defined in the Civil Liability Convention is excluded, WHETHER OR NOT compensation is actually payable under that Convention. This is the load-bearing clause. The exclusion bites on the definition, not on the outcome, so a gap in CLC recovery does not push the claim into HNS.
  • 4.3(b) - damage caused by class 7 radioactive material, which has its own nuclear liability regime.
  • 4.4 - warships and State-owned ships on non-commercial service.

The drafting principle to state in one line: these conventions are built to tile, not to overlap. Each defines its territory and then carves out what a neighbouring convention has already claimed.

6. (c) Why an oil products tanker needs certificates under all three

The examiner's word is "probably", and the hedge is deliberate - the requirement depends on what the ship is carrying and how she is registered. Explaining that dependency is the mark. The three conventions are each defined by a different thing:

Now apply that to the ship in the question. A products tanker's trade is precisely that she changes cargo:

  • She always burns bunkers. So the Bunkers Convention reaches her on every voyage, laden or in ballast, whatever the cargo. That certificate is never conditional on the trade.
  • Some parcels are persistent. A cargo of heavy fuel oil or a persistent product engages CLC. Two facts must both be true - that she is a CLC ship on that voyage, and that the oil is persistent - so CLC applicability is stated conditionally, never as a property of the ship. Note the trap: CLC reaches only persistent hydrocarbon MINERAL oil; a persistent oil of animal or vegetable origin is not a CLC oil at all and is treated as an HNS.
  • Some parcels are non-persistent, or are chemicals. Gasoline, naphtha and gas oil are low-flashpoint or non-persistent products that fall outside CLC and squarely inside HNS categories (i) and (vi); a parcel of a chemical falls inside (ii) or (iii); and residues of any of these keep the ship within HNS even after discharge.
  • The parcels change voyage by voyage, and often within one voyage. A products tanker frequently carries several grades at once.

The commercial conclusion follows from the operational one: certificates cannot be obtained at the loading berth. They are issued in advance by or on behalf of the flag State against evidence of insurance, and a missing certificate is a detainable deficiency and a breach of charter. Since which regime applies is decided by the cargo of the day, the only way a products tanker can trade freely is to hold all three certificates all the time. That, and not any single voyage, is why she "probably" requires all three.

7. (c) The certificate itself, and the two tiers behind it

  • Under article 12 the owner of a ship registered in a State Party and actually carrying HNS must maintain insurance or other financial security in the sums fixed by article 9, evidenced by a certificate in the Annex I model form, carried on board and mutually recognised between States Parties. There is a direct right of action against the insurer.
  • Tier one is the owner's strict liability under article 7, subject to the article 7.2 defences and to the channelling provision in 7.5 which keeps claims off the crew, pilot, charterer, manager, operator and salvor. Liability is limited under article 9, on the higher packaged scale or the lower bulk scale.
  • Tier two is the HNS Fund under article 14, financed by post-incident contributions from receivers of contributing cargo in States Parties, subject to an overall ceiling on the total payable. The polluter-pays burden is therefore shared between the ship and the cargo interest, which is the same architecture as the oil regime's Fund.
  • Article 37 - claims are extinguished three years from the date the claimant knew or ought reasonably to have known of the damage, and in no case later than ten years from the incident.

Close by returning to the premise. Because the Convention was not in force at this sitting, an HNS incident then fell to be dealt with under national law, limitation under the LLMC, and P&I cover - which is precisely the gap the Convention exists to close, and precisely why its entry into force was so long awaited.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints no mark allocation and three limbs, so give each limb visible space and let limb (a) run longest - it carries the most content. Deal with the premise first, in three or four lines: state that the Convention is adopted but not in force, and move on. Do not spend a paragraph on it, and do not argue with the examiner. Limb (b) is a legal question wearing technical clothes; answer it from the definition of damage and then add the four chemical risk types so both readings are covered. Limb (c) is the analytical limb and the one that separates candidates - it is not asking you to describe three conventions, it is asking why one ship needs three certificates.

The two ideas that carry the whole answer

  • HNS defines by reference, not by list. Article 1.5 points at seven other IMO instruments. Say this in your first sentence on limb (a) and the rest of the limb writes itself.
  • The three conventions tile. Each is defined by a different thing - persistence, use, category - and each carves out what its neighbour has claimed. Limb (c) is that sentence, applied to a ship whose cargo changes.

The premise is a trap, and the trap is agreeing with it too hard

The stem asserts that the Convention is expected shortly to come into force. It was not in force at this sitting and its entry-into-force conditions were not met. The correct handling is neither to repeat the prediction as fact nor to correct the examiner: state the position, name the kinds of condition that had to be satisfied, and give no date. An answer that supplies a confident entry-into-force date is asserting something no candidate in the room could have known - and is more likely to be marked wrong than generous.

  • Bunkers - use. Oil used or intended for the ship's operation or propulsion. Certificate above 1,000 gross tonnage. Applies whatever she is carrying, which is why it is the one certificate that is never conditional.
  • HNS - category. Whether the cargo falls in one of the seven article 1.5 categories. Certificate where she is actually carrying HNS.
  • The products tanker needs all three because the cargo of the day decides which regime is live, and certificates are obtained in advance, not at the berth.

The CLC exclusion phrase is worth memorising word for word

Both the Bunkers Convention and HNS exclude pollution damage as defined in the CLC, "whether or not compensation is payable in respect of it under that Convention". That phrase is doing real work: the exclusion attaches to the definition, not to the outcome. So if a claim falls inside the CLC definition but recovery fails - the limit is exhausted, a defence succeeds, the flag is not a party - it does not fall back into HNS or Bunkers. Candidates routinely assume the regimes act as a safety net for one another. They do not.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to list chemicals for limb (a) instead of giving the seven categories and the instrument each points at.
  • Forgetting that HNS covers substances carried as cargo only - bunkers and ship's stores are not HNS.
  • Missing residues under article 1.5(b), which is where a dirty empty tank comes back into scope.
  • Answering limb (b) as a description of chemical spills without ever giving the heads of damage.
  • Omitting that the first head - death and personal injury - requires no pollution at all. It is the widest difference from the oil regime.
  • Treating the three conventions as overlapping safety nets rather than as tiling regimes.
  • Saying a products tanker "is a CLC ship". She is a CLC ship on a voyage carrying persistent oil in bulk - state it conditionally.
  • Assuming any persistent oil is a CLC oil. CLC reaches only persistent hydrocarbon MINERAL oil; animal and vegetable oils are HNS however persistent.
  • Supplying an entry-into-force date.

Examiner traps

"Is the ship's fuel oil an HNS?" Not as bunkers - HNS reaches substances carried as cargo. The same fuel oil carried as a cargo parcel is a different matter, and if it is persistent it is more likely a CLC question.

"A cargo of persistent vegetable oil escapes. Which convention?" Not CLC - CLC is confined to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil. It is a noxious liquid substance under MARPOL Annex II and therefore an HNS in category (ii).

"CLC applies but the limit is exhausted. Can the balance be claimed under HNS?" No. Article 4.3(a) excludes CLC pollution damage whether or not compensation is payable. The balance goes to the Fund and Supplementary Fund tiers of the oil regime, not sideways into HNS.

"She is carrying drums of solvent and a bulk parcel of naphtha. Which limit?" Where the cargo is mixed, or cannot be determined, the packaged figures apply - and they are the higher scale.

Understand this first

The word at the centre of this question does not mean what it means in arithmetic. Here it means a partial loss - damage short of losing the thing altogether - and the whole question is about who pays for it. Two answers are possible. If the damage simply happened, by bad luck, then it stays with whoever owned the thing that got damaged, and that is the end of it. But sometimes damage does not simply happen: somebody in charge decides to give something up on purpose, because giving it up is the only way to save the whole venture - throwing cargo over the side to lift a ship off a bank, or flooding a hold to stop a fire spreading. Whoever owned that particular thing has suffered for everybody's benefit, and it would be unfair to leave the loss with them. So the loss is spread across everyone whose property was saved, in proportion to what they got back. That sharing has to be calculated by somebody who has no interest in the outcome - which is why a separate profession exists to do it, and why the last part of the question is about that person rather than about the ships.

Model written answer

1. Particular Average

Particular average is a partial loss. Four elements define it, and they are best given as a list because the examiner is looking for the contrast with general average that follows:

  1. It is a partial loss - not a total loss, actual or constructive.
  2. It is caused accidentally, by a peril insured against.
  3. It is not a general average loss.
  4. It therefore lies where it falls: the loss is borne solely by the interest that suffered it, and is recoverable, if at all, only from that interest's own underwriters.

The examples are ordinary marine damage. Heavy weather stoves in a hatch cover and wets a parcel of cargo; a wave damages the ship's rail; a container is crushed by the working of the ship in a seaway. Nobody chose any of it, nobody else benefited from it, and so nobody else pays for it. The word "average" here means partial loss, not an arithmetical mean - a point worth making in one line, because the everyday meaning of the word misleads candidates every year.

2. General Average, and how it differs

General average is the opposite case: a loss that somebody chose to suffer, deliberately, to save the whole adventure. The classical definition is Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules:

There is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure.

Because all three interests - ship, cargo and freight at risk - were saved by that act, all three contribute rateably in proportion to their saved values. That is the whole principle, and it is very old: it is a rule of sharing, not a rule of compensation. In Indian law it is given statutory expression in section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, which is drawn from section 66 of the English Act of 1906.

The two classic illustrations make the distinction visible. Cargo damaged by seawater entering a stove-in hatch is particular average. Cargo of the same kind, sound, but deliberately jettisoned to lighten a stranded ship, is general average sacrifice - and so is the water damage done to sound cargo by the ship's own fire main in fighting a fire in the hold, because that damage was the intended price of putting the fire out.

3. When General Average may be declared

The circumstances are simply the elements of Rule A, all of which must be present together. Take them one at a time, because each is a place where a claim fails:

  1. There must be a common maritime adventure - more than one interest at risk. A ship in ballast with no cargo aboard has no general average, because there is nothing to share with.
  2. There must be a real peril. The danger must be real and substantial, though it need not be immediate. A peril that is merely feared, or that the master has imagined, will not do.
  3. The act must be intentional and voluntary. Something must be chosen. Damage that simply happens to the ship in the casualty is not a sacrifice.
  4. The act must be reasonable. A disproportionate or negligent response is not protected merely because it was deliberate.
  5. The sacrifice or expenditure must be extraordinary. Ordinary running costs, wear and tear, and anything the shipowner was already bound to do under the contract of carriage do not qualify.
  6. It must be for the COMMON safety - for the benefit of the whole adventure, not for the ship alone or for one parcel of cargo alone.
  7. There must be something saved. Contribution is levied on saved values, so if nothing is preserved there is no fund to contribute from and the general average fails for want of a subject.

Two practical points complete the limb. First, fault does not prevent a general average act: the act is still general average even if the peril arose through somebody's negligence, though the party at fault may then face a defence to its own claim for contribution - which is exactly the difficulty the New Jason clause is drafted to meet. Second, it is the shipowner, through the master, who declares general average - not the adjuster, not the underwriters and not the cargo interests. The typical occasions are jettison, damage in extinguishing a fire, voluntary stranding, damage to machinery and boilers in refloating a stranded ship, putting into a port of refuge with the expenses that follow, temporary repairs, and salvage remuneration where the applicable Rules bring it into the adjustment.

4. How a declaration is made operative - security before release

A declaration on its own achieves nothing. The shipowner has a lien on the cargo for its general average contribution, and the practical machinery exists to convert that lien into security so that cargo can be released:

  • An average bond - the Lloyd's form is standard - signed by each cargo owner, undertaking to pay the contribution the adjustment finds due and to provide the values needed to calculate it.
  • An average guarantee from the cargo underwriters, or a general average deposit in cash where the cargo is uninsured or the underwriter is unacceptable.
  • Cargo is not released until security is in place. This is where general average is felt commercially, and it can be severe - after a major casualty the security demanded may be a very large proportion of cargo value, and cargo may be held for months.
  • The general average is then "declared" and an adjuster is appointed. In practice the appointment is made by the shipowner, but the adjuster's duty is owed to all the interests.

5. Average adjusters and their role

An average adjuster is an independent professional specialist - in practice a member of a recognised professional body such as the Association of Average Adjusters - who investigates a marine casualty and prepares the statement of general average, which is the document that says who pays what to whom.

The role, in the order it is performed:

  1. Investigate and collect. Obtain the logs, the protest, the survey reports, the port of refuge accounts, the salvage award and the cargo manifest, and establish what actually happened.
  2. Secure the fund. Arrange the average bonds, guarantees and deposits so the cargo can be released and the contributions can eventually be collected.
  3. Determine what is allowable. Apply the applicable edition of the York-Antwerp Rules and allow or disallow each claimed sacrifice and expenditure. This is the adjudicative heart of the job.
  4. Ascertain the contributory values of ship, cargo and freight at risk, at the place and time the adventure ends.
  5. Apportion. Divide the allowed general average between the interests rateably on those values, and state the result.
  6. Issue the adjustment and deal with the queries, and where necessary handle the parallel particular average and salvage claims so the accounts do not overlap.

Two things the adjuster is not are worth a sentence each, because they are the discriminating points. The adjuster does not declare general average - the shipowner does; the adjuster is engaged after the event. And the adjuster is not the shipowner's advocate: although appointed and paid by the shipowner, the adjuster acts impartially between all the interests, and that independence is what makes the adjustment acceptable to underwriters. An adjustment is nevertheless not binding as a judgment - it may be challenged in court or arbitration - but in practice it is very rarely disturbed.

6. Which rules actually apply, and on what basis

Finish on the point most answers miss. The York-Antwerp Rules are not law. They are not a convention, no State enacts them, and they bind nobody by their own force. They are a set of contractual rules published by the Comite Maritime International, and they apply only where the contract of carriage - the bill of lading or the charterparty - incorporates them and says which edition.

The single sentence that carries the whole answer

Particular average lies where it falls; general average is shared, because it was chosen. Everything else in the answer is elaboration of that contrast. If you open with it, the definitions organise themselves and the adjuster's role becomes obvious - somebody has to work out the shares.

Rule A, element by element - and where claims actually fail

  • Common maritime adventure - fails where there is only one interest. A ship in ballast has no general average.
  • Real peril - fails where the danger was merely apprehended. It need not be immediate, but it must be real and substantial.
  • Intentional and voluntary - fails where the damage simply happened. This is the line between sacrifice and accident, and it is the line between GA and PA.
  • Reasonable - fails where the response was disproportionate.
  • Extraordinary - fails for ordinary running costs, wear and tear, and anything the carrier was already contractually bound to do.
  • Common safety - fails where the act benefited only the ship, or only one parcel.
  • Something saved - fails where nothing was preserved, because contribution is levied on saved values and there is then no fund.

Who does what - the three roles candidates confuse

  • The shipowner declares general average, acting through the master. Nobody else can.
  • The average adjuster states the adjustment - investigates, allows or disallows, values, apportions. He is engaged after the declaration and he does not make it.
  • The underwriters pay, each in respect of its own assured's contribution, under the policy.
  • And the adjuster, though appointed and paid by the shipowner, acts impartially between all interests. Saying he is the owner's representative is a straightforward error.

The York-Antwerp Rules are NOT law - and this is a reliable mark

They are not a convention, no State enacts them, and no ship is bound by them merely by being at sea. They apply because the bill of lading or charterparty says so, and the contract also fixes the edition. Three editions are current in the trade - 1994, 2004 and 2016 - and a contract on the 1994 Rules is adjusted on the 1994 Rules regardless of how many later editions exist. Never write that "the latest edition applies". Where the Rules are not incorporated at all, the adjustment falls back on the general law of the place the adventure ends, and in India on the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

Illustrations that are safe to use

  • Jettison of sound cargo to lighten a stranded ship - the oldest example there is, and the origin of the whole doctrine.
  • Water damage from firefighting - the water damage to sound cargo is general average; the fire damage itself is not.
  • Voluntary stranding - beaching a ship deliberately to prevent her foundering.
  • Machinery and boiler damage in refloating - the classic engineering example, and the one a management-level candidate should reach for.
  • Port of refuge expenses - putting in, discharging and reloading, and the associated costs.
  • Maersk Honam, March 2018 - a container-ship fire, general average declared and very high salvage security demanded before cargo was released.
  • Ever Given, March 2021 - grounding in the Suez Canal, general average declared. Both casualties pre-date this sitting and are safe to cite.

Common mistakes

  • Treating "average" as an arithmetical mean. It means partial loss.
  • Answering "circumstances" with a list of casualties instead of the conditions in Rule A.
  • Forgetting the something saved element - no saved fund, no contribution.
  • Saying the peril must be immediate. It must be real and substantial; immediacy is not required.
  • Saying the adjuster declares general average.
  • Describing the adjuster as the shipowner's representative rather than as an impartial professional.
  • Saying the York-Antwerp Rules are law, or that the latest edition automatically applies.
  • Omitting the security stage - bond, guarantee, deposit - which is how general average is actually experienced by cargo.
  • Quoting a contributory-value percentage. Values are ascertained case by case at the termination of the adventure.

Examiner traps

"The ship is in ballast and puts into a port of refuge after an engine breakdown. Is that general average?" No - there is no common maritime adventure, because only one interest is at risk. The expense is the shipowner's alone.

"Cargo is damaged when the master floods a hold to put out a fire. Which average?" Both, in the same hold. The cargo actually burning suffers particular average; the sound cargo damaged by the water suffers general average sacrifice, because that damage was the intended price of the common safety.

"The casualty was caused by the owner's unseaworthiness. Can he still declare general average?" He can declare it - the act is still a general average act - but his own right to recover contribution may be defeated by that fault, which is precisely the mischief the New Jason clause addresses.

"The bill of lading says York-Antwerp Rules 1994. The 2016 Rules exist. Which applies?" The 1994 Rules. The contract fixes the edition and currency is irrelevant.

Understand this first

Two ships have hit each other, people have died and both ships are hurt. The question looks like a memory test about an Act, and it is really about sequence and authority. Sequence, because the things that must happen have a natural order that has nothing to do with the order of the statute: you find out whether everyone is alive, you help the other ship, you find out whether your own ship is still safe to move, you stop anything harmful escaping, and only then do you start telling people. Authority, because the second half of the question asks who acts, and the answer is that nobody does all of it. The master can do everything on board and nothing ashore. The owner can spend money and call in the insurers but cannot compel anything. The national administration can stop the ship from sailing, can order the owner to do something and can do it themselves and send the bill. The coast guard is the one that actually goes out. And the decision that matters most in a bad casualty - whether a damaged ship may come into shelter - belongs to none of them, but to the coastal state, weighing the harm of letting her in against the harm of keeping her out. Notice also what the question does not say: nothing has leaked. So the environmental part is about stopping a leak that has not happened, which is a different job from cleaning one up, and the legal powers are worded widely enough to reach it.

Model written answer

1. What has actually happened - three events in one casualty

Before any list of steps, break the casualty into its elements, because each element triggers a different part of the Act and the marks follow the elements:

  1. A collision - engaging both masters' duties after a collision, the COLREGs question of fault, and the apportionment of loss.
  2. Loss of life - which makes this a very serious marine casualty and the marine safety investigation mandatory.
  3. Damage to the vessels - engaging seaworthiness, survey, class, the power of detention, and possibly salvage.

Note carefully what the stem does not say: no pollution has occurred. The question nevertheless asks about steps "for the safety of the ships and the marine environment", so the environmental duty is squarely in issue - but it is in issue as a duty to prevent a discharge from a damaged ship, not as a response to a spill. That distinction is the discriminating point of this question and it should be stated explicitly, because the Indian statutory power is drafted to reach a threatened discharge as well as an actual one.

The governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and it is worth saying at the outset that the Act is enabling as much as prohibitory: much of what follows is a matter of powers conferred on the Director-General of Shipping and the Central Government, not merely of duties imposed on the master.

2. Immediate steps on board - the Master, and the order matters

  • Muster, account for all persons, and search for the missing. Lives have been lost; this is the overriding first duty and everything else waits on it.
  • Render assistance to the other vessel and to every person in danger, so far as he can without serious danger to his own ship and those on board; stand by; and exchange identifying particulars - name, port of registry, and the ports from and to which she is bound. This is the master's duty after a collision under the Act, reinforced by SOLAS regulation V/33.
  • Assess the damage before moving. Sound all tanks and bilges, establish the extent of flooding, contain it, and assess stability and residual longitudinal strength. A damaged ship that proceeds without that assessment is the second casualty.
  • Prevent a discharge. Even with no release so far, the ship is a damaged ship with oil aboard: isolate any breached or threatened tank, transfer internally to sound tanks where stability permits, and implement the SOPEP - which addresses the probability of a discharge, not only an actual one.
  • Broadcast and report - a distress or urgency message as the situation requires, and a danger message under SOLAS regulation V/31 where the casualty endangers navigation.
  • PRESERVE THE EVIDENCE. Secure the VDR before it overwrites, and preserve charts, deck and engine logs, bell book, movement and alarm printouts, ECDIS and radar records, and the position and time. Lives have been lost and there will be both an investigation and litigation.

4. Steps for the safety of the ships

  • Damage survey. Survey by the classification society and by the Administration; repairs, or approved temporary repairs, before she proceeds; conditions of class imposed as necessary.
  • DETENTION. Under sections 334 to 336 the Director-General of Shipping, or a surveyor authorised by him, may detain a ship that is unseaworthy or unsafe by reason of the condition of her hull, equipment or machinery, or by reason of overloading or improper loading. This is the power that stops a damaged ship sailing, and it is the clearest "who" in the whole answer.
  • Salvage and towage where she cannot proceed under her own power - typically on Lloyd's Open Form, no cure no pay - within the Act's Part XIII, sections 390 to 404, and the Merchant Shipping (Wrecks and Salvage) Rules, 1974.
  • A place of refuge. Where the ship must be brought into sheltered water, the coastal State decides, on the basis of a case-by-case assessment. The operative IMO guidance is resolution A.949(23), Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, read with A.950(23) on Maritime Assistance Services. There is no right to a place of refuge; there is a duty on the coastal State to weigh the request objectively.
  • DIRECTIONS. The powers in Part XA allow the Central Government and the Director-General to direct the owner, master or salvor to take, or to refrain from taking, specified action. This is what converts advice into a compellable order, and it is available on a threat of pollution.
  • Navigational safety - marking the casualty, navigational warnings, and an exclusion zone where necessary.

5. Steps for the marine environment - preventive, because nothing has yet escaped

This limb must be answered in the preventive voice. Nothing has been discharged; the object is to keep it that way, and to be ready if that fails.

  • The ship acts first. Implement the SOPEP, stop or prevent the source - isolate the damaged space, transfer oil internally where stability allows, plug and patch - and rig the ship's own containment and absorbent means.
  • Assess and declare the risk honestly to the Coast Guard and DG Shipping, including the quantity and grade of oil in the threatened spaces, so that a response can be staged before it is needed rather than after.
  • The national machinery is placed on standby. The Indian Coast Guard is the authority responsible for marine pollution response at sea in India's maritime zones, under the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan, which is organised on tiered response levels. On these facts the plan is engaged in its readiness rather than its response posture.
  • THE LEGAL ENGINE - Part XA, sections 356J, 356K and 356L. The Central Government and the Director-General may give directions to the owner, master or salvor for the prevention or containment of pollution; may themselves take whatever measures they consider necessary; and may recover the cost of doing so. It is the preventive reach of that power which makes it apply here, and saying so is the mark.
  • Coordination with the State pollution control board, the fisheries and environment departments and the port authority, so that a response, if it becomes necessary, is not being organised from a standing start.
  • Funding is already in place through the P&I entry and the compulsory insurance behind the ship's CLC or Bunkers certificate, with a direct right of action against the insurer. That is why those certificates matter operationally and not just at survey.

6. Investigation and inquiry - two systems, and both are engaged

Keep these apart. Merging them is the commonest way to lose marks on this question.

  • NATIONAL - under the Act. A casualty must be reported. A preliminary inquiry is then conducted by a person appointed for the purpose, and where the circumstances warrant it there is a formal investigation before a court with assessors. That court may find fault and may cancel or suspend the certificate of a master, mate or engineer. Its orientation is therefore partly disciplinary.
  • INTERNATIONAL - the Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84), made mandatory by SOLAS regulation XI-1/6. Its purpose is expressly the prevention of recurrence, and expressly not the apportionment of blame or liability.
  • It is MANDATORY here. A very serious marine casualty includes one involving a death, and a marine safety investigation into a very serious marine casualty must be conducted. Note that on these facts it is the loss of life that triggers it - environmental damage would have to be severe to do so, and here there has been none.
  • India, as flag State of the vessel, is the marine safety investigating State. If the other vessel is foreign, her flag State is a substantially interested State and is entitled to participate. The coastal State is likewise substantially interested. The final report goes to the IMO.
  • Hold the two apart. One can take a certificate away; the other must never be used to apportion blame. A candidate who says the Casualty Investigation Code determines liability has misunderstood the instrument.

7. Liability, and a direct answer to the examiner's 'who'

  • Collision liability is apportioned according to the degree of fault of each vessel, on the COLREGs breaches established. Claims are pursued in the admiralty jurisdiction of the High Courts under the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, by action in rem against the vessel with arrest, or in personam.
  • The loss of life claims rank high. Under section 9(1) of that Act, claims for loss of life or personal injury occurring in direct connection with the operation of the vessel carry a maritime lien, ranking second - immediately after crew wages.
  • Limitation of liability for maritime claims is available under the LLMC as amended, the 2012 increases having entered into force in 2015.
  • Pollution liability, if a discharge follows, under CLC 1992 where she is a CLC ship carrying persistent oil, and otherwise under the Bunkers Convention 2001. State CLC applicability conditionally - it turns on the ship and the cargo, never on the casualty.
  • Wreck removal under Part XIII if a wreck results.

The examiner asks who should initiate. Answer it in one line and do not leave it implied: the Master initiates everything on board and the first reports; the owner and the Designated Person Ashore mount the company response and notify the insurers and class; the Director-General of Shipping receives the report, detains, directs, and orders the inquiry; the Indian Coast Guard executes search and rescue and any pollution response at sea; the Central Government holds the Part XA powers to act and recover cost; the classification society and the Administration survey and approve repairs; and the coastal State decides on a place of refuge.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no marks and asks two things: what steps, and who initiates them. Most candidates answer the first and forget the second entirely, which is half the question. The safest structure is to break the casualty into its elements first - collision, loss of life, damage - because each element opens a different part of the Act and the answer then writes itself in the examiner's own order. Then take the steps in time order: on board, reported, ships made safe, environment protected, investigated, paid for. Finally, give the "who" explicitly in a closing paragraph, naming each actor against what he does. That closing line is cheap to write and it is a whole limb of the question.

The fact this paper does NOT give you - and why it changes the answer

Read the stem again: there is no oil spill. Later sittings of this same question add "and an oil spill" - this one does not. Yet it still asks about the marine environment. So the environmental limb is about preventing a discharge from a damaged ship, not about cleaning one up. Write it in the preventive voice: isolate and transfer, implement the SOPEP against the probability of a discharge, declare the risk honestly, place the response machinery on standby. The Indian power in Part XA still applies, because it is a power to prevent as well as to contain - and saying that in terms is the discriminating mark. An answer that describes booms, skimmers and shoreline clean-up is answering a different paper.

The three section-number groups you can safely quote

Understand this first

Most debts follow a person. This one follows a thing. If a ship runs up an obligation she cannot pay - unpaid crew, a salvor who saved her, a port whose dues are outstanding, someone killed by the way she was operated - the law gives the claimant a hold over the vessel herself rather than over whoever happens to own her. That matters because the owner is often a company formed to own one ship and nothing else, sitting in a country the claimant will never reach. The hold is invisible: nothing is registered and nothing appears on her papers, so a buyer can pay a fair price for a ship in good faith and still inherit the claim. There is only one way to buy her genuinely clean, and that is to buy her from a court, because the court's sale wipes the slate and moves everyone's claim onto the money instead of the hull. Once that happens the argument stops being about the ship and becomes an argument about a pot of cash, and the last part of the question is simply the queue: who gets paid, in what order, out of what is left.

Model written answer

1. What a maritime lien is

A maritime lien is a privileged claim against the vessel herself - against the res, the thing - and not against any person. Four features define it, and they should be given as a list because the rest of the answer follows from them:

  1. It attaches to the vessel by operation of law, not by agreement. Nobody grants it and nobody signs anything.
  2. It arises at the moment the claim arises. There is no registration and no notice - which is why it is sometimes called a secret lien. Nothing on the ship's papers reveals it.
  3. It is inchoate until enforced. It sleeps with the ship, and is crystallised by arrest and, ultimately, by forced sale.
  4. It ranks ahead of registered mortgages - which is its whole commercial significance, because the mortgagee has done everything the law asks of him and still comes second.

The justification is practical rather than technical. A seafarer left unpaid in a foreign port, a salvor who has saved a ship, a port whose dues are unpaid, the dependants of somebody killed by the ship's operation - none of them can realistically pursue an owner through a chain of single-ship companies in another jurisdiction. The lien gives them the ship instead.

2. How the lien travels with the property

This is the second printed demand and it has a precise answer. The lien follows the vessel - the phrase in both the Convention and the Indian Act is that it continues to exist on the vessel notwithstanding any change of ownership, or of registration, or of flag.

  • Sale does not defeat it. A buyer who pays full value in good faith, with no notice of the claim, still takes the ship subject to the lien.
  • Re-registration does not defeat it, and neither does a change of flag. The lien is attached to the hull, not to the register.
  • A change of name or of manager does not defeat it.
  • So it is defeated by only two things. Time - the lien is extinguished after one year unless, before that period expires, the vessel has been arrested or seized and that arrest has led to a forced sale; and for the crew wages lien the period is two years. Time runs continuously, without suspension or interruption, from the claimant's discharge from the vessel in a wages claim and from when the claim arises in the others - though any period during which the vessel was under arrest is excluded.
  • And forced sale by the court. On a judicial sale the vessel vests in the purchaser free from all encumbrances, liens, attachments, registered mortgages and charges. That is precisely what makes a court sale worth more than a private one: the buyer gets a clean ship, and the liens transfer to the proceeds of sale instead.

The connection between the two limbs of the question is the point worth making explicitly: the maritime lien is what gives the claimant a right in rem against the ship. Without it he has only a personal claim against whoever happens to own her.

In practice, arrest is the whole mechanism. The vessel is arrested by order of the court; she cannot trade; and the owner or his P&I club must put up security - a club letter of undertaking, a bank guarantee or a payment into court - to obtain her release. Most claims are settled at that point, because the daily cost of an arrested ship exceeds the claim. If security is not provided, the court orders a forced sale and the proceeds become the fund out of which the claims are settled. The two proceedings are not alternatives: a claimant may pursue in rem and in personam together, and in India the admiralty jurisdiction of the High Courts covers both.

4. Which convention deals with maritime liens

The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993, adopted at Geneva on 6 May 1993 and in force since 5 September 2004. It is the successor to the earlier Brussels Conventions of 1926 and 1967, neither of which attracted wide support.

State the Indian position honestly, because it is a fair oral question: India is not a party to the 1993 Convention. Its scheme nevertheless governs in India, because the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 reproduces it - the same five liens, in the same order, with the same travelling rule and the same time limits. So the Convention answer and the Indian answer coincide, and it is worth saying why they coincide.

5. What the convention secures - the five claims

Article 4 secures five claims by a maritime lien, and the order in which they are listed is the order in which they rank. Learn them in order, because the last demand of the question depends on it:

  1. Wages and other sums due to the master, officers and other members of the complement in respect of their employment on the vessel, including costs of repatriation and social insurance contributions payable on their behalf.
  2. Loss of life or personal injury occurring, whether on land or on water, in direct connection with the operation of the vessel.
  3. Reward for salvage services, including special compensation relating thereto.
  4. Port, canal and other waterway dues, and pilotage dues, and any other statutory dues related to the vessel.
  5. Claims based on tort arising out of loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel, other than loss or damage to cargo and containers carried on her.

Two exclusions complete the picture. No maritime lien attaches to secure a claim arising from damage in connection with the carriage of oil or other hazardous or noxious substances for which compensation is payable under another regime, or from the radioactive properties of nuclear fuel, products or waste. The reasoning is clean: those claims already have their own strict-liability conventions with compulsory insurance behind them, so they do not need a lien as well.

6. How the liens rank among themselves

  • All maritime liens rank ahead of registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges.
  • Among themselves they rank in the order listed above - wages, then loss of life, then salvage, then dues, then tort.
  • But salvage jumps. A salvage lien takes priority over all liens which attached to the vessel BEFORE the salvage operations. The logic is that without the salvor there would have been no ship for anybody else to claim against, so the last rescuer is paid before the earlier creditors.
  • Salvage liens rank among themselves in INVERSE order of time - the most recent salvage first, for the same reason.
  • Liens (a), (b), (d) and (e) rank pari passu among themselves where the fund is insufficient, and are abated rateably.
  • Other liens granted by national law rank after the five Convention liens and after registered mortgages, and are extinguished after six months, or sixty days after a sale to a bona fide purchaser, whichever expires first.

7. The order of settlement after forced sale

This is the last printed demand and it should be given as a clean numbered list. Under article 12, the proceeds of the forced sale are distributed in this order:

  1. The costs and expenses of the arrest, seizure and subsequent sale - including the costs of upkeep of the vessel and crew from the time of arrest, and the wages and associated sums incurred during that period. These come off the top, before any claimant, because without them there would be no fund at all.
  2. Optionally, the costs of wreck removal, where the national law of the State of forced sale gives them that priority.
  3. The five maritime liens of article 4, in their article 5 order - wages, loss of life, salvage, dues, tort - with salvage jumping ahead of any lien that attached before the salvage.
  4. The right of retention of a shipbuilder or shiprepairer, satisfied after the article 4 liens.
  5. Registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges.
  6. Other maritime liens granted under national law.
  7. Any residue to the owner, and the vessel is delivered to the purchaser free of every one of these claims.

8. The Indian statutory position, kept separate

Keep this as its own short section rather than mixing it into the Convention answer, and the examiner can see that you know they are two instruments.

  • The Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 - in force 1 April 2018 - consolidated Indian admiralty law and conferred admiralty jurisdiction on the High Courts.
  • Section 9(1) lists the same five maritime liens in the same order: wages first, loss of life second, salvage third, dues fourth, tort fifth.
  • Section 9(2) carries the travelling rule and the time limits - the lien continues notwithstanding any change of ownership, registration or flag, and is extinguished after one year, or two years for the wages claim, unless arrest or seizure has led to a forced sale.
  • Section 10 gives the overall order of priority in an admiralty proceeding: first, claims where there is a maritime lien; second, registered mortgages and charges of the same nature; third, all other claims - with claims in a single category ranking equally, and various salvages ranking in inverse order of time.
  • Section 8 is the vesting provision: on a court sale the vessel passes to the purchaser free from all encumbrances, liens, attachments, registered mortgages and charges.
  • Sections 5 and 6 provide respectively for arrest of a vessel in rem and for admiralty jurisdiction in personam - the two terms the question asks about, expressly separated by the Indian statute itself.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem sets five demands against sixteen marks with no printed allocation: define the lien, explain how it travels, explain in rem and in personam, name the convention, and give the order of settlement. That is roughly three marks each, so nothing may be allowed to run long. The commonest failure is a leisurely definition followed by a rushed or missing order of settlement - and the order is the single most concrete, most markable item in the question. Write the definitions tightly and give both ordered lists as numbered lists: the five liens, then the seven-step distribution. A numbered list is faster to write than prose and far easier to mark.

The single idea that carries the answer

The lien attaches to the ship, not to the owner. Everything follows from that one sentence. It is why the lien survives a sale - the ship has not changed even though her owner has. It is why the claimant sues the ship in rem rather than a person in personam. It is why arrest is the remedy. And it is why the whole question ends in a distribution of a fund: once the ship is sold, the ship is gone, and all that is left is the money she made.

The five liens, in order - and the one that jumps

  1. Wages - master, officers and complement, including repatriation costs and social insurance contributions.
  2. Loss of life or personal injury in direct connection with the operation of the vessel.
  3. Salvage reward, including special compensation.
  4. Port, canal, waterway and pilotage dues.
  5. Tort claims for loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel - excluding cargo and containers.

Wages first. This has been published the wrong way round before, with salvage first and wages second, and it is worth being certain: the seafarer comes ahead of the salvor in the list. But salvage jumps ahead of any lien that attached before the salvage operations - which is not the same thing as salvage ranking first in the list. Hold those two statements together and you have the whole of the ranking limb.

Why salvage jumps, and why the newest salvage is paid first

Both rules have the same justification and it is worth one sentence, because it makes them impossible to forget. Without the salvor there would be no ship to claim against at all. Every earlier creditor's security exists only because the salvor preserved it, so the salvor is paid before them. And where there have been several salvages, the most recent one preserved the fund for all the earlier ones - hence inverse order of time.

The three ways a lien ends

  • Time. One year - two years for wages - running continuously without suspension or interruption, unless arrest or seizure has led to a forced sale before it expires. The period under arrest is excluded.
  • Forced sale. The ship vests in the purchaser free of everything, and the liens transfer to the proceeds.
  • Payment or settlement of the underlying claim.
  • Note what does not end it: sale, change of registration, change of flag, change of name, or a purchaser's good faith and lack of notice.

India is not a party - say so, and say why it does not matter

India has not acceded to the 1993 Convention. A candidate who says "India is a party" is wrong, and a candidate who therefore concludes that the Convention scheme does not apply in India is also wrong. The correct position is that the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 reproduces the Convention's scheme in domestic law - the same five liens, the same order, the same travelling rule, the same one-year and two-year limits. So the answer is the same, but for a different reason, and being able to say that is a distinguishing mark.

Common mistakes

  • Putting salvage first in the list of liens. Wages are first; salvage is third but jumps ahead of earlier-attached liens.
  • Saying a lien must be registered. It arises by operation of law, with no registration and no notice - hence "secret lien".
  • Saying a bona fide purchaser without notice takes free of it. He does not; only a forced sale gives a clean ship.
  • Confusing in rem with arrest. Arrest is the procedural remedy by which an in rem claim is enforced.
  • Treating in rem and in personam as alternatives. They may be pursued together.
  • Forgetting the two-year period for wages - it is the one exception to the one-year rule.
  • Omitting that the costs of arrest, sale and upkeep come off the top, before any claimant.
  • Saying India is a party to the 1993 Convention.
  • Including cargo damage among the tort liens. Cargo and containers are expressly excluded.
  • Running out of time before the order of settlement, which is the most concrete thing in the question.

Examiner traps

"The ship was sold to a new owner and reflagged before your claim was brought. Is your lien gone?" No. It continues notwithstanding any change of ownership, registration or flag, and it binds the new owner even if he bought in good faith without notice.

"So how does anyone ever buy a ship safely?" Through a forced sale by the court. The vessel then vests in the purchaser free from all encumbrances, liens, attachments, mortgages and charges, and the claims attach to the proceeds instead. That is why a judicial sale commands a better price than a distressed private one.

"Salvors saved her in January and again in June. Who is paid first?" The June salvors. Salvage liens rank in inverse order of time, because the later service preserved the fund for the earlier claimant.

"A cargo owner's containers were crushed. Does he have a maritime lien?" No. The tort lien expressly excludes loss of or damage to cargo and containers carried on the vessel. He has a maritime claim and may arrest, but not a lien, so he ranks after the mortgagee.

  • Which convention? The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993 - Geneva, 6 May 1993, in force 5 September 2004.
  • Is India a party? No - but the Admiralty Act, 2017 reproduces its scheme.
  • How long does a lien last? One year, and two years for crew wages, running continuously.
  • What defeats it? Only forced sale, or the lapse of that period without an arrest leading to a forced sale.
  • What is paid first out of the sale proceeds? The costs and expenses of arrest, seizure, sale and the upkeep of vessel and crew.
  • Where do registered mortgages come? After all the maritime liens, and after a shipbuilder's or shiprepairer's right of retention.

  • Which Indian sections? Section 9 for the liens and their priority, section 10 for the overall order, section 8 for vesting on sale, sections 5 and 6 for in rem and in personam.

Memory framework

Uncertainty and evidence

This question rests on two independent evidence streams which agree, and that is unusually strong for a legal question in this paper. The Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 was read verbatim in the MIW corpus - sections 8, 9 and 10 in full - so the Indian limb is primary verified. The 1993 Convention is NOT held by the corpus, and its articles are carried through an MIW record of an answer whose verification states that the certified United Nations text was read directly; that limb is therefore internal verified reuse rather than a reading made here. The two were compared provision by provision and correspond exactly, which is what one would expect given that the Indian Act gives domestic effect to the Convention's scheme - and it means an error in one stream would have shown up as a disagreement with the other. Three restraints are deliberate. No monetary figure or SDR value appears, because maritime liens are not capped and the question does not reach limitation of liability. The optional wreck-removal priority is described as optional and is not asserted to be part of Indian law, because whether India has exercised that option is not established from held sources. And the printed stem's phrase "debtor's claims" is loose - the claims distributed are the creditors' claims against the debtor's vessel - but the printed wording is preserved and answered as plainly meant rather than corrected.

Understand this first

A propeller wastes energy in a small number of identifiable ways, and once you know what they are, every device in this question explains itself. Some of the energy goes into water thrown backwards faster than it needed to be - fixed by pushing a bigger column of water more gently, which means a larger, slower propeller. Some goes into spinning the water rather than moving it aft, and spinning water pushes nothing; that waste can be attacked twice, once by twisting the flow the opposite way before it reaches the blades so the propeller has less spin left to give it, and once by catching the spin afterwards and turning it back into forward push. Some is wasted because the hull does not hand the propeller a clean, even stream of water - the flow arriving at the top of the circle is sluggish and disturbed - so each blade is loaded and unloaded as it goes round, which costs efficiency and also shakes the ship and eats the blades. And some leaks away in tight whirlpools shed at the blade tips and at the hub. The reason all this suddenly became urgent is that fuel burnt and carbon emitted are the same quantity measured two ways, so anything that improves the hydrodynamics is now a compliance measure and not merely an economy.

Model written answer

1. Why these devices exist, and where the losses actually are

The examiner's opening words - "recent stress on the control of emission" - are, at this examination, very recent indeed, and the answer should say so precisely rather than generally.

  • EEDI has for some years required a new ship's attained Energy Efficiency Design Index not to exceed a required index that tightens by phase. That put a number on design efficiency and is what first drove these devices onto newbuildings.
  • The decisive change is only months old. Under MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4 as revised by resolution MEPC.328(76), the EEXI - the Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index - and the operational Carbon Intensity Indicator became applicable from 1 January 2023, with the SEEMP Part III required on board from the same date. EEXI extended a design-efficiency requirement to the existing fleet for the first time, and it is the single reason retrofit energy-saving devices moved from optional to commercially necessary.
  • CII is the new pressure and it has not yet bitten. 2023 is the first data-collection year for operational carbon intensity, so no ship has yet been assigned a rating - the first ratings follow in the next reporting cycle. Owners are therefore acting in anticipation of a rating, which is exactly why efficiency devices are being fitted now rather than later.
  • Policy context. The operative IMO policy instrument is the Initial IMO GHG Strategy of 2018, with its ambitions on carbon intensity and on total annual emissions. Commercially, charterers are already pricing efficiency, and fuel remains the largest controllable operating cost.
  • The engineering consequence is direct. CO2 emitted is a fixed multiple of fuel burnt, so a percentage saved in propulsive efficiency is the same percentage saved in emissions. There is no separate abatement step - which is why hydrodynamics became a compliance subject.

To discuss the devices sensibly, first name where the energy actually goes, because every device attacks one of these losses:

  • Axial loss - energy left behind in the accelerated slipstream. Reduced by a larger, more slowly turning propeller.
  • Rotational loss - the propeller leaves the water spinning, and that rotation does no useful work. Attacked before the disc by pre-swirl and after it by post-swirl.
  • Frictional and sectional loss - blade drag. Attacked by section shape and blade area.
  • Tip and hub vortex loss - concentrated vortices shed at the blade tips and at the boss. Attacked by tip-loaded blade designs and by boss cap fins.
  • Non-uniform inflow - the hull delivers the propeller a badly distorted wake field, which costs efficiency and causes cavitation and vibration. Attacked by wake equalizing devices.

2. The efficiency framework that classifies all four limbs

The four limbs are not an arbitrary list. They map exactly onto the components of quasi-propulsive efficiency, which is the product of three terms:

ComponentWhat it measuresWhich limb improves it
Open-water efficiencyHow well the propeller converts delivered power into thrust in uniform flow(d) - the propeller itself
Hull efficiency, (1 - t) / (1 - w)The interaction between hull and propeller - thrust deduction and wake fraction(a) - the flow the hull delivers
Relative rotative efficiencyThe correction for a non-uniform, rotating inflow(b) puts rotation in; (c) takes it back out

So the shape of the answer is: (a) works on the flow INTO the disc, (b) puts rotation IN ahead of it, (c) takes rotation OUT behind it, and (d) improves the disc itself. Say that in one sentence and the four limbs become a system rather than a list.

3. a) Wake Equalizing Devices

  • The problem. Behind a full-form single-screw hull the inflow to the propeller is strongly non-uniform: there is a wake peak at the top of the disc, where the water is moving slowly with the hull, and there is afterbody separation. Each blade therefore passes cyclically from fast to slow water, giving cyclic blade loading, cavitation, hull pressure pulses, vibration and erosion as well as lost efficiency.
  • The principle. An appendage fitted ahead of the propeller which accelerates and straightens the slow water in the upper part of the disc and suppresses separation. It raises hull efficiency and, just as importantly, improves the environment the propeller works in.
  • The forms. The classic wake equalising duct - a pair of half-ring nozzles on the afterbody either side of the sternframe; flow-guiding fins and stern spoilers; and, at design stage, stern tunnels and changes to the afterbody form itself.
  • Note that the categories are not watertight. A combined duct with internal pre-swirl fins belongs to both this limb and limb (b), and saying so shows understanding rather than confusion.
  • Gain and limits. Indicatively a few per cent, up to high single figures on a suitable hull - and the vibration and cavitation benefit is often worth as much as the fuel. Best on full-form, high block coefficient, single-screw ships at moderate speed. On fine, fast hulls the added appendage drag can exceed the gain, so the device must be matched to the hull and not simply bought.

4. b) Pre-swirl Devices

  • The problem. A propeller does not only push water aft; it also spins it. The rotational kinetic energy left in the slipstream is pure loss - it produces no thrust at all.
  • The principle. Impart rotation opposite to the propeller's own direction in the flow upstream of the disc. The propeller must first undo that counter-rotation before imparting its own, so less net swirl leaves the disc and more of the delivered power goes into axial thrust.
  • The forms. Fixed radial pre-swirl stators mounted on the hull or the stern boss ahead of the propeller - characteristically asymmetric, with different fin numbers, angles and lengths port and starboard, because the wake behind a single-screw hull is itself asymmetric. Pre-swirl ducts combining a nozzle with internal stator fins, which are the most widely retrofitted device on full-form ships. And at design stage, asymmetric or twisted afterbodies that build the pre-swirl into the hull.
  • Gain and limits. Indicatively a few per cent. The device is tuned to a design draught, speed and loading, so the benefit falls away in ballast or at part load, and a badly matched stator can cost efficiency rather than gain it.
  • Engineering cautions. The fins add wetted surface and drag; they must be designed not to excite blade-rate vibration; and they are a fatigue-critical welded appendage in unsteady flow, so weld detail, inspection and cathodic protection matter.

5. c) Post-swirl Devices

The principle is the mirror image of limb (b): whatever swirl the propeller does impart, recover it downstream - turn slipstream rotation into axial thrust, or remove the concentrated hub vortex.

  • Propeller boss cap fins. Small fins on the boss cap, rotating with the propeller, which break up the hub vortex. Indicatively low single figures, among the cheapest retrofits available, and they also reduce hub-vortex cavitation and the rudder erosion it causes.
  • Rudder bulb. A bulb on the rudder aligned with the propeller boss, filling the low-pressure hub region, reducing the hub vortex and the associated drag. Commonly supplied with boss cap fins and a twisted rudder as a matched package.
  • Rudder thrust fins. Fins set in the slipstream which convert residual swirl into forward force. They are angled oppositely above and below the shaft line, because the swirl reverses sense across the disc - a detail worth stating, because it shows you understand what they are doing.
  • Twisted and asymmetric rudders. The leading edge is twisted to match the local swirl angle, which reduces rudder drag and cavitation erosion and improves manoeuvring.
  • Free-rotating vane wheels. A larger-diameter freewheeling wheel abaft the propeller, theoretically attractive but structurally troublesome in service, and not widely adopted.
  • Gain and limits. Low single figures individually, and more when designed as a system - a boss cap fin, a rudder bulb and a twisted rudder developed together deliver more than the sum of three catalogue items. They sit in unsteady, cavitating, erosive flow, so material selection, cathodic protection and inspection matter, and a lost fin is a hazard to the rudder and propeller.

6. d) High-efficiency Propellers

  • Larger diameter at lower revolutions. This is the single most effective measure of all and should be stated first. Accelerating a larger mass of water to a smaller velocity increase cuts axial loss directly. It is enabled by long-stroke low-speed engines and a suitable aperture, and it aligns naturally with slow steaming.
  • Tip-loaded and contracted-tip designs. End plates or winglets at the blade tip weaken the tip vortex, so the tip can carry more load without the vortex loss that would normally follow.
  • Curved-tip designs achieve the same effect more smoothly, with a better pressure distribution and less cavitation risk.
  • Contra-rotating propellers. Two coaxial propellers turning in opposite directions, the after one recovering the rotational energy of the forward one. This offers the largest single gain available, but requires concentric shafting and a complex sterntube and bearing arrangement - which is why it remains rare in large merchant ships.
  • Ducted propellers. An accelerating (Kort) nozzle for heavily loaded propellers at low speed - hence tugs and trawlers, where thrust at bollard pull matters more than free-running efficiency. A decelerating nozzle where cavitation and radiated noise dominate.
  • Blade geometry. Skew spreads each blade's passage through the wake peak over a range of angles, cutting pressure pulses and vibration; and section shape, pitch distribution and blade area ratio are matched to the ship's actual operating profile - which, with slow steaming, is often no longer the profile she was designed for.

7. Selection, verification and the cautions common to all four

  • Nothing here is generic. Every device is matched to a particular hull, draught, speed and loading condition, and the design tools are CFD followed by model testing. A manufacturer's published gain was obtained on somebody else's hull.
  • The operating profile has moved. Slow steaming means many ships now run well away from their design point, so a device tuned to the original condition may deliver much less - and a propeller re-matched to the actual profile may deliver more than any appendage.
  • Devices interact. Fitting a pre-swirl stator changes the flow a post-swirl device sees. They are designed as a package, and gains are not additive.
  • Housekeeping first. A fouled hull or a rough, damaged propeller will lose more than any of these devices gains. Hull and propeller cleaning and coating come before an energy-saving device, and it is worth one sentence to say so.
  • Verification. A claimed gain must be demonstrated in service against a measured baseline - the ISO 19030 approach to measuring changes in hull and propeller performance - and not assumed from the brochure.
  • Structural and class consequences. These are welded appendages in unsteady, cavitating flow. They need classification society approval, fatigue-conscious detailing, cathodic protection, and inclusion in the docking and survey regime. A detached fin damages the rudder or the propeller.
  • Compliance credit. Because these devices reduce the power needed for a given speed, they feed directly into the EEXI calculation for an existing ship - alongside the alternative of engine or shaft power limitation - and into the operational carbon intensity that will be rated. That is the link back to the examiner's opening sentence.

Common mistakes

  • Letting limb (d) eat the answer. Four marks each - the examiner has said so.
  • Confusing pre-swirl and post-swirl. Pre-swirl is ahead of the disc and imparts rotation against the propeller's direction; post-swirl is behind it and recovers what the propeller imparted.
  • Treating a wake equalising duct as a pre-swirl device. It equalises the inflow; only a duct with internal stator fins does both - and it is worth saying so rather than blurring them.
  • Giving one precise percentage for a device, with no hull or condition attached.
  • Forgetting that a device is tuned to a condition and loses its benefit in ballast or at part load.
  • Omitting the vibration and cavitation benefit of wake equalisation, which is often worth as much as the fuel.
  • Ignoring the structural, class and inspection consequences of a welded appendage in cavitating flow.
  • Claiming gains are additive when devices are fitted together.

Examiner traps

"Your owner wants a pre-swirl stator fitted. What do you ask first?" What hull form, what draught and what speed she actually operates at - because the device is tuned to a condition, and a ship trading in ballast or slow steaming well below her design point may see very little. And whether the hull and propeller are clean, because that comes first.

"Are the gains additive?" No. A pre-swirl stator changes the flow the post-swirl device sees. They must be designed as a package, and the combined gain is less than the sum of the individual claims.

"Why is a Kort nozzle used on a tug and not on a container ship?" An accelerating nozzle helps a heavily loaded propeller at low speed, which is a tug's condition. On a fast, lightly loaded propeller the nozzle's own drag exceeds the benefit.

"Boss cap fins are cheap. Any downside?" They rotate with the propeller in unsteady, cavitating, erosive flow. Material, attachment and cathodic protection matter, and a detached fin is a hazard to the rudder.

Open-water efficiency is improved by the propeller design itself; hull efficiency, expressed as one minus thrust deduction over one minus wake fraction, is improved by wake equalizing devices; and relative rotative efficiency is where pre-swirl and post-swirl devices act.

It turns four apparently unrelated limbs into three efficiency terms, which is what separates a system answer from a list.

Pre-swirl devices sit ahead of the propeller and impart rotation opposite to the propeller's own direction, so the propeller must undo that counter-rotation first and less net swirl leaves the disc. Post-swirl devices sit behind the propeller and recover the swirl it has already imparted, converting slipstream rotation into axial thrust or removing the hub vortex.

Before or behind, and rotation put in or taken out - the two are constantly confused and the distinction is the heart of limbs (b) and (c).

Behind a full-form single-screw hull the inflow is strongly non-uniform, with a wake peak at the top of the disc and afterbody separation, so each blade passes cyclically between fast and slow water. The device accelerates and straightens the slow upper-disc water and suppresses separation. Besides fuel it delivers reduced cyclic blade loading, cavitation, hull pressure pulses, vibration and erosion - often worth as much as the fuel saving.

The vibration and cavitation benefit is the part most candidates omit, and on many ships it is the reason the device was fitted.

Because the wake field behind a single-screw hull is itself asymmetric - the propeller's rotation and the hull's flow interact differently on the two sides. The stator is therefore built with different fin numbers, angles and lengths port and starboard to match the flow it actually sees.

A single detail that demonstrates the device is matched to a real flow field rather than bolted on symmetrically for neatness.

Propeller boss cap fins, rotating with the propeller to break up the hub vortex. A rudder bulb aligned with the boss, filling the low-pressure hub region. Rudder thrust fins, converting residual swirl into forward force and angled oppositely above and below the shaft line because the swirl reverses sense. Twisted or asymmetric rudders, with the leading edge matched to the local swirl angle. And free-rotating vane wheels, attractive in theory but troublesome in service.

Five named devices with a mechanism each is exactly what a four-mark limb needs, and the thrust-fin angling detail shows real understanding.

A larger diameter turning at lower revolutions. Accelerating a larger mass of water to a smaller velocity increase cuts axial loss directly. It is enabled by long-stroke low-speed engines and a suitable aperture, and it aligns naturally with slow steaming.

Candidates reach for exotic blade designs when the biggest gain is the simplest change, and saying so first frames the whole limb.

An accelerating nozzle benefits a heavily loaded propeller working at low speed, which is a tug's or trawler's condition at bollard pull. On a fast ship with a lightly loaded propeller the nozzle's own drag exceeds any benefit. A decelerating nozzle is a different case, used where cavitation and radiated noise dominate.

It tests whether the candidate understands that a device is matched to a loading condition rather than being generally good.

No. The pre-swirl stator changes the flow that the post-swirl device sees, so the two interact and the combined gain is less than the sum of the individual claims. They should be designed as a matched package by CFD and model testing on the actual hull, and the result verified in service against a measured baseline.

Understand this first

Two ships can spill the same oil and, until fairly recently, only one of them would have been covered by a proper compensation system. The older system was written after tanker disasters, so it was built around the idea of a ship whose cargo is oil. That left an odd gap: a large container ship or bulk carrier is not carrying oil as cargo at all, but she is carrying several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel to burn, which is more than the whole cargo of a small tanker, and if she goes on the rocks that fuel comes out. Anybody harmed by it had to fall back on ordinary law - prove somebody was at fault, find out who actually owns the ship, and hope there is money behind the name. The newer convention copies the older one almost point for point to fix that: no need to prove fault, insurance made compulsory, a certificate carried aboard, and the right to sue the insurer directly. Two things are different, and they pull in opposite directions. It casts the net of who can be sued much wider, catching the manager and operator as well as the owner on paper. But it never wrote down a ceiling of its own, and it never set up a fund financed by anyone else - so when the money runs out, it runs out.

Model written answer

1. The background - the gap the Convention was written to close

The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage was adopted on 23 March 2001 and entered into force on 21 November 2008. It exists to close a specific and well-recognised hole.

  • CLC 1992 reaches only tankers. Its definitions confine it to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil carried in bulk as cargo - and to the bunkers of such a ship.
  • Every other ship in the world fleet fell outside it. A large bulk carrier, container ship or cruise ship may carry several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel oil in her own bunker tanks - more than the entire cargo of a small tanker - and a grounding could release it.
  • Before 2001 there was no dedicated regime for that oil. No strict liability, no compulsory insurance, no certificate and no direct action against an insurer. A claimant had to prove fault under national law and then hope the owner was solvent and reachable.
  • The Convention closes the gap by taking the CLC architecture and applying it to bunkers - which was deliberate, because it meant the existing P&I machinery could simply be extended rather than rebuilt.

2. Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture

Its relevance is best shown by putting it in its place among the three regimes, because that is also the frame for the comparison the question asks for:

RegimeReachesSecond tier?
CLC 1992Persistent oil carried as cargo by a tankerYes - the 1992 Fund and the Supplementary Fund
Bunkers 2001The fuel of every ship, whatever she carriesNo - there is no bunker fund
HNSOther cargoes by categoryYes - the HNS Fund

So the Bunker Convention completes the map, and it does so without a cargo-financed second tier. That single omission is the source of most of the differences the rest of this answer sets out - most importantly the fact that the Convention sets no limit of its own.

3. (a) Pollution damage and exclusions

Definition first, as the question directs. Under article 1.9, pollution damage means:

  • loss or damage caused OUTSIDE the ship BY CONTAMINATION resulting from the escape or discharge of bunker oil, wherever it occurs - provided that compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit from that impairment, is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken; and
  • the costs of preventive measures, and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

How it differs from CLC. The structure of the definition is identical - contamination outside the ship, the reinstatement proviso, preventive measures. What differs is the oil. CLC reaches only persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil carried as cargo; the Bunker Convention reaches bunker oil, defined at article 1.5 as any hydrocarbon mineral oil, INCLUDING LUBRICATING OIL, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of it. Note three things in that definition: it is not confined to persistent oil, it expressly includes lubricating oil, and it turns on the oil's use rather than on its carriage.

The exclusions:

  1. Article 4.1 - the CLC carve-out. The Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable in respect of it under that Convention. This is the load-bearing exclusion and the wording matters: the carve-out attaches to the definition, not to the outcome. So if a CLC claim fails - the fund is exhausted, a defence succeeds, the flag State is not a party - the claim does not fall back into the Bunker Convention.
  2. Article 4.2 - warships and State-owned ships used on government non-commercial service.
  3. Geographic scope, article 2 - the Convention applies exclusively to pollution damage in the territory, including the territorial sea, and in the exclusive economic zone of a State Party; but to preventive measures wherever taken, which is a deliberately wider reach so that a response mounted on the high seas is still covered.
  4. Note that the shipowner's defences are a different thing and should not be listed here - they go under limb (c).

4. (b) Time limits for claims

This limb is short, and its point is that there is no difference at all - which is itself the answer and should be stated plainly. Under Bunkers article 8, and identically under CLC article VIII:

  • Rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within THREE YEARS from the date the damage occurred;
  • and in no case later than SIX YEARS from the date of the incident;
  • where the incident consists of a series of occurrences, the six years run from the date of the first occurrence.

Two refinements are worth a line each. The provision extinguishes the right rather than merely barring the remedy, which is stronger than an ordinary limitation period. And the contrast that shows breadth is with HNS, where the period runs three years from KNOWLEDGE of the damage rather than from its occurrence, with a ten-year long stop - a deliberately more generous scheme, because chemical harm may not be apparent for years.

5. (c) Liability of the shipowner

Definition first. Under article 3.1 the shipowner at the time of an incident is strictly liable for pollution damage caused by any bunker oil on board or originating from the ship. Strict liability means no proof of fault is required. There are only three defences, under article 3.3, and they are the same three CLC provides:

  1. an act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection, or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon;
  2. an act or omission done by a third party with intent to cause damage;
  3. the negligence of an authority responsible for the maintenance of navigational aids.

Here is the sharpest difference in the whole question, and it runs in opposite directions:

CLC 1992Bunkers 2001

This is the difference candidates most often get backwards, so state it in one memorable line: CLC funnels liability in; Bunkers spreads it out.

6. (c) Limitation of liability - the structural difference

The second half of limb (c), and structurally the most important difference of all.

  • CLC has its own, self-contained limits. Article V sets a tonnage-based scale - 4,510,000 units of account for a ship not exceeding 5,000 gross tonnage, 631 units of account for each additional ton, subject to an overall ceiling of 89,770,000 units of account. The owner constitutes a limitation fund with the court, and the fund is available exclusively to pollution claimants. Behind it stand the 1992 Fund and the Supplementary Fund. The right to limit is lost where the damage resulted from the owner's personal act or omission committed with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that such damage would probably result.
  • The Bunker Convention sets NO figure at all. Article 6 expressly preserves the shipowner's right to limit under any applicable national or international regime, such as the LLMC as amended - and stops there.
  • Three consequences follow, and they are the marks in this half of the limb. First, the limit is tonnage-based under a general convention, not a pollution-specific one. Second, the bunker claim shares one general limitation fund with every other claim arising from the same incident - cargo, wreck removal where applicable, personal injury - instead of having a fund to itself. And third, there is no second tier: no bunker equivalent of the IOPC Fund exists, so once the LLMC fund is exhausted the claimant has nowhere else to go.
  • The practical result is that a large bunker spill from a very large ship can exhaust the available fund in a way a comparable CLC incident would not.

7. (d) Compulsory insurance and financial security

Definition first. Under article 7, the registered owner of a ship of more than 1,000 gross tonnage registered in a State Party must maintain insurance or other financial security - a bank guarantee or a certificate from a compensation fund - to cover liability for pollution damage, in an amount equal to the applicable limits of liability and not exceeding an amount calculated under the LLMC as amended. A certificate in the annexed model form is issued to each ship and carried on board.

  • Note the asymmetry inside the Bunker Convention itself, which is a good discriminating point: liability under article 3 is wide - owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator - but the duty to insure under article 7 falls on the registered owner alone. A claimant therefore has several possible defendants but only one certificate to look at.
  • Direct action against the insurer is provided in both. The insurer may invoke the defences the shipowner himself could have invoked, and - importantly - may limit liability even where the shipowner has lost the right to limit. The insurer may also plead the shipowner's own wilful misconduct.
  • In practice the security is a P&I club undertaking - the club issues a "blue card" to the flag State, which issues the certificate. Issue may be delegated to a recognised organisation.
  • Article 7.12 is the enforcement teeth: a State Party must ensure that ships of ANY flag entering or leaving its ports carry the certificate. So the Convention reaches ships registered in non-party States the moment they call at a party State's port, and a missing certificate is a detainable deficiency.

Taken together, the four limbs give the summary the question is really asking for: the Bunker Convention borrows CLC's definitions and its time limits, widens who is liable, narrows nothing, and gives up CLC's dedicated limitation fund and its second tier in exchange.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints four limbs at (4) each plus an unmarked opening on background and relevance, so budget the opening at about two or three minutes and give the four limbs equal space. Note the exact command: "Define the following under Bunker Convention and explain how they differ from other similar Convention such as CLC 92". That is a two-step instruction and the marks follow it: define the Bunker position FIRST, then compare. An answer that opens each limb with a comparison has inverted the examiner's own order and will read as a general essay on two conventions. Use the same shape in every limb - Bunker definition, then the CLC difference - and it is fast to write and impossible to mark down.

The one sentence that carries the comparison

The Bunker Convention borrows CLC's definitions and its time limits, widens who is liable, and gives up CLC's dedicated fund and its second tier. Every difference in the question is an instance of that sentence: limb (a) is a borrowed definition with a different oil, limb (b) is identical, limb (c) is the widening, and limb (d) is the different threshold that follows from having no cargo to measure.

CLC funnels IN, Bunkers spreads OUT - the difference most often reversed

  • CLC channels liability INWARD to the registered owner alone. Article III expressly bars claims against servants, agents, crew, pilot, charterer, manager, operator and salvor. One defendant, and everyone else is protected.
  • Bunkers spreads liability OUTWARD. Article 1.3 defines 'shipowner' to include the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator, and article 3.2 makes them jointly and severally liable.
  • But note the twist inside the Bunker Convention: the duty to insure under article 7 falls on the REGISTERED OWNER alone, even though liability is wide. Liability is broad; the certificate is narrow.

The limitation point is structural, and it is where the best marks are

CLC contains its own limits and its own fund. The tonnage scale runs from 4,510,000 units of account for a ship of not more than 5,000 gross tonnage, rising by 631 units of account per additional ton, to a ceiling of 89,770,000 units of account; the fund is constituted with the court and is available exclusively to pollution claimants; and the 1992 Fund and Supplementary Fund stand behind it. The Bunker Convention contains no figure whatever. Article 6 simply preserves limitation under whatever regime applies, in practice the LLMC as amended. Three consequences: the limit is tonnage-based under a general convention; the bunker claim competes with every other claim from the same incident in a single fund; and there is no second tier at all. A candidate who says "the Bunker Convention limit is X" has missed the entire point - the Convention has no limit of its own.

The two thresholds - cargo against ship

  • CLC certificate: more than 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo. A cargo threshold - so the same ship may need the certificate on one voyage and not on another.
  • Bunkers certificate: gross tonnage over 1,000. A ship threshold - so it does not change with the trade, which is exactly why the Bunker certificate is carried permanently.
  • That difference is not arbitrary. CLC measures the risk by the cargo, because the cargo is the hazard; Bunkers measures it by the ship, because the ship's own fuel is roughly a function of her size.

The carve-out phrase - memorise it word for word

"Whether or not compensation is payable in respect of it under that Convention." Article 4.1 excludes CLC pollution damage on that basis, and the HNS Convention uses the identical formula. It means the exclusion attaches to the definition and not to the result. A claim that falls within the CLC definition is outside the Bunker Convention even if CLC recovery fails entirely - because the limit is exhausted, because a defence succeeds, or because the flag State is not a party. The regimes tile; they are not safety nets for one another.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Bunker Convention has a limit. It has none - article 6 refers out to the LLMC.
  • Reversing the channelling: CLC is the exclusive one; Bunkers is the wide one.
  • Forgetting that the insurance duty falls on the registered owner alone although liability is wider.
  • Giving the CLC threshold as a tonnage or the Bunkers threshold as a cargo quantity. Cargo for CLC, ship size for Bunkers.
  • Saying bunker oil must be persistent. It need not be - the definition turns on use, and expressly includes lubricating oil.
  • Listing the shipowner's defences under the exclusions limb. Exclusions are what the Convention does not apply to; defences are answers to a claim it does apply to.
  • Saying a failed CLC claim can be brought under Bunkers. It cannot - whether or not compensation is payable.
  • Missing that preventive measures are covered wherever taken, while damage is confined to the territory, territorial sea and EEZ.
  • Forgetting that the time limits are identical - which is a complete answer to limb (b) and a mark in itself.

Examiner traps

"A tanker in ballast spills her own bunkers. Which convention?" CLC still reaches her - it covers oil in the bunkers of a ship constructed or adapted to carry oil in bulk as cargo, and the Bunker Convention's article 4.1 excludes CLC pollution damage. So the answer is CLC, and the reasoning matters more than the answer.

"The owner's LLMC fund is exhausted by cargo and injury claims. What does the bunker claimant get?" A rateable share and nothing more. There is no second tier behind the Bunker Convention, which is precisely the structural weakness the comparison should draw out.

"The ship is registered in a State that is not a party. Does she need a certificate in an Indian port?" Yes. Article 7.12 requires a State Party to ensure that ships of any flag entering or leaving its ports carry the certificate.

"The owner lost his right to limit. Can the insurer still limit?" Yes. On direct action the insurer may limit even where the shipowner is not entitled to, and may raise the defences the shipowner could have raised, plus the shipowner's own wilful misconduct.

  • When was the Bunker Convention adopted and when did it enter into force? Adopted 23 March 2001; in force 21 November 2008.
  • What gap does it fill? Pollution from the bunkers of non-tankers, which CLC never reached.
  • Define bunker oil. Any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of it.
  • Define pollution damage. Loss or damage outside the ship by contamination, with environmental impairment limited to reasonable reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken, plus the costs of preventive measures.
  • Who is the 'shipowner'? The registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator - jointly and severally liable.
  • What are the three defences? War or an exceptional natural phenomenon; a third party's intentional act; and negligence of a navigational-aid authority.

Understand this first

Two different organisations look after the people on ships, and they look at the same person for different reasons. One is interested in whether he can do the job safely - is he trained, is he fit, are there enough of him, is the ship run properly. The other is interested in whether he is being treated decently - paid, fed, housed, given time off, looked after when he is ill, and brought home at the end. For most purposes those are separate concerns, but there is one place where they become the same question, and that is how much sleep he gets. Too little sleep is both a working condition and a safety hazard, which is why the same limits appear in two entirely different rulebooks. The second half of the question is about the deeper problem behind those limits: they are written as arithmetic, so many hours in so many hours, and arithmetic cannot capture what actually restores a person. Sleep taken in two broken pieces at the wrong time of day, in a noisy cabin, in a ship that is moving, after weeks of the same, leaves someone tired even though the paperwork says he has had his entitlement. So the guidance is addressed not only to the tired person but to everyone whose decisions made him tired - the office that set the schedule, the designer who put the cabin over the engine room, and the inspector who reads the record.

1. a) The human element, and how IMO and ILO divide the work

The starting point is the finding that drives the whole subject: the great majority of marine casualties originate in human performance rather than in equipment failure. Steel and machinery have become steadily more reliable; the people operating them have not changed. IMO's own framing of this is resolution A.947(23), Human element vision, principles and goals for the Organization, adopted on 27 November 2003, which commits the Organization to address the human element in all its work.

The two organisations divide the subject cleanly, and stating the division early is what turns limb a) from a list of conventions into an overview:

  • IMO regulates the seafarer as the OPERATOR of a ship - is he competent, is he fit for duty, is the ship safely manned and safely managed?
  • The ILO regulates the seafarer as a WORKER - is he paid, housed, fed, rested, cared for when sick, and able to go home?
  • They interlock wherever working conditions become safety conditions, and hours of rest is the clearest example: it appears in STCW because a tired watchkeeper is dangerous, and in the MLC because excessive hours are an abuse of labour. The examiner's own words - "such as maritime labour and welfare" - point the answer squarely at that overlap.

2. The IMO regime - competence, safe operation and fitness for duty

  • STCW 1978 as amended is the competence instrument. The Manila amendments of 2010, in force 1 January 2012, are the operative revision. Part A of the Code is mandatory and Part B is guidance, and the Part A tables set out, for each function, the competence, the knowledge understanding and proficiency, the methods of demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so certification rests on demonstrated competence, not on time served.
  • The Manila amendments added the human-element competences directly: leadership and teamworking, and engine-room and bridge resource management, together with alcohol limits for masters, officers and seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Fitness for duty - regulation I/9 medical fitness standards; regulation VIII/1 rest periods, expressly for the purpose of preventing fatigue; regulation VIII/2 watchkeeping arrangements and principles.
  • Quality and responsibility - regulation I/6 training and assessment, I/8 quality standards systems operated by Administrations and independently evaluated, I/11 revalidation, and I/14 the responsibilities of companies for valid certificates, familiarisation and documentation.
  • Safe manning - SOLAS regulation V/14, with the principles in resolution A.1047(27) of 30 November 2011. Manning is where competence meets workload, and an under-manned ship generates fatigue no rest table can cure.
  • The ISM Code, as amended through MSC.353(92), is the management layer: element 6 resources and personnel, element 7 shipboard operations, element 8 emergency preparedness and drills, and element 9 reports and analysis of non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences - which is the feedback loop through which human error is supposed to be learned from rather than punished.

3. The ILO regime - MLC 2006, the fourth pillar

  • The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 was adopted at the 94th (Maritime) Session of the International Labour Conference and consolidated a large body of earlier ILO maritime instruments into one. It is universally described as the fourth pillar of the international regime, standing beside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW.
  • Three parts. The Articles set out general obligations; the Regulations state the principle; and the Code gives effect to it in Part A Standards and Part B Guidelines.
  • Article VI is the provision to quote. The Regulations and Part A Standards are MANDATORY; Part B Guidelines must be given DUE CONSIDERATION. And Article VI.3 permits substantial equivalence - a Member may implement a Part A Standard differently provided the provision is substantially equivalent, which is what allows a single Convention to work across very different national systems.
  • Article IV states the seafarers' employment and social rights the Convention exists to secure, and the Convention is organised into five Titles.

  • The provision to know precisely under Title 2 is Standard A2.3.5, which gives Members an election: either a maximum of 14 hours of work in any 24-hour period and 72 hours in any 7 days, or a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7 days. In practice almost every flag adopts the rest-hours formulation, because it aligns with STCW.
  • Abandonment is worth a line, because it is the provision most often tested: a financial security certificate must be carried on board, and the support it must fund is defined - adequate food, accommodation, drinking water, essential fuel for survival on board and necessary medical care, together with outstanding wages and repatriation.

5. Title 5 - what makes the labour regime enforceable

Title 5 is what distinguishes the MLC from earlier ILO conventions, which were rights without machinery. It borrows the enforcement architecture of the IMO conventions wholesale.

  • A Maritime Labour Certificate, valid for not more than five years, with an intermediate inspection.
  • A Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance in two parts - Part I drawn up by the competent authority, listing the national requirements, and Part II by the shipowner, stating the measures adopted to meet them. Appendix A5-I lists the matters to be inspected and certified.
  • Port State control under Regulation 5.2.1, in which the certificate and the DMLC are accepted as prima facie evidence of compliance - so an inspection normally begins and ends with the documents, unless there are grounds to look further.
  • Onshore seafarer complaint-handling under Regulation 5.2.2, and on-board complaint procedures under Title 5 - a seafarer may complain without going through the master alone.
  • Article V.7 - no more favourable treatment. Ships flying the flag of a State that has not ratified the Convention are not to be treated more favourably in the ports of a State that has. This is what gives the Convention practical universality.
  • Regulation 5.3 places responsibilities on labour-supplying States - which matters directly to India, as one of the world's largest suppliers of seafarers.

6. Where the two organisations act jointly, and the state of the regime at this sitting

  • Joint ILO/IMO Guidelines on the fair treatment of seafarers in the event of a maritime accident - a direct response to the criminalisation of seafarers after casualties.
  • Abandonment is handled jointly: the MLC financial security requirement on one side, and the joint ILO/IMO database on abandonment of seafarers on the other.
  • ILO Convention No. 185 on seafarers' identity documents, which underpins shore leave and transit.
  • The state of the MLC at this sitting must be stated carefully, and it is the discriminating temporal point of the whole question. The Convention is in force as amended through the 2018 amendments. A further set of amendments was adopted in 2022 - covering, among other things, the inclusion of social connectivity in the recreational facilities required on board, appropriately-sized personal protective equipment, and the facilitation of repatriation of the body or ashes of a seafarer who dies during a voyage. Those amendments are ADOPTED but NOT YET IN FORCE. They should be described as coming, not as required - and a candidate who states them as current obligations has made exactly the error the tacit-amendment procedure invites.

7. B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The instrument is MSC.1/Circ.1598, "Guidelines on fatigue", dated 24 January 2019, approved at MSC 100 and superseding MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. Two things about its status should be said at once: it is recommendatory, not mandatory, and it is guidance that sits on top of the mandatory rest-hour rules rather than replacing them.

It is arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party - which is itself the point, because fatigue cannot be managed by the seafarer alone:

  1. Module 1 - Fatigue. What it is, what causes it, and what it does. Causes are grouped as crew-specific factors (sleep and rest, body clock, health, stress), management factors ashore and on board, ship-specific factors (design, motion, noise, vibration, accommodation) and environmental factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods - which is the key idea, because it explains why a single good night does not repair a bad fortnight.
  2. Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company. Manning levels, scheduling, workload, port turnaround, realistic planning, and a reporting culture in which a seafarer can say he is too tired.
  3. Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer. Personal responsibility - sleep hygiene, use of off-watch time, diet, alcohol, caffeine, and recognising one's own symptoms.
  4. Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training. Fatigue must be taught, because its symptoms are not obvious to the fatigued.
  5. Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design. Accommodation location, noise and vibration, motion, lighting and habitability designed in from the start.
  6. Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities. Safe manning determination, inspection of rest records, and the enforcement side.

8. How the guidance and the mandatory rules fit together

This is where the two limbs of the question join, and it is the best place to finish.

  • The mandatory layer is a prescriptive formula. STCW regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, expressly for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require watch systems arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The numbers: a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period; daily rest divisible into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours; and no more than 14 hours between consecutive periods of rest. Where authorised exceptions apply, weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours, exceptions may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • MLC Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7 - hours of work and rest, entitlement to leave, and manning levels - run in parallel and to the same effect.
  • The central insight of the guidance is that compliance with the formula is not the same as being rested. Rest broken into two short periods against the body clock, in a noisy cabin, in heavy weather, at the end of a long tour, does not restore a seafarer the way an equal number of unbroken night hours would.
  • The practical consequence, and the sentence worth ending on: a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant and never breaches should be treated as a warning rather than a reassurance. Real operations are untidy; a perfect record usually means the record is being written to the rule rather than to the day.

The MLC architecture in four lines

  • Articles, Regulations, Code - and the Code splits into Part A Standards (mandatory) and Part B Guidelines (due consideration). That is Article VI, and it is the single most quotable provision in the Convention.
  • Article VI.3 - substantial equivalence. A Member may implement a Standard differently if the provision is substantially equivalent. This is what makes one convention workable across very different legal systems.
  • Five Titles: 1 minimum requirements, 2 conditions of employment, 3 accommodation and food, 4 health and welfare, 5 compliance and enforcement.
  • Article V.7 - no more favourable treatment. Non-ratifying flags get no advantage in a ratifying State's ports. It is why the MLC bites almost universally.

The numbers you must have exactly right

  • STCW A-VIII/1 and MLC Standard A2.3.5: 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours; 77 hours in any 7 days; no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours; no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods.
  • The MLC alternative formulation: maximum 14 hours of work in any 24 and 72 hours in any 7 days. Most flags adopt the rest-hours version.
  • Exceptions: weekly rest not below 70 hours; exceptions not for more than two consecutive weeks; daily rest then in no more than three periods.
  • Maritime Labour Certificate - not more than five years, with an intermediate inspection.
  • MSC.1/Circ.1598, dated 24 January 2019, six modules, superseding MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001.

The idea that earns the best marks in limb B

Compliance with the rest formula is not the same as being rested. The formula counts hours; fatigue is about the quality and timing of sleep. Ten hours split into two blocks, against the body clock, in a noisy cabin in heavy weather, at the end of a long tour, does not restore a person the way an unbroken night would - and sleep debt accumulates across consecutive days, which is precisely why the circular defines it. That is why the guidance is written in six modules addressed to six different parties: manning and scheduling are the Company's, ship design is the builder's and the owner's, and inspection is the Administration's. Fatigue is not something the watchkeeper can fix on his own.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting limb B entirely, or giving it two lines. It is a named instrument and the easiest full marks in the question.
  • Treating limb a) as "list the conventions". Give the division of labour first.
  • Ignoring the words "such as maritime labour and welfare" and answering only on STCW.
  • Stating the 2022 MLC amendments as being in force. They are adopted and not yet in force at this sitting.
  • Saying Part B is mandatory. Part A Standards are mandatory; Part B Guidelines require due consideration.
  • Getting the rest numbers wrong, or quoting the work-hours formula and the rest-hours formula as though both applied at once - they are alternatives.
  • Saying MSC.1/Circ.1598 is mandatory. It is recommendatory; the mandatory layer is STCW chapter VIII and the MLC.
  • Forgetting Article V.7, no more favourable treatment, which is what makes the MLC effective against non-parties.
  • Omitting safe manning - fatigue that comes from too few people cannot be fixed by a rest table.

Examiner traps

"Your rest records show full compliance every week for six months. Are you satisfied?" No - I would be suspicious. Real operations are untidy, and a record that never breaches usually means it is being written to the rule rather than to the day. I would look at port turnarounds, drills, surveys and unplanned work, and at whether the recorded rest was actually taken.

"Is the ILO fatigue circular binding?" There is no ILO fatigue circular - the guidance is IMO's, MSC.1/Circ.1598, and it is recommendatory. The binding layer is STCW chapter VIII and MLC Title 2.

"Must you provide internet access to the crew?" At this sitting, no - it is a Guideline, to be given due consideration, and the amendment strengthening the social-connectivity requirement is adopted but not yet in force.

"A seafarer complains and the master does nothing. What is his remedy?" The on-board complaint procedure, and then the onshore complaint-handling procedure under Regulation 5.2.2, through a port State control officer in any ratifying State's port.

Uncertainty and currency

One evidence issue dominates this question and it was handled by exclusion rather than by avoidance. The corpus copy of the MLC is LATER than this sitting: it is the ILO consolidated text which says on its own title page that it includes the amendments of 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2022. It was read for the architecture and for the Titles, which the 2022 layer does not disturb, and the 2022 additions were then identified provision by provision and excluded - social connectivity in the recreational facilities of Standard A3.1, appropriately-sized personal protective equipment in Standard A4.3, repatriation of the body or ashes under Standard A4.1, and the internet-access Guidelines. Each is recorded in this answer as adopted and not yet in force, which is what it was at this examination. MSC.1/Circ.1598 is not held by the corpus at all; its module structure, its definition of sleep debt and its supersession of MSC/Circ.1014 are carried through MIW internal records of two answers that trace it to a recorded full-text reading, and are labelled as such. Three smaller restraints are deliberate: no figure is given for the number of ILO instruments the MLC consolidated, because commonly quoted figures vary between sources; no party or ratification count is given for either the MLC or STCW, both being status facts that date; and no Indian implementing instrument is cited, since none is held and the printed question sets no Indian limb.

Understand this first

For most of its life the international safety convention did not say how strong a ship had to be. It said a great deal about lifeboats, fire doors and watertight compartments, but nothing about the thickness of a deck plate or the sizing of a shaft. That work had always been done by private organisations which grew out of the insurance market: they wrote their own rulebooks, inspected ships against them, and told underwriters whether a vessel was fit to insure. The odd result was that the most important technical requirements a ship had to meet were, strictly speaking, voluntary - a matter of contract between an owner and a private body. What changed is that the convention began to point at those private rulebooks and say, in effect, comply with these. That single move turned a commercial arrangement into a legal obligation, so a ship that quietly lost her standing with her society was no longer merely uninsurable but non-compliant, and could be stopped in port. The second half of the question is about the rhythm of checking. A certificate lasting five years is only as good as what happens in between, so there is a ladder of inspections of increasing depth - and the interesting detail is that the ladder is not uniform, because different things go wrong at different speeds and with different visibility.

Model written answer

1. What a classification society is, and its two distinct roles

Open by separating the two hats, because almost every confusion in this subject comes from merging them.

  • The classification role is private and contractual. The society publishes its own rules, and it assigns and maintains class to a ship built and kept in accordance with them. Its contract is with the owner. Nothing in that role is delegated by anybody.
  • The statutory role is delegated. Acting as a recognized organization under SOLAS regulation XI-1/1 and the RO Code, the society surveys and certifies on behalf of a flag Administration - the load line, the safety construction and equipment certificates, and so on.
  • The distinction that earns the mark: the Administration transfers the work, not the responsibility. It remains answerable for the ship's compliance, and the RO Code exists to govern how it authorises, oversees and monitors the organisation acting for it.

2. How class rules are made

The stem asks specifically about rule formation, which most answers skip. Class rules are not written from theory alone; they are an empirical body of knowledge fed by the fleet the society surveys.

  • Service experience and defect returns. The society surveys thousands of ships and records what cracks, wastes, buckles and fails. That feedback is the largest single input, and it is why a society with a large surveyed fleet has better rules.
  • Casualty investigation. Structural losses drive rule change directly - which is how bulk carrier hold and hatch requirements, and side-shell and fore-end strengthening, came about.
  • Research and direct calculation. Finite element analysis, fatigue assessment, model testing and full-scale measurement, increasingly replacing empirical formulae with direct strength assessment.
  • Technical committees of members, owners, builders, underwriters and Administrations, which subject a draft rule to the people who will have to build and live with it.
  • Harmonisation between societies - the unified requirements, unified interpretations and procedural requirements of the international association of classification societies, and the Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers, which removed the incentive to compete by lowering scantlings.

3. What the rules actually cover

Take the three headings the examiner printed, in his order:

4. Implementation through the ship's life

The stem names three phases, so answer them as three phases.

  • Design. Plan approval against the rules, direct strength and fatigue calculation where required, and type approval of equipment before it is ever ordered.
  • Construction. Material and component certification, welder and welding procedure qualification, non-destructive examination, survey during fabrication and erection, and tank, pressure and sea trials. The class certificate is issued on completion.
  • Maintenance - the whole of the ship's service life. The periodical survey cycle: annual, intermediate, renewal or special survey on a five-year cycle, together with docking or bottom surveys, tailshaft and boiler surveys. For bulk carriers and oil tankers the enhanced survey programme under the ESP Code adds close-up survey and thickness measurement.
  • The mechanisms that keep it honest. Continuous survey and approved planned maintenance schemes spread the work; a condition of class or recommendation records a defect that must be rectified by a date; and class may be suspended or withdrawn. That last point leads directly into limb a)'s closing question.

5. Why compliance is NOW mentioned in SOLAS chapter II-1

The word to explain is "now". For most of its history SOLAS contained no scantling rules at all. It required subdivision, stability, fire protection and life-saving appliances, but it never told a builder how thick a plate should be. That work was done by class rules, which meant class was technically indispensable but legally optional - a ship could in principle comply with SOLAS while her classification had lapsed.

Regulation II-1/3-1 closed that gap. Its words are worth reproducing, because the whole answer to this half of the limb is in them:

In addition to the requirements contained elsewhere in the present regulations, ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society which is recognized by the Administration in accordance with the provisions of regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration which provide an equivalent level of safety.

Five things follow from that sentence, and each of them is a mark:

  • It makes class rules a SOLAS obligation. Compliance with a private rulebook becomes compliance with the Convention - so loss or suspension of class becomes a SOLAS non-compliance, inspectable and detainable by port State control. That, in one sentence, is why the reference is there.
  • Note the three verbs - designed, constructed AND MAINTAINED. The obligation is continuing. It is not discharged at delivery, which is exactly the point an examiner is looking for.
  • Note the alternative - applicable national standards providing an equivalent level of safety. The regulation does not compel classification as such; it compels compliance with a recognized society's rules or with an equivalent national standard, which preserves the Administration's ultimate authority.
  • Note the link to XI-1/1, which is what makes "recognized" mean something, and behind it the RO Code and the Administration's duty of oversight.
  • And note where the logic ended up. Regulation II-1/3-10, goal-based ship construction standards, goes one step further for bulk carriers and oil tankers: the society's own rules are themselves verified against IMO goals and functional requirements. SOLAS first made the rules mandatory, then began auditing the rules.

6. The harmonized system, and where these surveys sit

  • The harmonized system of survey and certification was introduced by the 1988 SOLAS Protocol and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol, and extended to the MARPOL Annexes. Its object is simple: before it, each certificate ran on its own cycle with its own dates, so a ship was surveyed almost continuously and never conveniently.
  • What harmonisation actually did: a common five-year certificate cycle; a common anniversary date for all certificates; a three-month window before or after each anniversary in which a survey may be held without moving the anniversary; the ability to combine surveys in one attendance; and a common set of survey types.
  • The IMO Survey Guidelines under the HSSC give the detailed items for each survey. They are recommendatory, revised by the Assembly roughly every two years, and the edition in circulation at this sitting is the 32nd Assembly edition. The survey types themselves, however, are created by SOLAS chapter I - not by the guidelines - which is why the answer below is taken from the Convention.

This is where marks are won or lost, because the two words are widely used loosely and SOLAS uses them precisely. Both are intermediate checkpoints between renewal surveys, but they attach to different certificates and run on different intervals.

On the construction certificate the mid-cycle deepening is called an intermediate survey, not a periodical one - within three months before or after the second or third anniversary, again taking the place of one of the annual surveys. 8. Annual versus periodical - the relevance

The stem asks for relevance as well as difference, so give the reasoning rather than only the schedule.

  • A five-year certificate needs mid-cycle checkpoints to remain credible. Without them the certificate would assert a condition established up to five years earlier. The graded ladder - annual, then periodical or intermediate, then renewal - keeps a live statement of condition behind the paper.
  • Survey depth is matched to the rate at which things deteriorate. Structure wastes slowly and predictably, so it can be checked generally each year and thoroughly at the mid-point and at renewal. Equipment is consumed rather than worn - liferafts, extinguishers, immersion suits, pyrotechnics all have service lives and expiry dates - so it needs a fuller look sooner.
  • Radio is treated most strictly of all, and for a good reason. It gets a periodical survey at every anniversary rather than a general annual look, because distress communication can degrade invisibly - a battery, an EPIRB or an antenna may look perfect and be useless, and the failure is only discovered at the one moment it cannot be tolerated.
  • Harmonisation is what makes the ladder practical. Common anniversary dates and a common three-month window let the annual, the periodical and the intermediate be taken in a single attendance, so the ship is surveyed thoroughly without being taken out of service repeatedly. That is the direct answer to the words "toward harmonized survey and certification system".
  • And the whole scheme rests on regulation 11. The condition of the ship is to be maintained between surveys, no change is to be made after survey without the Administration's sanction, and defects are to be reported at the earliest opportunity. The surveys verify maintenance; they are not a substitute for it - which is the same continuing obligation that II-1/3-1 imposes in limb a), and the neatest way to tie the two limbs of this question together.

Examiner traps

"Is a ship that has lost class still SOLAS-compliant?" No. Regulation II-1/3-1 requires her to be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with a recognized society's rules, or an equivalent national standard - so loss of class is a SOLAS non-compliance and is inspectable and detainable by port State control.

"When is the periodical survey of the safety equipment certificate due?" Within three months before or after the second or third anniversary date, and it takes the place of one of the annual surveys.

"And of the radio certificate?" Within three months before or after each anniversary - there is no separate annual radio survey.

"Your annual survey window is closing and the ship is at sea. What happens?" The survey must be completed within the three-month window either side of the anniversary; missing it invalidates the endorsement. The window does not extend and the anniversary does not move.

"If class rules are private, how can SOLAS require them?" Because II-1/3-1 requires compliance with the rules of a society recognized by the Administration under XI-1/1, or with equivalent national standards - so the Administration retains authority over whose rules count.
