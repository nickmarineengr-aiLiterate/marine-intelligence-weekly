Understand this first

A wreck is not primarily an environmental problem in this Convention - it is an unallocated bill. Before 2015 a ship could sink outside a coastal State's territorial sea, in water that State polices but does not own, and there was no clear international power to compel the owner to move it and no certainty that anybody solvent would pay. UNCLOS gave the coastal State rights in the exclusive economic zone but no removal machinery. The Nairobi Convention supplies the machinery, and the whole of it turns on one word: HAZARD. A wreck that endangers nothing is left alone. Once it is a hazard, four things follow mechanically - report it, locate it, mark it, remove it - and the registered owner pays, whether or not anyone was at fault, backed by insurance the State can sue directly. Read the question that way and limb (c) writes itself: the owner carries the cost and the liability, the coastal State carries the judgement and the power to act, and the environment is the reason both exist.

Model written answer

1. The Convention and where it applies

The instrument is the Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks, 2007, adopted on 18 May 2007 and in force since 14 April 2015. It operates in the Convention area - the exclusive economic zone of a State Party, or an equivalent zone not extending beyond 200 nautical miles (Article 1(1)). A State Party may extend it to its own territorial sea by declaration under Article 3(2). Domestically at the date of this examination the governing Indian statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

2. What "wreck" means - the four categories

Article 1(4) defines a wreck as something arising following upon a maritime casualty. Four categories are identified:

  • a sunken or stranded ship;
  • any part of a sunken or stranded ship, including any object that is or has been on board;
  • any object lost at sea from a ship that is stranded, sunken or adrift at sea;
  • a ship that is about, or may reasonably be expected, to sink or to strand, where effective measures to assist the ship or property in danger are not already being taken.

Two points are commonly lost. First, the phrase "following upon a maritime casualty" is part of the definition, not scene-setting: a ship deliberately abandoned, or an object thrown overboard in the ordinary course, is not a wreck under this Convention. Second, the fourth category is anticipatory - a ship that has not yet sunk is already a wreck for these purposes if she may reasonably be expected to sink or strand and effective assistance is not already under way. That is what allows a coastal State to act before the casualty completes itself, which is usually the only cheap moment to act at all.

3. Hazard - the switch that turns the Convention on

A wreck engages the removal machinery only if it is a hazard - Article 1(5): a condition or threat that poses a danger or impediment to navigation, or that may reasonably be expected to result in major harmful consequences to the marine environment or damage to coastal and related interests. Article 6 gives the criteria the affected State applies:

  • the size, type and construction of the wreck, and the depth of water over it;
  • proximity to shipping routes, port approaches and anchorages, and the density and frequency of traffic;
  • the type and quantity of cargo, and the quantity and type of oil - bunkers included - on board;
  • the tidal range, currents and prevailing weather, and the vulnerability of port facilities;
  • the proximity of offshore installations, pipelines and telecommunication cables, and of areas of particular environmental sensitivity;
  • whether the wreck is likely to shift or deteriorate materially, and any damage already suffered.

The determination is therefore a reasoned assessment, not a reflex. A wreck lying deep, clear of routes, with negligible bunkers and stable structure may properly be left in place - and the Convention imposes no removal duty on its owner at all.

4. Objectives and key provisions

The Convention was written to close a gap. UNCLOS gave coastal States rights in the exclusive economic zone but no removal machinery, so a hazardous wreck beyond the territorial sea could sit there with nobody clearly obliged to move it and no guarantee that anyone solvent would pay. States were left choosing between an unfunded removal and a permanent danger. The objectives are therefore:

  • a uniform international rule for the prompt, effective and proportionate removal of hazardous wrecks beyond the territorial sea;
  • cost recovery from the owner and the owner's insurer rather than from the coastal State's public funds - the polluter-pays principle applied to obstruction as well as to pollution;
  • legal certainty: owners and insurers know the extent of the exposure, States know the powers they hold, and both know the time limits;
  • financial security that is real - compulsory insurance with a right of direct action, so the remedy does not evaporate with a single-ship company.

The operative provisions then run as a sequence, and it is worth learning them in that order because it is the order in which a real casualty unfolds:

ArticleStepWho acts

Behind that sequence stand the financial provisions - strict liability (Art 10), compulsory insurance with direct action (Art 12) and the time bar (Art 13). Article 9 also preserves the owner's right to contract with any salvor or contractor of his choice, subject to the State's conditions, which keeps commercial salvage inside the regime rather than displacing it.

5. The shipowner - strict liability and its limits

Article 10 makes the registered owner strictly liable for the costs of locating, marking and removing the wreck. Fault need not be shown. The exceptions are narrow: act of war or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; damage wholly caused by a third party acting with intent; or wholly caused by the negligence of an authority responsible for navigational aids. Article 11 stands the Convention down where CLC 1992, the HNS, Bunkers or nuclear regimes govern the same damage.

Liability is backed by Article 12: ships of 300 GT and above must maintain insurance or other financial security, carry a certificate to that effect, and the claim may be brought directly against the insurer. The security must be at least equal to the limits of liability under the applicable limitation regime, and an insurer sued directly may invoke the defences the owner could have invoked - but not the owner's insolvency or winding up. The practical consequence is that the coastal State need not find the owner, prove negligence, or hope a single-ship company still exists: it establishes the hazard, sets the deadline and, failing compliance, removes and claims against a certified insurer whose identity it already holds. Article 10(2) preserves the owner's right to limit liability under the applicable limitation regime, and Article 13 bars claims brought more than three years after the hazard was determined, and in no case later than six years after the casualty.

6. The coastal State - judgement, notice and intervention

The affected State decides whether the wreck is a hazard on the Article 6 criteria; informs the flag State and the registered owner; warns mariners; sets a reasonable deadline for removal and may attach conditions; and, if the owner fails to act - or immediately, where the hazard is severe - may remove the wreck itself and recover the cost. Those powers are not unlimited: measures must be proportionate to the hazard and exercised consistently with international law, and the State may not interfere with the rights of other States beyond what removal requires. Where the wreck lies in the territorial sea of a State that has not opted in under Article 3(2), the Convention does not govern and the State acts under its own domestic law - in India, the wreck and salvage provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, administered through the Receiver of Wreck.

The division is therefore clean and should be written that way: the State supplies the judgement and the compulsion; the owner supplies the work and the money. The State does not become the remover of first resort, and the owner does not decide whether the wreck is dangerous.

7. Environmental implications, and what the regime does not reach

A wreck is a continuing environmental source rather than a single event, and that is what distinguishes it from a spill:

  • Progressive release. Bunkers, lubricating and hydraulic oils and cargo escape as tanks corrode, sometimes years after the casualty and long after the response has stood down.
  • Cargo hazards. Chemicals, packaged dangerous goods and containers that break free and drift, each becoming an object within category three of the definition.
  • Structural contaminants. Antifouling coatings, refrigerants, batteries and other hazardous materials built into the ship.
  • Physical damage. Smothering and scouring of the seabed, reefs and fishing grounds; obstruction of ports, approaches and fairways; risk to fishing gear and to cables and pipelines.
  • Navigational risk feeding back into environmental risk. An unmarked wreck causes the next casualty, which is why marking under Article 8 sits inside an environmental instrument at all.

The Convention's contribution to all of this is essentially financial and procedural: strict liability plus compulsory insurance is what converts an environmental risk into a funded removal, promptly and without first litigating fault. It changes who pays and how quickly, rather than changing the physics of the casualty.

Its reach is bounded. It applies only to wrecks that are hazards, only within the Convention area unless a State has opted in for its territorial sea, and the owner's liability remains subject to the applicable limitation regime - so an exceptionally costly removal can still exceed the available fund and leave a residue for the coastal State to carry.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The limbs carry 5, 5 and 6 marks and the six-mark limb names two actors. Splitting sections 5 and 6 by actor is therefore not a stylistic choice - it is what makes both halves of the largest limb visible to the examiner. Sections 1 to 3 build the definition in the order the Convention itself uses: instrument, then definition, then trigger. Answering "define wreck" without "hazard" produces a technically correct definition that explains nothing about how the Convention works.

The two-part definition, and why it is two parts

Article 1(4) tells you what a wreck is. Article 1(5) tells you when anyone has to do anything about it. Most of the Convention - reporting, locating, marking, removal, liability - hangs off the hazard determination in Article 6, not off the definition. A wreck in deep water clear of routes, with no cargo of consequence, may be left where it is.

Strict liability, and the word that must be used carefully

Strict liability means liability without proof of fault - it does not mean absolute liability, because Article 10 admits three defences. Note the contrast with the Bunkers Convention, where the liable "shipowner" is defined widely to include the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator jointly and severally, and only the insurance duty falls on the registered owner alone. Under Nairobi and under CLC the registered owner is the correct focus; under Bunkers it is not, and that distinction has been examined.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting the fourth category - a ship about to sink or strand - and so defining only what is already on the bottom.
  • Saying the Convention applies in territorial waters. It applies in the EEZ; the territorial sea is an opt-in under Article 3(2).
  • Treating every wreck as removable. Without a hazard there is no removal obligation.
  • Writing limb (c) as one paragraph about pollution, leaving the coastal State's responsibilities unstated.
  • Confusing the Nairobi regime with salvage. Salvage is a voluntary service for reward; wreck removal is a compulsory duty imposed on the owner.

Examiner traps

  • Asking which conventions displace Nairobi - CLC 1992, HNS, Bunkers and the nuclear conventions, under Article 11.
  • Asking whether liability is unlimited. It is not: Article 10(2) expressly preserves limitation under the applicable regime.
  • Asking for the time limits and expecting only one. There are two - three years and six years, measured from different events.
  • Asking who reports the wreck. The master or operator of the ship, to the affected State - not the coastal State to the owner.

Understand this first

Limitation of liability is the oldest bargain in shipping law and it is not a loophole. A single ship can destroy a berth, sink another vessel, kill people and block a fairway in one afternoon; the exposure is unbounded, and no insurer can price an unbounded risk. If liability were unlimited, either the cover would not exist or its cost would sit on every tonne of cargo carried. So the international community fixed a ceiling and made it predictable: a claimant gets less than full compensation, but gets it from a fund that certainly exists. The 1976 Convention sharpened the bargain deliberately - the older test let a claimant break limitation by proving ordinary "actual fault or privity", which produced litigation in every casualty. LLMC 1976 made the right almost unbreakable in exchange for higher limits, and the 1996 Protocol raised those limits again. Read every part of this question through that trade and the structure follows: what the ceiling covers (Art 2), what it deliberately does not cover because another regime pays or because the claim would destroy the bargain (Art 3), when the right is forfeited (Art 4), and how much money the ceiling actually is (Arts 6, 7 and 11).

Model written answer

1. The instrument and its state at this sitting

The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 was adopted on 19 November 1976 and entered into force on 1 December 1986. The Protocol of 1996, in force 13 May 2004, raised the limits substantially and introduced a tacit acceptance procedure for raising them again. Under that procedure the limits were raised by the 2012 amendments, which entered into force on 8 June 2015. Those are the limits applicable at the date of this examination. India is a party to the Convention as amended, and it is given domestic effect through the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

2. Objectives - why limitation exists at all

A single maritime occurrence can generate loss out of all proportion to the value of the ship - a berth destroyed, a fairway blocked, another vessel sunk, lives lost. An unbounded exposure is an uninsurable one, and an uninsured claim is worth nothing. The Convention therefore exists to:

  • make catastrophic maritime exposure insurable, so that the money is actually there;
  • give claimants a certain fund in place of an uncertain full recovery against a possibly worthless defendant;
  • secure uniformity, so that the ceiling does not depend on which court is seised;
  • keep shipowners trading and salvors willing to intervene, by capping the downside of both;
  • strike a deliberate balance: limits materially higher than those of the 1957 regime, granted in exchange for a right to limit that is almost impossible to break.

That last point is the heart of the 1976 Convention. Under the earlier regime a claimant could break limitation by showing the owner's "actual fault or privity", which invited litigation in every serious casualty. LLMC 1976 replaced the test with a far harder one, and paid for the change with higher limits.

3. The governing principles

  • Who may limit (Art 1). Shipowners - defined to include the owner, charterer, manager and operator - together with salvors, any person for whose act the owner or salvor is responsible, and the liability insurer, who may limit to the same extent as the assured.
  • The measure is tonnage. The limits are a function of the ship's gross tonnage, calculated under Annex I to the Tonnage Convention 1969 - not deadweight and not displacement.
  • Global, per occurrence. The cap applies to the aggregate of all claims arising on any distinct occasion (Art 9), not claim by claim.
  • Separate funds. Loss of life and personal injury has its own, higher, fund; all other claims share a lower one; passenger claims are dealt with separately again under Article 7.
  • The right is nearly unbreakable (Art 4). See section 6.
  • A fund need not be constituted (Art 10). Limitation may be invoked as a defence without depositing money, although national law may require constitution.

4. Claims subject to limitation - Article 2

Article 2(1) makes the following limitable whatever the basis of liability - contract, tort or statute:

  • claims for loss of life or personal injury, or loss of or damage to property - expressly including damage to harbour works, basins, waterways and aids to navigation - occurring on board or in direct connexion with the operation of the ship or with salvage operations, and consequential loss;
  • claims for loss resulting from delay in the carriage by sea of cargo, passengers or their luggage;
  • claims for loss resulting from infringement of rights other than contractual rights, occurring in direct connexion with the operation of the ship or salvage operations;
  • claims for the raising, removal, destruction or rendering harmless of a ship which is sunk, wrecked, stranded or abandoned, including anything that is or has been on board;
  • claims for the removal, destruction or rendering harmless of the cargo;
  • claims by a person other than the person liable for measures taken to avert or minimise loss for which that person could limit, and further loss caused by those measures.

5. Claims excluded from limitation - Article 3

Article 3 takes five categories outside the Convention altogether:

Salvage - including special compensation under Article 14 of the Salvage Convention 1989 - and contribution in general averageBoth exist to reward voluntary intervention that benefits everyone. Capping them would discourage the very act the law is trying to buy

One further qualification is easily missed. Article 18(1) permits a State Party to reserve the right to exclude the wreck-removal and cargo-removal heads at Article 2(1)(d) and (e). Where that reservation has been made those claims are not limitable in that State, which is why wreck removal liability can be capped in one jurisdiction and uncapped in another.

6. Conduct barring limitation - the reason the list matters

Article 4 provides that a person liable is not entitled to limit if the loss resulted from his personal act or omission, committed either with intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result. Three features make this near-unbreakable: the conduct must be that of the person seeking to limit and not of the master or crew; recklessness alone is not enough without knowledge of the probable consequence; and the burden of proving it lies on the claimant. Negligent navigation, poor maintenance or a badly run ship will not by themselves defeat limitation.

7. Determining and calculating the fund

Constituting the fund (Art 11). A person alleged to be liable may constitute a fund with the court or other competent authority in any State Party where legal proceedings are instituted, either by depositing the sum or by producing a guarantee acceptable to that authority. The fund must equal the applicable limits for all claims arising from the occurrence, together with interest from the date of the occurrence to the date of constitution. A fund constituted by one person is deemed constituted by all persons entitled to limit for the same occurrence.

Consequences (Arts 12, 13, 14). The fund is distributed rateably among the established claims. Once it is constituted, a claimant may not pursue the person's other assets, and any ship or property arrested must be released. Constitution and distribution are governed by the law of the State Party in which the fund is constituted.

Calculating the amount (Arts 6 and 7, as amended by the 1996 Protocol). Two separate limits are computed on the ship's gross tonnage in successive bands, and the unit of account is the Special Drawing Right, converted as at the date the fund is constituted:

Where the personal-claims fund is insufficient, the unpaid balance of those claims ranks rateably with the property claims against the property fund - so life and injury claims are preferred, but not to the exclusion of everything else. Passenger claims are calculated differently again under Article 7: a fixed sum of 175,000 SDR multiplied by the number of passengers the ship is certified to carry, the 1996 Protocol having removed the overall monetary cap that the 1976 text imposed.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (b) carries 6 of the 16 marks and asks two questions in one sentence - which claims are subject to limitation, and which are excluded. Splitting it into two separately headed sections is the single highest-value structural decision in this answer, because an examiner marking a list can only credit what is visible as a list. Article 4 is then given its own short section: it belongs with the exceptions in logic - it is the other way limitation fails - but it is not part of the Article 3 enumeration and merging them muddles both.

The bargain, in one line

Every part of LLMC follows from one trade: higher limits in exchange for a right that is almost impossible to break. If you can state that, you can derive the objectives, explain why Article 4 is worded so severely, and explain why the 1996 Protocol needed a tacit acceptance mechanism - a bargain built on fixed sums decays with inflation unless the sums can be raised without a new conference.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (b) with only the Article 2 list and never reaching Article 3.
  • Saying oil pollution is limitable under LLMC. Pollution damage within the meaning of the CLC is excluded - but a bunker spill from a ship outside CLC is not excluded by Article 3.
  • Saying the right is lost by negligence, or by the master's recklessness. Article 4 requires the personal act or omission of the person seeking to limit.
  • Quoting one tonnage figure and forgetting that there are two funds plus a separate passenger limit.
  • Confusing constitution of the fund (Art 11) with the right to limit (Art 10). The right exists without a fund; the fund is what bars other actions.
  • Calling the unit of account "dollars". It is the SDR, converted at the date the fund is constituted.

Examiner traps

  • Asking whether the insurer may limit. Yes - Article 1(6), to the same extent as the assured.
  • Asking whether a salvor may limit when not operating from a ship. Yes - the 1976 Convention was drafted to cover salvors after The Tojo Maru, and salvage operations are expressly within Article 2.
  • Asking whether wreck removal is limitable. It is in the Article 2 list, but Article 18(1) lets a State reserve it out - so the honest answer names the reservation.
  • Asking what happens if the personal-injury fund runs out. The balance ranks rateably against the property fund; it is not simply lost.
  • Asking which convention pays the salvor in full. Salvage and general average are excluded by Article 3 precisely so that they are not cut down.

Understand this first

The two acronyms differ by one letter and by everything else. CAS is law and CAP is commerce. CAS - the Condition Assessment Scheme - was written after the Erika and hardened after the Prestige, and it exists for one purpose: to let a State satisfy itself that an ageing single-hull oil tanker that MARPOL would otherwise have retired is still structurally fit to carry oil. It is compulsory, it is administered by the flag Administration rather than by class alone, it looks only at structure, and it produces a pass: a Statement of Compliance, or nothing. CAP - the Condition Assessment Programme - is a voluntary classification-society product that an owner buys because charterers, oil majors, banks and buyers want independent evidence that a fifteen-year-old ship is better than her age suggests. It covers structure and machinery and cargo systems, and it produces a grade, not a pass. Hold that contrast - compulsory / voluntary, State / class, structure / whole ship, pass / grade - and all four limbs fall out of it.

Model written answer

1. Two schemes that are not two versions of one thing

The names are one letter apart and the schemes are opposites. The Condition Assessment Scheme (CAS) is mandatory international law, created by IMO, compelled by MARPOL Annex I and administered by the flag Administration. The Condition Assessment Programme (CAP) is a voluntary commercial product sold by classification societies and bought by owners because the market asks for it. CAS answers the question may this ship continue to trade?; CAP answers how good is this ship? Neither replaces the statutory survey regime or the enhanced survey programme, and neither is a survey in the SOLAS sense - each is a scheme built around one.

2. CAS - purpose and scope

Purpose. CAS was developed after the Erika and tightened after the Prestige, and adopted by resolution MEPC.94(46), as subsequently amended. Its purpose is to provide the Administration with an independent, prescribed and auditable verification that the structural condition of an ageing single-hull oil tanker is adequate - so that a ship which MARPOL would otherwise have retired may continue to carry oil only on measured evidence rather than on the strength of a clean class record.

Applicability. It is made compulsory by MARPOL Annex I regulation 20 for the single-hull categories permitted to continue trading beyond their phase-out date, and by regulation 21 in connection with the carriage of heavy grade oil. Ships come into scope on age and category, not by choice.

Scope. Deliberately narrow. CAS looks at the hull: extensive close-up survey, thickness measurement against prescribed acceptance criteria, the condition of coatings and corrosion-prevention arrangements, and the resulting structural strength. It is carried out in conjunction with the enhanced survey programme intermediate or renewal survey and is additional to it. The outcome is binary: the Administration reviews the Final Report and issues - or refuses - a Statement of Compliance.

3. CAP - purpose and scope

Purpose. CAP exists because age alone is a poor proxy for condition, and the market needs a better one. Charterers, oil majors operating vetting regimes, banks financing tonnage and buyers in the second-hand market all want independent evidence that a fifteen-year-old ship is materially better than her age implies. CAP supplies it as a graded, documented assessment.

Scope. Far wider than CAS. A full CAP typically assesses hull structure, machinery and cargo systems, and often electrical and cargo-handling equipment, each rated separately. The assessment goes well beyond class minimum: thickness measurement at a density greater than survey requires, strength and fatigue evaluation of the structure, performance testing and functional trials of machinery, and examination of maintenance records. The outcome is a graded rating - commonly a four-point scale on which the best grade denotes condition very much better than the classification minimum. It carries no statutory effect whatever.

4. Difference one - what triggers each

CAS is triggered by law and by the ship's objective characteristics: her single-hull category, her age relative to the regulation 20 phase-out, and whether she is engaged in the carriage of heavy grade oil under regulation 21. It cannot be declined, deferred by agreement, or narrowed to save cost. CAP is triggered by a commercial decision - a charterer's requirement, an oil major's vetting regime, a lender's condition, an intended sale, or simply an owner wishing to evidence quality. It need never be taken at all.

Ship safety. Both schemes replace an inference from age with a measurement of condition. Wastage, fatigue cracking, coating breakdown and locally deficient structure are found by close-up survey and gauging early enough to be repaired rather than discovered by failure. The prescribed, plan-driven scope of CAS in particular removes the discretion that lets a difficult tank go unexamined for another cycle.

Environmental protection. This is the reason CAS exists at all. The single-hull tanker casualties of 1999 and 2002 were structural failures of ageing ships, and the regulatory response was to make continued trading conditional on demonstrated structural fitness. CAP contributes the same effect through the market: a charterer that will not fix an unrated ship of a certain age imposes a condition standard without legislating one.

Operational efficiency. The measured data has commercial value beyond compliance. It supports vetting outcomes and charter access; it converts dry-dock and steel-renewal planning from guesswork into a budgeted programme; it provides credible residual-life and valuation evidence for sale, purchase and finance, and useful information for underwriters; and by finding defects on a planned basis it reduces unplanned repair and off-hire. Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four printed limbs need four visible regions, and the temptation on a question like this is to write one long essay about old tankers. The single most valuable structural decision is section 1: stating the law versus commerce contrast before either scheme is described. It costs three sentences and it makes every subsequent difference in limb (b) read as a consequence rather than as a list of unrelated facts. Limb (b) names three axes - triggers, survey requirements, reporting procedures - so the answer uses those three words rather than inventing its own comparison headings.

Where each scheme sits in the survey architecture

A useful mental stack, from the bottom up: classification survey keeps the ship in class; the enhanced survey programme (ESP Code, resolution A.1049(27), made mandatory through SOLAS XI-1/2) prescribes the close-up and gauging regime for tankers and bulk carriers; CAS sits on top of the ESP survey for a defined class of ageing single-hull tanker and adds a State-verified structural pass; and CAP sits outside the statutory stack altogether, buying a graded assessment for commercial purposes. Only the first three are compulsory, and only the middle two are surveys.

Why the outcomes differ - pass versus grade

The difference in outcome is not cosmetic. A regulator needs a binary answer, because a ship is either permitted to trade or not; a grading would leave the Administration deciding how much wastage is acceptable in each case, which is exactly the discretion the Scheme was written to remove. A charterer needs the opposite - a ranking, because it is choosing between ships that are all lawfully entitled to trade. Explaining that in one sentence usually earns more than another paragraph of description.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting CAP and CAS as two flavours of the same survey.
  • Saying CAS replaces the enhanced survey programme. It is carried out within it and is additional to it.
  • Giving CAP a statutory effect - a CAP rating is not a certificate and does not permit or prohibit anything.
  • Applying CAS to double-hull tankers or to bulk carriers. Its trigger is the single-hull category under regulation 20 and the heavy-grade-oil carriage under regulation 21.
  • Writing limb (c) as a paragraph on safety only. The stem names three benefit headings and expects all three.
  • Skipping limb (d) for time. It is the smallest limb but it is a plain list - the cheapest marks on the question.

Examiner traps

  • Asking who issues the Statement of Compliance. The Administration, after reviewing the Final Report - not the classification society on its own authority.
  • Asking what happens if CAS is failed. The ship may not continue in the trade that required it; there is no graded outcome to fall back on.
  • Asking whether CAP is recognised by IMO. It is not - it is a class and market instrument.
  • Asking what CAS actually measures. Structure. Machinery condition is a CAP question, not a CAS one.
  • Asking when the survey plan must be submitted, expecting a lead time rather than "before the survey".

Understand this first

Two things sit in this question that look separate and are not. Fouling is a fuel problem before it is an environmental one: a fouled hull can add tens of per cent to propulsive power for the same speed, and since MARPOL Annex VI made EEXI and CII operative that extra fuel is no longer just a cost - it moves the ship's annual rating and can force a corrective action plan. So the pressure to keep hulls clean has risen at exactly the moment the AFS Convention has narrowed what may be used to do it: organotins first, and now cybutryne. That squeeze - rising pressure to keep the hull clean, falling permission to use the chemistry that did it - is the background the question is built on. Cathodic and anodic protection are corrosion techniques, not anti-fouling ones, and only one of them is used on a ship's hull at all - but both share the same steel surface as the paint, are set up in the same dry dock, and a cathodic system that is over-protecting will destroy the coating it sits under. The comparison is therefore not a digression: it is the reason a compliant paint scheme still fails if the rest of the hull system is wrong.

Model written answer

1. The Convention and its controls at this sitting

The International Convention on the Control of Harmful Anti-fouling Systems on Ships, 2001 entered into force on 17 September 2008. It prohibits or restricts the anti-fouling systems listed in Annex 1, and provides through Annex 4 for survey, certification and control of ships. Annex 1 originally targeted organotin compounds acting as biocides - the tributyltin systems whose effects on non-target shellfish caused the Convention to be written.

The 2021 amendments added cybutryne to Annex 1, entering into force on 1 January 2023. From that date a cybutryne-containing system may not be applied or re-applied. A ship already carrying one complies either by removing it or by applying an overcoating sealer that forms a barrier preventing the substance leaching, and must do so at the next scheduled renewal of the anti-fouling system, subject to a backstop of 60 months from last application. At the date of this examination the control is therefore in force but still inside its transition window - many ships lawfully continue to carry cybutryne systems applied before 2023.

2. Why energy-efficiency regulation raised the stakes

Fouling is a propulsion problem first. Slime, weed and shell growth raise frictional resistance, so more power and more fuel are required to hold the same speed, and the effect grows steadily between dockings. Since MARPOL Annex VI regulation 23 (EEXI) and regulation 28 (CII) became operative, that extra fuel is no longer only a cost: the CII is computed from fuel consumed against distance and capacity, so a fouled hull worsens the annual rating directly, and a poor rating obliges the owner to produce a corrective action plan through the SEEMP. Hull and propeller condition has become a compliance variable.

The same surface is also the subject of the IMO biofouling guidelines - the 2011 Guidelines, revised at MEPC 80 in 2023 - which address the transfer of invasive aquatic species and remain recommendatory. So the owner is pushed to keep the hull clean by three separate forces, while the Convention narrows what may be used to do it.

The comparison is therefore asymmetric and should be stated as such: cathodic protection is the marine technique, and anodic protection is the one whose conditions a seawater hull cannot satisfy.

4. Why the comparison matters to a paint scheme

The coating is the primary barrier and the cathodic system protects only what the coating fails to cover; the two are designed and commissioned as one system. The critical interaction runs the other way as well. An over-protecting cathodic system generates hydroxyl ions and hydrogen at the steel surface, and the resulting alkalinity lifts the coating from the substrate - cathodic disbondment - typically first in the halo around anodes and ICCP anode positions. A newly applied, fully compliant anti-fouling scheme can therefore be destroyed from underneath by a system intended to protect it. Anode and ICCP anode locations, dielectric shields, boot-topping treatment and the protective potential setting all belong in the coating specification.

5. Applying a compliant scheme on an existing ship

On an existing ship the first question is not which paint to buy but what is already there.

  • Establish the existing system. Read the Record of Anti-Fouling Systems, previous dry-dock specifications and paint receipts, and where necessary test the coating. Decide between complete removal and the sealer coat route permitted for a controlled system.
  • Dock and wash. High-pressure fresh-water washing to remove fouling, slime and the spent leached layer. Washings and removed coating containing controlled substances are hazardous waste: they are contained, collected and landed to a reception facility, not allowed into the dock basin.
  • Prepare the surface. Assess the condition of the existing anticorrosive; abrasive blast or sweep to the specified preparation grade where removal is required. Check surface profile, soluble salt contamination and dust to the specified standards before coating.
  • Control the environment. Steel temperature at least a few degrees above the dew point, with relative humidity and air and steel temperature inside the product data sheet window, throughout application and cure.
  • Apply the specified system in order. Anticorrosive primer, tie coat where the system requires one, then the anti-fouling coats - at the specified wet and dry film thickness and within the minimum and maximum overcoating intervals. Exceeding the maximum interval destroys intercoat adhesion just as surely as under-thickness destroys the leaching rate.
  • Treat the difficult areas. Stripe-coat edges, welds, cut-outs and rough areas; attend to sea chests and gratings, boot-topping and flat bottom; polish the propeller; renew sacrificial anodes and verify ICCP anodes and shields.
  • Respect the end of the process. Observe the minimum drying time before undocking and the immersion window specified for the anti-fouling coat.

6. The additional supervision that makes it effective

A compliant product badly applied is neither compliant in practice nor effective, so the supervision is part of the specification:

  • Verify the product before it is landed. Obtain the manufacturer's written declaration that the system contains no organotin compound acting as a biocide and no cybutryne, together with product and safety data sheets.
  • Appoint a qualified coating inspector and agree written hold points - after washing, after blasting, before each coat, and at completion - at which work stops until the stage is accepted.
  • Evidence the preparation by recorded surface profile, soluble salt and dust testing.
  • Evidence each coat by wet film thickness during application and dry film thickness after cure, measured to the specified acceptance rule, with holiday detection and adhesion testing where the specification calls for them.
  • Reconcile what was actually applied - batch numbers and quantities against delivery notes and the theoretical consumption - so that the certificate rests on evidence rather than on assumption.
  • Record it. Dated photographs and written inspection reports for each stage, retained on board.
  • Close the loop. Arrange the AFS survey so that the certificate and the Record are re-issued or endorsed, and confirm compatibility between the new coating and the cathodic protection arrangement before the system is put back into service.

3. Risk criteria and ALARP - the yardstick

Risk is frequency multiplied by consequence, and FSA expresses it in two ways: individual risk, the risk to a particular person, and societal risk, the risk of multi-fatality events, displayed on an F-N curve giving the frequency of accidents causing N or more fatalities. Against these are set three regions: intolerable, where the activity is not permitted at that level; tolerable if As Low As Reasonably Practicable; and negligible. In the ALARP region a control is adopted unless its cost is grossly disproportionate to the benefit - which is precisely the judgement that step 4 makes measurable.

4. Cost effective indices

These convert a risk control option into a single comparable number - money per unit of risk removed - so that competing options and competing proposals can be ranked on the same scale.

  • GCAF - Gross Cost of Averting a Fatality. The cost of implementing and operating the option over the ship's life, divided by the reduction in expected fatalities it achieves.
  • NCAF - Net Cost of Averting a Fatality. The same calculation after subtracting the economic benefit the option itself produces - avoided damage, avoided cargo loss, avoided off-hire. An option that pays for part of itself has a lower NCAF than GCAF.
  • The criterion. An option is treated as cost-effective if the index falls below an agreed threshold, of the order of US$3 million per averted fatality, derived from societal indicators of what is spent to save a life and updated for inflation.
  • An environmental analogue - the cost of averting a tonne of oil spilled - has been proposed for pollution-driven assessments but has not been adopted by IMO as a criterion.

5. Human Reliability Analysis

HRA is the systematic study of the human contribution to risk: what people are required to do, how they can fail to do it, why, and what would make failure less likely. It is not a sixth step - it runs through the model, contributing human-related hazards in step 1, human error probabilities in step 2, and human-centred risk control options in step 3.

It has a qualitative half - define the context, analyse the task, identify the possible errors and their causes - and a quantitative half, in which human error probabilities are assigned and then modified for performance shaping factors: fatigue and hours of rest, training and experience, the quality of procedures, ergonomic design of the interface, workload and time pressure, communication, and organisational culture. Its purpose is error reduction, not attribution of blame; the output is design, procedural and training measures.

6. Extended Task Analysis

Conventional task analysis decomposes a job into tasks and sub-tasks and records what the operator must do, in what order, with what information. Extended Task Analysis adds the context of each step: the equipment and its interface, the procedures and their usability, the information actually available at that moment, the physical environment, the training assumed, and the organisational pressures acting on the operator.

That context is where the performance shaping factors live, so XTA is the input that turns human error identification from a generic list into a specific one - it shows where errors are likely and why before anything is quantified. It is diagnostic rather than numerical, and it feeds the quantification methods that follow.

7. Technique for Human Error Rate Prediction

THERP is the classical first-generation method for putting a number on the reliability of an operator task. Its procedure is:

  1. Break the task into sub-tasks, using the task analysis, and draw it as an HRA event tree in which each sub-task branches into success and failure.
  2. Assign each failure branch a nominal human error probability from generic data tables for that class of action.
  3. Modify the nominal values for the performance shaping factors applying to the real situation.
  4. Apply the dependence model between successive actions - zero, low, moderate, high or complete - because an operator who has just made one error is more likely to make the next.
  5. Credit genuine recovery factors: an alarm, an independent check, a second person.
  6. Combine the branch probabilities to give the probability of task failure, which feeds the step 2 risk model.

Its strength is that it is transparent and auditable; its weakness is that it depends on generic data and on the analyst's judgement of the shaping factors and dependence.

9. Failure Mode and Effect Analysis

FMEA is inductive and bottom-up. Each component or function is taken in turn and examined for the ways it can fail. For every failure mode the analysis records the cause, the local effect, the effect on the system, the means of detection, the existing safeguards and the action required. FMECA extends it by adding criticality - severity, likelihood of occurrence and difficulty of detection combined into a risk priority number - so that limited effort can be ranked.

Its limitation is structural and examinable: FMEA looks at single failure modes, so combinations of failures and common-cause failures are outside it and must be caught by fault tree analysis. IMO requires FMEA in specific places - notably for high-speed craft - and it commonly supports the alternative design and arrangements approval route, where a design departing from the prescriptive rule must be shown to be at least as safe.

Model written answer

1. Two sources of obligation

Every liability discussed in this question comes from one of two places. Either the defendant promised - and the law holds him to a duty he assumed voluntarily, on terms he helped choose - or the law imposed a duty on him regardless of any agreement. Contract is a private arrangement that the courts enforce; tort is a public standard that the courts apply. Across both runs a second and independent question, which is what limb (b) asks: how much must the claimant prove?

2. Contractual liability

Contractual liability arises from a valid agreement, governed in India by the Indian Contract Act, 1872. Its distinguishing features are:

  • The duty is defined by the parties, not by the court - they may enlarge it, limit it, exclude it or transfer it.
  • Privity - only a party to the contract may sue upon it. A person injured by a breach who is a stranger to the contract has no claim on it.
  • The measure of damages is expectation - the claimant is put in the position he would have occupied had the contract been performed.
  • Limitation and exception clauses operate, which is why package limitation, laytime and demurrage regimes, and indemnities exist at all.

Examples. The ocean carrier's liability to the cargo owner for loss or damage under a bill of lading, subject to the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925; a charterer's liability for demurrage under the charterparty; a repair yard's liability for defective work under the docking contract; a bunker supplier's liability for off-specification fuel under the supply contract.

3. Liability in tort

Tortious liability is imposed by law, owed to persons generally, and requires no contract between claimant and defendant. The principal tort in shipping is negligence, which the claimant establishes in five elements: a duty of care; breach of that duty, judged against the standard of a reasonable person in the defendant's position; causation; damage; and damage that is not too remote. Nuisance, trespass and conversion of goods also arise. The measure of damages is restitutionary - to restore the claimant to the position he was in before the wrong.

Examples. Collision damage to another ship, to a berth or to a navigational aid; injury to a stevedore working on board; oil escaping and fouling fishermen's gear and nets; damage to a submarine cable or pipeline by anchoring.

4. Where the two overlap, and how the industry manages it

The categories are not exclusive. One act can be both a breach of contract and a tort - negligent stowage that damages cargo is the standard illustration, being a breach of the contract of carriage owed to the cargo owner and a negligent act owed to whoever suffers the loss. Where both are available the claimant's choice is not academic: it can change the limitation period, the measure of damages, and above all who can be sued.

That last point is why privity has produced its own commercial machinery. The Himalaya clause extends the carrier's defences, exceptions and limits to servants, agents and independent contractors - notably stevedores - who would otherwise be sued directly in tort and be unprotected by a contract they are not party to. Indemnity clauses and knock-for-knock allocations achieve the same reallocation by agreement where the law would not.

5. Legal liability and strict liability

Legal liability is the general category: an obligation, enforceable by a court, to make compensation for loss - whatever its source. In its ordinary fault-based form the claimant must prove breach of a duty or of a contractual term, which in negligence means proving all five elements above.

Strict liability removes the fault element. The claimant proves the damage and the causal connection, and nothing more; the defendant escapes only on the narrow exceptions the statute itself allows. It is imposed where proving fault would be practically impossible or would defeat the purpose of the rule - a ship lost in a storm leaves no evidence of negligence, but the oil is still on the beach.

Three reasons, and they are worth stating explicitly. Evidence - a casualty at sea usually destroys the proof of how it happened, so a fault requirement would defeat most claims. Identification - the victim on the beach has no relationship with the ship and no means of investigating her. Funding - once liability is automatic it can be insured against on known terms, so compulsory insurance and direct action become possible, and the claimant gets money instead of a judgment. Understand this first

The whole subject exists because of one accounting mistake that was easy to make and expensive to keep. The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy adopted exactly that basis. Everything else in this question - the stages, the boundaries, the well-to-tank and tank-to-wake split, and the argument about feasibility - is machinery for producing that one number honestly.

Model written answer

1. The problem that makes life-cycle accounting necessary

Every mandatory greenhouse gas measure in force at the date of this examination - the EEXI, the CII and the fuel oil Data Collection System - counts emissions at the funnel. That is simple to administer and it produces a serious distortion. A ship burning ammonia or hydrogen emits no carbon dioxide, because there is no carbon in the molecule, whether the fuel was made from natural gas with the carbon vented or from renewable electricity. Measured at the funnel the two are identical.

The emissions have not gone; they have moved ashore, into a production plant that the shipowner does not own and the regulation does not see. That is carbon leakage, and a rule that permits it does not merely fail to reduce emissions - it directs investment towards the wrong technology. Funnel-only accounting also mis-ranks the real candidates: LNG looks better than it is because methane slip and upstream leakage are invisible, and a biofuel looks the same whatever its feedstock or the land-use change behind it.

2. What life cycle analysis of a marine fuel is

Life Cycle Analysis is the systematic, quantified assessment of the greenhouse gas emissions - and more broadly the environmental burdens - associated with a fuel across its entire life, from the resource in the ground or the field through to its conversion in the ship's engine. Its output is a single comparable figure: the fuel's GHG intensity, expressed in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per megajoule of energy delivered. It is equivalent because methane and nitrous oxide are converted to a carbon dioxide basis at their global warming potentials, which matters greatly for gas fuels.

The IMO basis at this sitting. The 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships, resolution MEPC.377(80) of 7 July 2023, requires its levels of ambition and indicative checkpoints to take account of well-to-wake emissions as addressed in the LCA guidelines. The current guidelines edition is the 2024 Guidelines on life cycle GHG intensity of marine fuels, resolution MEPC.391(81), which superseded the 2023 edition, resolution MEPC.376(80). Note carefully what these instruments are: a strategy and a set of guidelines. Neither is mandatory on a ship at this sitting.

3. The four methodological stages of an assessment

A life cycle assessment is conducted in four stages, which are iterative rather than strictly sequential:

StageWhat is done
1. Goal and scope definitionWhy the study is being done and for whom; the functional unit - here, one megajoule of energy delivered for propulsion; the system boundary, deciding what is inside the study and what is excluded; and the assumptions and allocation rules
2. Inventory analysisQuantify every input and output crossing the boundary at each stage - energy consumed, feedstock used, emissions released, co-products generated
3. Impact assessmentConvert the inventory into impact categories. For fuels the dominant category is global warming potential, with methane and nitrous oxide expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent over a stated time horizon
4. InterpretationTest the sensitivity of the result to the assumptions, state the uncertainty, check completeness and consistency, and draw conclusions that are honest about their limits

The iteration is real and examinable: an inventory result that is dominated by one excluded process sends the analyst straight back to the system boundary.

4. The physical stages of a marine fuel pathway

Alongside the method, the fuel chain itself has stages, and these are what the inventory is built on:

  • Feedstock - extraction, recovery or cultivation. Fugitive methane from gas production, and land-use change where a crop is grown for fuel, both enter here.
  • Processing and conversion - refining, liquefaction, synthesis or electrolysis. The electricity mix used is decisive for hydrogen and its derivatives, and any carbon capture is credited here.
  • Transport, storage and distribution - moving the fuel to the bunkering port, including boil-off and handling losses.
  • Bunkering - transfer to the ship.
  • Onboard conversion - combustion in the engine or conversion in a fuel cell, including methane slip, nitrous oxide, the pilot fuel in a dual-fuel engine, and any onboard carbon capture.

Cutting across all five are the accounting decisions that decide the answer as much as the physics does: allocation of burdens between a process's main product and its co-products, the treatment of biogenic carbon, and end-of-life.

5. Well-to-Tank, Tank-to-Wake and Well-to-Wake

TermCoversControlled by
Well-to-Tank (WtT)Feedstock, production and conversion, distribution and bunkering - ending when the fuel is in the ship's tankThe fuel producer and supplier. The shipowner buys it
Tank-to-Wake (TtW)Conversion onboard - combustion or fuel-cell operation, including methane slip, nitrous oxide and pilot fuelThe shipowner and the crew
Well-to-Wake (WtW)The sum of the two - the complete figureNobody alone, which is the administrative difficulty

The relationship is arithmetic: WtW = WtT + TtW. It follows that well-to-tank is a component of well-to-wake rather than a rival to it, and the choice the question poses is really between the whole and one of its parts. That distinction is worth making explicitly before answering the second half of the limb.

6. Why well-to-wake is the feasible basis

Taking the three candidates in turn:

  • Well-to-tank alone is not usable for shipping at all. It measures the fuel supply chain and says nothing whatever about the ship - two ships burning the same fuel with entirely different efficiency would carry the same figure. It cannot be applied to a vessel.
  • Tank-to-wake alone is measurable but incentivises the wrong thing. It is easy to administer, because bunker delivery notes and consumption records already exist - which is exactly why CII was built that way - but it permits carbon leakage and treats grey and green ammonia as the same fuel.
  • Well-to-wake is the only defensible basis. It closes leakage, because an emission can be removed from the boundary only by not occurring; it ranks the candidate fuels correctly, distinguishing production pathways that the funnel cannot see; and it captures both ends of the methane problem - upstream leakage and onboard slip - which neither half does alone.

It is also the basis the Organization has already chosen: MEPC.377(80) states the ambitions and checkpoints on a well-to-wake footing, and the LCA guidelines exist to make that operable. And it is the basis of the first binding instrument in the field, which is regional and not IMO: FuelEU Maritime, Regulation (EU) 2023/1805, which applies from 1 January 2025 to the greenhouse gas intensity of energy used by ships calling at European ports, alongside the extension of the EU Emissions Trading System to shipping from 2024.

Conclusion. Well-to-wake is the feasible methodology for shipping. Well-to-tank is indispensable as its upstream half but is not a metric for a ship, and tank-to-wake alone would reward the appearance of decarbonisation over the fact of it.

7. What makes it hard, and how the difficulty is managed

The objection to well-to-wake is practical rather than conceptual, and it should be stated fairly:

  • The shipowner does not control, observe or audit the upstream half of his own fuel's emissions.
  • Upstream figures vary with feedstock, plant, electricity grid mix, season and route, and much of the underlying data is commercially confidential.
  • Allocation between co-products and the treatment of land-use change are methodological choices that can change the result by more than the technology does.
  • Verification requires certification schemes and audited chains of custody, with a real risk of double counting where regional and global regimes overlap.

The practical resolution is a two-part system. The upstream half is handled by conservative default values for each pathway, which any supplier may improve upon by certifying an actual value through an audited scheme - so the burden of proof sits with the party that benefits from the better number. The onboard half is handled by measured consumption, which ships already record. That combination is why the guidelines matter as much as the strategy: without an agreed method for the well-to-tank half, a well-to-wake obligation could be declared but not administered.

Understand this first

Ordinary rolling is something the sea does to the ship: a wave pushes her over and she swings back. Parametric rolling is different in kind - the sea does not push her over at all. Instead the passing wave changes the ship's stiffness, and the ship pumps herself over. Picture a modern container ship from above: a sharply flared bow, a wide flat transom, and a comparatively fine middle. When a wave crest sits amidships, the bow and stern are in the troughs and their wide flared sections come out of the water - so the waterplane loses area exactly where it was widest, the transverse inertia of the waterplane collapses, BM falls and the ship goes soft. Half a wave later a trough is amidships, the flare and transom immerse, the waterplane fills out and she goes stiff again. If that stiffening and softening happens at about twice her natural roll frequency, every small roll is met by a restoring force timed to reinforce it - the same physics as pumping a swing by rising and crouching twice per swing. There is no roll-exciting wave moment at all, which is why it happens in head seas, where a mariner expects pitching and not rolling, and why it can go from nothing to thirty degrees in a handful of cycles.

Model written answer

1. What parametric rolling is

Parametric rolling is a resonant roll motion that arises because the ship's own transverse stability varies periodically as waves pass along the hull. The term "parametric" is exact: what oscillates is a parameter of the system - the restoring stiffness - and not the force applied to it.

That distinguishes it sharply from synchronous rolling, in which a beam-sea wave moment acts directly on the ship at her natural roll frequency. In parametric rolling there is no significant direct roll excitation at all, which is why it develops in head and following seas - precisely where a mariner expects pitching rather than rolling. Once established it grows very quickly, and roll angles of thirty degrees and more can appear within a handful of cycles.

2. How it originates - the stability variation

The mechanism is a consequence of modern container-ship hull form: a pronounced bow flare, a wide flat transom stern with large overhangs, and comparatively fine sections amidships.

Wave positionWhat happens to the hullEffect on stability
Crest amidshipsBow and stern lie in the troughs; the flared forward sections and the transom emergeWaterplane area and its transverse inertia fall, so BM and GM fall and the GZ curve flattens - the ship becomes soft
Trough amidshipsCrests at the ends immerse the flare and the transomWaterplane inertia rises, BM and GM rise - the ship becomes stiff

One wave passage therefore produces two stability minima and two maxima. If the ship encounters waves at a frequency close to twice her natural roll frequency, the stiffening and softening are timed so that energy is fed into the roll on every half cycle, and the motion grows instead of decaying - the same physics as pumping a playground swing by rising and crouching twice per swing. Any small disturbance is enough to start it: a yaw, a gust, an off-centre weight.

3. The conditions that must coincide

  • Wavelength comparable to the ship's length, so that the crest-to-trough change is felt across the whole hull at once.
  • Sufficient wave height for the stability variation to be significant.
  • Encounter period close to half the natural roll period - equivalently, roll period about twice the encounter period.
  • Low roll damping: modest speed, bilge keels small relative to the hull, and stabiliser fins that are ineffective on a head-sea heading.
  • Heading and speed: head or bow seas at speed, or following and quartering seas where ship speed approaches wave celerity.

These must coincide, and that is the practical key to section 8: breaking any single one of them stops the motion.

4. Why it has become important today

The phenomenon is not new, but three things have made it a live commercial and regulatory problem:

  • Hull form and ship size. The flare and the wide transom exist to maximise container slots on a given length and beam, and post-Panamax hulls are long enough to match ocean wavelengths. Deck stacks are high, so the vertical centre of gravity and the lever arm to the outermost boxes are both large.
  • Casualty evidence. The APL China casualty in the North Pacific in 1998, in which a container ship rolled heavily and lost and damaged a very large number of boxes, is the event that put parametric rolling into naval-architectural, regulatory and legal focus.
  • Economics and operations. Container losses, cargo claims, lashing and structural damage and off-hire are expensive, and charterers, insurers and class now ask about the phenomenon directly. Slow steaming - adopted for fuel cost and reinforced by carbon-intensity regulation - reduces roll damping and lengthens the time spent in a given seaway.

Above all it matters because it is a stability failure the mandatory criteria do not see, which is the subject of the next section.

6. Reading the scenario - what 1300 boxes actually means

The loaded condition in the question is doing real work and should not be read past. A post-Panamax container ship carrying 1300 containers is light and part-loaded, not full. It follows that:

  • draught and displacement are reduced and freeboard is large;
  • much of the flared bow and the transom is above the waterline at rest, so a given wave produces a larger proportional change in waterplane inertia;
  • GM is high and the natural roll period correspondingly short, which brings the resonance condition within reach at more combinations of speed and heading;
  • less hull and less bilge keel are immersed, so roll damping is lower;
  • windage area is large relative to displacement, so a squall produces significant wind heel and yaw - a ready-made initial disturbance.

The printed weather requires a word of honesty. A hailstorm is a convective squall of short duration; it does not by itself raise a seaway of ship-length wavelength, and that wavelength is a necessary condition. The sensible reading, and the one taken here, is that the squall represents the sudden onset of severe weather - strong and shifting wind, heavy precipitation, poor visibility and a rapid rise in sea state - superimposed on the swell already running, which is what supplies the wavelength. The disturbance that triggers the resonance comes from the squall; the resonance itself comes from the swell.

8. Justification, and the action that follows

Why those effects and not others. Every item above follows from a single cause: the ship is being driven at resonance, so the energy put in by each wave encounter accumulates instead of dissipating. Two consequences explain the pattern. First, the accelerations rise faster than the roll angle alone suggests, because acceleration depends on angle and on the square of the roll frequency - and this ship, being light, has a short roll period. Second, the damage concentrates at the deck stacks because acceleration scales with distance from the roll axis, and a part-loaded container ship puts the cargo high while the axis stays low. That is why the boxes go before the ship does.

What follows operationally is to break the resonance, not to endure it.

  1. Recognise it. Roll developing in head or following seas, growing over a few cycles, strongly coupled to pitch, with the ship rolling alternately to each side - that pattern is diagnostic and is not ordinary heavy-weather rolling.
  2. Change course and speed together, so that the encounter period moves away from half the natural roll period. Altering to put the seas on the bow or quarter also restores some direct damping.
  3. Do not simply slow down. In head seas reducing speed lengthens the encounter period, and if the ship was above the resonant condition that moves her into it. The change must be calculated against the roll period, not applied as a reflex.
  4. Treat ballasting as a computed change. Adding ballast alters GM and therefore the roll period, but increasing GM shortens the roll period and may create the very resonance it was meant to cure. Press up or empty slack tanks to remove free surface.
  5. Secure the ship. Warn the crew before the motion builds, secure the engine room and stores, reduce load on machinery vulnerable to racing, and prepare for propeller emergence.
  6. Afterwards, record and report. Inspect lashings, stacks, hatch covers and structure; log the sea state, heading, speed and roll period; and feed it back into the ship-specific guidance and the loading computer - which are only as good as the behaviour actually reported to them.

The underlying justification for all of it is section 3: the phenomenon needs several conditions to coincide, and the master and Chief Engineer between them can change the two that are under the ship's control - the timing and the damping.

The mental model, in one paragraph

Do not think of a wave pushing the ship over. Think of the wave changing how stiff she is. A crest amidships takes the wide flared ends out of the water and she goes soft; a trough amidships puts them back and she goes stiff. That happens twice per wave, which is why the dangerous encounter frequency is twice the roll frequency and not equal to it. Everything else - the head-sea occurrence, the rapid growth, the cure by changing course and speed - falls out of that one picture.

Why the mandatory criteria miss it

The 2008 IS Code Part A criteria are static: they evaluate a GZ curve computed in calm water, at a fixed displacement and trim. A ship can pass every static criterion comfortably and still be vulnerable, which is exactly the gap the second generation criteria were written to close - and at this sitting those criteria are still interim guidelines. Being able to say that clearly is worth more than another paragraph on the effects.

Common mistakes

  • Explaining it as a wave moment acting on the ship. There is no direct roll excitation.
  • Saying it only happens in following seas. Head seas are the classic case.
  • Treating the scenario ship as fully loaded.
  • Recommending "reduce speed" without qualification.
  • Giving effects only for the cargo, and omitting structure, machinery and people - which on a Marine Engineering Management paper are the parts the examiner is most entitled to expect.
  • Calling the second generation criteria mandatory.
  • Silently converting the hailstorm into a storm without saying so - or, worse, inventing a mechanism by which hail causes rolling.

Examiner traps

  • Asking why it happens in head seas. Because the excitation is parametric, not direct.

  • Asking which IMO document a master would actually consult, and whether it is mandatory.
  • Asking about propeller emergence and its effect on the main engine - torque swings, governor hunting and overspeed protection.

Understand this first

A solid bulk cargo does not float in water and it does not dissolve. What makes it dangerous is something subtler: it is only a solid because of friction between its particles, and that friction depends on the particles pressing against each other rather than against water. Load a fine, moist ore into a hold and it looks and behaves like rock. Then run the ship for two days: the main engine vibrates the hull, the sea rolls her, and the cargo compacts. As the void spaces between particles shrink, the water that was sitting in them has nowhere to go, so the pore water pressure rises. Every increment of pore pressure takes an equal increment off the contact force between grains. When the contact force reaches zero the grains are no longer touching - they are floating in their own water - and the cargo flows. Nothing was added and nothing was spilled; the same material simply stopped being a solid. That is why the whole regulatory answer is about a number measured before loading - the moisture content against the transportable moisture limit - and why the master's can test, crude as it is, matters: once the ship is at sea there is nothing anyone can do about it.

Model written answer

1. The mechanism - why a solid starts behaving as a liquid

A fine-particled bulk cargo is a solid only because of inter-granular friction and the capillary forces between wet particles. It does not need to be dry, and it does not need to be strong; it needs its grains to be pressing on each other.

Once at sea the cargo is subjected to continuous vibration from the machinery and propeller and to rolling and pitching. Both progressively compact the mass, reducing the void volume between particles. The water occupying those voids cannot drain away at the same rate, so the pore water pressure rises. Rising pore pressure removes, in equal measure, the effective stress - the actual contact force - between adjacent particles. When the effective stress approaches zero the grains are supported by the water rather than by each other, and the mass flows.

Nothing has been added and nothing has escaped. The same material has changed state because the water already inside it moved - which is why the cargo can look perfectly sound at the moment of loading and be a liquid two days later.

2. The conditions and the contributing factors

Three conditions must coincide.

  1. A cargo of a type that can liquefy - established from the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its current individual schedule in the IMSBC Code, never from the commodity name used in conversation.
  2. A moisture content above the transportable moisture limit for that cargo.
  3. Sufficient ship motion and vibration to compact the mass.

Remove any one and the cargo will not liquefy - which is why the whole regulatory scheme concentrates on the second condition, the only one that can be measured and controlled before the ship sails.

Contributing factors on the supply side: fine particle size with a proportion of clay or very fine material; open stockpiles exposed to weather; rain during storage, transfer or loading; wet mining and washing processes; barge transfer in an open roadstead; poor drainage at the terminal; and loading in the monsoon or wet season. On the ship side: engine and propeller vibration, rolling and pitching, long voyages, and heavy weather. And separately, sampling error: a stockpile is not homogeneous, and a certificate is only as good as the sample behind it.

3. TML and FMP - the numbers that decide acceptance

TermMeaning
Flow Moisture Point (FMP)The moisture content at which the cargo begins to lose shear strength and behave as a fluid under the prescribed test conditions
Transportable Moisture Limit (TML)The maximum moisture content of the cargo considered safe for carriage. For most cargoes it is ninety per cent of the FMP - but the individual schedule governs, and some cargoes use a different determination

Determination is by laboratory test under Appendix 2 - the flow table test, the penetration test, or the Proctor-Fagerberg test. Two certificates then matter and they are not interchangeable: the TML certificate, which is a property of the material and is valid for a period before loading, and the moisture content certificate, which must be taken much closer to loading because moisture changes with the weather while the TML does not.

The master's can test - a sample in a cylindrical can, struck repeatedly against a hard surface, watched for free moisture or fluid behaviour at the surface - is a complementary check on board. It can raise an alarm and justify stopping loading, but it is not a substitute for the certified laboratory determination and it cannot establish that a cargo is safe.

4. Liquefaction is not dynamic separation

Two phenomena are routinely confused and it is worth separating them:

  • In liquefaction, the cargo mass itself loses shear strength and flows.
  • In dynamic separation, moisture migrates upward through the cargo and forms a liquid slurry above a still-solid mass - a wet base with a free surface - without the bulk of the cargo liquefying at all.

The consequence for the ship is comparable and equally dangerous: a free surface where the design assumed none, and a mobile upper layer that can slop and shift. The distinction matters for two reasons. The bauxite casualty most often cited as a liquefaction case is now generally understood as dynamic separation; and the Code addresses the two through different schedules and provisions, so the correct precautions follow from the correct diagnosis.

5. The dangers to the ship

The danger is not that the cargo is wet. It is that the ship's stability has been calculated on an assumption that has silently become false.

  • A free surface appears in a hold designed on the assumption that solid cargo cannot move. The virtual rise in the centre of gravity reduces GM.
  • The mass shifts to the low side and does not come back. Roll becomes asymmetric and a progressive list develops.
  • The cargo can re-solidify at the angle of list, locking the ship heeled and removing the reserve she needs for the next sea.
  • The process compounds. Each shift increases the list; each increase in list makes the next shift larger.
  • Capsize can follow with very little warning, which is what makes these among the most sudden losses in the bulk trades - and why the survival rate is so poor.
  • Secondary consequences: loss of steering and manoeuvrability; structural damage as cargo works against bulkheads and hatch coamings; inability to launch survival craft from a heavily heeled ship; and extreme danger to anyone in or near the hold.

7. Prevention and the regulatory framework

The instrument. The IMSBC Code, made mandatory through SOLAS, and at the date of this examination as amended by amendment 07-23, which entered into force on 1 January 2025 having been available for voluntary application from 1 January 2024. It supersedes amendment 06-21, in force since 1 December 2023. The relevant provisions are section 4 (assessment of acceptability of consignments), section 7.3 (cargoes which may liquefy), section 8 (test procedures and the complementary can test) and Appendix 2 (laboratory test procedures), read with the individual schedules.

Before and during loading.

  • The shipper declares the cargo under its Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and supplies the section 4 information, with certificates of TML and of moisture content.
  • The ship checks the declaration against the individual schedule and the section 7.3 conditions, and checks the dates on both certificates.
  • The acceptance rule: a cargo which may liquefy is not accepted unless its moisture content is below the TML, or the ship is specially constructed or fitted for such carriage, or the cargo is carried under the specific alternative conditions the Code allows.
  • Inspect the cargo and the barges: watch for splatter, free water, and a surface that flattens or will not hold a slope.
  • Carry out the can test as a complementary check.
  • Suspend loading in rain and close the hatches; trim as the schedule requires.
  • Refuse or stop loading if the cargo does not match its declaration. This is the master's only real protection, and it exists only before the ship sails.

On passage. Sound bilges and wells regularly and record what is found; watch the roll for the asymmetry that signals a shift; route to avoid heavy weather and reduce motion by adjusting course and speed; and never enter a hold to inspect. If liquefaction or separation is suspected: minimise motion, consider shelter or a port of refuge, inform the company, the flag State and the nearest coastal State, and prepare the crew. The condition is not recoverable at sea.

At discharge. Expect the cargo to have compacted or set, possibly at an angle; brief on the hazards of working a shifted or crusted cargo, including voids and collapse; and record and report what was found, so that the loading terminal, the shipper and the charterer are held to the certificate they issued. Supporting the whole framework are SOLAS chapter VI for cargo information, SOLAS chapter XII for bulk carriers, and the BLU Code for the loading and unloading process itself.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) asks for the phenomenon "including the conditions under which it occurs and its contributing factors" - that is an instruction to explain why, not what, and a definition alone cannot satisfy it. Limb (b) expressly asks for "examples of incidents", so the casualties get their own section rather than a parenthesis. Limb (c) carries the largest allocation and names three stages - loading, transportation and discharge - so section 7 is organised on those three stages, in that order, using the examiner's own words.

The one sentence of soil mechanics that carries the question

Effective stress equals total stress minus pore water pressure. Everything else follows: compaction raises pore pressure, pore pressure eats effective stress, effective stress is what makes the cargo a solid. A candidate who writes that one relationship has explained liquefaction; a candidate who writes "the cargo becomes wet and slides" has described it.

Why TML and moisture content are certified separately

The TML is a property of the material and does not change with the weather, so its certificate is valid for a longer period. The moisture content is a property of the consignment on the day, and it changes with every shower - which is why its certificate must be much closer to loading, and why the Code requires re-checking after significant rain. Confusing the two, or accepting a stale moisture certificate, is the documentary failure behind several of the casualties in section 6.

Common mistakes

  • Naming amendment 06-21 as the current amendment at this sitting. The Code is as amended by 07-23, in force 1 January 2025.
  • Stating a cargo's group from the commodity name. Classification and carriage requirements follow the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its current individual schedule.
  • Quoting TML as ninety per cent of FMP without the qualification. It is the general rule, not a universal one.
  • Presenting the can test as the test of acceptability. It is complementary, and a negative result proves nothing.
  • Describing the danger as "loss of stability" without the free surface and the irreversible shift - which is what distinguishes this from an ordinary stability problem.
  • Offering no named casualty when the stem asks for examples.
  • Suggesting that a list from liquefaction can be corrected by ballasting. It cannot; the cargo may simply shift further.

Examiner traps

  • Asking which amendment of the IMSBC Code is in force - the sharpest date on this paper.
  • Asking whether a can test can clear a cargo for loading. No.
  • Asking what the master does if the shipper offers an indemnity for loading a doubtful cargo. An indemnity is not a defence and does not change the Code.
  • Asking about the difference between liquefaction and dynamic separation.
  • Asking why the cargo does not simply return to the high side as the ship rolls back. Because it has lost shear strength and can re-solidify at the angle of list.
  • Asking whether you would open a hold at sea to look. Never.
  • Asking which SOLAS chapter makes the Code mandatory, and which chapter carries the additional bulk carrier measures.
