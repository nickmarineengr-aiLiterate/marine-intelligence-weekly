Understand this first

Fatigue is an impairment you cannot feel. A tired watchkeeper does not experience himself as unfit - he experiences himself as normal and performs badly, and the first hard evidence is usually the incident itself. That single fact explains the whole of section A-VIII/1. Because fitness for duty cannot be left to self-assessment, the Convention converts it into something that can be scheduled in advance, measured in hours, written down and inspected by somebody else. So the criteria come in two layers: a quantity of rest and a shape for that rest, which control the fatigue itself; and posted schedules, standardized records and a signed copy for the seafarer, which exist purely so that the first layer can be enforced against a company that would otherwise be tempted to run the ship one person short.

Model written answer

1. What Chapter VIII requires, and where the criteria are written

Chapter VIII of the STCW Convention is headed Watchkeeping and contains two regulations. Regulation VIII/1, 'Fitness for duty', is the one this question asks about. It is short, and it is a duty on the Administration rather than a set of numbers: for the purpose of preventing fatigue, each Administration must (i) establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those whose duties involve designated safety, security and prevention of pollution duties, in accordance with section A-VIII/1; and (ii) require that watch systems are so arranged that the efficiency of watchkeeping personnel is not impaired by fatigue, and that the first watch at the commencement of a voyage and subsequent relieving watches are sufficiently rested and otherwise fit for duty. Paragraph 2 adds the same duty for drug and alcohol abuse.

The measurable criteria therefore live in the mandatory Code, section A-VIII/1, with non-mandatory guidance in section B-VIII/1. Note who is covered: not only the watch, but anyone with designated safety, pollution-prevention or security duties.

2. Criterion one - the minimum hours of rest

  • Not less than 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period; and
  • 77 hours of rest in any 7-day period.
  • The criterion is expressed as rest, not as hours of work, and it is a rolling measure - any 24 hours and any 7 days, not the calendar day or the calendar week.

3. Criterion two - how that rest may be broken up

Hours are worthless if they are shredded, so the Code controls the shape of the rest as well as its quantity. Rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest must not exceed 14 hours. The 6-hour block is what protects a consolidated sleep; the 14-hour ceiling is what stops a long unbroken stretch of duty.

A Party may allow exceptions, but they are themselves bounded: rest must still be not less than 70 hours in any 7-day period; rest may then be split into no more than three periods, one of at least 6 hours and neither of the others less than one hour; the 14-hour interval still applies; exceptions may not extend beyond two 24-hour periods in any 7-day period, may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and must be separated by an interval of at least twice the duration of the exception.

4. Criterion three - drugs and alcohol

Each Administration must establish a limit of not greater than 0.05% blood alcohol level, or 0.25 mg/l alcohol in the breath, for masters, officers and other seafarers while performing designated safety, security and marine environmental duties. This is a fitness criterion in the literal sense - a threshold of impairment - and it is enforced through company drug and alcohol policy, unannounced testing and port State inspection.

5. Criterion four - the administrative criteria that make the rest rules real

  • Watch schedules shall be posted where easily accessible, in a standardized format, in the working language of the ship and in English.
  • Records of daily hours of rest shall be maintained in the same standardized format and languages, so that compliance can be monitored and verified; the seafarer receives a copy, endorsed by the master or an authorized person and by the seafarer.
  • A seafarer on call - the standing case of an unattended machinery space - must be given adequate compensatory rest if the normal rest period is disturbed by call-outs.
  • Drills must be conducted so as to minimize the disturbance of rest periods and not induce fatigue.

6. The master's overriding authority, and what follows it

The rest requirements need not be maintained in an emergency or in other overriding operational conditions, and nothing in the section impairs the master's right to require any hours of work necessary for the immediate safety of the ship, persons on board or cargo, or to assist others in distress. The master may suspend the schedule until the normal situation is restored. The provision is not a loophole, because it carries its own closing obligation: as soon as practicable afterwards the master must ensure that anyone who worked during scheduled rest is given an adequate period of rest.

7. How these criteria produce a safe and effective watch

They work as a system rather than as a list. Fatigue degrades exactly what a watch is for - vigilance, reaction time, judgement and the ability to detect a slow-developing abnormality - and it does so without the sufferer noticing, which is why a subjective 'fit to stand a watch' test is useless and a quantified, recorded one is not. The quantity rule sets a floor; the division rule ensures the floor is usable sleep rather than scattered fragments; the alcohol limit removes a second impairment that would compound the first; the posted schedule turns compliance into something that must be planned in advance, which is what forces adequate manning rather than heroic individuals; the records make it auditable by the flag State, by port State control and by the company's own ISM system; and the master's override, bounded by compensating rest, keeps the regime credible in a real emergency instead of inviting routine falsification. Regulation VIII/1.1.2 completes the loop by making the relieving watch the test: a watch system is compliant only if the officer who takes over is genuinely rested and fit for duty.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem is two questions joined by 'How'. The first - the criteria - is concrete and is marked by counting: the numbers, the division rule, the exceptions, the alcohol limit, the schedules and the records. The second - how they ensure safe and effective watchkeeping - is the discussion mark, and it is the half most candidates omit. Steps 2 to 6 bank the concrete marks; step 7 exists solely to answer the second half of the question, and without it a technically perfect list of numbers is only half an answer.

Note also where the criteria live. The question says 'as outlined in STCW Chapter 8', but regulation VIII/1 contains almost no detail - it delegates to section A-VIII/1 of the Code. Saying so in one sentence at the start shows the examiner you know how the Convention and Code fit together, and it costs nothing.

The idea underneath the question

Fatigue is an invisible impairment. A tired officer does not feel unfit; he feels normal and performs badly, and the first evidence is usually the incident. Every criterion in section A-VIII/1 is an attempt to control something that cannot be self-assessed, by turning it into something that can be scheduled, measured, recorded and inspected. Once you see that, the structure of the section explains itself: a quantity rule, a shape rule, a chemical-impairment rule, and three administrative rules that make the first three enforceable.

Common mistakes

  • Giving the MLC hours-of-work figures instead of the STCW hours-of-rest figures, or mixing the two frameworks. STCW A-VIII/1 is written as minimum rest: 10 in 24 and 77 in 7.
  • Quoting 72 hours in 7 days. That is the MLC maximum hours of work. The STCW rest figure is 77.
  • Giving the 10 and the 77 and stopping. The division rule - two periods, one of at least 6 hours, 14-hour maximum interval - carries marks of its own.
  • Omitting the exceptions regime entirely, or giving the 70-hour floor without the conditions that fence it.
  • Restricting the criteria to watchkeepers. They also cover those with designated safety, security and prevention of pollution duties.
  • Treating the master's override as an unqualified power. It is followed by a mandatory compensating rest obligation.
  • Forgetting that the records must be in English as well as the working language, and that the seafarer gets a signed copy.
  • Answering only the first half of the stem and never addressing how the criteria deliver a safe watch.

Examiner traps

  • 0.05% BAC and 0.25 mg/l are two expressions of one limit, and the Code says not greater than - an Administration may set a stricter figure, and many companies operate zero tolerance. Do not present 0.05% as an entitlement.
  • 'Any 24-hour period' and 'any 7-day period'. The measure is rolling. A schedule that complies on calendar days can still breach it.
  • The exception permits three periods, not two - and the two additional periods must each be at least one hour. Candidates routinely give the normal rule and the exception the same shape.
  • Two consecutive weeks maximum, and the gap afterwards must be at least twice the duration of the exception. That second condition is almost always missed.
  • The on-call provision is the engineer's provision. An unattended machinery space is the example the Code itself gives, so expect to be asked how you compensate an engineer called out overnight.
  • Drills are expressly required not to induce fatigue - a favourite follow-up, because it sits at the junction of two safety obligations that appear to conflict.

Understand this first

The old way of regulating ship construction was to write down the answer: this thickness, that spacing. Two problems followed. Nobody could say how safe a ship built to those numbers actually was - the safety level was buried in tradition rather than stated - and a genuinely better design could be rejected simply for not matching the words. Worse, the numbers themselves had long since moved into classification society rules, which IMO did not govern. Goal-based standards fix all three at once by changing what IMO writes. IMO now writes the goal - a ship that stays safe and environmentally friendly for at least twenty-five years, damaged as well as intact - and the functional requirements underneath it, and then audits the class rules to confirm they deliver it. That is the whole idea, and every detail in this question hangs off it: IMO owns the top three tiers, class owns the numbers, and the check runs upward from the numbers to the goal.

Model written answer

1. a) What the Goal-Based Standards are, and the gap they close

Traditional SOLAS construction requirements are prescriptive: they say what to fit and how thick to make it. Prescription has two structural weaknesses. It does not say what level of safety is being bought, so nobody can tell whether a rule is generous or marginal; and it freezes technology, because a novel design that is demonstrably as safe may still fail the letter of the rule. Detailed scantling requirements had also migrated almost entirely to classification society rules, over which IMO had no direct oversight - the Organization mandated the outcome but did not govern the rules that actually determined it.

Goal-based standards (GBS) invert this. IMO states the goal and the functional requirements that must be met, and then verifies that the detailed rules - principally class rules - deliver them. GBS was made mandatory by SOLAS regulation II-1/3-10, in force 1 January 2012, with the standards themselves in resolution MSC.287(87) and the verification guidelines in MSC.296(87).

2. a) The five-tier framework

TierContentOwned by

IIFunctional requirements - what must be satisfied to meet the goalsIMO

IVRules and regulations for ship design and construction - class rules and national requirementsRecognized organizations and Administrations

Tiers I to III are the GBS instrument. Tiers IV and V are where the numbers live. The whole design of the system is in that split.

3. a) The goal, and the functional requirements

The Tier I goal is that ships shall be designed and constructed for a specified design life to be safe and environmentally friendly when properly operated and maintained, under the specified operating and environmental conditions, in intact and specified damage conditions, throughout their life - with a specified design life of not less than 25 years. Note that environmental protection is inside the goal itself, not bolted on.

Tier II converts that into functional requirements, which fall into three natural groups:

  • Design - design life; the environmental conditions to be designed for (North Atlantic); structural strength; fatigue life; residual strength in a damaged condition; and protection against corrosion, including coating life.
  • Construction - structural redundancy; watertight and weathertight integrity; human element considerations; design transparency; and construction quality procedures.
  • In service - survey; maintenance; structural accessibility for inspection; and recycling considerations.

4. a) Application, and the Ship Construction File

GBS applies to oil tankers and bulk carriers of 150 m in length and above contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2016. The most visible shipboard consequence is the Ship Construction File, required by SOLAS II-1/3-10 and delivering the design transparency functional requirement: the information needed to operate, maintain, repair and respond to an emergency - structural drawings, scantlings, design assumptions, coating specifications and inspection access - kept on board and ashore and maintained through the ship's life.

5. b) How the framework delivers safety and environmental protection

It works on the hull as the primary barrier. Bulk carriers and tankers were chosen because their failure modes - progressive structural failure, and loss of oil cargo - kill crews and pollute coastlines. Design life, fatigue life, corrosion protection and residual strength after damage attack exactly those modes: a hull that retains strength when damaged is a hull that gives time to evacuate and does not break up. Environmental protection follows from structural integrity, because the overwhelming majority of pollution from these ship types comes from hull failure rather than from operational discharge. Recycling considerations extend the goal to end of life, and structural accessibility ensures the ship can actually be inspected, which is what keeps the design margin real in year twenty rather than only on delivery.

6. b) Verification - IMO audits the rules, not the ship

This is the structural innovation, and it is what the question is really testing. Under Tier III, an Administration or recognized organization submits its ship construction rules to IMO. An audit team appointed by the Secretary-General assesses whether those rules conform to the Tier I goal and Tier II functional requirements, reports to the Maritime Safety Committee, and the Committee determines conformity. The IACS Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers were the principal submission in the initial round and were confirmed as conforming.

Verification does not stop at the first audit. Maintenance of verification requires rule amendments to be notified and reassessed, with periodic re-verification, so that a rule set cannot drift away from the goals after it has been approved once.

7. b) Validation - the ship-level chain

Verification answers are the rules right? Validation answers was this ship actually built to them?, and that runs through the ordinary machinery: plan approval against verified rules, survey during construction, class certification, and statutory certification by or on behalf of the flag State, with the Ship Construction File as the documentary evidence and port State control as the independent check in service. The two together form a closed chain - IMO assures the rules, class and flag assure the ship - and a break at either end defeats the system.

8. b) What GBS actually changed

Three things worth saying at management level. First, it brought class rules inside IMO's oversight for the first time, without IMO having to write scantlings. Second, it makes the safety level explicit and auditable rather than implicit in tradition, which is the precondition for the Safety Level Approach now being developed and for applying goal-based methodology to other ship types and to novel technology. Third, for a Chief Engineer the practical residue is the Ship Construction File: it is the ship's design intent in writing, it must be kept current when structural repairs are made, and it is what allows a repair to be judged against the original assumptions rather than against opinion.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs of eight marks each, and they test different things. Limb (a) is descriptive and countable: the five tiers, the goal, the functional requirements, the application, the Ship Construction File. Put the tiers in a table - it banks five marks in the space of four lines and it is the single most recognisable feature of GBS. Limb (b) is analytical, and it contains an explicit sub-demand - 'the role of verification and validation' - which is where most of the (b) marks sit.

The commonest way to lose limb (b) is to describe verification and validation as vague synonyms for 'checking'. They are not. Give them two separate steps, as steps 6 and 7 do, and say plainly what each one is looking at.

The one distinction that unlocks limb (b)

Verification looks at the RULES. Validation looks at the SHIP.

  • Verification is a Tier III activity at IMO. A class society submits its construction rules; an audit team assesses them against the Tier I goal and Tier II functional requirements; the MSC determines conformity; and maintenance of verification keeps the assessment alive as the rules are amended. Nobody looks at a ship.
  • Validation is the ordinary chain that confirms a particular ship was built to those verified rules - plan approval, survey during construction, class and statutory certification, the Ship Construction File, and port State control in service.

If you can state that one sentence and then develop each half, limb (b) is secure.

Why bulk carriers and oil tankers, and not everything

Because that is where the deaths and the spills were. Bulk carrier losses in the late 1980s and 1990s, and tanker structural failures, both had the same root: progressive structural degradation in a hull whose design margin nobody could audit. GBS was written for those two ship types deliberately, as the proving ground for a methodology that IMO intended to generalise later. Saying so shows you understand GBS as a method rather than as a rule about ships over 150 metres.

Common mistakes

  • Giving four tiers, or getting the order wrong. It is five: goals, functional requirements, verification of conformity, rules and regulations, industry practices - and I to III are IMO's.
  • Saying IMO verifies the ship. It verifies the rules. That is the whole point of the framework.
  • Treating verification and validation as the same word twice.
  • Omitting the Ship Construction File. It is the one part of GBS a serving engineer actually handles, and it is the examiner's favourite bridge from theory to the ship.
  • Claiming GBS applies to all new ships. It is oil tankers and bulk carriers of 150 m and above contracted on or after 1 July 2016.
  • Answering limb (b) on safety only and never mentioning environmental protection, when the stem names both - and when 'environmentally friendly' is inside the Tier I goal.
  • Forgetting maintenance of verification. A one-off audit that could never be revisited would be worthless.

Examiner traps

  • 'Goal-based' does not mean 'less safe' or 'self-regulated'. It means the required outcome is stated explicitly and the detailed rules are audited against it. If anything it is more demanding than prescription, because the rule-maker must now justify the rule.
  • The design life of not less than 25 years is a favourite number. So is the North Atlantic environmental condition.
  • Who does the audit? An audit team appointed by the Secretary-General, reporting to the MSC - not the class society itself, and not the flag State.
  • Where does the Ship Construction File live? On board and ashore, and it must be kept updated, including after structural repair or modification.
  • Being asked which tier a class rule sits in. Tier IV.
  • Being asked how GBS relates to the Safety Level Approach. SLA is the intended development - moving from qualitative goals to a quantified safety level - and at this sitting it is under development, not in force.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Name the five tiers in order, and say who owns each.
  • Does IMO approve your ship's scantlings?
  • What is in the Ship Construction File, and where is it kept?
  • You renew a section of side shell in a bulk carrier. Does anything have to happen to the SCF?
  • What is the difference between verification and validation here?
  • Which ships does GBS apply to, and from what contract date?
  • How does a construction standard protect the environment?
  • What happens if a class society amends its rules after they have been verified?

Understand this first

The Strategy is not a rule and it does not make anybody do anything. It is the Organization writing down, in one place, how much the shipping industry must reduce, by when, and by what process the binding rules will be produced - and that third part is the one candidates skip. Read it as three layers stacked on each other. At the top is a destination: net zero by or around 2050. Underneath are waypoints - the levels of ambition, which are mostly about intensity, and the indicative checkpoints, which are about total emissions and are the ones that actually test whether the world fleet is on track. At the bottom is a schedule for making law: short-term measures already in MARPOL Annex VI, a mid-term basket to be agreed by 2025, and long-term measures deliberately left blank until 2028. Everything else in the question - why the basket has two halves, why the principles matter, why the industry is investing in dual-fuel ships - falls out of that structure. And the reason a date matters so much here is that this examination sits in the middle of the schedule, with the architecture agreed and nothing yet adopted.

Model written answer

1. What the 2023 Strategy is, and what it replaced

The 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships was adopted by resolution MEPC.377(80) on 7 July 2023, and it expressly revokes the 2018 Initial Strategy from that date. It is a policy instrument, not law: it binds nobody directly, and it works by committing the Committee to develop measures which then become law as amendments to MARPOL Annex VI. The Committee also agreed to keep it under review with a view to adopting a further revised Strategy in 2028.

2. The vision and the levels of ambition

The vision is to reduce GHG emissions from international shipping and, as a matter of urgency, to phase them out as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition. Four levels of ambition follow:

  1. Carbon intensity of the ship to decline through further improvement of energy efficiency for new ships, reviewing with the aim of strengthening the design requirements.
  2. Carbon intensity of international shipping to decline - CO2 per transport work to fall, as a fleet average, by at least 40% by 2030 against 2008.
  3. Uptake of zero or near-zero GHG emission technologies, fuels and/or energy sources to reach at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030.
  4. Net zero - to peak emissions as soon as possible and reach net-zero GHG emissions by or around, i.e. close to, 2050, taking into account different national circumstances.

Two features change everything. The targets are GHG, not CO2 alone, so methane and nitrous oxide count - which bears directly on LNG. And they are measured well-to-wake under the LCA guidelines (MEPC.376(80)), so a fuel that is clean at the funnel but dirty to produce earns no credit.

3. The indicative checkpoints

The levels of ambition are intensity-based; the checkpoints are absolute, and they are what make the pathway real - to reduce total annual GHG emissions from international shipping, against 2008, by:

  • at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030; and
  • at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040.

They are indicative - checkpoints on the way to net zero, not binding limits - and the 'striving for' figures are the compromise that allowed adoption.

4. The guiding principles

These constrain how any measure may be designed, and they are why the mid-term measures took so long. They include non-discrimination and no more favourable treatment as enshrined in MARPOL; common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in the light of different national circumstances, from the UNFCCC and the Paris Agreement; that all ships give full and complete effect regardless of flag; the need to consider impacts on States, in particular LDCs and SIDS; and evidence-based decision-making balanced with the precautionary approach. The first two pull in opposite directions - one says treat every ship alike, the other says treat States differently - and the whole negotiation on a GHG price is an attempt to reconcile them.

5. The short-term measures - already in force

The short-term measures are those finalized between 2018 and 2023, and they are operative today under MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4: the EEDI for new ships, the EEXI for existing ships, the SEEMP, the fuel oil consumption data collection system, and the operational carbon intensity rating (CII). The Strategy requires their review, and requires the mid-term measures to be designed in synergy with them - CII is named expressly.

6. The mid-term measures - the basket

This is the core of the question. The Strategy requires a basket comprising both elements together, to be finalized and agreed by 2025:

  1. a technical element - a goal-based marine fuel standard regulating the phased reduction of the GHG intensity of marine fuel, on a well-to-wake basis; and
  2. an economic element - a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism.

The logic of pairing them is that the standard compels the transition while the price funds and accelerates it and closes the cost gap between fossil and zero-carbon fuel. Both are subject to a comprehensive impact assessment of effects on States. Other candidate mid-term measures - annual emission estimates, R&D, port and operational efficiency, national action plans - sit alongside the basket.

7. Long-term measures and the 2028 review

The Strategy deliberately does not specify long-term measures. They are defined only as measures finalized and agreed beyond 2030, to be developed as part of the 2028 review, once the mid-term basket is in force and its effect can be measured. That is an honest position rather than a gap: the 2040 checkpoint and net zero cannot be delivered by a fuel standard alone, but what is added should depend on evidence that does not yet exist.

8. Where the process stood at this examination

The Strategy's own milestone table places this sitting exactly. MEPC 81 (spring 2024) has happened - the interim impact-assessment report and the finalization of the basket. MEPC 82 (autumn 2024) has not; the finalized impact assessment is due there. Approval of the measures is scheduled for MEPC 83 in spring 2025, adoption at an extraordinary MEPC session about six months later, and entry into force 16 months after adoption, in 2027. So at this date: the architecture is agreed and nothing is adopted. Any answer that describes a binding IMO carbon price or fuel-intensity limit as existing law is wrong for September 2024.

9. The potential impact on the shipping industry

Six consequences follow, and they are where the evaluation marks are. Fuel cost becomes the dominant variable: a GHG price plus a tightening intensity standard converts an environmental obligation into an operating-cost line, and the well-to-wake basis decides which fuels actually qualify. Newbuilding choice becomes a risk decision - a ship ordered today trades into the 2040 checkpoint, so dual-fuel and retrofit-ready design is bought as optionality against asset stranding. The methane question is sharpened, because a GHG target measured well-to-wake treats slip as emissions. Commercial terms must change: the party that buys the fuel and the party that directs the speed are usually different, so the cost must be allocated in the charterparty. Crew competence and safety become the limiting factor - the resolution itself acknowledges the human element in safe implementation, and ammonia and hydrogen raise toxicity and containment problems that training and the safety framework must reach before the fuels do. And regulatory fragmentation is the risk a global measure exists to cure: with the EU Emissions Trading System already extended to shipping from 1 January 2024, a ship on a European trade already pays for carbon under a regional scheme, which is precisely the outcome the guiding principle of no more favourable treatment is meant to prevent.

Model written answer

1. Where the Funds sit - a three-tier system

The IOPC Funds are not a stand-alone regime. They are the second and third tiers of a layered compensation system for pollution by persistent oil carried as cargo in bulk by a tanker:

TierInstrumentWho pays
1CLC 92 - strict liability, channelled exclusively to the registered owner, with its own tonnage-based limits and compulsory insurance with direct actionThe shipowner and his insurer
21992 Fund Convention - the 1992 FundOil receivers in Member States
32003 Supplementary Fund Protocol - the Supplementary Fund, optional for StatesOil receivers in States that have joined it

The design principle is shared burden: the shipowner carries the first layer, and the cargo interest - the industry whose oil is being moved - carries the layers above it. The older 1971 Fund has been wound up and no longer exists.

2. The role and functions of the Funds

The primary role is to pay compensation where CLC alone does not produce it, in three situations: where the shipowner is exempt from liability under CLC; where the shipowner is financially incapable of meeting the claim and his insurance is insufficient; and - the usual case - where the damage exceeds the CLC limit.

Around that sit the working functions: assessing and settling claims, usually through a joint claims office with the shipowner's P&I club so that a claimant deals with one process; publishing the Claims Manual that tells claimants what is admissible before they claim; attending casualties and monitoring clean-up so that costs are controlled while they are being incurred; taking part in legal proceedings; and contributing the accumulated claims experience back into IMO's work.

3. The funding mechanism

This is the feature that most distinguishes the Funds, and it is the most commonly misunderstood. The Funds are not financed by governments, and not by shipowners.

  • Contributions are levied on any person who received, in a calendar year, more than 150,000 tonnes of contributing oil - crude oil and heavy fuel oil - carried by sea and received in a port or terminal installation in a Member State.
  • The obligation falls on the receiver, and the threshold is applied to the receiver, not to the State - so a State with many small receivers may contribute very little.
  • States report annual oil receipts; the Assembly then levies annual contributions according to what the Fund actually needs, so it is a post-event, pay-as-you-go system rather than a standing reserve.
  • For the Supplementary Fund a minimum aggregate quantity is deemed to have been received in each Member State, so that a State with little oil import still bears a floor share.

Two industry agreements, STOPIA 2006 and TOPIA 2006, sit voluntarily on top of this: tanker owners' P&I interests indemnify the 1992 Fund in respect of small tankers, and indemnify the Supplementary Fund for a share of its payments, rebalancing the burden between shipowners and oil receivers without amending the Conventions.

4. Eligibility - which incidents and which damage

The gateway conditions are narrow and are where most refused claims fail:

  • the oil must be persistent oil carried as cargo in bulk by a tanker - non-persistent oil such as gas oil is outside, and so are bunkers spilled from a non-tanker, which belong to the Bunker Convention;
  • the damage must occur in the territory, territorial sea or exclusive economic zone of a Member State, with preventive measures compensable wherever taken;
  • the categories of admissible damage are property damage, clean-up and preventive measures, consequential economic loss to those whose property was contaminated, pure economic loss where the link of causation is close enough - the fisherman or hotelier whose property was never touched - and reasonable measures of reinstatement of the environment;
  • claims are time-barred on the same terms as CLC: three years from the date of the damage and six years from the incident.

5. The admissibility criteria

The Claims Manual reduces admissibility to four tests, and a claim must pass all of them. The expense or loss must have been actually incurred, not projected. The measures must be reasonable and justifiable when judged objectively at the time. The loss must be quantifiable and supported by documentary evidence. And there must be a reasonable degree of proximity - a sufficiently close link of causation between the contamination and the loss.

One restriction does more work than the others: compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit from that impairment, is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken. There is no compensation for environmental damage as such, and no abstract theoretical valuation of a damaged ecosystem is admissible.

6. The limits, and what happens at the ceiling

The total payable by the 1992 Fund for any one incident - including the sum actually paid under CLC - is 203 million SDR, rising to 300,740,000 SDR where three Member States collectively received 600 million tonnes or more of contributing oil. Where the Supplementary Fund applies, the total available including CLC and the 1992 Fund is 750 million SDR.

If admitted claims exceed the ceiling, the Fund does not pay in full and does not pay first-come-first-served: it pays pro rata, so every claimant receives the same percentage. In a large casualty the Fund therefore makes interim payments at a reduced percentage until the total exposure is known - which is prudent, and is also why victims of the biggest spills wait longest.

7. Effectiveness - what the system does well

Judged against what preceded it, the regime works. Strict liability removes the need to prove fault; compulsory insurance with direct action means there is always a solvent defendant; and the tiers mean the money does not stop at the shipowner's limit. The overwhelming majority of claims are settled without litigation, because the Claims Manual tells claimants in advance what will be paid and the Funds and the P&I club assess jointly. And the burden is shared between ship and cargo, which is the political achievement that made a fund possible at all - the receivers of the oil pay for the risk of moving it.

8. Effectiveness - where it is weak

Four criticisms are fair and should be made. The ceiling can be exhausted: a major spill on a developed coastline can generate admitted claims beyond even the Supplementary Fund, and the consequence is pro-rata payment and years of delay. Pure economic loss and environmental damage are tightly restricted - the proximity test excludes claimants who suffered real loss, and reinstatement-only recovery means the ecological harm itself is never compensated. The third tier is optional, so the protection a coastal community actually has depends on whether its State joined the Supplementary Fund Protocol - the very States most exposed and least able to absorb loss are often those that have not. And the scope is narrow: it reaches only persistent oil from tankers, so a large containership bunker spill, an HNS release or a non-persistent product spill falls to other regimes with lower limits or, in some cases, to none.

The honest verdict is that the regime is very effective at what it was designed to do and is deliberately not designed to do more. It guarantees prompt, predictable compensation for quantifiable loss from tanker oil spills; it does not, and does not attempt to, place a price on environmental harm.

The idea underneath the question

The ship pays first, then the cargo pays. That single sentence is the political and structural core of the whole regime. Common mistakes

  • Saying the Funds are financed by governments or by shipowners. They are financed by receivers of contributing oil. This is the single most common error on this question.
  • Forgetting that the threshold is more than 150,000 tonnes of contributing oil received in a calendar year, and that contributing oil is crude oil and heavy fuel oil - not every petroleum product.
  • Treating the Fund limits as being on top of CLC. Each ceiling is a total including what has already been paid under the tier below.
  • Writing about the 1971 Fund as though it still existed. It has been wound up.

  • Saying environmental damage is compensated. Only the costs of reasonable reinstatement measures actually undertaken or to be undertaken are.

Understand this first

Nearly all of the MLC works the same way: it puts a duty on the shipowner and enforces it through the flag State, with the maritime labour certificate as the proof. Social security cannot work like that, and understanding why is the whole question. A pension, an invalidity benefit or a widow's benefit falls due decades after the engagement ends, when the shipowner may no longer exist and the ship has changed flag three times; and a typical seafarer's career is a series of short contracts with foreign employers, so there is no continuous national employer to attach the obligation to. Title 4 therefore switches the connecting factor from the flag to the State where the seafarer ordinarily lives. That single move explains everything else in the question: why the duty is on the Member rather than the shipowner, why it is expressed as progressive rather than immediate, why only three of nine branches are required at ratification, why the Convention insists on cooperation to keep rights portable - and why an Indian limb belongs in the question at all, since India owes duties to its resident seafarers no matter whose flag they sail under.

Model written answer

1. a) Where social security sits in the MLC

The MLC is built in five Titles, and Title 4 is 'Health protection, medical care, welfare and social security protection'. It runs in a deliberate sequence: Regulation 4.1 medical care on board and ashore, 4.2 shipowners' liability, 4.3 health and safety protection and accident prevention, 4.4 access to shore-based welfare facilities, and 4.5 social security. The first four are what the shipowner must provide during the engagement; 4.5 is what the STATE must provide, and it reaches beyond the voyage into old age, invalidity and the seafarer's family.

2. a) Regulation 4.5 - the obligation

Regulation 4.5 imposes three duties. Each Member must ensure that all seafarers and, to the extent provided in national law, their dependants have access to social security protection. Each Member undertakes to take steps, according to national circumstances and through international cooperation, to achieve progressively comprehensive protection. And seafarers subject to its social security legislation must be entitled to protection no less favourable than that enjoyed by shoreworkers.

Two features are distinctive. The obligation is progressive rather than immediate - the Convention accepts that a State cannot deliver everything at once. And the benchmark is parity with shoreworkers, not an absolute standard: the test is whether the seafarer is treated worse than a shore employee of the same State.

3. a) Standard A4.5 - the nine branches and the minimum

The mandatory Code names nine branches of social security to be considered:

  1. medical care
  2. sickness benefit
  3. unemployment benefit
  4. old-age benefit
  5. employment injury benefit
  6. family benefit
  7. maternity benefit
  8. invalidity benefit
  9. survivors' benefit

These complement - they do not replace - the protection under Regulations 4.1 and 4.2. At the time of ratification a Member must provide at least three of the nine, and Guideline B4.5 recommends that those three should at least be medical care, sickness benefit and employment injury benefit. Each Member specifies its branches at ratification and notifies the Director-General of any it adds later; the Director-General maintains a register.

4. a) How the protection is delivered

Standard A4.5 is deliberately flexible about the machinery. Protection may sit in laws or regulations, in private schemes, in collective bargaining agreements, or in a combination. Where coverage in a branch is inadequate, the Member must consider comparable benefits by other means. Members must cooperate, through bilateral or multilateral agreements, to maintain rights acquired or in the course of acquisition regardless of residence - the portability problem that arises when a seafarer works for foreign employers all his life. And each Member must provide fair and effective procedures for the settlement of disputes.

The critical allocation is in paragraph 3: complementary protection is owed by the State to all seafarers ordinarily resident in its territory. Social security therefore follows residence, not the flag - which is the opposite of most of the Convention and is the provision that matters most to a labour-supplying country.

Two later amendment sets reinforce this from the shipowner's side and were in force at this sitting: the 2014 amendments requiring financial security for abandonment and for contractual claims arising from death or long-term disability, and the 2018 amendments continuing wages and entitlements while a seafarer is held captive as a result of piracy or armed robbery.

5. b) India's position and framework

India is a party to the MLC and a major labour-supplying State, which makes Standard A4.5 paragraph 3 - the ordinary residence obligation - the operative provision for it, because a large proportion of Indian seafarers serve on foreign-flag ships and are therefore outside India's flag State chain altogether. The Convention takes effect in India through the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 and rules made under it, with the Directorate General of Shipping as competent authority, which inspects and certifies Indian ships and exercises port State control over foreign ships.

6. b) The Indian initiatives

  • Welfare board and welfare fund machinery for seafarers, financing hostels, medical facilities, recreation and shore welfare - the direct counterpart of Regulation 4.4.
  • The Seamen's Provident Fund, a contributory scheme providing an old-age and retirement benefit for seafarers within its scope.
  • Licensing and regulation of recruitment and placement services by the Administration, so that seafarers are recruited only through approved agencies and are not charged fees for employment.
  • The Indian National Database of Seafarers and the seafarer identity document scheme, which give every seafarer a verifiable identity and service record - the precondition for claiming any entitlement years later.
  • Medical examination standards and an approved-doctor system, supporting the medical-care and fitness limbs.
  • Online grievance redressal and e-governance channels through the Administration, for complaints about wages, contracts, abandonment and recruitment abuse.
  • Training capacity and its regulation, which is welfare in the practical sense that employability is what secures the income the other benefits are calculated on.

7. b) How they align with the MLC - and where they do not

The alignment is real and can be stated precisely. The welfare board machinery answers Regulation 4.4; the provident fund answers the old-age branch; the medical examination and approved-doctor system supports medical care; recruitment regulation answers Regulation 1.4; and the identity and database schemes make the portability duty in Standard A4.5 paragraph 8 administratively possible. Because a Member need only provide three of the nine branches at ratification, and because protection may be delivered through private schemes and collective agreements as well as legislation, a mixed system of this kind is squarely within what the Convention contemplates.

Three gaps are worth stating honestly, and they are what turns a list into an answer. First, unemployment benefit is among the hardest branches to deliver for a workforce engaged voyage by voyage, and the gap between contracts is precisely when a seafarer is most exposed. Second, the foreign-flag problem: the seafarer resident in India but employed on a foreign ship is protected by Regulation 4.5 through residence, yet the practical machinery - the shipowner, the P&I cover, the collective agreement - all sits abroad, so the State's obligation is easier to state than to enforce. Third, portability under paragraph 8 depends on bilateral and multilateral agreements, and rights acquired under a foreign employer are only as secure as the agreements that carry them home.

The fair conclusion is that India's initiatives satisfy the Convention's architecture - a minimum set of branches, delivered by mixed means, benchmarked against shore employment - while the progressive obligation in Regulation 4.5.2 remains genuinely unfinished, which is what that word is there to acknowledge.

Why this structure scores

Two limbs of eight. Limb (a) is countable and the marks are concentrated in one place: the nine branches and the three-branch minimum. Write the nine as a numbered list - never as prose - because that is how they are marked, and add the three-branch rule and Guideline B4.5's recommended three immediately afterwards. Limb (b) is evaluative: 'how do these initiatives align with the MLC's requirements' is a comparison, so every Indian initiative should be tied to the MLC provision it answers, and the answer must be willing to say where alignment is incomplete.

The single strongest move available in this question is to notice that Standard A4.5 paragraph 3 attaches the obligation to ORDINARY RESIDENCE, not to the flag. That is why limb (b) exists at all: India's duty to its seafarers does not depend on which flag they serve under. Say that once, and limb (b) stops being a list of schemes and becomes an argument.

The idea underneath the question

Almost the whole MLC allocates duties to the shipowner and enforces them through the flag. Social security cannot work that way. A pension, an invalidity benefit or a survivor's benefit is paid decades after the engagement ends, long after the shipowner and the flag have gone; and a seafarer typically works for a series of foreign employers with no continuous national employer at all. So Title 4 changes the connecting factor: the State of ordinary residence takes the responsibility. Once you see that, the three-branch minimum, the progressive obligation, the cooperation duty on portability and the whole Indian limb all follow.

Common mistakes

  • Giving eight branches, or ten. There are nine, and the ones dropped are usually family benefit and maternity benefit.
  • Saying a Member must provide all nine. It must provide at least three at ratification and work progressively towards comprehensive coverage.

Model written answer

1. The background - the gap the Convention was written to close

By the late 1990s the international regime for oil pollution was substantial but narrow. The 1992 CLC, backed by the 1992 Fund, applied only to persistent oil carried as cargo in bulk by a tanker. It said nothing about the far more common case of a bulk carrier, container ship or car carrier spilling its own fuel. A large modern non-tanker may carry several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel oil - more than the entire cargo of a small tanker - and a claimant faced with such a spill had no dedicated convention, no guaranteed insurer to sue and no certainty of recovery. The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage was adopted on 23 March 2001 and entered into force on 21 November 2008 to close exactly that gap.

2. Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture

Its relevance is that it completes the map. Taken with CLC and the Fund for tanker cargo, and the HNS Convention for hazardous and noxious substances, the Bunkers Convention brings the fuel of essentially the whole world fleet inside a regime of strict liability, compulsory insurance and direct action against the insurer. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and exclusive economic zone of a State Party, and to preventive measures wherever taken. Its structural weakness, dealt with at step 7, is that it stops there: it creates no limitation fund of its own and no second tier of compensation.

3. (a) Pollution damage

Bunkers article 1.9 defines pollution damage as loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination resulting from the escape or discharge of bunker oil, provided that compensation for impairment of the environment other than loss of profit from that impairment is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken; and it includes the costs of preventive measures and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Against CLC 92: the definition is deliberately the same in substance. The drafters copied the CLC formula so that the two regimes would be interpreted consistently and no claimant would be better or worse off according to which convention applied. The difference lies not in what pollution damage means but in which oil, from which ship, triggers it.

4. (b) Bunker oil

Bunkers article 1.5: any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of such oil. Three features matter. It expressly catches lubricating oil, which candidates routinely forget. It catches residues, so sludge and tank washings are within it. And unlike CLC it is not limited to persistent oil - a marine gas oil spill is bunker oil for these purposes.

Against CLC 92: CLC applies to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil - crude, fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil - carried as cargo, and to such oil in the bunkers of a ship carrying that cargo. So the same drum of lubricating oil is CLC oil in a laden tanker's bunkers and Bunkers Convention oil in a bulk carrier's sump.

5. (c) Time limits for claims

Bunkers article 8. Rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within three years from the date the damage occurred. In no case may an action be brought more than six years from the date of the incident which caused the damage. Where the incident consists of a series of occurrences, the six-year period runs from the date of the first occurrence.

Against CLC 92: substantially identical - CLC article VIII sets the same three-year and six-year periods on the same terms. This is one of the places where the drafters deliberately mirrored CLC. Note the wording: the right is extinguished, not merely barred, so it cannot be revived.

6. (d) Exclusions

Three different things are commonly called exclusions and the answer should separate them:

  1. Excluded damage - the CLC carve-out. Article 4.1: the Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable under it. This is the hinge that stops the two regimes overlapping. A laden tanker's bunker spill is a CLC matter, not a Bunkers matter.
  2. Excluded ships. Article 4.2: it does not apply to warships, naval auxiliaries or other State-owned or operated ships used on government non-commercial service, unless a State Party decides otherwise.
  3. The shipowner's defences - strictly not exclusions at all but a different route to no liability. Under article 3.3 the shipowner is not liable if the damage resulted from an act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection or a natural phenomenon of an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible character; was wholly caused by a third party's act or omission done with intent to cause damage; or was wholly caused by the negligence or wrongful act of a Government or authority responsible for maintaining lights or other navigational aids. Contributory fault of the claimant may also reduce or exonerate.

Against CLC 92: the defences are the same three, in the same terms. The excluded-ship provision is likewise mirrored. The CLC carve-out itself has no CLC equivalent, because CLC is the senior regime.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed question gives you your own headings - (a), (b), (c), (d) - so the structure is free. The mark is lost in a different way: by defining the four terms and never doing the comparison the stem asks for. Every one of steps 3 to 6 therefore ends with an explicit 'Against CLC 92' sentence. That single habit converts a half answer into a full one.

Step 7 exists because the most important difference does not live under any of the four definitions. It is structural - who is liable, what limit applies, and what stands behind it. Putting it in a table at the end banks a large number of comparison marks quickly.

The idea underneath the question

CLC is a cargo regime. Bunkers is a fuel regime. Everything else follows. CLC could afford exclusive channelling to the registered owner and a dedicated fund because the industry paying for the fund - oil receivers - is identifiable and finite. There is no equivalent constituency to levy for bunkers, so the Bunkers Convention got neither a fund nor its own limit, and had to widen the definition of 'shipowner' instead so that a claimant has more than one solvent defendant.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting lubricating oil from the definition of bunker oil. It is expressly in the text.
  • Saying bunker oil must be persistent. It need not be - that is a CLC requirement, not a Bunkers one.
  • Writing that liability is channelled to the registered owner. That is CLC. Under Bunkers it reaches the bareboat charterer, manager and operator as well.
  • Saying the Bunkers Convention has its own limits of liability. It does not - it borrows the LLMC.
  • Confusing the six-year long stop with the three-year period, or giving only one of the two.
  • Treating the shipowner's defences as if they were the article 4 exclusions. They are separate provisions doing separate work.
  • Forgetting the CLC carve-out - it is the single most important exclusion and the one that makes the system coherent.

Examiner traps

  • 'Whether or not compensation is payable under CLC.' Those words in article 4.1 matter. Even where the CLC claim fails, the Bunkers Convention does not step in. The claimant does not get a second bite.
  • The insurance thresholds are different units. Bunkers is 1,000 GT, a tonnage of the ship. CLC is 2,000 tonnes of oil carried, a quantity of cargo. Candidates frequently swap them.
  • 'Extinguished', not 'barred'. The right ceases to exist.
  • Being asked what happens after the LLMC limit is exhausted on a bunker spill. The honest answer is that the convention regime provides nothing further.
  • Being asked whether the Bunkers Convention applies on the high seas. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and EEZ - but to preventive measures wherever taken.

Understand this first

Every IMO convention creates reporting duties - tell the Organization about your casualties, your recognized organizations, your reception facilities, your fuel supply. For decades those reports arrived as paper, in whatever format the sending State chose, and once filed they were almost useless: nobody could search them, aggregate them or see a pattern in them. So each is paired with a duty on States, to promote compliant fuel and to provide adequate reception facilities. GISIS is where a ship reports that the shore side failed, and where enough such reports become evidence that a port has a real problem.

Model written answer

1. a) What GISIS is, and the problem it solves

The Global Integrated Shipping Information System is the IMO Secretariat's web-based platform for the information that Member States are required or invited to report under IMO instruments. Before it, that information arrived as paper circulars and correspondence: it was slow, inconsistently formatted, effectively unsearchable, and once filed it was of little use to anyone. Conventions imposed reporting duties without creating anywhere useful for the reports to go.

GISIS turns those duties into a single, structured, electronic repository. Its purpose is threefold - to collect what States must report, to disseminate it to those who need it, and to make it analysable so that the Organization can see patterns rather than incidents.

2. a) The components - the module structure

GISIS is organised into modules, each corresponding to a reporting obligation or an information need. The principal ones include:

  • Marine casualties and incidents - casualty reports and investigation reports;
  • Piracy and armed robbery against ships;
  • Port reception facilities - what exists at each port, and reports of alleged inadequacy;
  • Contact points - the national focal points a State must designate;
  • Ship and company particulars, built on the IMO identification number schemes;
  • Recognized organizations - which ROs each flag has authorized, and for what;
  • Status of multilateral conventions - who is party to what, and from when;
  • Port State control, and survey and certification;
  • Marine environment and MARPOL reporting, including fuel oil availability;
  • Maritime security and the condition assessment scheme.

The list is not exhaustive, and it should not be memorised as though it were fixed: modules are added and reorganised administratively as reporting needs change, without any instrument being amended.

3. a) Access, and how the data gets in

Content is divided between public and restricted. Much is openly accessible after free registration - convention status, ship particulars, recognized organizations, reception facility details - while operationally or commercially sensitive material is confined to Member State users. Data is entered and maintained by each State's designated focal points, with the Secretariat administering the system rather than authoring the content.

That division of labour is the system's strength and its weakness together: the data is authoritative because the reporting State owns it, and it is incomplete wherever a State does not report.

4. a) How it facilitates data sharing among Member States

  • One repository, one format. A State reports once, electronically, in a defined structure, instead of circulating documents bilaterally.
  • Immediacy. What is entered is visible at once, rather than after a publication cycle.
  • Searchable and analysable. Data can be filtered, aggregated and exported, which is what allows the Organization's own sub-committees to analyse casualties and identify systemic causes rather than reading individual reports.
  • It feeds regulatory work. Casualty analysis informs amendments; port State control data supports targeting; convention status underpins every enforcement decision a port State takes.
  • Transparency and accountability. Because the record is visible, a State's failure to report is visible too - which is itself a compliance mechanism, and is used in the Organization's member State audit work.
  • It directs assistance. Gaps revealed by the data show the Organization where technical cooperation and capacity building are actually needed.

5. b) Reporting on compliant fuel oil

MARPOL Annex VI regulation 18, 'Fuel oil availability and quality', is the hook. It sits behind the sulphur limits - 0.50% m/m globally since 1 January 2020 and 0.10% m/m in emission control areas - and it does two things that reach the Organization.

  • Parties must promote the availability of compliant fuel oil and inform the Organization about it, including where it is and is not obtainable. That is what makes a global limit administrable.
  • Where a ship cannot obtain compliant fuel oil despite best efforts, it records the fact in a fuel oil non-availability report, notifying its Administration and the competent authority of the port of destination, and the information reaches the Organization.

GISIS is where those two streams are collected and made visible. The value is that an individual non-availability report is an excuse, but many reports naming the same port is evidence of a supply failure - and only a central register can convert one into the other. It also protects the honest operator: a port State officer can see whether non-availability at that port is corroborated, and the shipowner is not left arguing an unverifiable case.

6. b) Reporting inadequacy of port reception facilities

MARPOL Annex VI regulation 17, 'Reception facilities', and its counterparts in the other Annexes, require Parties to ensure the provision of adequate reception facilities. The port reception facilities module serves that obligation from both ends.

  • It holds a database of the facilities available at each port - waste types accepted, capacities and contacts - so that a ship can plan its discharges before arrival rather than discovering the position alongside.
  • It carries the alleged inadequacy reporting mechanism. Where a facility is unavailable, insufficient, unreasonably delayed or effectively inaccessible, the master reports the alleged inadequacy; the report goes to the flag Administration, which forwards it to the port State and to the Organization; and the port State is expected to investigate and respond, with the exchange recorded.

The word alleged is deliberate and is worth noticing: the system records a claim and a response, not a finding. It is a structured complaint channel, not an adjudication.

7. b) What the reported data is actually useful for

Both limbs of the printed question turn on one idea. A prohibition addressed to the ship is only enforceable, and only fair, if the shore side delivers. A ship may be required to burn compliant fuel only if compliant fuel can be bought; a ship may be prohibited from discharging waste at sea only if there is somewhere ashore to land it. In each case the obligation on the shipowner is matched by an obligation on States - to promote fuel availability, and to provide adequate reception facilities - and GISIS is the feedback channel that shows whether those State-side obligations are being met.

So the usefulness runs at four levels. For the ship, it is operational: plan the waste discharge and the bunkering with knowledge of what is actually there. For the company, it is evidential: a documented report is the defence against a deficiency, and it is what turns a commercial dispute into a regulatory record. For the port and flag States, it creates an obligation that can be seen to be discharged or not. And for the Organization, aggregation is the whole point - it converts scattered individual complaints into evidence of a systemic shortage at a named port or in a named region, which is what justifies technical cooperation, guidance or, in the end, an amendment. Its limitation follows from the same design: GISIS reports only what is reported, so under-reporting appears in the data as compliance.

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) has two demands - the key functions and components, and how it facilitates data sharing. Give the modules as a list, because a list of modules is counted, and then give the data-sharing benefits as a second list. Two lists, eight marks, no prose padding.

Step 7 is the discussion, and it is what lifts the limb above description.

The printed question has a looseness worth handling deliberately

Limb (b) says 'this module', in the singular, and then asks about two different things - compliant fuel oil, and inadequacy of port reception facilities. These are handled in different parts of GISIS: fuel oil availability sits with the MARPOL and marine environment reporting, and reception facilities have their own module. Do not try to force them into one. Answer both, and note in a clause that they are separate reporting streams serving the same purpose. Recognising the looseness and dealing with it cleanly reads as competence, not pedantry.

The one idea that unifies limb (b)

Every obligation MARPOL places on the ship has a matching obligation on the State, and GISIS is how you find out whether the State kept its side.

  • The ship must burn compliant fuel - so Parties must promote its availability, and non-availability must be reportable.
  • The ship must not discharge waste at sea - so Parties must provide adequate reception facilities, and inadequacy must be reportable.

State that once and the whole of limb (b) becomes an argument rather than two descriptions. It is also the honest answer to 'why does the Organization bother collecting this?'

Common mistakes

  • Describing GISIS as a ship-tracking or AIS-type system. It is a reporting and information system, not an operational traffic picture.
  • Saying it is entirely public. It is mixed - some modules open after free registration, others restricted to Member State users.
  • Saying IMO writes the content. Member States enter it through designated focal points; the Secretariat administers the platform.
  • Reciting a memorised list of modules as though it were fixed and complete. The set changes administratively, and saying so is safer and more accurate than a confident wrong list.
  • Answering limb (b) without naming a single regulation. Regulation 18 for fuel oil availability and quality, regulation 17 for reception facilities - both MARPOL Annex VI, chapter 3.
  • Treating a non-availability report as a waiver or exemption. It is a report of fact made after best efforts; it does not authorise burning non-compliant fuel as a matter of course, and it invites scrutiny rather than ending it.
  • Forgetting that a reception facility report is of an ALLEGED inadequacy, and that the port State is expected to respond.

Examiner traps

  • Who enters the data? The Member State's designated focal point - not IMO, and not the ship.
  • What is the sulphur limit that makes fuel availability an issue? 0.50% m/m globally from 1 January 2020, and 0.10% m/m in emission control areas.
  • Where does a non-availability report go? To the ship's Administration and to the competent authority of the port of destination - and from there onward to the Organization. Not to IMO directly from the ship.
  • Who reports an inadequate reception facility, and to whom? The master, through the flag Administration, which forwards to the port State and the Organization.
  • Does GISIS decide anything? No. It records claims, responses and reported facts. It is not an adjudicating body.
  • What is its principal weakness? It reflects only what States report, so a gap in the data can mean either that there is no problem or that nobody reported one - and those are not the same thing.

Understand this first

Fouling costs fuel, so ships are painted with something that stops marine growth. There are only two ideas: keep releasing a biocide in a controlled way, or make the surface so slippery that nothing can hold on. The four named paint types are variations on those two ideas.

Model written answer

1. a) The Convention - what it does and why

The International Convention on the Control of Harmful Anti-fouling Systems on Ships, 2001 - the AFS Convention - entered into force on 17 September 2008. Its purpose is to prohibit or restrict anti-fouling systems that are harmful to the marine environment while allowing ships to continue to control fouling.

It exists because of organotin compounds, principally tributyl tin. TBT was an outstandingly effective antifouling biocide and an outstandingly damaging pollutant: it persists, it accumulates in sediment and in the food chain, and it causes imposex and shell deformity in shellfish at concentrations of a few parts per trillion, with severe damage recorded around ports and shellfish grounds.

The Convention applies to ships entitled to fly the flag of a Party and to ships operating under a Party's authority. The substances actually controlled are listed in Annex 1, and that list can be added to by amendment - which is exactly what has since happened. Compliance is enforced through survey and certification and through inspection by port State officers, who may examine the certificate or declaration and may take a sample of the coating.

2. The controls - organotins and cybutryne

Organotin compounds acting as biocides in anti-fouling systems:

  • no ship may apply or re-apply them - effective 1 January 2003;
  • from 1 January 2008 a ship must either not bear such a system, or must carry a coating that forms a barrier to it leaching from the underlying non-compliant system.

Cybutryne was added to Annex 1 by amendment adopted on 17 June 2021, which entered into force on 1 January 2023. It was therefore fully in force at the date of this paper, and an answer that describes only the tin position is incomplete.

  • All ships: no application or re-application of an anti-fouling system containing cybutryne, from 1 January 2023.
  • Ships already bearing cybutryne in the external coating layer on that date must either remove the system or apply a coating forming a barrier to it leaching - at the next scheduled renewal of the anti-fouling system after 1 January 2023, and no later than 60 months after the last application of a cybutryne-containing system.
  • Limited exclusions apply, including fixed and floating platforms, FSUs and FPSOs constructed before that date which have not since been in dry dock, ships not engaged on international voyages, and ships under 400 GT on international voyages where the coastal State accepts it.

The practical lesson is that 'tin-free' does not mean 'unregulated'. Cybutryne was a common booster biocide in tin-free paints, so the second wave of control fell on the very coatings that replaced TBT.

3. a) Documents and records to demonstrate compliance

What a surveyor or port State control officer will expect to be produced:

  • International Anti-Fouling System Certificate (IAFS Certificate) - for ships of 400 gross tonnage and above engaged in international voyages, issued by or on behalf of the Administration after the required survey.
  • The Record of Anti-Fouling Systems - attached to and forming part of the certificate. It records the system applied, the facility that applied it and the date, and which compliance option applies to each controlled substance: never applied, applied and since removed, applied and covered with a sealer coat, or not in the external coating layer.
  • Declaration on Anti-Fouling System - for ships of 24 metres or more in length but less than 400 gross tonnage engaged in international voyages, signed by the owner or the owner's authorized agent.
  • Supporting documentation for that Declaration - a paint receipt or contractor's invoice, or equivalent evidence of what was applied.
  • Manufacturer's technical documentation - product data sheets and declarations confirming the composition of the system and the absence of controlled substances, together with application records: product, batch, area, dry film thickness and dates.
  • Survey records and reports - of the initial survey, and of any survey carried out when the anti-fouling system is changed or replaced, with the certificate endorsed or reissued accordingly.
  • Record of any sealer or barrier coat applied over a non-compliant system, naming the facility and the date, since that is what the certificate itself must state.
  • Dry-docking and planned maintenance records evidencing the last application and the next scheduled renewal - these establish the 60-month position for cybutryne.

One point specific to this period: since the cybutryne amendment the certificate must be in the amended model form, which carries cybutryne as well as organotins. Affected ships were given a transitional period to obtain a certificate in that form, and that transition was still running at the date of this paper.

4. b) Controlled depletion polymer (CDP)

A soluble matrix coating, traditionally rosin-based. Seawater slowly dissolves the binder, and as it dissolves it releases the biocide dispersed within it - essentially a refinement of the old soluble-matrix paints.

Because release depends on physical dissolution rather than a controlled chemical reaction, the release rate is not well regulated and tails off with time, and a thick, uneven leached layer builds up on the hull. That layer increases roughness and drag, so fuel performance degrades over the docking cycle. It is the cheapest of the four, easy to apply and easy to overcoat, and is generally suited to shorter drydock intervals and lower-activity ships.

5. Self-polishing copolymer (SPC)

The important difference is chemical. The biocide is chemically bonded into an acrylic copolymer backbone rather than merely mixed into a soluble binder. In seawater the surface layer hydrolyses, releasing the biocide at a controlled, near-linear rate, and the spent binder then becomes soluble and erodes away - so the coating polishes itself smooth as it works.

The consequences are a thin and stable leached layer, a predictable release rate that can be engineered to the ship's speed and activity, longer service life - commonly to a full five-year docking cycle - and better sustained hydrodynamic performance because the surface stays smooth. It costs more than CDP. Modern tin-free SPCs use copper, zinc or silyl acrylate chemistries in place of the original tin ones.

6. Hybrid TBT-free paints

A blend of the two technologies above - rosin and copolymer chemistry combined in one binder - intended to capture some of the controlled polishing behaviour of SPC at closer to CDP cost.

The result is intermediate on every axis: more predictable release and less leached-layer build-up than pure CDP, but less precisely controlled than a true SPC; intermediate service life; intermediate price. It is a sensible compromise for moderate trading patterns and medium docking intervals. Note that 'hybrid' is a commercial label rather than a defined term, and formulations sold under it vary considerably.

7. Fouling release coatings

These are fundamentally different: they contain no biocide at all and work by physics rather than by poisoning. A silicone or fluoropolymer elastomer gives a surface of very low surface energy and very high smoothness, to which fouling organisms cannot attach firmly. Such growth as does settle is sheared off by the water flow once the ship is moving.

Strengths: the lowest drag of any of the four, hence the best fuel performance, and no biocide released into the sea, which is the strongest environmental position. Limitations are real: performance depends on regular activity and adequate speed, so the ship fouls during prolonged idle periods or slow steaming; the film is mechanically soft and easily damaged and is difficult to repair; it requires a full tie-coat system and careful surface preparation; and it carries the highest initial cost.

8. Choosing between them

The choice follows the ship's trading pattern, service speed, proportion of idle time and intended drydock interval. A fast, regularly operating ship on a five-year cycle rewards a fouling release or a good SPC; a slower ship with irregular employment and long port stays is better served by a biocidal SPC or hybrid; CDP remains defensible where the docking interval is short and first cost dominates. Whatever is selected must be free of the substances controlled under Annex 1 - now including cybutryne - and must be documented so that compliance can be demonstrated.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) contains a trap of drafting. It says 'briefly discuss' the Convention and then 'state ALL documents and records'. The word all converts the second half into a checklist, and a checklist is marked by counting items. So the discussion is kept genuinely brief and the documents are written as an explicit list, one line each. A flowing paragraph that mentions the certificate in passing will lose marks that a list would have collected.

Limb (b) names four technologies at two marks each. Each is given mechanism, performance and limitation in that order. Step 8 is not asked for explicitly, but 'discuss various alternatives' invites a comparison, and a short selection paragraph is what distinguishes an engineer's answer from a paint catalogue.

The date of this paper - the trap runs BACKWARDS

The intake flag for this question said STABLE / LOW and it was wrong. The usual temporal danger is importing something that came later. Here the danger is the opposite: leaving out something that had already happened.

Cybutryne controls entered into force on 1 January 2023 - twenty months before this sitting. Much of the study material on this topic was written when the AFS Convention meant TBT and nothing else, and an answer built from that material describes only the tin position and is incomplete for September 2024. This is worth internalising as a general point: an old paper's correct answer is not always the old answer.

Two further details are specific to this sitting. The 60-month rule was live - ships bearing cybutryne had to remove it or seal it over at the next scheduled renewal after 1 January 2023 and no later than 60 months after the last application. And the transitional period for obtaining a certificate in the amended form was still running, so at this date a ship could legitimately still hold a certificate in the older form.

The one idea that organises limb (b)

There are only two ways to keep a hull clean, and the four named products are variations on them. Either you release a biocide, or you make the surface impossible to grip.

  • Release a biocide - and then the only question is how well you control the release. CDP dissolves and controls it poorly. SPC hydrolyses and controls it well. Hybrid sits between them. That single axis explains cost, service life, leached-layer thickness and drag for all three.
  • Make the surface impossible to grip - fouling release. No biocide, lowest drag, but it depends on the ship moving, and the film is soft and expensive.

If you can state that framing in one sentence at the start of limb (b), the four descriptions write themselves and you will not confuse CDP with SPC, which is the commonest error in this topic.

Common mistakes

  • Answering the Convention as a TBT-only instrument. Cybutryne was controlled from 1 January 2023 and was in force at this sitting.
  • Giving the certificate but forgetting the Record of Anti-Fouling Systems, which is attached to and forms part of it, and the Declaration for the ship between 24 m and 400 GT.
  • Confusing CDP and SPC. CDP dissolves; SPC hydrolyses and polishes. The biocide is mixed into a CDP binder and chemically bound into an SPC backbone.
  • Calling fouling release paint an antifouling in the biocidal sense. It contains no biocide; that is its defining feature.
  • Presenting fouling release as simply the best option. It needs the ship to move, and it is soft, costly and hard to repair.
  • Writing the organotin dates the wrong way round. 1 January 2003 is when application stopped; 1 January 2008 is when ships had to stop bearing it or seal it over.
  • Treating 'briefly discuss' as permission to write half the answer on the history of TBT.

Examiner traps

  • 'All documents and records'. The examiner is counting. Include the Record, the Declaration, the supporting paint documentation and the survey records - not just the certificate.
  • Remove OR seal. For both organotins and cybutryne the requirement is a choice: remove the system, or apply a coating forming a barrier over it. Candidates routinely give only removal.
  • The tonnage and length thresholds. 400 GT and above gets a certificate; 24 m or more but under 400 GT gets a declaration. Expect to be asked what a 30 m, 300 GT ship carries.
  • Who signs the Declaration. The owner or the owner's authorized agent - not the master, and not a surveyor.
  • The 60-month rule. It is not a flat deadline: it is the next scheduled renewal after 1 January 2023, subject to an outer limit of 60 months from the last application.
  • Port State sampling. An officer may do more than read the certificate - a sample of the coating may be taken.

Uncertainty and source limits

MEPC.331(76) was read at source from the IMO's own published copy, so the cybutryne controls, the 60-month rule, the exclusions, the replaced Annex 4 regulation 2(3) and the amended certificate form are primary. The AFS Convention's own text was not read this session: the organotin dates, the 400 GT and 24 m thresholds and the Declaration requirement rest on authoritative restatements of the Convention, including national implementing legislation. MIW holds no licensed copy of the Convention.

The four coating types are commercial technology categories, not defined regulatory terms, and 'hybrid' in particular is used inconsistently across manufacturers. No manufacturer, product name, price, biocide release rate or fuel-saving percentage is quoted anywhere - those figures are installation-specific and vendor-sourced, and false precision would be worse than the qualitative statement.

Understand this first

A propeller does not convert all the power it is given into useful thrust. Some of what it leaves behind is axial - water thrown astern faster than it needs to be - and some is rotational: the race leaves the disc spinning, and that spin is wasted energy. Behind the propeller of a single-screw ship sits a large flat object in exactly that spinning race: the rudder. Every device in this question does one of two things with it - it either recovers some of the rotational energy and turns it into forward thrust, or it stops wasting more, by removing a vortex or a separation the rudder itself creates. Read the four limbs that way and they stop being four unrelated gadgets.

Model written answer

1. Where the energy to be recovered is

A screw propeller working behind a hull typically converts only about 65 to 72 per cent of delivered power into useful thrust. The losses are of three kinds: axial, in the sternward momentum of the race; rotational, in the swirl the blades impart to it; and frictional and vortex losses, principally the hub vortex shed from the boss. The rotational component alone is commonly of the order of 8 to 12 per cent of delivered power on a single-screw merchant ship.

The rudder is the only large body standing in that race. It is therefore both an opportunity - the swirl arrives at it already rotating, so a correctly oriented surface there sees an angle of attack for nothing - and a liability, because a plain rudder behind a bare boss adds drag and lets the hub vortex run on unchecked. The four devices below exploit the first and reduce the second.

2. (a) Rudder thrust fins

A rudder thrust fin is a horizontal foil, usually fitted as a pair port and starboard, mounted on the rudder at or near the level of the propeller shaft, inside the slipstream. Because the race is rotating, the flow arrives at the fin with a tangential component: upward on one side of the shaft and downward on the other. Each fin is set at the angle that turns that oncoming flow into lift, and because the flow is inclined, the lift vector is tilted forward. Its horizontal component is thrust, generated with no additional power.

  • The two fins are set at different angles, or in mirror image, because the swirl direction differs above and below the shaft.
  • Gains quoted in industry literature are typically of the order of 2 to 5 per cent in delivered power, and are strongly dependent on span, chord and angle of attack.
  • The angle is fixed at build. The device is optimised for one draught and speed and gives less, or in extreme off-design conditions nothing, elsewhere.
  • It is a source of added appendage drag when not loaded, and a candidate for cavitation erosion if it sits too close to the blade tips.

3. (b) Asymmetric and twisted rudders

Here the recovery is built into the rudder itself rather than added to it. On a single-screw ship the propeller turns one way, so the race strikes the upper half of the rudder from one side and the lower half from the other. A conventional symmetrical rudder, aligned with the centreline, therefore meets the flow at an angle of attack of the wrong sign over half its span, producing side force, drag and early cavitation.

An asymmetric rudder cures this by making the two halves different. In the developed form - the twisted leading edge rudder - the leading edge is twisted in opposite directions above and below the shaft line, so that each half is aligned with the swirl it actually meets.

  • The rudder becomes a swirl-recovery surface: the side force on the two halves is turned into a net forward component.
  • It reduces drag and cavitation, and with it rudder erosion and propeller-excited vibration.
  • It is handed - built for one direction of rotation - so it is a design decision, not a universal retrofit.
  • Because it changes the rudder body rather than adding to it, it carries no added appendage and its benefit degrades more gracefully off design than a fixed fin.

4. (c) Rudder bulbs

The propeller boss sheds a strong hub vortex, and behind a bare boss the flow separates, leaving a low-pressure wake that pulls the ship astern. A rudder bulb is a streamlined body of revolution fitted to the rudder leading edge, coaxial with the shaft and sized and positioned so that it forms an almost continuous body with the propeller hub cap, closing the gap between them.

  • It suppresses or displaces the hub vortex, removing a pure loss.
  • It restores pressure behind the boss by giving the flow a body to close around instead of separating, which is a genuine reduction in resistance rather than a recovery of energy.
  • The diameter and the axial gap govern the result; too large or too far aft and the bulb adds more drag than it saves.
  • It is most often fitted together with fins, the bulb conditioning the flow the fins work in, so the two are designed as one installation and their savings are not simply additive.

5. (d) Grim vane wheels

The vane wheel, patented by Otto Grim in 1966, is the most ambitious of the four and the only one that moves. It is a freely rotating bladed wheel mounted on the shaft line immediately aft of the propeller, of larger diameter than the propeller, with no mechanical connection to it or to the shaft. It runs at its own speed, typically around a third to a half of propeller revolutions, driven entirely by the water.

Its cleverness is that one blade does two jobs at different radii:

  1. The inner portion, standing inside the propeller race, works as a turbine. It extracts energy from the swirl and axial velocity of the slipstream, and that is what drives the wheel round.
  2. The outer portion, standing outside the race in undisturbed water, works as a propeller. Driven by the torque the inner part has extracted, it produces additional thrust over a larger disc area and therefore at higher efficiency.

The wheel thus takes wasted energy from a small, fast, disturbed stream and returns it as thrust in a large, slow, clean one. Gains of the order of 5 to 10 per cent were claimed and some were demonstrated. In service the device proved difficult: the long, thin, unsupported blades of the outer portion are fatigue-loaded and blade loss and shedding occurred; performance falls away in a seaway and off design, when the wheel can windmill unhelpfully; and it adds a rotating component aft of the propeller with its own bearing and clearance problems. It is now of more importance as a principle - and as an examination question - than as a device in general service.

6. Whether a claimed gain is real on this ship

All four claims are hull-specific. A device recovers only what the propeller behind that hull actually wastes, so the gain depends on the wake field, block coefficient, propeller loading, draught and service speed. Three consequences follow, and they are where the marks are at this level. First, a quoted percentage means nothing without the condition it was measured in, and model-scale results require a scale-effect correction before they can be believed at full scale. Second, devices interact: a bulb changes the flow the fins see, so savings must be assessed for the installation as a whole. Third, the real case is the operating profile - a ship that spends its life slow-steaming at part draught will not realise a gain optimised for design condition, and the payback calculation must be built on the actual profile, on class approval of the attachment and its structure, and on the effect on manoeuvring, which a rudder-mounted device necessarily touches.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has done the structuring for you. Four devices are named and each carries 4 marks, so the answer is four equal blocks and nothing else can be substituted for them. The single most common way to lose marks here is to write a good general essay on energy-saving devices - pre-swirl stators, ducts, propeller boss cap fins, air lubrication - none of which is asked for. Answer the four printed limbs.

The one idea that unlocks all four

Rotational loss. The propeller leaves the race spinning, and that spin is energy already paid for and thrown away. Two of these devices recover it - the fin and the twisted rudder turn swirl into forward force, and the vane wheel takes it mechanically. Two stop further waste - the bulb removes the hub vortex and the separation behind the boss. If you can say which of the two categories a device is in, you can reconstruct its explanation from first principles.

Keep recovery and drag reduction apart

  • Recovery - thrust fins, asymmetric and twisted rudders, vane wheels. They convert energy already in the water into thrust.
  • Drag and vortex reduction - rudder bulbs. They do not recover anything; they stop a loss from happening.
  • The bulb is often described loosely as recovering hub-vortex energy. The defensible statement is that it suppresses the vortex and restores pressure behind the boss. Say that and the point is safe either way.

Common mistakes

  • Describing an asymmetric rudder as a rudder offset to one side of the centreline. The asymmetry is in the section and the twist about the shaft line, matching the opposite swirl above and below.
  • Saying the Grim vane wheel is geared to or driven by the shaft. It is entirely free-running; there is no mechanical connection, and that is the whole point.
  • Making the vane wheel the same diameter as the propeller. It is larger, and it must be, because the outer portion has to stand outside the race to act as a propeller.
  • Quoting a saving as a hard number. Every figure is conditional on the hull and the operating condition.
  • Adding the percentages of several devices together.
  • Ignoring manoeuvring. Anything bolted to a rudder changes the way she steers, and class will want to see the structure.

Examiner traps

  • "Currently in use" in the stem. The Grim vane wheel is the odd one out - historically important, largely not in service. Say so rather than pretending it is common; an answer that quietly treats all four as current practice is weaker, not stronger.
  • Limb (d) is worth the same 4 marks as the others even though it is the least used. Do not short it.
  • The stem says the devices contribute to propulsion effectiveness, not to steering. Manoeuvring belongs in the closing judgement, not in place of the hydrodynamics.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which way does the race rotate behind a right-handed propeller, and which way must the upper fin be set?
  • Your ship has a rudder bulb. What would you look for at drydocking?
  • Why is a vane wheel larger than the propeller?
  • The owner is offered a 4 per cent saving from a thrust fin. What would you ask before agreeing?
  • How would you verify a claimed saving after installation?

Work outward from the shaft centreline and the four devices fall into place in order: bulb at the hub, fins just clear of it, the rudder body itself around them, and the vane wheel beyond the propeller disc. Then attach the verb: bulb fills, fin lifts, rudder twists, wheel turns.

Regulation and source map

Uncertainty, jurisdiction and currency

Nothing in this question is time-sensitive: the hydrodynamics is unchanged and all four devices long predate this sitting. The uncertainty is of a different kind and is worth being honest about. No manufacturer datasheet, model test or sea-trial record was read in preparing this answer, so every percentage given is an industry-quoted range with its dependency stated, not a verified figure for any ship. MIW has also not established how many vane-wheel installations remain in service, so the answer says the device is of principally historical importance without asserting that none survives.
