Understand this first

Regulation traditionally told a builder what to do: this plate thickness, that scantling. The trouble with a prescriptive rule is that it freezes yesterday's design and gives a designer no credit for a better idea. Goal-based standards invert that. The regulator states the outcome the ship must achieve, and for how long she must go on achieving it; somebody else decides how. That produces a layered structure - at the top a plain statement of what a safe ship is, beneath it the things she must functionally be able to do in order to be that, and beneath that the detailed rules a classification society writes and must then prove actually deliver the goal above them. That proof step is the real novelty: class rules are no longer simply accepted, they are audited upward against the goal. Everything else in the question - who does what, and the file handed over at delivery - follows from the fact that the goal and the rule are now written by different people.

Model written answer

1. What goal-based standards are, and why they exist

A prescriptive rule tells a designer what to build. A goal-based standard states what the ship must achieve and leaves the detailed rules to be written beneath it, then verifies that those rules deliver the goal. The International Goal-based Ship Construction Standards for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers were adopted by resolution MSC.287(87) on 20 May 2010. They are given force through SOLAS regulation II-1/3-10, adopted by resolution MSC.290(87) and, with the definition at II-1/2.28, in force from 1 January 2012. They apply to bulk carriers and oil tankers of 150 m in length and above for which the building contract is placed on or after 1 July 2016.

The reason for the change of approach is that prescriptive scantling rules had grown from experience of past failures, differed between societies, and gave the Organization no way of saying what level of safety they were actually delivering. GBS fixes the required outcome at IMO level and makes the rule-writer demonstrate conformity with it.

2. The tier structure - and how many tiers the instrument actually contains

This is the discrimination the question rewards, and it is where most published summaries go wrong. Section 1.5 of MSC.287(87) records that the Committee agreed to use a five-tier system:

  • Tier I - Goals
  • Tier II - Functional requirements
  • Tier III - Verification of conformity
  • Tier IV - Rules and regulations for ship design and construction
  • Tier V - Industry practices and standards

But section 3, STRUCTURE, states that "These Standards consist of the following three tiers: Tier I, Tier II, Tier III." The framework is five tiers; the instrument itself contains three. Tiers IV and V are the class and national rules and the industry practice that sit beneath the Standards and are measured against them - they are not part of the Standards. Say both halves of that and the point is made.

3. Tier I and Tier II - the goal and the functional requirements

Tier I is a single goal: ships shall be designed and constructed for a specified design life to be safe and environmentally friendly when properly operated and maintained under the specified operating and environmental conditions, in intact and specified damage conditions, throughout their life. Its sub-goals require adequate strength, integrity and stability; construction from materials permitting environmentally acceptable recycling; safe access, escape, inspection and maintenance; specified operating and environmental conditions; and a specified design life.

Tier II contains fifteen functional requirements, and they fall into four lifecycle groups, which is the natural skeleton for this part of the answer:

  • Design - design life (not less than 25 years); environmental conditions (North Atlantic); structural strength; fatigue life; residual strength; protection against corrosion, including coating life and corrosion addition; structural redundancy; watertight and weathertight integrity; human element considerations; design transparency.
  • Construction - construction quality procedures; survey during construction.
  • In-service considerations - survey and maintenance; structural accessibility.
  • Recycling considerations - recycling.

4. Why this enhances safety and environmental protection

Safety is raised because the goal is expressed over the whole life rather than at delivery: a minimum design life of 25 years, North Atlantic environmental conditions as the design basis, explicit fatigue and residual-strength requirements, and structural redundancy so that a single failure is not immediately catastrophic. Requiring structural accessibility and design transparency means the ship can actually be inspected and maintained, and that the assumptions behind the scantlings survive the designer.

Environmental protection is not an afterthought bolted on to a safety instrument. It is written into the Tier I goal itself - the ship must be environmentally friendly, must be built of materials that allow environmentally acceptable recycling, and carries a Tier II functional requirement for recycling. Coating life and corrosion addition also matter environmentally, because the loss of a tanker or bulk carrier through corrosion-driven structural failure is a pollution event before it is anything else.

5. How conformity is verified

Tier III is the verification layer. Under section 6.1, the rules of an organization recognized by an Administration in accordance with SOLAS regulation XI-1/1, or the national rules of an Administration, shall be verified as conforming to the goals and functional requirements. Verification is done at IMO, not by the rule-writer certifying itself: an International GBS Audit Team established by the Secretary-General evaluates the submission and reports to the Maritime Safety Committee for approval.

The guidelines used are the current revision, not the original. Conformity is now assessed against the Revised guidelines for verification of conformity with goal-based ship construction standards, resolution MSC.454(100). Citing the original MSC.296(87) as the operative guideline is a currency error.

6. Who does what - the stakeholders

  • IMO / MSC - owns Tiers I to III, appoints the audit teams and approves the verification outcome.
  • The Administration - recognizes organizations under SOLAS XI-1/1, may submit its own national rules for verification, and remains responsible for the flag State duty however much it delegates.
  • Classification societies as recognized organizations - write the Tier IV rules, submit them for GBS verification, and carry out survey during construction under Tier II. Collectively, through IACS, they produced the Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers as the harmonised Tier IV instrument.
  • Ship designers - deliver the design life, the human element considerations and the design transparency the Standards require.
  • Shipbuilders - work to the construction quality procedures, accept survey during construction, and compile the Ship Construction File.
  • Shipowners and operators - keep the ship to the assumptions the design was built on, and maintain and update the Ship Construction File through the ship's life.

7. The Ship Construction File

The ship-level output of the whole scheme is the Ship Construction File, required by SOLAS regulation II-1/3-10.2.1, with the content addressed by MSC.1/Circ.1343. It is provided at delivery, kept on board and/or ashore, and updated through the ship's life so that the information needed for safe operation, maintenance, survey, repair and emergency response stays available. It is the practical answer to design transparency: without it, the reasoning behind the structure is lost the moment the yard closes the file.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs of eight marks each, and they ask different things. Limb (a) is concept plus significance - so it needs the tier structure, the goal and functional requirements, and then a deliberate paragraph that answers why this makes ships safer and cleaner. Limb (b) is process plus people, and the examiner has named three of the stakeholders for you. A very common failure is to write one long essay on what GBS are and never reach the verification process or the stakeholder roles, forfeiting half the question. Sections 1 to 4 are limb (a); sections 5 to 7 are limb (b).

The tier count is the discriminator

Almost every summary of GBS states a single number of tiers, and they disagree with one another. The instrument settles it, and it says two things that are both true: the Committee agreed a five-tier system (section 1.5), and the Standards themselves consist of three tiers (section 3). An automated summary of this very resolution asserted a "four-tier hierarchical framework", which is neither. If you write "five tiers, of which the Standards contain the first three, with class and national rules at Tier IV and industry practice at Tier V", you have said something no summary-reader can say, and it is correct.

Goal-based is not the same as non-prescriptive

GBS does not abolish prescriptive rules - it subordinates them. Tier IV is still a thick book of scantling rules, and a ship is still built to it. What changed is that the rule book must now be demonstrated, at IMO, to deliver a goal that IMO wrote. Candidates who describe GBS as "performance-based instead of prescriptive rules" have half the idea; the sharper statement is that GBS puts a verified goal above the prescriptive rules.

Common mistakes

  • Giving a single tier count without saying whether it is the framework or the instrument.
  • Citing MSC.296(87) as the verification guideline. It has been superseded; the operative revision is MSC.454(100).
  • Saying GBS applies to all ships. It applies to bulk carriers and oil tankers of 150 m and above, by building-contract date.
  • Confusing the adopting resolutions: MSC.287(87) adopts the Standards, MSC.290(87) adopts the SOLAS regulation that makes them mandatory.
  • Treating GBS as purely structural. The Tier I goal expressly requires the ship to be environmentally friendly and recyclable.
  • Forgetting the Ship Construction File, which is the only part of GBS a serving engineer actually handles.

Examiner traps

"Who verifies that a classification society's rules meet the goals?" Not the society, and not the flag State alone - an International GBS Audit Team established by the IMO Secretary-General, reporting to the MSC for approval. The society cannot self-certify conformity.

"Is a ship built to class rules automatically GBS-compliant?" Only if those rules have been verified as conforming and the ship falls within the application. The chain is: verified Tier IV rules, applied to a ship of the right type, size and contract date, with a Ship Construction File delivered.

"What is the design life?" Not less than 25 years, with North Atlantic environmental conditions as the design basis. These two numbers are the ones most often asked and most often missed.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Name the four lifecycle groups of the Tier II functional requirements. Design, construction, in-service considerations, recycling.
  • What is the Ship Construction File and where is it kept? SOLAS II-1/3-10.2.1; on board and/or ashore, updated through the ship's life.
  • Which regulation governs recognition of the organization whose rules are verified? SOLAS XI-1/1.

Uncertainty and currency

The Standards, the SOLAS regulation and the tier structure were read in the adopting resolution and are settled. Two things are not fully closed. First, MSC.454(100) is identified as the operative verification revision but no check has been made for a further revision adopted after MSC 100 - that is recorded as a currency check rather than asserted as final. Second, MIW holds no licensed consolidated SOLAS edition, so the text of II-1/3-10 and II-1/2.28 rests on the adopting resolutions and authoritative secondary reproduction rather than on a licensed consolidated text. Neither affects the substance of the answer, and both are stated rather than hidden. The outcome of the initial GBS verification audits was not established from primary documents and is deliberately not claimed.

Model written answer

1. What port State control is

Port State control is the inspection of foreign ships in national ports to verify that the condition of the ship and its equipment, and the competence and manning of the crew, comply with the requirements of international conventions, and that the ship is operated in accordance with them. It is a safety net, not the primary line of defence: the primary duty to ensure compliance rests with the flag State, and port State control exists because some flag States do not discharge it effectively. It does not transfer that duty, and it does not certify a ship as fit.

It is exercised in practice through regional memoranda of understanding, which pool inspection data, target ships by risk profile and publish detention records. India is a member of the Indian Ocean Memorandum of Understanding.

2. The legal framework

The power is not free-standing: it is conferred by the conventions themselves, and the current procedures are set out in resolution A.1206(34), Procedures for Port State Control, 2025, adopted 3 December 2025. Section 1.4 lists the provisions relied on:

  • SOLAS 1974 - regulations I/19, IX/6.2, XI-1/4 and XI-2/9, as modified by the 1988 SOLAS Protocol;
  • Load Lines 1966 - article 21, as modified by the 1988 LL Protocol;
  • MARPOL - articles 5 and 6, and regulation 11 of Annex I, regulation 16.9 of Annex II, regulation 9 of Annex III, regulation 14 of Annex IV, regulation 9 of Annex V and regulation 10 of Annex VI;
  • STCW 1978 - article X;
  • TONNAGE 1969 - article 12;
  • AFS 2001 - article 11; and BWM 2004 - article 9.

A.1206(34) revokes A.1185(33), the 2023 Procedures, which had itself followed a long line of revoked predecessors. A great deal of published material still cites A.1185(33) or A.1155(32); at this sitting those are superseded.

3. No more favourable treatment

A ship flying the flag of a State that is not party to a convention, or a ship below convention size, must not thereby receive more favourable treatment than a ship entitled to fly the flag of a Party. The provisions relied on are SOLAS PROT 1988 article I(3), LL PROT 1988 article I(3), MARPOL article 5(4), STCW article X(5), AFS article 3(3) and BWM article 3(3). Without this rule, non-ratification would be a commercial advantage; with it, the port State applies the convention standard regardless of flag.

4. How an inspection becomes a detention

The port State control officer begins with certificates and documents and a general impression of the ship's condition. If there are clear grounds for believing that the condition of the ship or its equipment does not correspond substantially with the certificates, or that the crew are not familiar with essential shipboard procedures, the officer proceeds to a more detailed inspection. Deficiencies that are clearly hazardous to safety, health or the environment justify detention: the ship is stopped from proceeding to sea until they are rectified. Detention is reported to the flag State, and the ship may in defined circumstances be permitted to proceed to another port for repair.

5. The right of appeal - what the instrument actually says

This is the operative provision, and it answers the question directly. Section 2.3.11 of A.1206(34) provides that:

  • the company or its representative has a right of appeal against a detention taken by the authority of a port State;
  • the appeal should not cause the detention to be suspended; and
  • the port State control officer should properly inform the master of the right of appeal.

Three consequences follow, and the question's own framing sets up the first. The right is the company's, not the Chief Engineer's personally. The question asks about "you as the Chief Engineer or the Company", and the instrument answers the disjunction: the Chief Engineer does not hold an independent right of appeal, and acts through, and on behalf of, the company. Second, appealing does not release the ship - the detention runs on while the appeal is heard, so an appeal is never the fastest route back to sea. Third, the officer carries a positive duty to tell the master that the right exists.

6. What the Chief Engineer should actually do

Because the appeal does not lift the detention, the practical sequence is:

  • Record the evidence at once - photographs, parameters, alarm and maintenance printouts, the planned maintenance record, the deficiency as written on the report form. Once the ship sails, the evidence is gone.
  • Ask the officer to identify the convention requirement relied on for each deficiency. A deficiency that cannot be tied to a requirement is the strongest ground of appeal there is.
  • Notify the company, the flag State and the recognized organization immediately. The flag State may comment on the detention report, and that channel is often more effective than a formal appeal.
  • Rectify and seek release in parallel. Rectification and release is the mechanism that actually gets the ship to sea.
  • Let the company lodge the appeal, through the regional MoU's own review mechanism and the port State's administrative or judicial process, supported by the flag State's comments.

7. Undue delay and compensation, and one trap

Separate from the appeal, the parent conventions require that all possible efforts shall be made to avoid a ship being unduly detained or delayed, and provide that a ship unduly detained or delayed is entitled to compensation for loss or damage suffered - MARPOL article 7(2) and SOLAS regulation I/19. That is a claim for loss, not a mechanism for release, and it is a different remedy from the right of appeal.

The trap. A.1206(34) contains a chapter headed "Review Procedures", and it is not the appeal route. That chapter concerns the Organization's periodic evaluation of summaries of deficiency reports to improve the consistency of port State control worldwide. The owner's remedy is section 2.3.11, inside the general procedural guidelines. The chapter with the right-sounding name is the wrong one.

Whose right is it? The question sets a trap and the instrument springs it

An appeal is not a release mechanism

This is the point most answers get wrong, and it is stated in one sentence in the instrument: the appeal should not cause the detention to be suspended. A candidate who writes that the Chief Engineer should appeal in order to get the ship moving has inverted the commercial reality. The ship sails when the deficiencies are rectified and the detention is lifted. The appeal decides whether the detention was right, which matters for the company's record, the ship's risk profile and any claim for loss - but not for tomorrow's sailing.

Port State control does not replace the flag State

The whole architecture rests on the flag State holding primary responsibility, with port State control as a safety net against under-performance. That is why detention is reported to the flag State, why the flag State may comment on the report, and why the flag State route is often the effective one. A ship is not "approved" by passing a port State control inspection; nothing in the Procedures gives an inspection that status.

Model written answer

1. The types of loss in marine insurance

Losses divide first into total and partial, and the partial losses divide again. Under the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 the scheme is:

ClassSub-classWhat it is
Total lossActual total lossThe subject-matter is destroyed, or so damaged that it ceases to be a thing of the kind insured, or the assured is irretrievably deprived of it. A missing ship, after a reasonable time, is presumed an actual total loss.
Total lossConstructive total lossThe subject-matter is reasonably abandoned because actual total loss appears unavoidable, or because it could not be preserved from it without expenditure exceeding its value when saved. Claiming for a constructive total loss requires notice of abandonment.
Partial lossParticular averageA partial loss caused accidentally by a peril insured against, which is not a general average loss. It falls solely on the interest damaged - no contribution.
Partial lossGeneral average lossA loss caused by, or directly consequential on, a general average act. It is shared rateably by all the interests saved.
Partial lossSalvage chargesCharges recoverable by a salvor independently of contract, for services rendered in saving the property from peril.
Partial lossParticular chargesExpenses incurred by or on behalf of the assured for the safety or preservation of the subject-matter, other than general average and salvage charges - the sue and labour expenses. They are not particular average.

The distinction that carries the marks is particular average versus general average: accidental and borne alone, against deliberate and shared.

2. The general average act, as the York Antwerp Rules 1994 define it

Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules 1994:

"There is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure."

Rule A goes on to provide that general average sacrifices and expenditures shall be borne by the different contributing interests on the basis the Rules provide. This wording is unchanged in the 2016 Rules - the later edition merely numbers Rule A's two paragraphs 1 and 2. So the definition asked for is both correct as at 1994 and still correct today, and it is worth saying so rather than leaving the examiner to wonder whether the candidate knows.

3. When general average may be declared

Every element of Rule A must be present. The absence of any one of them defeats the claim:

  • there must be a real peril to the common adventure - apprehension alone is not enough;
  • the act must be extraordinary - not something the ship is already bound to do under the contract of carriage;
  • it must be intentional and voluntary, not accidental;
  • it must be reasonable - and the Rule Paramount, which is present in the 1994 Rules, provides that in no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred;
  • it must be for the common safety of the property in a common maritime adventure, not for one interest alone;
  • there must be a saving of property - if nothing is saved there is nothing to contribute to.

Typical acts: jettison of cargo, voluntary stranding, water damage from firefighting, machinery damage sustained in refloating, salvage and tug costs, and port-of-refuge expenses. The shipowner declares general average, in practice through the Master; the average adjuster does not declare it, but prepares the adjustment afterwards. Note also that under Rule C, present in the 1994 Rules, there is no allowance in general average for damage to the environment or for loss arising from the escape of pollutant substances.

4. What the 1994 Rules say that the current Rules do not

The question names a superseded edition deliberately, and the honest answer states the position as at 1994 while identifying the current edition. The York-Antwerp Rules are not law: they are a contractual code, effective only when incorporated into the bill of lading or charterparty, which is why those documents name the edition - and why older contracts incorporating YAR 1994 remain live. YAR 2016 is the current edition. The material differences are:

5. Warranties in marine insurance, and what breach does

A warranty in marine insurance is a promissory warranty - an undertaking by the assured that some thing shall or shall not be done, that some condition shall be fulfilled, or affirming or negativing the existence of a particular state of facts. Two features make it far more severe than a warranty in general contract law:

  • it must be exactly complied with, whether or not it is material to the risk; and
  • on breach, the insurer is discharged from liability as from the date of the breach, subject to any express provision in the policy.

Breach is excused only in narrow cases - where a change of circumstances makes the warranty no longer applicable, or where compliance is rendered unlawful by a subsequent law. A jurisdiction point matters here. English law was changed by the Insurance Act 2015, under which breach of warranty now suspends cover rather than discharging the insurer. The Indian position under the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 remains discharge from the date of breach. In an Indian examination, answer on the Indian Act.

6. Express warranties, with a hull and machinery example

An express warranty is one the parties write into the contract. It must be included in or written upon the policy, or contained in a document incorporated by reference into it. No particular form of words is required - what matters is that the intention to warrant is clear.

Example in a hull and machinery policy: a classification warranty - "warranted that the vessel is classed with a classification society agreed by underwriters and that class is maintained". If class is suspended or withdrawn and the assured does not tell underwriters, the warranty is broken. Equally common are navigation or trading limits - warranting that the vessel will not trade beyond stated geographical limits, or outside stated dates in ice-bound waters - and lay-up warranties requiring the vessel to be laid up in a named port for a stated period.

7. Implied warranties, and the hull and machinery twist

An implied warranty is imposed by the Act itself and needs no words in the policy. Two matter:

  • Legality - there is an implied warranty that the adventure insured is a lawful one and that, so far as the assured can control the matter, it shall be carried out in a lawful manner. This warranty cannot be excluded and applies to every marine policy, including hull and machinery.
  • Seaworthiness - in a voyage policy there is an implied warranty that at the commencement of the voyage the ship shall be seaworthy for the purpose of the particular adventure.

The twist, and it is the discriminator for this limb. A hull and machinery policy is normally a time policy, and in a time policy there is no implied warranty of seaworthiness at any stage of the adventure. But the Act does not leave the point there: where, with the privity of the assured, the ship is sent to sea in an unseaworthy state, the insurer is not liable for any loss attributable to that unseaworthiness. So the correct statement for an H&M policy is that seaworthiness is not warranted, but knowing unseaworthiness defeats the claim on causation - and the best implied-warranty example to give for H&M is therefore legality, which does apply, with the seaworthiness position explained alongside it.

The edition question - and the reason it is less dangerous than it looks

The paper names the York Antwerp Rules 1994, an edition superseded twice over. The instinct is to answer on YAR 2016 because it is current. Do not. Answer the question as asked - and then note the current edition. What makes this safe is a fact worth knowing: Rule A is word-for-word identical in 1994 and 2016. So the definition you give is simultaneously the 1994 answer and the modern answer, and saying so demonstrates control of the edition question rather than ignorance of it.

The editions do differ, and the differences are worth two lines: the 1994 Rules allow a 2 per cent commission on disbursements and fix interest at 7 per cent, both of which the modern Rules changed; and the 1994 Rules contain no time bar at all - they end at Rule XXII, and the one-year Rule XXIII first appeared in 2004.

The Rules are contractual, not law

This is the point that explains why the examiner can meaningfully name a 1994 edition in a 2026 paper. The York-Antwerp Rules are not a convention and are not enacted anywhere. They bind only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them, and the incorporating clause names the edition. A contract written on an older form still applies YAR 1994 today. Absent incorporation, general average falls back on the applicable national law - in India, the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

Why the warranty limb is harsher than it sounds

In ordinary contract law a "warranty" is the minor term - breach sounds in damages, not termination. In marine insurance it is the opposite: the warranty is the severe term. It must be complied with exactly whether or not it is material to the risk, and breach discharges the insurer from the date of the breach. That inversion is what candidates trip over. Note also that this is where Indian and English law have diverged: the UK Insurance Act 2015 replaced discharge with suspension, so English textbooks now describe a different rule. For an Indian examination, the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 governs.

  • Saying a hull and machinery time policy carries an implied warranty of seaworthiness. It does not - but privity defeats the claim on causation.
  • Applying the English Insurance Act 2015 suspension rule in an Indian examination.

Model written answer

1. Why decarbonisation changes what a marine engineer must be competent at

For a century the marine engineer dealt with one family of fuels whose hazards were broadly the same: a combustible hydrocarbon, hot, oily and familiar. The energy transition replaces that with fuels whose primary hazard is not fire. Ammonia is acutely toxic. Methanol burns with a nearly invisible flame and is toxic by ingestion and absorption. Hydrogen has an extremely wide flammability range and leaks through joints that hold oil. Batteries store energy that cannot be shut off. The competence required is therefore not an incremental extension of existing training - the hazard model itself has changed, and with it the emergency response, the personal protective equipment, the gas detection philosophy and the bunkering procedure.

The driver is regulatory as well as technical. The 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships sets the trajectory, and MARPOL Annex VI regulations 25 (EEXI) and 28 (CII) are already in force and already require operational competence in efficiency management and data integrity. At the date of this examination the IMO Net-Zero Framework had been approved but NOT adopted - the extraordinary session of the Marine Environment Protection Committee adjourned in October 2025 without agreement and is due to reconvene later in 2026. The direction is settled; the instrument was not yet in force, and an answer that describes it as adopted is wrong for this sitting.

2. Where the mandatory training framework reaches - and where it does not

This is the structural point the question rewards. There is presently a gap. Mandatory STCW training exists for gas and low-flashpoint fuels through STCW regulation V/3 and STCW Code section A-V/3, which set basic and advanced training for seafarers serving on ships subject to the IGF Code. But ammonia, methanol, hydrogen and battery-electric propulsion are not yet covered by a mandatory STCW competence standard. Ships are being delivered faster than the Convention can be amended.

IMO has responded with interim guidelines rather than waiting. The Sub-Committee on Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping, meeting in February 2026, four months before this examination, agreed draft interim training guidelines for seafarers on ships using methyl/ethyl alcohol and on ships using ammonia as fuel, prioritised further work on hydrogen fuel cells, LPG, hydrogen, battery-powered ships and wind-assisted propulsion, and carried forward the comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code, the long-term route by which these competences will become mandatory. The same session opened a new workstream on fatigue and hours of work and rest.

3. The anticipated areas of concern

AmmoniaAcute toxicity, not flammability, is the governing hazard. It is also not a zero-emission fuel: there is no carbon in the molecule, so no direct CO2, but combustion produces N2O - a greenhouse gas far more potent than CO2 - and unburnt ammonia slip, and the lifecycle result depends on how the ammonia was produced. 4. The competences the transition demands that are not about fuel

  • Efficiency and data competence. EEXI and CII make the engineer's operating decisions a compliance matter. Hull and propeller condition, trim, speed management and the integrity of the reported data all now carry regulatory consequence, and deliberate or careless misreporting is a serious matter.
  • Dual-fuel and fuel-flexible operation. Many ships will run on two fuels and change between them at sea, which doubles the failure modes and makes changeover procedure a core competence.
  • Risk assessment for a novel plant. Where no rule yet exists, the engineer must reason from first principles and from the ship's own risk assessment - which is precisely what an alternative-design approval requires.
  • Leadership and managerial skill. Introducing an unfamiliar hazard to an experienced crew is a change-management problem before it is a technical one.

The scale of the task is significant: industry research by Lloyd's Register's Maritime Decarbonisation Hub with the Maersk Mc-Kinney Moller Center for Zero Carbon Shipping has estimated that of the order of 800,000 seafarers will require upskilling to handle ammonia safely. That is an industry estimate, not an IMO figure, and should be quoted as such. The Maritime Just Transition Task Force exists to keep seafarers at the centre of that transition rather than at the receiving end of it.

5. Why communication is a causal factor, not a contributory nicety

Casualty investigation repeatedly finds that the physical failure was survivable and the communication failure was not. A defect known to one watch and not passed to the next, an instruction given but not read back, an assumption that somebody else had closed a valve, a permit issued without the working party understanding its limits - in each case the information existed on board and did not reach the person who needed it. That is why communication is classed as a causal factor: it is not that people communicated badly and an accident coincidentally followed, but that the absence of the information was a link in the causal chain.

Communication failure has recognisable forms: vertical failure, where a junior does not challenge a senior; horizontal failure, between departments - classically engine room and bridge, or ship and terminal; handover failure, at watch or crew change; and ship-to-shore failure, where the vessel's real condition and the office's understanding of it diverge.

6. What the instruments actually require

  • ISM Code 1.4.2 makes defined levels of authority and lines of communication between, and amongst, shore and shipboard personnel a functional requirement of the safety management system itself - communication is not an optional good practice, it is something the SMS must provide.
  • ISM Code 6.6 requires the Company to ensure ship's personnel receive relevant SMS information in a working language or languages understood by them, and 6.7 requires the Company to ensure they can communicate effectively in executing their SMS duties.
  • SOLAS regulation V/14 requires a working language to be established and recorded in the log-book, with plans and lists translated into it where it is not an official language of the flag State, and requires English on the bridge for bridge-to-bridge, bridge-to-shore and pilot communications unless those involved share another common language.
  • STCW Code section A-VIII/2 requires watches to be carried out on bridge and engine-room resource management principles, and the management-level competences include leadership and managerial skill.
  • The IMO Standard Marine Communication Phrases exist to remove ambiguity where a shared natural language cannot be assumed.

7. Why present-day crewing makes this harder

  • Multinational, multilingual crews are now the norm rather than the exception. A common working language is a legal requirement but a declared working language is not the same as a shared one, and comprehension under stress falls well below comprehension in conversation.
  • Reduced manning and high turnover. Short contracts and rotating agency crews mean the informal knowledge that once covered gaps in the written system no longer accumulates. What is not written down is lost at every crew change.
  • Steep hierarchy and cultural power distance. Where challenging a senior is socially costly, the junior who sees the error says nothing. Resource management training exists precisely to make the challenge legitimate - a closed loop, read back and confirmed, is a technique for defeating rank.
  • Ship-to-shore intensity. Continuous performance monitoring and remote support mean more decisions are influenced ashore, and the risk is a divergence between the ship's actual condition and the office's picture of it.
  • Fatigue. Comprehension, assertiveness and read-back discipline all degrade first under fatigue - which is why IMO opened a dedicated workstream on hours of work and rest.
  • Novel hazards raise the cost of ambiguity. On a conventional ship a misunderstood instruction usually costs time. On an ammonia-fuelled ship, an unclear instruction during bunkering can cost a life - which is the point at which limb (b) rejoins limb (a).

Examiner traps

"Why is a read-back required if the order was clear?" Because clarity is judged by the receiver, not the sender, and because a closed loop is what makes a junior's correction socially possible.

Model written answer

1. The Code, and the system that implements it

The International Safety Management Code was adopted by resolution A.741(18) on 4 November 1993 and made mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX. Its distinguishing feature is that it prescribes almost no technical requirement. It requires a system, and the system is the Safety Management System. The relationship is precise and worth stating at the outset: the ISM Code states the objectives and the functional requirements; the SMS is the Company's own documented means of meeting them on its ships. Two companies may comply with the same Code with very different systems.

The objectives at paragraph 1.2.1 are to ensure safety at sea, prevention of human injury or loss of life, and avoidance of damage to the environment - in particular to the marine environment - and to property. Paragraph 1.2.2 then requires the Company's safety management objectives to provide for safe practices and a safe working environment, to assess all identified risks to its ships, personnel and the environment and establish appropriate safeguards, and to continuously improve safety management skills of personnel ashore and aboard, including preparation for emergencies.

2. The components - the functional requirements of an SMS

Paragraph 1.4 sets out what every safety management system must contain:

  • a safety and environmental protection policy;
  • instructions and procedures to ensure safe operation of ships and protection of the environment in compliance with relevant international and flag State legislation;
  • defined levels of authority and lines of communication between, and amongst, shore and shipboard personnel;
  • procedures for reporting accidents and non-conformities with the provisions of the Code;
  • procedures to prepare for and respond to emergency situations; and
  • procedures for internal audits and management reviews.

The Code then expands these across its sections - the safety and environmental protection policy; Company responsibility and authority; the Designated Person ashore with direct access to the highest level of management; the Master's responsibility and overriding authority; resources and personnel; shipboard operations; emergency preparedness; reports and analysis of non-conformities and accidents; maintenance of the ship and equipment, including the identification of critical equipment under 10.3; documentation; and Company verification, review and evaluation.

3. How the Code is given effect

Compliance is certificated on two levels, and the split matters. The Document of Compliance is issued to the Company, is valid for five years and is subject to annual verification. The Safety Management Certificate is issued to the ship, is valid for five years and is subject to at least one intermediate verification. A ship cannot hold a valid SMC unless its Company holds a valid DOC for that ship type. Certification is the mechanism by which a documented system becomes an enforceable obligation, and it is what a port State control officer will examine first.

4. Continuous improvement - where it actually lives in the Code

Continuous improvement is not an aspiration attached to the Code; it is written into it in three places and delivered by a closed loop:

  • 1.2.2.3 requires the Company's objectives to include continuous improvement of safety management skills of personnel ashore and aboard.
  • Section 9 requires procedures for reporting and analysing non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences, and for implementing corrective action - so failure is required to be surfaced rather than concealed.
  • Section 12 requires the Company to carry out internal audits, to periodically evaluate the efficiency of the system, and to conduct management review.

The loop runs: policy → procedures → operate → report and analyse → corrective action → audit → management review → revised procedures. Its significance is that it makes the SMS a learning system rather than a filing system. This is also the Code's principal failure mode in practice: a system in which non-conformities are never raised is not a compliant system operating perfectly, it is a broken loop - which is why auditors treat a nil non-conformity return with suspicion rather than approval.

5. How the Code has evolved - and mostly without amendment

The evolution the question asks about is real, but the mechanism is the interesting part, and it is the observation that lifts this limb. The ISM Code itself has been amended relatively little - the most recent substantive amendments, by resolution MSC.353(92), entered into force on 1 January 2015. Yet the obligations carried by a modern SMS have expanded enormously.

That is possible because the Code was written as a container. Paragraph 1.2.2.2 requires the Company to assess all identified risks - not a listed set of risks - and paragraph 1.4.2 requires procedures ensuring compliance with relevant international and flag State legislation, whatever that legislation becomes. So a new hazard or a new convention obligation enters the SMS without the Code needing to be reopened. The Code has evolved by absorption rather than by amendment.

6. Cyber risk

Cyber risk is the clearest illustration. Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted 16 June 2017, affirms that an approved safety management system should take into account cyber risk management in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code, and encourages Administrations to ensure that cyber risks are appropriately addressed in safety management systems no later than the first annual verification of the Company's Document of Compliance after 1 January 2021. It did not amend the ISM Code. A cyber risk was simply treated as one of the "all identified risks" paragraph 1.2.2.2 already required to be assessed.

The practical guidance sits in the Guidelines on maritime cyber risk management, MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3, dated 4 April 2025, which supersedes the earlier revision. Maritime cyber risk is defined there as a measure of the extent to which a Computer Based System is threatened by a potential circumstance or event which may result in shipping-related operational, safety or security failures. The guidance distinguishes information technology from operational technology - the systems that control physical processes - and requires OT to be segmented from IT and protected from internet-facing systems.

The current revision sets out six functional elements, not five. They are Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. Govern was added by this revision and requires the risk management strategy and policies to be established and monitored, personnel roles defined, and a person or entity designated as accountable for cyber security with the authority and expertise to discharge it. The elements are expressly not sequential - all should be concurrent and continuous. Much published material still lists five elements, omitting Govern.

7. Technological advancement and environmental sustainability

Technological advancement presses on the Code in two ways. Increasing automation and integration mean the systems whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations - the critical equipment of section 10.3 - are now as much software and network as they are machinery, and the standby arrangements that section requires to be regularly tested are harder to define for an integrated system. Further ahead, autonomous and remotely operated ships challenge two of the Code's structural assumptions: the Master's overriding authority and the presence of a crew to exercise it. That is a live question for the Organization rather than a settled one.

Environmental sustainability is not new to the Code - avoidance of damage to the environment has been in paragraph 1.2.1 from the beginning, and the required policy is a safety and environmental protection policy. What has changed is the weight of environmental obligation the SMS now carries: garbage and ballast water management, biofouling, and the energy-efficiency regime of MARPOL Annex VI, whose EEXI and CII requirements make operational and data-reporting discipline a compliance matter. Each arrived as procedures under paragraph 1.4.2, without the Code being amended - and the same route will carry the greenhouse gas measures that were approved but not yet adopted at the date of this examination.

The strongest argument in limb (b): the Code evolved without being amended

A candidate who lists ISM amendment resolutions is answering a different, duller question. The powerful observation is that the ISM Code has changed remarkably little while the obligations inside a modern SMS have multiplied. That is by design: paragraph 1.2.2.2 requires assessment of all identified risks, and paragraph 1.4.2 requires compliance with relevant legislation - both open-ended. Cyber risk entered the SMS through resolution MSC.428(98) without a single word of the Code being amended. Say that, and the rest of the limb writes itself.

The cyber currency point - six elements, not five

This is the sharpest factual discriminator on the question. The Guidelines were revised to MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3 on 4 April 2025, approved by MSC 108 and FAL 49. That revision added a sixth functional element, Govern, ahead of Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. An enormous volume of training material, industry guidance and class publication still says five. Writing six, naming Govern, and saying what it requires - accountability, authority and expertise assigned to a named person or entity - is current where almost everything else is not. Note also that the elements are expressly not sequential: they run concurrently and continuously.

Continuous improvement is a loop, and a nil return is a symptom

The mark-winning insight in limb (a) is that continuous improvement is a mechanism, not an attitude: section 9 forces failure to the surface, section 12 audits and reviews, and the corrective action feeds back into the procedures. It follows that a ship reporting no non-conformities is not demonstrating excellence - it is demonstrating that the reporting limb of the loop is not working. Auditors read a nil return as a finding. That single observation shows the candidate understands the Code as a system rather than as a document set.

"Give me the functional elements of cyber risk management." Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover - six, and not a sequence.

"Who has overriding authority?" The Master, and it is one of the Code's structural assumptions that autonomous operation will have to confront.

The three cost families are not three sizes of bill - they are three different triggers, and that distinction is what earns the marks. Model written answer

1. The three cost families, and what actually distinguishes them

The three are separated by what makes the money go out, not by size. That test is what allows the Chief Engineer to see where he has leverage:

FamilyTriggered byBehaves like
Capital costOwning the asset at allFixed. Incurred whether the ship trades, waits or lies idle.
Operating costKeeping the ship in a state fit to tradeLargely fixed in the short run. Incurred whether or not the ship is on a voyage.
Voyage costPerforming a particular voyageVariable. Incurred only when, and because, the ship sails.

2. What falls into each

  • Capital costs - the newbuilding or purchase price, debt service and interest, depreciation, and the return required on the owner's equity. Major capital expenditure such as a retrofit or a conversion also falls here rather than in maintenance.
  • Operating costs - crew wages, victualling and travel; stores and consumables including lubricating oil and chemicals; spares; repairs and maintenance; insurance, both hull and machinery and protection and indemnity; registration and flag State fees; classification fees; and shore management and administration. Periodic maintenance - drydocking and special survey - is often separated out because it is lumpy rather than continuous.
  • Voyage costs - bunker fuel, which normally dominates; port dues and charges; canal transit dues; pilotage and towage. Cargo-handling costs are sometimes treated as a fourth family, since whether they fall on owner or charterer depends on the charterparty.

3. The Chief Engineer's leverage - and where he has none

Being honest about this is what separates a management answer from a list:

  • Capital cost - effectively no short-run influence. The price was paid and the loan was signed before the Chief Engineer joined. His influence is indirect and long-run: a ship maintained to a good standard with complete, credible records commands a better residual value and a cleaner class record, and a well-argued technical business case is what gets a retrofit approved or rejected.
  • Voyage cost - the largest single lever he holds, because fuel is the dominant voyage cost and fuel consumption is a function of machinery and hull condition and of how the plant is operated.
  • Operating cost - shared with the office. Crew cost, insurance and flag fees are set ashore. Maintenance, spares, stores, lubricating oil and the drydock specification are substantially his.

4. Optimising voyage cost

  • Hull and propeller condition. Fouling raises the power required for a given speed and is often the largest single recoverable loss on a ship. Systematic performance monitoring - power and consumption against speed, corrected for draught, trim, wind and sea - is what turns "the ship feels slow" into a costed case for a hull clean or propeller polish.
  • Machinery condition and performance. Indicator and pressure-rise data, turbocharger condition, air and gas differential pressures, cooler and economiser fouling, and correct injection condition all show up directly in specific fuel consumption.
  • Correct operation for the loading in use. Slow steaming saves fuel but pushes the engine into a region it was not optimised for; running it there without the correct measures raises maintenance cost and can cost more than the fuel saved.
  • Fuel management. Bunker quantity survey and sampling at delivery, correct treatment and settling, avoiding commingling of incompatible fuels, and minimising sludge - a fuel dispute or an off-specification bunker can erase a year of careful savings.
  • Auxiliary and hotel load. Generator loading, boiler and steam management, shaft generator use where fitted, and in-port load reduction.
  • The regulatory overlay. Under MARPOL Annex VI regulations 25 and 28, the EEXI and CII requirements make efficiency a compliance outcome, not merely a cost. The same engineering decisions now carry a rating consequence and a data-integrity obligation.

5. Optimising operating cost

  • Maintenance strategy. Breakdown maintenance is the most expensive way to run machinery; time-based maintenance overhauls items that were still serviceable; condition-based maintenance spends the money when the evidence says it is needed. The Chief Engineer's real contribution is choosing the right strategy for each item rather than applying one to all.
  • Off-hire avoidance. A day off-hire usually costs more than the repair that caused it. Ranking maintenance by the consequence of failure, rather than by convenience, is the discipline.
  • Lubricating oil management. Feed-rate optimisation, correct base number for the fuel in use, and onboard and laboratory analysis to catch wear early.
  • Drydock specification. The docking is the single largest operating-cost event the Chief Engineer materially controls. A specification written from real condition data, with growth work anticipated, prevents the change orders that make dockings overrun.
  • Spares and stores control - which is the third part of the question.

6. Modern principles of inventory control - classifying the stock

Modern inventory control begins by recognising that not all items deserve the same control. The techniques are ways of deciding which item gets which treatment:

TechniqueClassifies byWhat it is used for

VED analysisCriticality of the consequence of not having itVital, Essential, Desirable. The marine-critical technique, because a cheap item can be vital.
FSN analysisMovementFast, Slow, Non-moving. Exposes dead stock and obsolete holdings.
SDE analysisProcurement difficultyScarce, Difficult, Easy. Drives lead time and safety stock.

The important move is to combine them. ABC alone will under-protect a cheap but vital item; VED alone will over-protect an expensive but non-critical one. The practical shipboard method is the ABC-VED matrix: value on one axis, criticality on the other, with the highest control applied to anything Vital regardless of value. This is where the regulatory constraint enters: ISM Code paragraph 10.3 requires the Company to identify equipment whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations and to provide measures promoting its reliability. That identified list and the V of a VED classification should be the same list.

7. Modern principles - ordering, and the limits of just-in-time at sea

  • Economic order quantity balances the cost of ordering against the cost of holding, giving the order size at which their sum is least. It is a useful idea afloat and an unreliable formula, because the "ordering cost" for a moving asset is dominated by logistics to a port the ship has not reached yet.
  • Reorder level and minimum-maximum. Reorder level is average consumption over the lead time plus a safety stock. On a ship the lead time is the hard variable - it is not the supplier's despatch time but the time until the ship is somewhere the part can reach it.
  • Safety stock buffers variability in both demand and lead time, and should be set by criticality rather than uniformly.
  • Lead-time and voyage-plan integration. Ordering is planned against the trading pattern, so that deliveries fall where the ship will actually be and agency and freight costs are contained.
  • Standardisation and fleet pooling. Common makers and models across a fleet cut the number of distinct items held, allow stock to be shared between ships and pooled at a hub port, and improve buying power.
  • Vendor-managed inventory and framework agreements with makers and service providers, which move some holding cost and obsolescence risk onto the supplier.
  • Computerised inventory integrated with the planned maintenance system, so that consumption is booked against the job that used it and future demand becomes predictable rather than anecdotal. Condition-based maintenance strengthens this further: the better the prediction of when an item will be needed, the less stock is required to achieve the same availability.
  • Obsolescence and shelf-life control. Rubber, gaskets, batteries and chemicals expire on the shelf; first-in-first-out issue and a periodic dead-stock review under FSN prevent a store full of unusable items.

Just-in-time deserves a qualification rather than a recommendation. Minimising holding is sound for consumables and for non-critical, easily obtained items. It is not appropriate for critical spares, because a ship is a moving asset whose supply lead time is uncertain, and because the consequence of a stockout is not a delayed production line but a hazardous situation of exactly the kind ISM 10.3 requires to be guarded against. The correct modern position is just-in-time where the consequence of being wrong is commercial, and safety stock where it is not.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Sixteen marks, no printed limbs, but three unmistakable tasks: the cost families, the Chief Engineer's role, and inventory control. Treat them as roughly a third each and none can be forgotten - the commonest failure on this question is a long, confident description of shipping costs followed by three lines on inventory, discarding a third of the marks. The examiner has put "What are the modern management principles used in Inventory control?" as a separate sentence precisely because it is a separate block of credit.

Define the families by their trigger, not by examples

Weak answers list items under three headings and hope the classification is self-evident. Strong answers state the test first: capital cost is triggered by owning the ship, operating cost by keeping it fit to trade, and voyage cost by performing a particular voyage. Once that is on the page, every borderline item can be placed and defended - and it explains why the same expense can move between families, which is exactly what an examiner will probe.

Be honest about where the Chief Engineer has no influence

This is a management question, and management judgement includes knowing what you do not control. A candidate who claims to optimise capital cost is not credible: the price was paid and the finance arranged before he joined. The strong answer says so, and then makes the two real long-run points - that maintained condition and complete records protect residual value, and that the technical business case is what gets capital expenditure approved. Conceding the point costs nothing and buys authority for the rest of the answer.

This question rests on practice, not on regulation - and that is not a weakness

There is no IMO instrument on ship cost structure and none on inventory control. The correct response is not to decorate the answer with SOLAS, MARPOL or ISM references that do not bear on the point. Reach for regulation only where it genuinely applies, and there are exactly two places: ISM Code 10.3, which makes critical-equipment identification a requirement and therefore constrains spares policy; and MARPOL Annex VI regulations 25 and 28, which turn fuel efficiency into a compliance outcome. Two well-placed citations in an otherwise engineering-and-management answer read as competence. Six irrelevant ones read as padding.

VED matters more than ABC at sea, and ABC-VED matters more than either

ABC analysis is the technique everyone remembers, and on a ship it is the less useful of the two. ABC ranks by value; a ship is stopped by a cheap item as easily as by an expensive one. VED ranks by the consequence of not having it, which is the question that actually matters afloat. The mark-winning statement is that the two are combined - highest control to anything Vital regardless of value - and that the Vital list should be the same list the Company already has to identify under ISM 10.3. That single sentence connects a management technique to a regulatory obligation correctly, which is rare.

Common mistakes

  • Answering only the cost half and leaving inventory control to a closing paragraph.
  • Putting bunkers in operating cost. Fuel burned on a voyage is a voyage cost.
  • Putting drydocking in capital cost. It is maintenance - normally treated within operating cost, often shown separately as periodic maintenance. A conversion or retrofit is capital.
  • Claiming to reduce capital cost from the engine room.
  • Recommending just-in-time for critical spares without qualification.
  • Explaining ABC analysis and stopping, without VED - and so never reaching criticality.
  • Quoting specific percentage shares of cost as though they were universal. They vary by ship type, trade, flag and market.
  • Attaching SOLAS, STCW or ISM references to management techniques they have nothing to do with.

Examiner traps

"Would you run just-in-time on this ship?" For consumables and easily obtained non-critical items, yes. For critical spares, no - the lead time to a moving ship is uncertain and the consequence of a stockout is a hazardous situation, not a production delay. The qualification is the answer.

"Your A-class item and your V-class item are different items. Which gets the tighter control?" The V item. Value tells you what the stock is worth; criticality tells you what its absence costs.

"You have saved a great deal of fuel by slow steaming. Have you saved money?" Not necessarily. Sustained low-load operation outside the range the engine was optimised for raises maintenance cost, and the net position has to be argued, not assumed.

"Which cost family does the Chief Engineer influence most?" Voyage cost, through fuel - and it is worth saying that this is also the one now carrying a regulatory consequence under EEXI and CII.

  • Which is the largest voyage cost? Bunker fuel, normally by a wide margin.
  • Where does insurance sit? Operating cost. Where do canal dues sit? Voyage cost.

  • What is the reorder level? Average consumption over the lead time, plus safety stock.

Uncertainty and the limits of authority for this question

This is the question on the paper with the thinnest regulatory foundation, and the answer says so rather than disguising it. Model written answer

1. What protects these islands now - and what does not

The starting point must be accurate, because the question's wording invites an error. The Lakshadweep group and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands are unquestionably critical habitat - coral atolls, reefs, seagrass, mangrove and turtle nesting grounds - and they carry substantial protection under Indian domestic law. But neither group is a designated IMO Particularly Sensitive Sea Area. Nineteen PSSAs have been designated worldwide and none of them is in India, and no Indian waters are a MARPOL Special Area or an Emission Control Area either.

So the question - how these islands could be protected - is properly answered as a programme of measures, some already in place domestically and some that India would have to seek. The distinction between an environmentally sensitive area and a formally designated area under an international instrument is the discrimination this limb rewards.

2. The Indian statutory and administrative layer

  • Coastal and island zoning. The Island Coastal Regulation Zone (ICRZ) Notification, 2019, made under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, applies specifically to the Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep groups and superseded the earlier Island Protection Zone Notification of 2011. It classifies the coastal stretches into zones of decreasing sensitivity, restricts what may be built, discharged or operated in each, and provides for Integrated Island Management Plans. This is the single most directly applicable Indian instrument.
  • Habitat protection. Marine national parks and wildlife sanctuaries declared under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 - the Mahatma Gandhi Marine National Park in the Andamans is the best-known example - give the reefs and their species statutory protection independent of the shipping regime.
  • Ship-source pollution. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025, which came into force on 15 March 2026 and is therefore the operative Indian statute at this examination, carries the prevention and containment of pollution from vessels and the response to it. It is the vehicle by which India gives effect to MARPOL, and the subordinate Merchant Shipping Rules made under it are the working detail. Do not cite the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 - it has been repealed.
  • Response capability. The Indian Coast Guard, under the Coast Guard Act, 1978, is the central coordinating authority for marine pollution response in Indian waters, and the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan is the framework under which a spill in these remote archipelagos would actually be fought. For islands this far from the mainland, pre-positioned response equipment and realistic response times matter more than any additional rule.
  • Reception facilities and port control. Adequate reception facilities at the island ports, and port State control inspection of ships calling there, close the loop between the paper requirement and the discharge that actually happens.

3. The international layer India could seek

  • PSSA designation. India, as a Member Government, could submit a proposal to the Marine Environment Protection Committee under the PSSA Guidelines. This is the measure the question is pointing at, and it is the one India does not presently hold.
  • Routeing measures under SOLAS regulation V/10 - an Area To Be Avoided around the most sensitive reefs, a traffic separation scheme, a no-anchoring area to protect coral from anchor damage, or a recommended deep-water route keeping laden tankers clear.
  • Mandatory ship reporting under SOLAS regulation V/11, so that the authorities know which ships are where, which is the precondition for any effective response.
  • MARPOL Special Area designation under Annexes I, II, IV or V, or an Emission Control Area under Annex VI, each of which imposes stricter discharge or emission standards within a defined sea area.
  • UNCLOS article 211(6), which allows a coastal State, after consultation through the competent international organization, to adopt special mandatory measures in a clearly defined area of its exclusive economic zone where the recognised technical reasons and the ecological conditions justify it.
  • Compulsory pilotage in defined approaches, which is what protects the Torres Strait within the Great Barrier Reef PSSA.

4. What a coherent programme would look like

Protection is not a single instrument but a sequence: establish the science - the habitat surveys and traffic studies that would support a proposal; apply the domestic controls already available under the ICRZ Notification and the Wild Life (Protection) Act; seek the international measures - a PSSA supported by an Area To Be Avoided and mandatory reporting, or Special Area status; and build the response capability, because a designation that cannot be enforced or responded to protects nothing. Enforcement is the weak point in remote archipelagos, and any credible answer says so.

5. What a Particularly Sensitive Sea Area is

A Particularly Sensitive Sea Area is an area that needs special protection through action by IMO because of its significance for recognised ecological, socio-economic or scientific reasons, and because it may be vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities. The governing instrument is resolution A.982(24), the Revised Guidelines for the Identification and Designation of Particularly Sensitive Sea Areas, adopted 1 December 2005, as amended by resolution MEPC.267(68).

Two features define it. First, it is a designation by IMO, not a declaration by the coastal State - which is what distinguishes it from a national marine protected area. Second, and decisively, a PSSA has no legal effect of its own: it is a flag placed on the chart that identifies an area as needing care. What actually regulates shipping is the Associated Protective Measure. A PSSA without an APM would change nothing, which is why the Guidelines will not allow one.

6. The criteria for identification

The Guidelines set three groups of criteria, and an area must meet at least one of them - not all three:

GroupCriteria within it
EcologicalUniqueness or rarity; critical habitat; dependency; representativeness; diversity; productivity; spawning or breeding grounds; naturalness; integrity; fragility; and bio-geographic importance.
Social, cultural and economicSocial or economic dependency - for example on fisheries, tourism or recreation; human dependency, including subsistence and traditional use; and cultural heritage.
Scientific and educationalValue for research; value for baseline and monitoring studies; and value for education.

Meeting a criterion is necessary but not sufficient. The proposal must also demonstrate that the area is vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities, and that is assessed on two sets of factors: vessel traffic characteristics - operational factors, the types of vessel and cargo, the traffic density and the harmful substances carried - and natural factors, meaning the hydrographic, meteorological and oceanographic conditions that make a casualty more likely or its consequences worse. An area of great ecological value with no international shipping near it does not qualify.

7. Designation, and the Associated Protective Measure

Designation follows a defined sequence:

  • A Member Government - only a Member Government may propose - submits an application to the MEPC, describing the area, showing that it meets at least one criterion, demonstrating vulnerability to international shipping, and proposing at least one Associated Protective Measure.
  • Every APM must have an identified legal basis. It may be a measure already available under an existing IMO instrument - a routeing measure under SOLAS V/10, a ship reporting system under V/11, a discharge restriction or Special Area under MARPOL, or compulsory pilotage; a measure not yet available but which could be made available through IMO; or a measure adopted under UNCLOS article 211(6) for an area of the exclusive economic zone.
  • MEPC may grant designation in principle. The proposed APM is then referred to the appropriate IMO body for adoption - routeing and reporting measures to the navigation sub-committee and the Maritime Safety Committee, discharge measures to MEPC itself.
  • Once the APM has been adopted, MEPC grants final designation of the PSSA, and the area is charted and promulgated so that mariners can see it.

Nineteen PSSAs have been designated, beginning with the Great Barrier Reef in 1990 and including the Wadden Sea, the Galapagos Archipelago, the Baltic Sea Area, the Canary Islands, Papahanaumokuakea, the Strait of Bonifacio, the Tubbataha Reefs and the north-western Mediterranean. None is in Indian waters - which is precisely why limb (a) of this question is written in the conditional.

Why this structure scores

Both limbs say briefly, twice in limb (b). The examiner is asking for coverage, not depth: a broad, well-organised sweep will beat a deep essay on one measure. Limb (a) is a programme - domestic measures, international measures, and the honest observation that enforcement and response capability matter as much as designation. Limb (b) is definition, criteria, and procedure. The single most valuable thing in the whole question is one sentence: a PSSA has no legal effect of its own; the Associated Protective Measure is what regulates shipping.

The trap in the question's own wording - and it is deliberate

The stem asserts that these islands are "known for their critical habitat and required to be protected". That is true, and it tempts the candidate into writing that they are Particularly Sensitive Sea Areas. They are not. Nineteen PSSAs have been designated worldwide and none is in India; Indian waters hold no MARPOL Special Area and no Emission Control Area either. Notice that the examiner wrote "could be protected", in the conditional - the question is prospective, and answering it prospectively is answering it correctly. A candidate who writes "the Andaman and Nicobar Islands were designated a PSSA" has stated something false with confidence, which is the worst outcome available on this question.

Environmentally sensitive is not the same as formally designated

Hold the two ideas apart. Ecological sensitivity is a fact about the place. Designation is a legal act by a named body under a named instrument. India has done a great deal of the first kind of protection - the ICRZ Notification, marine national parks under the Wild Life (Protection) Act - and none of the second kind at IMO. Being able to say which protections exist under which instrument, and which do not exist at all, is what a management-level answer looks like.

Get the Indian statute right - the sitting date decides it

This examination falls after 15 March 2026, when the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 came into force and repealed the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. Any ship-source pollution limb must be answered on the 2025 Act. But note the discipline that goes with it: the 2025 Act does not swallow every maritime subject. The environmental protection of these islands rests principally on the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the ICRZ Notification made under it, and on the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 - specialist legislation that the shipping Act does not touch. Naming the right statute for each limb is the point; mechanically substituting the 2025 Act everywhere is the opposite error.

Criteria: at least one, plus vulnerability

The commonest error in limb (b) is to imply that an area must satisfy all three criteria groups. It must satisfy at least one. The second, and more interesting, error is to stop at the criteria - because meeting a criterion is not enough. The proposal must also demonstrate vulnerability to damage by international shipping activities, assessed on vessel traffic characteristics and on natural factors. A pristine, unique, scientifically precious area with no shipping near it does not qualify, and saying so shows the candidate understands what a PSSA is for.

Common mistakes

Model written answer

1. What a classification society is, and its two distinct roles

A classification society is a technical body that publishes its own rules for the design, construction and survey of ships, assigns class to a ship that meets them, and maintains that class by periodical survey. Its origin is commercial: underwriters needed an independent judgement of the ship they were asked to insure. Everything else follows from that.

The distinction that must be made at the outset is that a society today wears two hats:

  • Classification - a private, contractual relationship with the owner, under the society's own rules. Class is not a government function, and the society does not act for the flag State when it assigns it.
  • Statutory work as a Recognized Organization - work delegated by the flag State under SOLAS regulation XI-1/1, in which the society surveys and certifies against the conventions on the Administration's behalf and subject to the Code for Recognized Organizations, resolutions MSC.349(92) and MEPC.237(65), mandatory since 1 January 2015. Here the society acts for the flag State, which retains responsibility.

2. How class rules are made

Rules are not written from theory alone. They are built from service experience - damage and defect returns from the surveyed fleet; from casualty investigation; from research and direct calculation, including finite element analysis and fatigue assessment; and from technical committees drawing on owners, builders, makers and the society's own surveyors. Because a ship trades worldwide and changes flag and society, divergence between societies is itself a hazard, so the members harmonise through IACS: Unified Requirements setting minimum standards each member must implement, Unified Interpretations of convention text, Procedural Requirements, and jointly developed rules - most importantly the Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers.

3. What the structural, mechanical and electrical rules cover

  • Structural - scantlings of plating, stiffeners and primary members; longitudinal and local strength; fatigue life; corrosion additions and coating requirements; loading conditions and hull girder limits; and the enhanced close-up survey requirements for bulk carriers and tankers.
  • Mechanical - main propulsion and essential auxiliary machinery; shafting, propellers and gearing; boilers and pressure vessels; piping systems; steering gear; and the type approval of components and materials.
  • Electrical - generation, distribution and protection, including discrimination and short-circuit capability; essential and emergency services and their sources; cable installation; high-voltage systems; and equipment for hazardous areas.

4. Implementation through the ship's life

  • Design - plan approval of structure, machinery and electrical installations against the rules, review of direct calculations, and type approval of equipment so that approved components need not be re-justified on each ship.
  • Construction - approval of materials and their manufacturers; certification of components; qualification of welding procedures and welders; witnessing of non-destructive testing and tank testing; survey during construction at the yard; and attendance at trials before the certificate is issued.
  • Maintenance in service - the periodical survey cycle that keeps class alive: annual, intermediate and renewal or special surveys, docking and in-water surveys, tailshaft and boiler surveys. Alternatives exist where they can be justified - continuous survey of hull or machinery spreading the items across the cycle, and an approved planned maintenance scheme allowing certain machinery items to be credited on the Chief Engineer's records. Where a defect is found, the society may impose a condition of class or recommendation with a date for rectification, and class may be suspended or withdrawn if it is not met.

5. Why compliance with class rules is now mentioned in SOLAS chapter II-1

The reference is SOLAS regulation II-1/3-1, "Structural, mechanical and electrical requirements for ships". It provides that ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society which is recognized by the Administration in accordance with the provisions of regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration which provide an equivalent level of safety.

The reason it is there is the gap it closes. SOLAS regulates subdivision and damage stability, machinery and electrical installations, fire protection and life-saving. But SOLAS never contained scantling rules - it does not state how thick a plate must be or what section modulus a hull girder must have. That had always been left to the classification societies as a matter of private contract between owner, society and underwriter. The consequence was that, as a matter of convention law, there was no requirement that a ship be built to any structural standard at all: class was commercially indispensable and legally optional.

Regulation II-1/3-1 converts that commercial arrangement into a statutory obligation, and four things follow:

  • compliance with a recognized society's rules becomes a SOLAS requirement, enforceable by the flag State and inspectable by port State control;
  • the obligation runs to maintenance, not only to design and construction - so loss or suspension of class becomes a SOLAS non-compliance, not merely a commercial and insurance problem;
  • the society whose rules are relied on must be recognized under XI-1/1 and must operate under the RO Code, so the standard is not simply whatever any body chooses to publish; and
  • an Administration may still use its own national standards, but only where they provide an equivalent level of safety - preserving flag State sovereignty without opening a loophole.

This also completes the picture begun by goal-based standards: for bulk carriers and oil tankers, the class rules relied on under II-1/3-1 must themselves have been verified at IMO as conforming to the goals and functional requirements under SOLAS II-1/3-10. SOLAS therefore requires compliance with class rules, and separately requires those rules to have been shown to be good enough.

(The printed question refers to "SOLAS ch.ll-1", using two lowercase letter L's. The reference intended is chapter II-1, and within it regulation 3-1.)

6. The harmonized system, and where these surveys sit

Before harmonization, each certificate carried its own survey dates, so a ship could be under survey almost continuously and a certificate could expire while the ship was at sea. The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification, introduced by the 1988 SOLAS and Load Lines Protocols and extended to MARPOL, aligns the certificates on a common five-year cycle with common anniversary dates and a common window of three months either side, so that surveys can be taken together. The current guidance is the Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2025, resolution A.1207(34), adopted 3 December 2025, which revokes A.1186(33). The system uses initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal and additional surveys, plus inspection of the outside of the ship's bottom.

The heart of limb (a): what gap does II-1/3-1 close?

Most candidates will state that SOLAS II-1/3-1 requires compliance with class rules. Very few will say why that regulation had to exist, and that is the whole question. SOLAS has never contained scantling rules - it does not say how thick a plate must be. Structural standards were always a matter of private contract between owner, society and underwriter. So before II-1/3-1, a ship could in principle satisfy SOLAS while being built to no structural standard whatever, because class was commercially indispensable but legally optional. The regulation makes it legally compulsory, and extends the duty to maintenance - which is what turns loss of class from a commercial problem into a convention breach that port State control can act on.

The two-hats distinction is the other half of limb (a)

Classification is private and contractual; statutory certification as a Recognized Organization is delegated by the flag State under SOLAS XI-1/1 and governed by the RO Code. The same surveyor may do both on the same day, which is exactly why the distinction is worth stating. Note the resolution numbers carefully: the RO Code is MSC.349(92) with MEPC.237(65), mandatory from 1 January 2015. Getting the session number wrong here is an easy and visible slip.

Limb (b) has one fact almost nobody knows

The periodical survey is not simply "the survey that happens periodically". It attaches to only two certificates - Safety Equipment and Safety Radio - and it has a different frequency for each. For the Safety Equipment Certificate it falls at the second or third anniversary and replaces one of the annual surveys. For the Safety Radio Certificate it falls at every anniversary. A candidate who states both frequencies and notes that the radio survey is annual because distress communication cannot be allowed to degrade unnoticed has said something precise where most answers say something vague.

Why harmonization was needed at all

The point of HSSC is not the survey types; it is the alignment. Before it, certificates carried independent dates, so a ship might be under survey almost continuously and could still find a certificate expiring at sea. HSSC puts everything on a common five-year cycle with common anniversary dates and a common three-months-either-side window, so surveys can be combined into one attendance. If limb (b) explains the alignment rather than merely listing survey names, it has answered "relevance" as well as "difference".

Common mistakes

  • Treating classification and statutory certification as the same activity. One is private contract; the other is delegated flag State authority.
  • Saying SOLAS contains the structural rules. It does not - that is precisely why II-1/3-1 refers out to class rules.
  • Citing the RO Code as MSC.349(90). It is MSC.349(92), with MEPC.237(65).
  • Saying periodical surveys apply to all certificates. They apply only to the Cargo Ship Safety Equipment and Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificates.
  • Giving one frequency for the periodical survey. There are two, depending on the certificate.
  • Confusing the periodical survey with the intermediate survey. Both may fall at the second or third anniversary, but the intermediate attaches to different certificates and has a different scope.
  • Citing A.1186(33) as the current Survey Guidelines. It was revoked by A.1207(34), adopted 3 December 2025.
  • Forgetting that II-1/3-1 permits national standards of equivalent safety as an alternative to class rules.

Examiner traps

"Your ship loses class mid-voyage. Is that a SOLAS matter?" Yes. II-1/3-1 requires the ship to be maintained in compliance with the rules of a recognized society, so loss of class is a convention non-compliance and not merely a commercial and insurance issue.

"Must a ship be classed?" Strictly, it must comply with the requirements of a recognized society or with national standards of the Administration providing an equivalent level of safety. In practice class is universal, but the regulation offers the alternative and saying so shows the text has been read.

"When is your periodical survey due?" Ask which certificate. Safety Equipment: second or third anniversary, in place of an annual. Safety Radio: every anniversary.

"Who is responsible if the recognized organization gets it wrong?" The Administration. Recognition delegates the work, not the responsibility - which is the point of the RO Code.

Understand this first

The point of FSA is that it argues about a rule before the rule exists, instead of after a casualty. It forces the Organization to say what could go wrong, how likely and how bad it would be, what could be done about it, what that would cost, and only then what should be required. What it does not do - and says so plainly - is tell anyone how much risk is acceptable. That judgement stays with Member States; FSA simply makes them state it out loud.

Model written answer

1. What FSA is, and its objectives

The governing instrument is MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, the Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment for use in the IMO rule-making process, dated 9 April 2018. It defines FSA as:

"a structured and systematic methodology, aimed at enhancing maritime safety, including protection of life, health, the marine environment and property, by using risk analysis and cost-benefit assessment."

Its objectives follow from that:

  • to evaluate new regulations, or to compare existing regulations with possible improvements, before they are made;
  • to achieve a balance between the technical and operational issues involved, including the human element, and between safety or environmental protection and cost;
  • to let decision makers at IMO see the effect of a proposed regulatory change in terms of benefits - lives saved, pollution avoided - and of the costs falling on the industry as a whole and on individual parties; and
  • to produce regulatory change that is equitable between the parties affected, and so to aid the achievement of consensus.

It may be applied by a Member State or an organization in consultative status proposing amendments to safety or pollution-related instruments, or by a Committee or a subsidiary body asked to give a balanced view of a framework of regulations. It is not intended to be applied in all circumstances: it is particularly relevant to proposals with far-reaching implications in cost or in legislative and administrative burden, and to situations where risk reduction is needed but the right action is unclear.

2. The characteristics that define it

  • Proactive rather than reactive - designed to find hazards before they produce a casualty, which is why step 1 uses creative as well as historical technique.
  • Structured and systematic, so different parties analysing the same problem reach comparable conclusions.
  • Transparent, documented and auditable, so it can be understood and challenged by parties who are not risk specialists.
  • Generic, not ship-specific. It works on a generic model - a set of functions common to all ships or areas under consideration - because it supports rule-making for a class of ships.
  • Proportionate - depth commensurate with the significance of the problem, with a preliminary coarse qualitative screening to focus effort.
  • Iterative, not linear. Step 5 interacts with each of the other steps, and decision makers may revise the problem and send the study back.
  • Cost-aware - it explicitly weighs the cost of a safety measure against the risk it removes.

3. The process

The study begins with the decision makers defining the problem and any boundary conditions or constraints - a preparatory step, not one of the numbered five, in which the generic model is established. The methodology then comprises five steps:

  • Step 1 - identification of hazards;
  • Step 2 - risk analysis;
  • Step 3 - risk control options;
  • Step 4 - cost-benefit assessment;
  • Step 5 - recommendations for decision-making.

Two definitions from the Guidelines carry the whole method: a hazard is a potential to threaten human life, health, property or the environment, and risk is the combination of the frequency and the severity of the consequence.

4. Step 1 - Hazard Identification

The purpose is to produce a list of hazards and associated scenarios, prioritized by risk level. The approach combines two kinds of technique deliberately:

  • the creative element - structured group review by experts in ship design, operation and management, aimed at identifying causes and effects of accidents. This is what keeps the process proactive and not confined to hazards that have already materialised;
  • the analytical element - ensuring that previous experience is properly taken into account, using applicable regulations and codes, accident statistics and known hazard lists.

A coarse analysis of possible causes, initiating events and outcomes is carried out for each accident scenario. The identified hazards are then ranked using a risk matrix, in which clearly defined frequency and consequence categories combine to give a risk level, so that scenarios judged to be of minor significance can be discarded and effort concentrated where it matters.

The output of step 1 is a list of hazards and their associated scenarios, including initiating events, together with an assessment of those accident scenarios prioritized by risk level.

5. Step 2 - Risk Analysis

Step 2 is a detailed investigation of the causes, initiating events and consequences of the more important scenarios identified in step 1. Where step 1 casts a wide net and ranks coarsely, step 2 models the risk properly, so that attention can be focused on high-risk areas and the factors that influence the level of risk can be identified and evaluated. Risks to people, to the environment and to property are addressed as appropriate to the problem.

The techniques model the sequence from cause to consequence - fault trees working backwards from an event to its causes, event trees working forwards from an initiating event to its outcomes, combined in a risk contribution tree. Quantification uses accident and failure data; where data are unavailable, calculation, simulation or established techniques for expert judgement may be used. The Guidelines require incident reports, near misses and operational failures to be reviewed objectively and their reliability, uncertainty and validity reported, and require sensitivity and uncertainty analysis to be considered and reported with the quantitative data.

The output of step 2 is the identification of the high-risk areas which need to be addressed, and an explanation of the risk models used.

6. Step 3 - Risk Control Options

Step 3 generates measures to control the risks that step 2 identified. A risk control measure is a means of controlling a single element of risk; a risk control option is a combination of such measures. Options may act on the frequency of an event - prevention - or on its consequences - mitigation - and may be engineering, operational or managerial. The output is a range of practicable options, grouped so that they can be costed.

7. Step 4 - Cost Benefit Assessment

Step 4 identifies and compares the benefits and costs of each option from step 3. It proceeds in five stages: consider the risks assessed in step 2 to define the base case; arrange the options so their costs and benefits can be understood; estimate the costs and benefits for all options; estimate and compare their cost-effectiveness, expressed as the cost per unit of risk reduction, by dividing the net cost by the risk reduction achieved; and rank the options so that those which are not cost-effective or not practical can be screened out.

  • Costs are expressed as life-cycle costs, and may include initial, operating, training, inspection, certification and decommissioning costs.
  • Benefits may include reductions in fatalities, injuries, casualties, environmental damage and clean-up, indemnity of third-party liabilities, and an increase in the average life of ships.
  • The assessment is carried out for the situation overall and then for the interested entities most affected - the person, organization, company, coastal State or flag State touched by the accident or by the cost of the proposed rule.

Cost-effectiveness in relation to safety of life is expressed through two indices. Gross Cost of Averting a Fatality (GCAF) is the ratio of the marginal, additional cost of the risk control option to the reduction in risk to personnel measured in fatalities averted. Net Cost of Averting a Fatality (NCAF) is the same ratio but takes account of the economic benefits of the option as well as its cost. The Guidelines recommend considering GCAF first, precisely because NCAF's inclusion of economic benefits can be misused to favour a preferred option by crediting it with more benefit than its rivals.

One point must be stated carefully. The Guidelines do not impose a mandatory monetary threshold for averting a fatality. The values tabulated in the appendix are described as provided for illustrative purposes only, are not static and are to be updated, and any study must explicitly define the values it has used. Quoting a single universal figure as though IMO had mandated it is wrong.

8. Step 5 - Recommendations, and what FSA does not settle

Step 5 defines recommendations for the decision makers, presented in an auditable and traceable manner and understandable by parties who are not risk specialists. They rest on the comparison and ranking of the hazards and their causes, the comparison and ranking of the risk control options by cost and benefit, and the identification of those options which keep risk as low as reasonably practicable. Recommended options are to be submitted in SMART terms - specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound - and accompanied by their intended application, such as ship type and construction date.

Finally, the Guidelines are candid about their own limit: "There are several standards for risk acceptance criteria, none as yet universally accepted." FSA does not tell the Organization what level of risk is tolerable. It requires the criteria used to be made explicit, so that the judgement is visible and can be argued about - which is a decision for Member States, not an output of the method.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Sixteen marks, no limbs, but the stem is a specification: objectives, characteristics and processes, and then "specific explanation" of three named steps. That is five deliverables, and an answer that lists the five steps and stops has delivered one of them. Characteristics in particular are almost always omitted, and they are cheap marks - proactive, systematic, transparent, generic, proportionate, iterative, cost-aware. Give steps 1, 2 and 4 a heading and a proper paragraph each, because the examiner has told you they carry extra weight, and treat steps 3 and 5 more briefly.

Use the instrument's own step names

The question uses the term "Risk Analysis" for step 2, and that is the Guidelines' own name for it. Some coaching material calls it "assessment of risks", which is not the instrument's term. Use the five names exactly: identification of hazards, risk analysis, risk control options, cost-benefit assessment, recommendations for decision-making. Precision here is free and it signals that the source has been read.

Do not quote a mandatory cost per life

This is the sharpest trap on the question. A figure for the cost of averting a fatality circulates widely and is repeated as though IMO had mandated it. The Guidelines do not prescribe one. Appendix 7 says its values are provided for illustrative purposes only, that they are not static and should be updated, and that a study must explicitly define the values it uses. Section 9.2.2 goes further: "There are several standards for risk acceptance criteria, none as yet universally accepted." The correct answer explains what GCAF and NCAF are, notes the recommendation to use GCAF first, and says that the criterion value is a matter for the study to state explicitly - not a number handed down.

The preparatory step is not one of the five

Problem definition comes first, and candidates often either omit it or number it as step 1, displacing everything. The Guidelines are clear: the process begins with the decision makers defining the problem and its boundary conditions, and the five numbered steps then follow. Getting this right, and adding that step 5 interacts with each of the other steps so the process is iterative rather than a one-way chain, shows the flow chart has been understood rather than memorised.

Why FSA is generic - and why that matters

FSA is applied to a generic model, a set of functions common to all ships or areas under consideration. It is not a risk assessment of one ship, and it is not the shipboard risk assessment required under the ISM Code. It exists to support rule-making for a class of ships. Candidates who describe FSA as something a Chief Engineer performs before a critical job have confused it with operational risk assessment, and that confusion is easy for an examiner to expose.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the five steps and stopping, without objectives or characteristics.
  • Numbering problem definition as one of the five steps.
  • Quoting a monetary criterion for averting a fatality as an IMO requirement.
  • Confusing GCAF with NCAF, or failing to say that GCAF should be considered first.
  • Describing FSA as a shipboard risk assessment rather than a rule-making methodology applied to a generic model.
  • Omitting the outputs of each step - the Guidelines specify them, and they are easy marks.
  • Forgetting that sensitivity and uncertainty analysis must be considered and reported.
  • Presenting FSA as linear, when step 5 interacts with every other step.
  • Saying FSA determines what risk is acceptable. It does not - it requires the criteria to be made explicit.

Examiner traps

"What is the value IMO puts on a human life?" The Guidelines do not set one. They define GCAF and NCAF as cost-effectiveness indices and say the criterion values are illustrative, not static, and must be explicitly defined by the study. Refusing the invented figure is the correct answer.

"Why use GCAF rather than NCAF?" Because NCAF credits the option with its economic benefits, which can be misused to push a preferred option by attributing more benefit to it than to its rivals. Consider GCAF first; NCAF may be considered if the result is near the criterion.

"Step 1 uses accident statistics. Is that enough?" No - the method deliberately pairs the analytical element with a creative element, so that hazards which have not yet caused an accident are still identified. That is what makes FSA proactive.

"You did an FSA for your ship's crane." Then it was not an FSA in this sense. FSA is applied to a generic model for IMO rule-making, not to a single ship's equipment.
