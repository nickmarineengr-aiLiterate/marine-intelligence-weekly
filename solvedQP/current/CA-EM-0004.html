Surface Cracks on the Tail-End Shaft Keyway

One limb of a recurring multi-part question — B. Surface cracks on the keyway of the tail-end shaft
During a tail-shaft survey in dry dock, surface cracks are found on the keyway of the tail-end shaft. State the steps you would take as Chief Engineer to deal with the problem, and explain the effects if cracks of this kind are not dealt with at the proper time.

Understand first

A keyed propeller is not held on by its key. It is held by the interference grip of the taper, and the key is a safeguard that stops the propeller turning on the cone if that grip is ever lost. Candidates who believe the key transmits the torque write the wrong answer to this question, because they then treat a cracked keyway as a torque problem. It is a stress-concentration problem. The keyway is a square-cornered slot cut into the most highly stressed part of the shaft - the forward end of the taper, where the bending stress reverses once per revolution and where sea water can reach the steel past a worn aft seal. Sharp corners, a reversing stress field and an electrolyte is the classic recipe for corrosion fatigue, which is why the class rules single out this exact location for crack detection, and why a crack found here is a serious defect rather than a blemish.

Exam plan

Answer in two halves, because the question is in two halves

The examiner asks for steps and then for the effects of delay. Give them separately and label them. A single narrative that mixes actions with consequences loses the second half of the marks, because the consequences never get stated as consequences.

  • Steps - contain and record; establish the extent by NDT; notify Company, Class and underwriters; agree a repair route with the attending surveyor; re-examine and re-certify; find the root cause and close it out.
  • Effects of delay - state the mechanism first (crack growth under reversed bending, accelerated by corrosion fatigue), then the physical outcome (shaft fracture, propeller loss, an open stern tube), then the statutory and commercial outcome (class withdrawn, casualty report, off-hire, and an insurance argument about known damage).
  • Name the location precisely. 'Forward end of the taper, at the keyway corner' earns more than 'on the shaft', because it shows you know why that is where it cracks.
  • Do not offer to weld it. Welding a tail-end shaft is not a repair a Chief Engineer proposes. Say instead what the class-approved routes are.
Model answer — current framework

1. Fix the situation before fixing the shaft

The shaft is out of service in a dock and the propeller is off, so there is no operational emergency. What there is instead is an evidence and authority problem: a defect affecting classification has been found, and everything that follows depends on it being recorded properly and reported to the right people before anybody starts grinding.

  • Stop work on the shafting and prevent any further dressing, cleaning or handling of the cracked area. A crack that has been ground before it was measured cannot be measured.
  • Record it as found - photographs with a scale in frame, the position relative to the big end of the taper and to each end of the keyway, the orientation of the crack relative to the shaft axis, and the condition of the surrounding surface (corrosion pitting, fretting marks, evidence of a working key).
  • Raise it in the safety management system as a non-conformity or hazardous occurrence and inform the Designated Person Ashore, so that the report exists in the SMS and not only in a dock diary.
  • Preserve the surrounding evidence - aft seal and liner condition, stern tube oil analysis history, bearing wear-down readings, the propeller bore and key fit, and the shaft's own alignment records. Every one of these is an input to root cause, and every one of them disappears once the job is reassembled.

2. Establish the nature and extent - this is a measurement job

A visible surface crack is a symptom. What matters to the repair decision is how deep it goes, how far it runs, and whether it is one crack or a family of them.

  • Clean and examine by an approved surface crack detection method - magnetic particle inspection on the steel shaft, or dye penetrant where magnetisation is impractical - over the whole forward portion of the taper including the keyway and both its ends, not merely the visible defect. The class rules require crack detection over that region precisely because a single crack there is rarely alone.
  • Determine depth. Ultrasonic examination, and progressive local grinding with re-examination at each stage, establish whether this is a shallow surface indication or a developed fatigue crack.
  • Examine the matching features - the propeller keyway and bore, the key itself for signs of having carried load, and the taper contact area by blue bedding. A contact area short of the rule minimum tells you the grip was never right.
  • Have the examination witnessed by the attending classification surveyor and by the underwriters' surveyor, and get the NDT operator's certification and report into the file. An unwitnessed measurement will be argued about later.
  • Check the shaft against its rule diameter. How much metal may lawfully be removed is set by the shaft's designed scantling, and that number governs every repair option below.

Notice to underwriters is early and factual. A Chief Engineer's report should describe what was found and measured and should not offer a conclusion about cause, because the cause is exactly what the policy turns on.

4. Agree the repair route with the surveyor

The routes below are in the order they are normally considered. Which one applies is decided by depth, by how much metal the rule diameter allows to be lost, and by the surveyor - not by the Chief Engineer alone.

  1. Local removal and blending. The crack is ground out completely and the excavation blended into a generous radius, with the keyway corners radiused rather than left square, and the area re-examined by NDT to prove the crack has gone. This is the usual outcome for a shallow indication, and it also improves the detail that caused it.
  2. Machining the taper. Where cracking and corrosion are spread over the cone, the taper may be re-machined to a fair surface. This moves the propeller forward on the cone, so the axial position must be recovered - typically by a spacer or distance piece at the coupling - and the bore must be re-bedded and the contact area re-proved.
  3. Convert to a keyless fit, where depth or repeated cracking makes the keyway itself the problem. Removing the keyway removes the stress raiser altogether, and an oil-injection or press fit then transmits the torque entirely by friction. This is a design change: it needs class approval and the maker's calculation, and it is the permanent answer to a recurring keyway crack.
  4. Renew the shaft. Where the crack is deep, where grinding it out would take the diameter below the rule scantling, or where the crack runs circumferentially rather than axially, the shaft is replaced, in class-approved certified material.
  5. Welding is not a routine option. A tail-end shaft is a highly stressed forging and welding on it is not permitted as a general repair. Any proposal of that kind belongs to the society and the shaft maker, not to the ship.

Close the job properly. Re-assemble to the maker's procedure - pull-up or oil-injection figures recorded, contact area proved, key fit checked, aft seal renewed, stern tube oil renewed and sampled - and record the final NDT clearance. Where the society is not fully satisfied it will impose a condition of class with a date; that date is then a maintenance commitment, and letting it lapse suspends class as surely as the original crack would have.

5. Root cause, because the crack is a message

A keyway crack is a fatigue crack, and fatigue cracks come from a stress the shaft was not designed to see, from a detail that concentrates the stress it was, or from an environment that helps the crack along. Each of those has a different fix.

  • Loss of taper grip. If the interference fit was never right, or was lost through corrosion or incorrect pull-up, the propeller works on the cone and drives the key - which puts a torque load into a slot that was never meant to carry one. Evidence: fretting in the bore, a bright or hammered key, contact area below the rule minimum.
  • Sea water at the steel. A worn or leaking aft seal admits sea water into the taper and keyway. With a reversing stress field this becomes corrosion fatigue, in which a crack grows at a stress well below the dry fatigue limit. Evidence: pitting, rust staining out of the keyway, water in the stern tube oil.
  • The detail itself. Sharp keyway corners, a badly cut keyway, or a keyway ending too close to the big end of the cone. Fix it in the repair - radius the corners rather than simply restoring the geometry that failed.
  • Abnormal loading. Prolonged running in or near the barred speed range, a torsional resonance, heavy propeller-induced vibration, a damaged or fouled propeller running out of balance, ice contact or a grounding, or severe misalignment and bearing wear-down putting bending into the cone.
  • Fix the cause in the plan, not in the report. Whatever is found should come back as a change to the maintenance system - seal renewal interval, stern tube oil sampling, wear-down recording, barred-speed discipline, propeller inspection each dock - and it should be verified at the next internal audit.

6. The effects if the cracks are not dealt with at the proper time

This is the half of the question most answers under-write. Give the mechanism first, then the outcomes.

  • The crack grows, and it grows fastest here. Every revolution reverses the bending stress at the forward end of the taper. A crack at a square keyway corner is a ready-made initiation site, and once started it propagates under a load the intact shaft carried comfortably. Sea water accelerates it. Growth is not linear: it is slow, and then it is sudden.
  • Fatigue failure of the tail-end shaft, typically at or just forward of the taper - and there is no warning stage a watchkeeper can act on.
  • Loss of the propeller and the after length of shaft. Total and immediate loss of propulsion, leaving a drifting vessel - dangerous anywhere, and far worse in a traffic separation scheme, in restricted water, or on a lee shore.
  • Loss of watertight integrity. Once the shaft leaves the stern tube, the tube is an open hole below the waterline. Progressive flooding of the after spaces, loss of stability margin, and in the worst case loss of the ship - which is what makes this a safety question rather than a maintenance question.
  • Secondary damage. A shaft failing under load can wreck the stern tube bearings and seals, damage the after peak structure, and overspeed the main engine at the instant the load disappears.
  • Statutory and class consequences. Class suspended or withdrawn; the certificates founded on class fall with it; a casualty report and investigation follow; and flag State and port State will both want the survey history.
  • Commercial and insurance consequences. Emergency dry docking and towage, a far larger repair than the one avoided, off-hire and cargo claims - and an argument with underwriters that the owner is likely to lose, because a defect that was found, recorded and then left is known damage and the duty to maintain the ship's condition after survey was not discharged. A crack that could have been ground out in a scheduled dock becomes an uninsured casualty.

The one-sentence answer to the second half. A cracked propeller blade costs efficiency and, at worst, a blade. A cracked tail-end shaft keyway costs the propeller, the propulsion and potentially the ship - which is why the two limbs of this question are not the same question.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks in this limb sit in three places: the authority sequence (Company, Class, underwriters - and knowing what each of them decides), the named repair routes together with the constraint that governs them (the rule diameter), and the mechanism of delay. Weak answers describe a repair in loving mechanical detail and never mention the classification society, which is the one party that decides whether the repair is acceptable at all.

The two traps

  • Treating the key as the torque path. It is not, and the whole diagnosis changes if you think it is. The taper grip transmits the torque; the key is a safeguard. A working key is evidence of a defective fit.
  • Answering the propeller-blade limb twice. The blade limb and the keyway limb share an opening scenario and nothing else. If your answer would be equally true of a cracked blade, you have not answered this limb.

What this answer deliberately does not claim

It gives no permissible crack depth, no grinding limit, no minimum taper contact percentage, no pull-up figure and no survey interval. Those are set by the individual society's rules and the shaft maker's data for the specific shaft, and they differ between societies and by seal and lubrication arrangement. A universal figure would be worse than none, because it would be quoted in an exam as though it were a rule. What the answer does state is the correct source for each of them.

Critical numbers
  • None quoted, on purpose. Permissible crack depth, grinding limits, minimum taper contact area and pull-up figures are set by the individual classification society's rules and by the shaft maker's data for that shaft, and they differ between societies - see the study guide.
Critical regulation
IACS Unified Requirement Z21 and the individual society's rules govern the screwshaft survey and require crack detection by an approved method in way of the forward portion of the taper INCLUDING the keyway, with the propeller drawn where the shaft is keyed. SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 requires the condition of the ship to be maintained after survey and requires damage or defect to be reported. The ISM Code makes it a non-conformity to be reported to the DPA. Nothing in SOLAS prescribes the repair - that is class and the shaft maker.
Major trap
Writing the propeller-blade answer again, or proposing to weld the tail-end shaft. The second is worse: it is the one answer that tells the examiner you do not know what kind of component you are dealing with.
