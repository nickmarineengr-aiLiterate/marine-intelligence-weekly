Preserving the Main Propulsion Diesel Engine for a Three-Month Commercial Lay-Up

Your vessel is to be laid up afloat for three months for commercial reasons, and all machinery is to be preserved for re-commissioning when required. Describe the actions you would take as Chief Engineer to prepare and preserve the main propulsion diesel engine for the lay-up period, and the records you would keep so that it can be brought back into service.

Understand first

A lay-up is not a long weekend. The distinction that carries the marks is between STOPPING an engine and PRESERVING one, and they are almost opposite activities: stopping is what you do to make the engine safe now, preserving is what you do to make it startable later. Everything that keeps a running engine healthy is a by-product of running it - the oil film is renewed by the pumps, the water side is kept moving and inhibited, the combustion spaces are kept dry and above dew point by the fire, and the whole machine is kept warm. Take the fire away and every one of those protections stops at once, while the seawater, the humid air and the acidic soot residues do not. So the engine does not merely sit idle; it corrodes, and it corrodes fastest in exactly the places that are most expensive to repair - liner running surfaces, bearing shells, turbocharger rotors and cooler tube plates. The second thing to see is that three months is SHORT. It is long enough to do real damage and too short to justify a full cold preservation with the engine opened up, so the correct regime is a warm lay-up in which the engine is preserved but recoverable at reasonable notice. An answer that describes a two-year cold lay-up has answered a different question. The third thing, and the one weak answers omit entirely, is that a preservation is only as good as the record of it: every blank fitted is a blank that must be removed, and an engine returned to service with a blank still in place is destroyed in minutes.

Exam plan

Shape the answer by system, not by chronology

The examiner is looking for coverage. A narrative that walks through one afternoon will cover three systems well and forget five. Head the answer with the decision on the lay-up regime, then take the engine system by system - lubricating oil, fuel, combustion spaces and running gear, cooling water, air and exhaust, starting air, control and safety - and close with the turning programme and the records. That structure makes an omission visible to you while you are writing it.

  • Open by fixing the regime. One short paragraph: warm lay-up afloat, engine preserved but recoverable at notice, and why three months settles that.
  • Then the systems. One short heading each, two or three concrete actions under it. Concrete beats complete - "dose the jacket water to the recommended nitrite level and circulate weekly" scores where "maintain the cooling system" does not.
  • Then the turning programme. This is the single most cited preservation action for a main engine and it deserves its own heading.
  • Close on records and the blanking register. Two or three lines. It costs almost nothing and it is what separates a Chief Engineer's answer from a rating's.

What to say about regulation, and what not to

Do not manufacture an instrument. No IMO convention prescribes how to preserve a diesel engine, and an answer that invents a regulation number will be marked down harder than one that says none applies. What IS worth two marks is the point that a lay-up suspends none of the ship's statutory obligations: SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 requires the ship's condition to be maintained so that it remains fit to proceed to sea after survey, and it is the classification society - not a convention - that operates the laid-up notation and the survey arrangements that go with it.

Model answer — current framework

1. Fix the lay-up regime before touching anything

My first act is a decision, not a job. Three months, afloat, for commercial reasons means a warm (hot) lay-up: the ship stays manned at a reduced level, essential auxiliaries keep running, and the main engine is preserved in a condition from which it can be recovered in days rather than weeks. I would not adopt a cold lay-up regime - opening the engine, draining every system and preserving internally for a long idle period - because the recovery cost is out of proportion to a three-month stop and because the owner will want the option to trade at short notice.

Having fixed that, I would establish and record the engine's condition before it stops, because a preservation programme that is not measured against a known starting condition proves nothing on reactivation. Before the final shutdown I would take a full set of performance readings, indicator or PMI cards, exhaust temperature deviations, turbocharger speeds and pressures, a lubricating oil sample for analysis, the latest cylinder liner and ring wear figures, running hours to the next scheduled overhaul, and the list of outstanding defects and Conditions of Class. That record is the datum for everything that follows.

I would also confirm - as the Chief Engineer's contribution to a decision the owner and master make - that the classification society and the flag Administration have been notified, that the society's laid-up requirements are known and held on board, and that the underwriters have been advised, since lay-up affects cover and may attract a return of premium. The engine-room programme is then written to satisfy those requirements rather than being reconciled with them afterwards.

2. Lubricating oil system - keep the film, keep the oil, keep it dry

  • Keep the system circulating rather than static. I would run the main lubricating oil pump for a period each week, with the purifier in circuit and the sump heating maintained, so that every bearing, the crosshead guides and the camshaft or hydraulic system are wetted and the oil is kept above the temperature at which condensed water separates out.
  • Analyse before you decide. A sample sent ashore before shutdown tells me whether the charge is fit to be preserved in place or should be renewed. Oil already carrying water, a depleted additive package or high insolubles will not improve over three months in a cold sump - it will become the corrosive agent I am trying to exclude.
  • Keep the crankcase dry. Condensation is the enemy: I would keep the sump heating on at a low setting, keep the crankcase closed except for planned inspections, and run the crankcase extraction or a dehumidifier so that the space stays above dew point. Where the manufacturer's guidance calls for it, a vapour-phase corrosion inhibiting oil can be used in place of the running charge.
  • Attend to the cylinder oil system separately - drain and blank the daily tank if the oil is prone to separation, and keep lubricator hydraulic or mechanical drives free by turning them with the engine.

3. Fuel system - get the heavy fuel out before it sets

  • Change over to distillate and run through. Before the last shutdown I would change over from heavy fuel to a low-sulphur distillate and run long enough to clear the supply and circulating system, the injection pumps and the injectors. Heavy fuel left to cool in an injection system gums, waxes and seizes plungers and needles, and the damage is discovered on reactivation when it costs most.
  • Drain and blank the heavy fuel service and settling tanks that will not be kept hot, and secure the heating and trace heating so that fuel is not cooked in a static system.
  • Blank or isolate injectors where the manufacturer recommends removal, and preserve removed injectors with a light oil in a clean, dry store, tagged against the register.

4. Combustion spaces and running gear - the surfaces that cannot be replaced cheaply

  • Coat the liner running surfaces. Through the scavenge ports or the open scavenge space, I would apply a preservation oil to the exposed liner walls, and repeat it after each turning so that the surfaces the piston has just uncovered are also protected.
  • Stop the through-draught. The single most damaging condition is moist air moving through a cold engine, because it condenses on the coldest surfaces and carries the acidic sulphur residues of combustion into the liner and ring pack. I would blank the scavenge air inlet and the exhaust outlet so that the gas path is closed at both ends.
  • Dry the space. With the path blanked, I would circulate dehumidified air through the scavenge space and, where fitted, the under-piston spaces, and record the relative humidity so that the preservation can be shown to be working rather than assumed to be.
  • Clean the scavenge spaces and under-piston drains before closing them. Preserving a space full of oily carbon deposits preserves the deposits, and those deposits hold both moisture and acid.

5. Turning programme - the action the examiner is looking for

A main engine left in one position for three months develops corrosion and settled contact at exactly the loaded interfaces - the piston rings against the liner, the bearing shells at the point of contact, and the thrust faces. I would therefore turn the engine on the turning gear at a defined interval - typically weekly, and to the interval the engine builder specifies - taking care to stop it in a different position each time so that no ring, journal or thrust surface stays loaded in the same place.

The turning is done with the lubricating oil pump running so that the bearings and guides are supplied while the engine moves, with indicator cocks open, the starting air shut off and locked, and the turning gear engaged and the engine blocked from starting in accordance with the ship's permit-to-work system. Each turn is logged: date, position stopped in, oil pressure, anything unusual felt or heard. That log is one of the two or three documents a reactivation surveyor will actually ask to see.

6. Cooling water systems - inhibit or drain, never half of either

  • Jacket cooling water: keep it in and keep it treated. The safest condition for a three-month lay-up is a full system, correctly dosed with the manufacturer's corrosion inhibitor at the recommended concentration, circulated periodically by the jacket water pump and with the heater kept on. I would test the inhibitor level and the chlorides before shutdown and again during the lay-up, because an under-dosed full system corrodes faster than a properly drained dry one.
  • Never leave a system partly drained. A half-empty jacket is the worst of both worlds: it presents a wetted surface, an air space and a waterline, and the waterline is where the pitting concentrates.
  • Seawater side: drain, flush and dry. The seawater circuit - central coolers, lubricating oil and jacket water coolers, the charge air cooler seawater side and the associated pumps - would be flushed through with fresh water to remove salt, then drained down and left open to dry, with the pumps' casings drained and their shafts turned periodically.
  • Check and record the sacrificial anodes and any impressed-current arrangement in the coolers and boxes while the system is open, since this is the cheapest opportunity to renew them that the ship will get.

7. Air and exhaust side - turbocharger, charge air cooler and uptake

  • Turbocharger. Water-wash the turbine and compressor before the final shutdown if the arrangement allows, then blank the air intake and the exhaust connections. Where the bearings are separately lubricated, preserve them to the maker's instruction; where they run on the engine's system, they are covered by the weekly oil circulation. Rotate the rotor by hand at intervals and record it, and confirm the rotor is free before it is left.
  • Charge air cooler. Drain the water side, flush the air side and drain the condensate trap. A charge air cooler left with standing water is a tube-plate failure waiting for reactivation.
  • Exhaust uptake and economiser. Blank the uptake at deck level to keep rain and soot out, and drain and dry the exhaust gas economiser. Wet soot is strongly acidic and it attacks the very tubing that is most awkward to renew.

8. Starting air and control systems - keep the means of recovery alive

  • Keep one air compressor and one receiver in service at a reduced but useful pressure, drained regularly of moisture, so that the ship retains a means of starting and of operating pneumatic controls. Preserve the second set fully. Drain the receivers, the automatic drains and the low points of the starting air line at every check, since the water that accumulates there is what corrodes the starting valves.
  • Keep the control and safety systems powered where possible. Electronic governors, the engine control system and, on an electronically-controlled engine, the hydraulic power supply are better kept energised and warm than left cold. Where power must be removed, fit space heaters or desiccant in the cabinets and record the desiccant renewal.
  • Preserve and record the position of the safety devices - overspeed trip, oil mist detector, crankcase relief valves, jacket water and lubricating oil low-pressure trips. They must be proved on reactivation, so their condition at shutdown must be known.

9. The records - a preservation nobody can reverse is not a preservation

Two documents carry disproportionate weight and I would open both on the day the decision is taken.

  • A blanking and isolation register. Every blank fitted, every valve locked, every system drained, every component removed - numbered, tagged on the machine in a distinctive colour, signed for when fitted and signed for again when removed. An engine started with a scavenge or exhaust blank still in place is wrecked, and the register is the only thing that reliably prevents it.
  • A preservation log. The programme itself, written out as a schedule with a frequency against each task, and then signed as each is done: weekly turning with the stop position, weekly lubricating oil circulation, humidity readings, inhibitor and chloride tests, receiver draining, rotor rotation, and any defect observed.

Alongside them I would keep the pre-shutdown condition record, the oil analysis results, the maker's preservation instructions actually used, and the correspondence with class. Together these are what allow the engine to be handed to the next Chief Engineer, or to a reactivation surveyor, as a machine whose three idle months are accounted for rather than merely survived.

10. What a lay-up does not suspend

Finally, the point that is worth stating explicitly because it is frequently forgotten. A commercial lay-up suspends trading; it does not suspend the ship's statutory obligations. Under SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 the condition of the ship and its equipment must be maintained so that the ship remains fit to proceed to sea without danger, and no alteration may be made after survey without the Administration's sanction. What the classification society may do - and this is a class arrangement, not a convention one - is place the ship in a laid-up condition under an agreed lay-up plan, hold certain periodical surveys in abeyance for the duration, and require a lay-up condition survey and a reactivation survey before the ship returns to service. The detail differs between societies, so the governing document is the society's own lay-up requirements for this ship, obtained and held on board before the lay-up begins.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The mark scheme for a question like this rewards coverage of systems and specificity of action. Candidates lose marks in two predictable ways. The first is answering a different question - describing a long cold lay-up, or describing how to shut an engine down safely, neither of which is what was asked. The second is generality: "maintain the cooling system" is worth nothing, "keep the jacket water full, dosed to the recommended inhibitor level and circulated weekly, and test the inhibitor and chlorides" is worth the mark and the two beside it.

The single highest-yield paragraph is the turning programme, because it is the action most specific to preserving a large diesel engine rather than any machine. The second is the blanking register, because almost nobody writes it and it is the one thing in the answer that is unmistakably a Chief Engineer's thinking rather than a watchkeeper's.

The distinction to hold on to

Preservation excludes three things: moisture, oxygen and acid. Every action above is one of those three, and if you can remember that you can rebuild the answer under pressure. Coating the liner excludes oxygen from the steel. Blanking and dehumidifying exclude moisture. Cleaning the scavenge spaces and draining the economiser remove the acid. Inhibiting the jacket water does all three at once, which is why leaving it full and dosed beats draining it.

Where this connects

This question is one end of a cycle and MIW holds the other end. The reactivation side - the preparation and trials before offering a ship for survey after a lay-up, and what the laid-up notation does to the survey cycle - is a separate examinable task with a separate answer, and it is not a substitute for this one. Preserving a machine and proving a machine to a surveyor are different jobs, and an examiner asking for one will not accept the other.

Critical numbers
  • Three months - short enough to rule out a cold lay-up, long enough to require a real preservation programme
  • Weekly - the working interval for turning, lubricating oil circulation and receiver draining, subject to the engine builder's instruction
Critical regulation
SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 - the condition of the ship and its equipment must be maintained after survey. The laid-up notation, the survey abeyance and the reactivation survey are CLASSIFICATION SOCIETY arrangements, not convention requirements.
Major trap
Answering about a long cold lay-up, or about shutting the engine down safely. The question fixes three months, afloat, commercial, with re-commissioning expected - that combination is a warm lay-up and the answer must read like one. The second trap is inventing an IMO instrument that governs engine preservation. None does.

  • ENGINEERING_JUDGEMENTEngine-builder preservation practice for large two-stroke and four-stroke marine diesel engines - distillate changeover before shutdown, liner surface coating through the scavenge ports, closure of the gas path, dehumidification of the scavenge and under-piston spaces, periodic turning to a changed stop position with the lubricating oil pump running, and full inhibited jacket water in preference to partial draining
    Settled engineering with no governing instrument, and declared as such rather than dressed as regulation. The technical content is stable practice independent of any framework: it rests on the corrosion mechanisms - condensation on cold surfaces, acidic sulphur residues in the gas path, and standing contact at loaded interfaces - none of which has changed, and none of which any convention addresses. The specific interval and the specific preservation fluid are always the engine builder's instruction for the engine in question, and the answer says so instead of naming a product.
