Why a Ship Needs Marine Insurance, and How Hull and Cargo Claims Are Made

Why does a ship require marine insurance cover? Explain hull claims and cargo claims in marine insurance. State the documents and information required from the ship in support of such claims, and indicate their validity.

Understand first

The single mistake that costs most marks here is treating 'cargo claim' as one thing. It is two, and they sit on opposite sides of the ship. When cargo is damaged, the cargo owner claims on HIS OWN cargo policy - that is a cargo underwriter paying his own assured, and the ship is not a party to it. What then reaches the ship is the cargo underwriter's SUBROGATED claim against the carrier under the bill of lading. That is a third-party liability, so it is met by the P&I club and not by the hull and machinery policy. A candidate who writes that 'cargo claims are settled under the H&M policy' has said something structurally wrong about the whole market, and no amount of correct detail afterwards recovers it. The second thing to hold onto is that insurance is a contract of INDEMNITY resting on utmost good faith: it puts the assured back where he was, no better, and it can be defeated by what the ship failed to disclose, failed to maintain or failed to document. That is why the third part of this question - the documents and their validity - is not administrative padding. It is the part that decides whether the claim is paid.

Exam plan

Three headed parts, in the examiner's own order

Head the answer Why cover is required, Hull claims, Cargo claims, Documents and their validity. The last part is worth real marks and is the one most often left as a hurried list, so give it a table and give each document a reason.

  • Why - three reasons, not one: indemnity against a loss no owner can absorb; compulsion by convention and by contract; and the fact that no bank, charterer or shipper will deal with an uninsured ship.
  • Hull - name the policy (Institute Time Clauses Hulls, or the International Hull Clauses), then the claim types: particular average, general average contribution, salvage, sue and labour, collision liability, unrepaired damage, constructive and actual total loss - and the deductible.
  • Cargo - draw the two-sided distinction above explicitly. Then the carrier's duties and defences under the carriage regime, the package/weight limitation, and the one-year time bar.
  • Documents - group them (statutory, class, operational, cargo, financial), and against each say what it proves and what makes it valid. State that the ship's certificates matter because cover is contingent on class and on seaworthiness.
  • Say 'no survey, no claim'. Damage repaired before the underwriter's surveyor has seen it is the commonest way a good claim is lost.
Model answer — current framework

1. Why a ship requires marine insurance cover

Marine insurance is a contract of indemnity: for a premium, the insurer agrees to put the assured back into the position he was in before an insured loss. In India the contract is governed by the Marine Insurance Act 1963, which codifies the doctrines the whole market runs on - insurable interest, utmost good faith and the duty of disclosure, warranties, the classes of loss, general average, sue and labour, and subrogation. Three separate reasons make cover necessary rather than prudent.

  • The exposure is larger than the asset. A single casualty can produce a total loss of the ship, wreck removal, a pollution liability, cargo liability, salvage and third-party property damage at once. No shipowner can carry that on his balance sheet, and it is uninsurable exposure - not the day-to-day maintenance bill - that the market exists to absorb.
  • Compulsion by convention. A ship cannot trade without evidence of financial security in several regimes: the CLC 1992 certificate for oil pollution from a laden tanker, the Bunkers Convention 2001 certificate for bunker oil pollution, the Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention 2007 certificate, the passenger liability certificate under the Athens Convention as amended by the 2002 Protocol, and the MLC 2006 financial security for repatriation and for death or long-term disability. These are carried certificates, they are inspected at port State control, and they are all evidence of insurance.
  • Compulsion by contract. The mortgagee bank requires hull cover assigned to it; the charterparty requires the ship to be entered with a club and classed with an IACS society; the shipper's letter of credit assumes a documented, insured carriage. Cover is what makes the ship a tradeable and financeable asset.

The practical division of the market follows the risk. Hull and machinery insures the owner's own property. Protection and indemnity, written by mutual clubs, insures his liabilities to other people - cargo, crew, pollution, wreck, collision liability beyond the hull policy's share. Loss of hire insures earnings. Cargo insurance belongs to the cargo interest and not to the ship at all. Knowing which policy answers a given loss is the skill this question is testing.

2. Hull claims

A hull claim is made by the owner on his own hull and machinery policy, which in practice is written on the Institute Time Clauses Hulls or on the International Hull Clauses 2003. The ship is insured for an agreed value, each accident carries a deductible, and the policy responds only to the perils it names.

Type of hull claimWhat it isThe point that earns the mark
Particular averageAccidental partial damage to the ship itself, borne by the ship's own interestThe ordinary hull claim - grounding, heavy weather, machinery damage from an insured peril
General average contributionThe ship's share of a sacrifice or extraordinary expenditure made for the common safetyAdjusted under the York-Antwerp Rules by an average adjuster, and recovered from the hull policy. GA is a division of loss between ship, cargo and freight; the hull policy pays the ship's share of it

Actual total lossThe ship is destroyed, or so damaged that it is no longer the thing insured, or is missingNo notice of abandonment is needed

How a hull claim is actually run. The sequence is what a Chief Engineer is examined on, and it is unforgiving of the wrong order:

  1. Act to limit the damage, and record what was done and when - that is the sue and labour duty and it is also the best evidence of good seamanship.
  2. Notify at once - the Company and DPA, the classification society where class is affected, and the underwriters through the broker. Note protest where a peril of the seas or a third party is involved.
  3. Survey before repair. The underwriter's surveyor and the class surveyor attend, damage is examined, measured and photographed in the found condition, and the cause is recorded. Repairing first and claiming afterwards is the commonest way a valid claim is lost, because the extent and cause can no longer be proved.
  4. Repair specification and competitive quotations, submitted for the underwriter's approval, with the repair carried out by an approved repairer under class attendance and the damage repairs separated from owner's work on the yard account. An unsplit dry-dock invoice invites a dispute over every line of it.
  5. Claim submission - the survey report, invoices, log extracts and the cause statement - and where general average or a collision is involved, adjustment by an average adjuster.
  6. Settlement, less the deductible, and the insurer is then subrogated to the owner's rights against any third party responsible.

What defeats a hull claim. Ordinary wear and tear, gradual deterioration and inherent vice are excluded, because the policy is not a maintenance contract. Beyond that, the classification clause requires the ship to be in class with an approved society and the class maintained, with recommendations and conditions of class discharged within their dates; unseaworthiness with the owner's privity, want of due diligence, breach of an express warranty, non-disclosure of a material fact, and unreasonable delay in notification all give the underwriter a defence. Every one of those is an evidential question - which is what part three of this question is about.

3. Cargo claims - and which side of the ship they land on

Two different claims share the name, and the distinction is the heart of this part.

  • The cargo owner's claim on his own cargo policy. Cargo is insured by the cargo interest, in the London market normally on the Institute Cargo Clauses (A), (B) or (C) - (A) being the widest all-risks cover, (B) and (C) named-perils cover of decreasing scope. All three respond to a general average contribution. The ship is not a party to this contract and does not defend it.
  • The subrogated claim against the ship. Having paid its assured, the cargo underwriter steps into the cargo owner's shoes and sues the carrier under the contract of carriage evidenced by the bill of lading. To the shipowner this is a third-party liability, and it is answered by the P&I club, not by the hull policy.

The carriage regime, and the Indian statute book is new. The governing statutes are the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 (Act 19 of 2025), whose Schedule carries the carriage regime, and the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 (Act 18 of 2025), which governs the transfer of rights and liabilities under the bill and therefore who may sue. Both came into force on 10 September 2025, repealing the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1925 and the Indian Bills of Lading Act 1856 respectively. A candidate citing the 1925 or 1856 Acts as current Indian law is citing repealed statutes.

Under the Schedule the carrier must exercise due diligence - not an absolute duty, and not a continuing one - to make the ship seaworthy, properly man, equip and supply her, and make the holds and other cargo spaces fit and safe for the reception, carriage and preservation of the goods before and at the beginning of the voyage; and must properly and carefully load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care for and discharge the goods. Against that stand the carrier's defences - among them error in the navigation or management of the ship, fire without the carrier's actual fault or privity, perils of the sea, inherent vice, insufficiency of packing and latent defect not discoverable by due diligence - together with a limitation of 666.67 units of account per package or unit, or 2 units of account per kilogramme of gross weight, whichever is the higher, and a one-year time bar. Any clause lessening the carrier's liability below the Schedule is void.

And the Indian Schedule is not Hague-Visby renumbered. It enacts the Hague Rules as amended with modifications, and three of the departures are worth marks. "Goods" INCLUDES live animals and cargo carried on or under deck, which Hague-Visby excludes - so the familiar sentence "deck cargo and live animals are outside the rules" is false in India. The one-year time bar may be extended by three months by the court. And Article IV bis is not in the Schedule, so the tidy proposition that the carrier's defences and limits apply equally in tort and extend to servants and agents cannot be asserted from the Indian text. Answer from the Indian Schedule, not from the international instrument.

What the ship does about a cargo claim. Prevent it where possible and document it where not: reject or clause a bill of lading that does not describe the goods as they were received, never sign a clean bill against a letter of indemnity for cargo known to be damaged, tally and record apparent order and condition, keep the ventilation, temperature, bilge-sounding and hold-cleaning records that answer the usual allegations, hold hatch covers weathertight and keep the ultrasonic test report, attend a joint survey with the claimant's surveyor at discharge, and issue a letter of protest where the cargo's condition or the shore handling is the cause. Notify the club early: the club appoints the correspondent and the surveyor, and its cover assumes it was told in time.

4. The documents and information required from the ship, and their validity

The ship is the only source for most of this, and a claim is built from it. Two different senses of validity matter, and both should be stated. A record is valid if it is contemporaneous, complete and unaltered; a certificate is valid if it is in date and properly endorsed.

The time limits are part of validity. Notice of the casualty is due under the policy without unreasonable delay; a note of protest is normally taken before breaking bulk; a cargo claim against the carrier is barred one year after delivery; notice of abandonment must be given to claim a constructive total loss; and general average security must be taken before the cargo leaves the ship. A perfect evidence pack produced after the bar has run is worth nothing, and that is the honest summary of this part of the question.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has asked three distinct things and the marks are split between them, so an answer that spends fourteen marks' worth of writing on hull claims and two lines on documents cannot score well however good the fourteen are. The document part also rewards the one thing a candidate genuinely knows better than a lawyer does: which records exist on a ship, who holds them, and how quickly they stop being usable.

The three traps

  • Putting cargo claims on the hull policy. Cargo damage reaches the shipowner as a liability under the bill of lading and is met by P&I. Say so explicitly - it is a discriminating point and it is easy marks.
  • Confusing general average with particular average. Particular average is accidental partial damage borne by the interest that suffered it. General average is a deliberate sacrifice or extraordinary expenditure for the common safety, shared between ship, cargo and freight, and adjusted under the York-Antwerp Rules.
  • Forgetting the classification warranty. Candidates treat class and insurance as separate subjects. They are joined: the policy requires class to be maintained, so an overdue survey or an undischarged condition of class is an insurance problem as much as a statutory one.

What this answer deliberately does not claim

  • No section numbers from the Marine Insurance Act 1963. The Act is named as the governing Indian statute and its doctrines are stated, because those are what the question needs. Section numbers are not quoted, since MIW has not read the Gazette text for this entry and a mis-cited section is worse in an answer script than no section at all.
  • The Indian carriage position is stated from MIW's own primary reading, not from the international instrument. Both 2025 Acts were read at source in the Gazette of India during MIW's verification of the solved QP2510 Q2 - the Bills of Lading Act 2025 as Act 18 of 2025 and the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 2025 as Act 19 of 2025, including the entire Schedule - and their commencement on 10 September 2025 is established from the notifications S.O. 4083(E) and S.O. 4082(E) of 8 September 2025. That is why this entry states the Indian departures from Hague-Visby rather than treating the Schedule as Hague-Visby by another name: relying on 'the content common to both' would have carried the deck-cargo exclusion into an answer where it is false.
  • The package and weight limitation IS given, because MIW read it in the Indian Schedule - 666.67 units of account per package or unit, or 2 per kilogramme, whichever is higher. No monetary limit is given for any of the liability conventions, and none should be: those are unit-of-account figures which are periodically raised, no rupee conversion is asserted anywhere, and quoting a stale one is a false claim about current law.

Where the solved corpus takes this further

MIW's solved papers already answer several neighbouring tasks in more depth than this entry does, and they are worth reading beside it: the types of marine insurance policy and their exclusions, P&I compared with H&M, the running down clause with general average expenses and a claim for unrepaired damage, and general average under the York-Antwerp Rules. None of them answers THIS question - none states the documents required from the ship and their validity - which is why this entry exists. But they are the deeper treatment of the parts they do cover.
