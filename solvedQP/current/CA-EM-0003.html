Understand first

The trap in this limb is that the three words are not three parallel things. Treaty is the generic legal category; convention and protocol are both treaties, distinguished by the job they do rather than by their legal force. Once that hierarchy is stated the rest of the answer writes itself, and an answer that opens with it has already earned most of the limb. The second thing worth seeing is why protocols exist at all in the IMO system. A convention that has entered into force is extremely hard to reopen, because reopening it means every State party has to consent again and the instrument can stall for a decade. A protocol is the device that lets the Organization change or extend an instrument without that. MARPOL is the standing example: the 1973 Convention never entered into force on its own and was rescued by being absorbed into the 1978 Protocol - which is why the instrument is properly cited as MARPOL 73/78 and not as MARPOL 1973.

Model answer — current framework

Treaty - the generic category

A treaty is the general term in international law for a binding agreement between States. The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 defines it in Article 2(1)(a) as an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whatever its particular designation. That last phrase is the point of the definition: the NAME does not determine the legal character. A convention, a protocol, an agreement, a charter and a covenant are all treaties, and all bind a State that has consented to be bound by them.

A treaty binds only those States that have consented to be bound - by ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, evidenced by depositing an instrument with the depositary. Adoption of the text creates no obligation on any State.

Convention - the principal multilateral instrument

A convention is a multilateral treaty, usually negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference, that establishes a substantial body of law on a subject. In the IMO system the conventions are the primary instruments: SOLAS 1974, MARPOL, STCW 1978, Load Lines 1966, COLREG 1972, MLC 2006 (adopted by the ILO), the Salvage Convention 1989, and so on.

Two features are worth stating because they are what the examiner is testing:

  • A convention carries its own entry-into-force conditions. These are written into the instrument, typically a minimum number of contracting States representing a minimum percentage of world merchant tonnage, followed by a stated waiting period. Until they are met the convention is adopted but not in force, and it binds nobody.
  • A convention normally carries its own amendment procedure. Modern IMO conventions use tacit acceptance: an amendment adopted by the relevant committee enters into force on a stated date unless a specified proportion of parties objects. This is why annexes and technical requirements can be kept current while the parent convention itself is left alone.

Protocol - the instrument that modifies or supplements a convention

A protocol is itself a treaty, subordinate to a parent convention, used where the convention is to be modified or extended in a way its own amendment procedure cannot achieve - or, historically, where the convention cannot be brought into force at all. A protocol has its own signature, ratification and entry-into-force provisions, and a State may be party to a convention without being party to a protocol to it.

Three uses, with the standard examples:

  • To rescue a stalled convention. The International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships, 1973 attracted too few ratifications to enter into force. The 1978 Protocol absorbed it, and the combined instrument entered into force as MARPOL 73/78. This is the example to give if only one is wanted.
  • To modernise a convention wholesale. The 1988 Protocols to SOLAS 1974 and to the Load Lines Convention 1966 introduced the Harmonized System of Survey and Certification, aligning survey intervals and certificate validity across instruments.
  • To extend a convention's scope or limits. The 1992 Protocols to the Civil Liability Convention and to the Fund Convention raised the limits of liability and widened the geographical scope of the oil-pollution compensation regime.

The distinction to hold on to

Below all three sit the codes - the ISM Code, the ISPS Code, the IGF Code, the FSS and LSA Codes and the Polar Code. A code is not a treaty. It acquires binding force only by being made mandatory through a convention, by an amendment to that convention adopted in the usual way. Saying that the ISM Code binds a ship is loose; the ISM Code binds because SOLAS chapter IX makes it mandatory. Getting that right in one sentence is frequently the difference between a good limb and an average one.

Study guide

How to spend the limb

Open with the hierarchy in one sentence - treaty is the category, convention and protocol are both treaties doing different jobs. Then one short paragraph each, and give ONE named example for each so the answer is anchored: Vienna Convention 1969 for the definition, SOLAS 1974 for a convention, MARPOL 73/78 for a protocol. Close with the codes point if there is room, because it is the sentence most candidates do not write.

What this limb is NOT

It is not the four stages of an IMO convention. That is a question about PROCESS - proposal and drafting, adoption, acceptance, entry into force - and MIW answers it separately on a solved paper. The two are commonly confused because both involve the words 'convention' and 'entry into force', but an examiner asking for short notes on three instrument types will not accept a narrative about committee procedure. Read the stem, then decide which of the two you are being asked for.
