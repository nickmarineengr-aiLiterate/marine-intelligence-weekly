Understand first

MIW EXPLANATION - secondary to the legal foundation set out in the model answer, and not itself the definition. The trap in this limb is that the three words are not three parallel things, and the trap behind that one is assuming the difference between them is a difference of RANK. It is not. Treaty is the generic category, defined by Article 2(1)(a) of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969. Convention and protocol are designations given to instruments, and Article 2(1)(a) applies to an agreement meeting its criteria 'whatever its particular designation' - so an instrument titled Protocol may itself be a treaty, and its title places it above or below nothing. These titles do not create a hierarchy, and neither designation occupies a position on a ladder. The second thing worth seeing is what a protocol actually did in one concrete case. MARPOL is the standing example - the 1973 Convention did not enter into force on its own, and it was after the 1978 Protocol that the combined instrument entered into force on 2 October 1983. That is MARPOL's own history, and it is not a general rule about what protocols do.

Model answer — current framework

Treaty - the generic category

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 (VCLT), Article 2(1)(a) defines a treaty as an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, whether embodied in a single instrument or in two or more related instruments, and whatever its particular designation. That last phrase is the operative one for this limb: the designation of an instrument does not determine whether it falls within that definition. An instrument is a treaty if it meets those criteria, whatever its particular designation.

Read Article 2(1)(a) with VCLT Article 3, which preserves the legal force of international agreements that fall outside the Convention's scope - those concluded with other subjects of international law, or not in written form. Article 2(1)(a) is therefore the definition used for the purposes of the Convention, not an exhaustive statement of every international agreement that can bind.

Consent to be bound

VCLT Article 11 sets out the MEANS by which the consent of a State to be bound by a treaty may be expressed - signature, exchange of instruments constituting a treaty, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, or by any other means if so agreed. VCLT Article 2(1)(b) is the definition provision for four of those named acts: 'ratification', 'acceptance', 'approval' and 'accession' mean in each case the international act so named whereby a State establishes on the international plane its consent to be bound by a treaty.

Article 11 therefore identifies several possible means of expressing consent to be bound, and ratification is not the only means it lists. This is why no rule of the form 'instruments of type X always require ratification' is safe to state.

Convention - Article 2(1)(a) applies whatever the designation

An instrument titled Convention is a treaty where it meets the Article 2(1)(a) criteria. Article 2(1)(a) applies to an agreement meeting those criteria whatever its particular designation.

The designation Convention does not determine whether it falls within the Article 2(1)(a) definition of a treaty.

Protocol - a treaty where Article 2(1)(a) criteria are met

A protocol that meets the Article 2(1)(a) criteria is itself a treaty. Because Article 2(1)(a) applies whatever the instrument's particular designation, its title alone does not alter that conclusion.

The 1978 MARPOL Protocol is related to the 1973 MARPOL Convention. VCLT Article 11 identifies several possible means of expressing consent to be bound, and ratification is not the only means it lists, so there is no universal rule that a protocol must be ratified. The Protocol of 1978 relating to MARPOL, Article IV(1) is the worked maritime example, and it permits three distinct routes: signature without reservation as to ratification, acceptance or approval; signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval followed by that act; or accession.

MARPOL is the worked example. The International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships was adopted on 2 November 1973 but had not entered into force on its own. Following the tanker accidents of 1976-77 the 1978 Protocol was adopted, and the combined instrument entered into force on 2 October 1983. The 1997 Protocol later added Annex VI. It is given here as what happened to MARPOL - not as a general rule about what protocols do.

How the three fit together

TermSource-safe distinction
TreatyThe category defined by VCLT 1969, Article 2(1)(a): an international agreement concluded between States in written form and governed by international law, and that definition applies whatever its particular designation.
ConventionAn instrument titled Convention is a treaty where it meets the Article 2(1)(a) criteria, and Article 2(1)(a) applies whatever its particular designation.
ProtocolAn instrument titled Protocol is a treaty where it meets the Article 2(1)(a) criteria, and the title alone does not alter that conclusion. For the Protocol of 1978 relating to MARPOL, Article IV(1) permits three routes to becoming a Party: signature without reservation as to ratification, acceptance or approval; signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval followed by that act; or accession.

The distinction to hold on to

The key legal point is that the titles alone do not determine treaty status. Apply Article 2(1)(a) to decide whether an instrument is a treaty, whatever its particular designation.

Study guide

How to spend the limb

MIW GUIDANCE - secondary to the legal foundation above, and not a substitute for it. Open with the CATEGORY point in one sentence: treaty is the generic category under VCLT Article 2(1)(a), and convention and protocol are both treaties, because these titles do not create a hierarchy. Then one short paragraph each, anchored on the two instruments this answer actually reads at source: the Vienna Convention 1969 for the definition, and the 1973 MARPOL Convention with its 1978 Protocol for the convention/protocol pair. If you say anything about ratification, say it about the instrument in front of you - the Protocol of 1978 relating to MARPOL, Article IV(1) permits three different routes - and never as a universal rule about protocols.

What this limb is NOT

It is not the four stages of an IMO convention. That is a question about PROCESS - proposal and drafting, adoption, acceptance, entry into force - and MIW answers it separately on a solved paper. The two are commonly confused because both involve the words 'convention' and 'entry into force', but an examiner asking for short notes on three instrument types will not accept a narrative about committee procedure. Read the stem, then decide which of the two you are being asked for.

Critical regulation
Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969 - Article 2(1)(a), the definition of a treaty and the words 'whatever its particular designation', which are what make convention and protocol both treaties and prevent any ranking by title; Article 3, which keeps that a definition for the Convention's purposes rather than an exhaustive one; Article 2(1)(b), which defines ratification, acceptance, approval and accession; and Article 11, which sets out the means by which consent to be bound may be expressed. Protocol of 1978 relating to MARPOL, Article IV(1) - the worked maritime example of an instrument for which several of those means are agreed.
Major trap
Asserting a legal HIERARCHY - that a treaty outranks a convention which outranks a protocol, or that a protocol is subordinate because of its title. VCLT Article 2(1)(a) applies 'whatever its particular designation', so the designation does not determine whether an instrument falls within that definition. The second trap is the universal claim that a protocol always requires ratification: the means of expressing consent to be bound are those of VCLT Article 11, which identifies several possible means and does not make ratification the only one, and the Protocol of 1978 relating to MARPOL, Article IV(1) permits three different routes. The third is answering the FOUR STAGES of an IMO convention instead, which is a process question MIW answers elsewhere.
