What the Manila Amendments Changed for the Engine Department

One limb of a recurring multi-part question — A. The major changes and amendments made by the Manila amendments for the engine department
The 2010 Manila amendments to the STCW Convention and Code are now simply STCW as amended. State the major changes they introduced affecting the engine department, and explain their present standing.

Understand first

The single most important thing to get right in this limb is its TENSE. The Manila amendments are not a proposal, a transition or a recent development; they are the STCW Convention. They were adopted at a diplomatic conference in Manila on 25 June 2010, entered into force on 1 January 2012, and the transitional provisions that allowed the pre-2010 standards to run alongside them expired on 1 January 2017. So a candidate should describe the changes historically - 'the amendments introduced' - and then say plainly that all of them are now simply in force, with nothing grandfathered. The term 'STCW 95' has no present legal meaning and using it as though it named a live parallel regime is the error this limb is most often written with. The second thing to hold onto is that the engine-department changes fall into four groups: NEW CERTIFICATES that did not exist before, NEW COMPETENCES added to certificates that did, TIGHTER FITNESS AND REST rules, and TIGHTER ADMINISTRATION of how training and certification are approved and revalidated. Grouping them that way turns a memory dump into an answer.

Exam plan

Four groups, then one paragraph on standing

Do not write a list of amendments in the order you remember them. Group them, because the grouping is itself a mark-earning demonstration that you understand what the amendments were for.

  • New certificates and ranks - the electro-technical officer, the electro-technical rating, and the able seafarer engine.
  • New and strengthened competences on existing engine certificates - engine-room resource management, leadership and managerial skill, high-voltage systems, and later the gas and low-flashpoint fuel training.
  • Fitness for duty - revised minimum hours of rest, and a mandatory alcohol limit.
  • Administration and quality - revalidation and refresher requirements, tightened medical fitness standards, recognition of simulator training and distance learning, and stronger obligations on Administrations and companies.
  • Present standing - in force since 1 January 2012, transitional arrangements ended 1 January 2017, nothing grandfathered, and the comprehensive review of the Convention now under way is the next change and is not yet adopted.
  • Do not write about the bridge. The limb says engine department. ECDIS and celestial navigation earn nothing here.
Model answer — current framework

1. What the Manila amendments are, and where they stand today

The 2010 Manila amendments were adopted by a Conference of Parties to the STCW Convention held in Manila and were adopted on 25 June 2010. They were the first comprehensive revision of the Convention and its Code since 1995, and they entered into force on 1 January 2012 under the tacit acceptance procedure. Transitional arrangements allowed seafarers who had begun approved seagoing service, training or education before 1 July 2013 to continue to be certificated against the previous standards for a limited period; those arrangements ended on 1 January 2017.

The consequence matters more than the dates. Since 1 January 2017 there is no parallel regime. Every certificate in current use, and every competence table a candidate is examined against, is the Manila text. The expression 'STCW 95' no longer describes anything in force, and the historical comparison between an 'existing' chapter III and an 'amended' chapter III - which older versions of this question invite - has no present-day content. What follows is therefore written as history with a present effect: these were the changes, and they are now simply the requirements.

2. New certificates and ranks in the engine department

This is the most visible change and the one that altered the composition of an engine-room crew.

RegulationWhat it createdWhy it was needed
III/5Able Seafarer Engine - a certificated rating grade above the engine-room rating, for service in a manned engine room or designated duties in a periodically unmanned engine roomThe rating structure previously stopped at a single certificated level, so there was no international standard for the experienced hand the department actually relies on
III/6Electro-technical Officer - an officer certificate with its own approved education, training, seagoing service and standard of competenceShips had become electrically and electronically dominated - high-voltage distribution, power management, variable-speed drives, automation and control - and there was no certificate for the person responsible for them
III/7Electro-technical Rating - the rating-level standard supporting the ETOThe same reasoning one level down: the senior electrical hand had no certificated standard

Alongside these, the amendments retained and revised the existing engine certificates - the officer in charge of an engineering watch and the second and chief engineer officer at management level - so the chapter as a whole became a graded structure from rating to Chief Engineer rather than a small set of officer certificates with an undifferentiated crew beneath them.

3. New and strengthened competences on existing engine certificates

  • Engine-room resource management became a required competence. Watchkeeping must be carried out on resource-management principles - allocation, assignment and prioritisation of resources, effective communication, assertiveness and leadership, obtaining and maintaining situational awareness, and consideration of team experience. This was the human-element lesson of two decades of casualty investigation written into the Code.
  • Leadership and managerial skill became a certificated competence, at operational level and, in a fuller form, at management level - knowledge of shipboard personnel management and training, of the applicable international conventions and recommendations, of task and workload management, of effective resource management, of decision-making techniques, and of the development, implementation and oversight of standard operating procedures. For a Chief Engineer this is the formal basis of the management-level examination itself.
  • High-voltage systems. Explicit competence in the operation, maintenance and safe working practice of high-voltage installations, reflecting the shift to high-voltage distribution in large and electrically propelled ships.
  • Motional and environmental competences strengthened - marine engineering at management level extended in the direction of plant management, machinery performance and the prevention of pollution, and the requirement to use modern technology in maintenance and repair.
  • Security awareness and duties. The amendments brought security training into the Convention itself, with mandatory security-awareness training for all seafarers and additional requirements for those with designated security duties - which for the engine department means the personnel with duties under the ship security plan.
  • Later additions built on the same structure. The training and qualification requirements for personnel on ships subject to the IGF Code - basic and advanced training for service on ships using gases or other low-flashpoint fuels - were added to the Convention and Code by amendments adopted in 2015 and in force from 1 January 2017. They are not part of the Manila package, and a careful answer says so, but they are the clearest illustration that the Manila structure was designed to be extended.

4. Fitness for duty - rest hours and alcohol

  • Minimum hours of rest were revised and aligned with the maritime labour regime, and were extended in scope to cover not only watchkeeping personnel but those whose duties involve designated safety, prevention of pollution and security duties. The figures are set out in the revision card and are carried in MIW as a competent-authority restatement rather than as a quotation from a text MIW holds; the structural points are that there is a daily minimum, a weekly minimum, a limit on how the daily rest may be divided, a maximum interval between rest periods, and a narrow and recorded exceptions regime.
  • Records of daily hours of rest must be maintained, and this is the change that has the largest practical effect on a Chief Engineer, because the department's overtime and callout pattern is what the record has to survive.
  • A mandatory alcohol limit was introduced for the first time - not more than 0.05% blood alcohol level or 0.25 mg/l alcohol in the breath - applying to masters, officers and other seafarers while performing designated safety, security and marine environmental duties, with Administrations required to prescribe measures to prevent alcohol and drug abuse.
  • Medical fitness standards were tightened, with clearer requirements for medical examination, for the form and period of validity of medical certificates, and for the qualifications of the practitioners who issue them.

5. Administration, quality and the route back to sea

  • Revalidation and refresher training. Certificates continue to require periodic revalidation on evidence of continued professional competence, and the amendments introduced or reinforced refresher and updating training, so that basic and advanced safety competences have to be re-demonstrated at intervals rather than held for life on the strength of one course.
  • Quality standards and approved training. Education, training and assessment must sit inside an Administration-monitored quality standards system, and instructors, supervisors and assessors must themselves be appropriately qualified for the training or assessment they conduct.
  • Simulators and modern methods. Performance standards for simulator training and assessment, and express recognition of distance learning and web-based learning, which is what made structured shore-based updating practical for serving seafarers.
  • Onboard training as approved training. Seagoing service counts toward a certificate only when it is approved, documented and structured - which is the subject of the second limb of this question and is answered separately.
  • Obligations on Parties and on companies. Tighter requirements on Administrations to communicate information to IMO and to be subject to independent evaluation, and on companies to ensure seafarers are certificated, familiarised and able to communicate effectively - the point at which STCW meets the ISM Code.

6. Present standing, and what is coming

All of the above is current, mandatory and fully phased in. Nothing in the Manila package is transitional any longer and nothing is grandfathered.

The next change is the comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code, which IMO's Sub-Committee on Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping has been carrying out with a view to modernising the Convention for automation, digitalisation, alternative fuels and the human element. It is not adopted and not in force, and the meeting at which amendments would be adopted - an extended session of the Maritime Safety Committee or a diplomatic conference - has not yet taken place. A candidate should say that the review is under way and must not present anything from it as a requirement. In the meantime IMO has bridged the alternative-fuel training gap with interim guidelines rather than amendments, which is itself a useful thing to be able to say: they are guidance for the development and approval of training, and they are not a mandatory standard of competence.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner is testing whether you know the modern certificate structure and where the human-element competences came from. Grouping the changes - new certificates, new competences, fitness for duty, administration - shows the purpose of the amendments rather than a memorised list, and the closing paragraph on present standing is what separates a candidate who understands the framework from one who has learned a page about it.

The three traps

  • Writing in the wrong tense. Treating the Manila amendments as new, transitional, or as running alongside 'STCW 95'. They entered into force on 1 January 2012 and the transitional arrangements ended on 1 January 2017. There is nothing to compare them with.
  • Answering for the bridge. ECDIS, bridge resource management as such, and navigational competences are not this limb. Engine-room resource management is.
  • Attributing the IGF Code training to Manila. It was added by later amendments, adopted in 2015 and in force from 1 January 2017. Saying so correctly is a discriminator; saying it wrongly is a dated error.

What this answer deliberately does not claim

  • No STCW Code table or paragraph numbers beyond the chapter III regulation numbers, which are stable and well established. MIW does not hold the STCW Code under licence, so competences are described by their subject matter rather than quoted, and no function/column reference is invented.
  • No rest-hour figures in the body. They are in the revision card, labelled as a competent-authority restatement, so the strength of the claim travels with the number.
  • No revalidation or refresher interval in years. The requirement to revalidate and to refresh is stated; the interval is set by the relevant regulation and by the Administration, and MIW has not read the Code text for this entry. A wrong interval would be quoted in an exam as though it were the rule.
  • No date, session or content for the comprehensive review. It is recorded as under way and not adopted, and nothing more.

How this limb sits beside its sibling

This family has two genuinely independent examinable concepts, and they are owned separately for that reason. This limb is about what the Convention now requires and where it came from. The second limb - CA-EM-0008 - is about how onboard training and the assessment of competence actually work, and what a Chief Engineer has to do about them. Verifying one has never verified the other, and each carries its own review.

Adopted but NOT yet in force — do not write these as current law
  • The comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code is in progress at IMO and NOTHING from it is adopted or in force. The adoption meeting - an extended session of the Maritime Safety Committee, or a diplomatic conference - has not taken place. A candidate may say the review is under way; a candidate may not say that any of its content is required.
  • The interim guidelines on training for seafarers on ships using alternative fuels and new technologies, and the fuel-specific interim training guidelines for methyl/ethyl alcohol and for ammonia, are GUIDANCE approved by the Maritime Safety Committee. They are not amendments, they create no mandatory standard of competence, and they must not be described as an STCW requirement.
Superseded — do not cite
  • 'STCW 95' - the label for the Convention as amended in 1995 - describes nothing in force. The Manila amendments superseded that text on 1 January 2012 and the transitional arrangements that let the earlier standards run alongside them ended on 1 January 2017. Older question wordings, and most published notes on this subject, still set up a comparison between 'existing' and 'amended' chapter III; that comparison has no present-day content and reproducing it is the characteristic dated error in this answer.
