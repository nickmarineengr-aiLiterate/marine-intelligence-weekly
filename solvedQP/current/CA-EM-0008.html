Onboard Training and the Standard of Competence under STCW Chapter III, and the Chief Engineer's Role

One limb of a recurring multi-part question — B. Onboard training and the standard of competence under STCW chapter III, the Chief Engineer's role, and the criteria for evaluating competence
Explain the salient factors for onboard training and the standard of competence required under chapter III of STCW as amended. Underline the specific roles a Chief Engineer must perform towards the satisfactory training of engine-room personnel, and state the criteria for evaluating competence in onboard training.

Understand first

There is one structural fact that unlocks this whole limb, and a candidate who knows it will out-score one who does not by a wide margin. The competence tables in part A of the STCW Code are written in FOUR COLUMNS: the competence itself; the knowledge, understanding and proficiency that underpins it; the methods for demonstrating competence; and the CRITERIA FOR EVALUATING COMPETENCE. So when the examiner asks for 'the standard of competence' and then for 'the criteria for evaluating competence', those are not two ways of saying the same thing - they are two named columns of the same table, and the second is the column that says what an acceptable performance looks like. Name the four columns and the rest of the answer writes itself. The second thing to hold onto is what makes onboard training count at all: seagoing service earns a certificate only when it is APPROVED, STRUCTURED, DOCUMENTED and SUPERVISED BY A QUALIFIED PERSON. Time served is not training. A cadet who has spent twelve months on board being useful has not necessarily completed a day of approved onboard training, and the Chief Engineer is the person who determines which of those two things happened.

Exam plan

Three headed parts, because the examiner asked three questions

Head them Onboard training and the standard of competence, The Chief Engineer's specific roles, and The criteria for evaluating competence. The third part is the discriminator, so give it its own section and its own examples rather than folding it into the first.

  • Open with the four columns of the STCW Code competence table. It frames everything and it takes two lines.
  • Say what makes onboard training approved - a documented programme, a training record book, a qualified supervisor and assessor, tasks tied to real work, and records the Administration can audit.
  • Name chapter III by regulation - III/1 for the officer in charge of a watch, III/2 for management level, III/4 and III/5 for ratings - and note that each has its own seagoing service and onboard training requirement.
  • Make the Chief Engineer's roles concrete and personal. 'Ensure training is carried out' is worth little; 'plan the trainee's tasks against the record book, allocate a qualified supervising officer, sign only what I have seen, and report the gap to the Company' is worth a lot.
  • Give real criteria, in the Code's own idiom - observed performance judged against a stated standard - and say plainly that attendance and time served are NOT criteria.
  • Bring in the ISM Code at the end. Onboard training is also a Company duty and is auditable, which is why it has to leave a record.
Model answer — current framework

1. How chapter III is built, and where onboard training sits in it

Chapter III of the Convention sets the mandatory minimum requirements for the engine department. Each certificate has its own regulation, and each regulation requires three things together: approved education and training, approved seagoing service, and the standard of competence specified in the corresponding section of part A of the STCW Code.

RegulationCertificateThe point about onboard training
III/1Officer in charge of an engineering watchRequires approved seagoing service which includes a specified period of engine-room watchkeeping duties under the supervision of a qualified engineer officer, and, where the route is a cadetship, an approved onboard training programme documented in a training record book
III/2Chief engineer officer and second engineer officerManagement level. Competence at this level includes the management of personnel, of training and of the safety management system - so the holder is not only trained, he is the trainer
III/3Chief and second engineer officer on ships below a specified propulsion powerSame structure at a reduced power band
III/4 and III/5Rating forming part of a watch, and able seafarer engineBoth rest on approved seagoing service together with training - onboard or ashore - and on the corresponding competence standard
III/6 and III/7Electro-technical officer and electro-technical ratingIntroduced by the 2010 Manila amendments, and built on the same three-legged structure

What makes onboard training count. Sea time by itself earns nothing. Approved onboard training has five salient features, and they are the marks in the first part of this question:

  1. An approved, structured programme. The training scheme is approved by the Administration and is planned rather than opportunistic - a defined set of tasks and objectives covering the competences the certificate requires.
  2. A training record book. The tasks are recorded as they are completed, with the date and the signature of the officer who supervised and verified each one. The record book is the documentary bridge between what happened in the engine room and what the Administration will accept.
  3. Qualified supervision and assessment. The person supervising the training, and the person assessing it, must themselves be appropriately qualified for the task or competence concerned and must have an appreciation of the training programme. A senior hand who is excellent at the job is not automatically a qualified assessor.
  4. Real work, properly sequenced. Training tasks are tied to the ship's actual operation and maintenance, planned so the trainee builds from supervised observation, through assisted work, to unsupervised performance - and always within the safety of the plant and of the trainee.
  5. Quality standards and auditability. The whole arrangement sits inside a quality standards system monitored by the Administration, and inside the Company's safety management system, so that it can be evaluated rather than asserted.

2. The standard of competence, and the four columns

Competence in STCW is not knowledge. It is the demonstrated ability to perform a defined task to a defined standard, and the Code expresses each one in a table of four columns:

ColumnWhat it statesEngine-room illustration
1. CompetenceThe task itself, stated as an abilityOperate main and auxiliary machinery and associated control systems
2. Knowledge, understanding and proficiencyWhat must be known and understood to underpin the abilityOperational principles, preparation for and starting of machinery, safe and effective operating limits, and the associated procedures
3. Methods for demonstrating competenceHow the ability may be shown and by what evidenceApproved in-service experience, approved training-ship experience, approved simulator or laboratory training, or assessment of practical performance on board
4. Criteria for evaluating competenceWhat an ACCEPTABLE performance looks like - the standard the assessor judges againstThat the machinery is operated in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions and the ship's procedures, that parameters are kept within safe limits, and that departures are detected and corrected

Because the fourth column exists, the Convention does not allow competence to be certified on the strength of a course attended or a period served. It requires a judgement about performance against a stated standard, made by a qualified person, and recorded. That is the whole architecture, and it is the answer to the third part of this question.

3. The specific roles a Chief Engineer must perform

This part should be written in the first person and made concrete. A Chief Engineer's duty here is not general encouragement; it is a set of identifiable acts.

  • Own the programme on board. Read the training record book and the approved programme on joining, establish what each trainee still needs, and turn that into a plan for the voyage rather than reacting to whatever breaks.
  • Allocate qualified supervision. Nominate for each trainee an officer who is competent in the task and who understands the programme, and make that allocation known. Do not leave supervision to whoever happens to be on watch.
  • Integrate training into real work. Plan planned-maintenance jobs, surveys, bunkering, tank entry, dry-dock work and drills so that trainees are deliberately included - the opportunity is what the ship is doing anyway, and it is lost if nobody plans it.
  • Ensure familiarisation before duty. Under the ISM Code and STCW, no one takes up a duty until they are familiar with the ship's arrangements, equipment and procedures for that duty. This is a safety obligation and it comes before any training objective.
  • Assess, and sign only what I have seen or had verified. The signature in a record book is a professional attestation of competence. Signing a page to keep a cadet's paperwork moving is a false statement that will be relied upon by an Administration, and it is the failure mode a Chief Engineer must refuse.
  • Give feedback and appraise. Debrief tasks, tell the trainee where the performance fell short of the criteria and what to do about it, and complete honest appraisals - the value of an appraisal is entirely in its honesty.
  • Instruct in safety and in limits. Permits, isolation and lock-out, enclosed-space entry, hot work, electrical safety including high voltage, and the discipline of stopping a job. A trainee's first competence is knowing when not to proceed.
  • Keep the records and report the gaps. Maintain the training records, hours of rest and drill records, and where the programme cannot be completed - the ship's trade does not present the task, or there is no qualified assessor on board - report that to the Company as a deficiency rather than closing the book. That report is the mechanism by which the system corrects itself.
  • Manage the department so that training is possible. Realistic workload, protected rest and a no-blame reporting culture. Nobody learns anything useful in a department that is behind on everything and afraid to admit a mistake.
  • Set the standard personally. Watchkeeping discipline, record-keeping, tidiness of work, use of the correct tool and the correct procedure. Trainees copy what the Chief Engineer tolerates, not what he says.

4. The criteria for evaluating competence in onboard training

Stated generally first, then illustrated. The criteria are always of the same shape: an observable performance, judged against a defined external standard, in circumstances that are representative of the real duty.

  • The performance conforms to the applicable standard - the manufacturer's instructions, the ship's procedures, the safety management system, and the relevant convention requirement. Not to the assessor's personal preference.
  • Operating parameters are kept within safe and effective limits, and the trainee can state what those limits are and why.
  • Deviations are detected early and correctly interpreted, and the response is appropriate and timely. Recognising an abnormal condition is a competence in its own right.
  • The correct priority is chosen under pressure - safety of life, then the ship, then the environment, then the plant - and help is called at the right point rather than too late.
  • Communication is effective: orders and reports are given, received, and acknowledged unambiguously in the working language, and the trainee reports what they have actually done.
  • Safety precautions are complete before work starts - permit, isolation, ventilation, testing, personal protective equipment - and are verified rather than assumed.
  • The task is completed to a serviceable standard, with clearances, settings and tests recorded and the work handed back properly.
  • The performance is repeatable, in more than one instance and, where the duty demands it, without supervision.

And the criteria that are not criteria. Time served, attendance at a course, willingness, seniority, or the fact that nothing went wrong. Say this explicitly - it is the sentence that shows the examiner you have understood the fourth column rather than memorised it.

How the assessment is actually made. Direct observation of practical performance on board is the primary method, supported by oral questioning to test the underpinning knowledge, by examination of the trainee's records and reports, by simulator or workshop evidence where the ship cannot present the task, and by the record-book verification that ties each item back to a named qualified officer on a named date.

5. Where this joins the Company's obligations

Onboard training is not only a Convention matter. The ISM Code requires the Company to establish procedures to identify any training which may be required in support of the safety management system and to ensure it is provided; to ensure new personnel and personnel transferred to new assignments are properly familiarised; and to verify, review and evaluate the whole arrangement. So the training record book, the familiarisation checklist, the drill record and the Chief Engineer's training plan are auditable documents - examined at internal audit, at the ISM verification behind the Safety Management Certificate, and at port State control. That is the practical reason the records matter as much as the teaching does.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three parts were asked and three parts are given, in the examiner's order. The four-column structure of the Code's competence tables is the intellectual centre of the answer and it does two jobs at once: it answers 'the standard of competence' and it makes the 'criteria for evaluating competence' a precise question with a precise answer instead of an invitation to generalise. Very few candidates name the four columns; almost none say which of them the examiner is asking about.

The three traps

  • Answering 'criteria for evaluating competence' with methods of assessment. Those are column 3. Column 4 is what an acceptable performance looks like. Give both, labelled, and the distinction earns the mark on its own.
  • Treating sea time as training. Approved onboard training is structured, documented, supervised by a qualified person and auditable. Twelve useful months is not twelve months of training.
  • Writing the Chief Engineer's role as a policy statement. 'Ensure adequate training is provided' could have been written by someone who has never been to sea. Name the acts: read the record book, allocate a qualified supervisor, plan tasks against the programme, sign only what was seen, debrief against the criteria, and report the gap to the Company.

What this answer deliberately does not claim

  • No table numbers, section paragraph numbers or quoted Code wording. The chapter III regulation numbers are given because they are stable and well established. Beyond that, MIW does not hold the STCW Code under licence, so competences and criteria are described in their own idiom rather than quoted, and the four column headings are named as the structure of the tables - which is documented and stable - without any claim to reproduce a particular table's contents.
  • No periods of seagoing service in months. The requirement that approved seagoing service include a specified period of supervised engine-room watchkeeping is stated; the figure is not, because the qualifying periods differ between certificates and between training routes and are in each case set by the regulation and by the Administration. A single number here would be quoted in an exam as though it applied to every route.
  • No claim that the training record book is a Convention-named document for every certificate. It is the normal instrument of an approved onboard training programme and is required where that route is used; the answer places it there rather than presenting it as universal.
