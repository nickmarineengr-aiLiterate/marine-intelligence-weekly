Understand first

The mark is lost by treating Lloyd's Open Form as a synonym for salvage law. It is not law at all - it is a CONTRACT, a standard printed form published by Lloyd's, and the reason it matters is entirely practical. A ship in danger needs a salvor now, and the two masters have neither the time nor the information to negotiate a price. LOF solves that by agreeing everything except the price: the salvor will use best endeavours, the contract is no cure no pay, and what the service is worth will be settled afterwards by arbitration in London. That single deferral is the whole invention, and it is why the form can be agreed by radio in one line. The second half of the answer is that no cure no pay, left alone, produces a perverse result: a salvor faced with a laden tanker aground in a sensitive area is being asked to accept enormous environmental risk for a reward that pays nothing if the ship is lost. The Salvage Convention 1989 and then the SCOPIC clause exist to correct that, and an answer that stops at 'no cure no pay' has stopped one step before the point.

Model answer — current framework

What it is

Lloyd's Open Form - LOF - is a standard-form salvage agreement published by Lloyd's of London. The current edition is LOF 2020. It is a contract between the owner of the property in danger (agreed on their behalf by the master) and the salvor, and its defining characteristic is that it is "open": every term is settled by the printed form except the one term that would take longest to negotiate, namely the amount of the salvage award.

The four features worth the marks

  • No cure, no pay. If the salvage services produce no useful result, the salvor recovers nothing. This is the historic principle the form embodies, and it is what makes a salvor bear the commercial risk of the operation.
  • Best endeavours, both ways. The salvor undertakes to use best endeavours to salve the property and to prevent or minimise damage to the environment; the owner and master undertake to co-operate fully, including in obtaining a place of safety.
  • No price is agreed. The award is fixed afterwards by arbitration in London under the Lloyd's Salvage Arbitration Branch, applying English law. This is what makes the form agreeable in minutes, in an emergency, by two people who cannot possibly value the service at the time.
  • Security, not cash, at the outset. The salvor's remuneration is secured against the salved property and its cargo, with the parties providing security to Lloyd's; the property is released once security is in place.

How the Salvage Convention 1989 changed what sits on top of it

The International Convention on Salvage, 1989 is the governing international instrument, and LOF incorporates its scheme. Three articles matter here:

  • Article 13 sets the criteria by which the award is assessed - the salved value, the skill and efforts of the salvors, the measure of success, the nature and degree of danger, the time and expenses incurred, the risk of liability, the promptness of the service, and, importantly, the skill and efforts of the salvors in preventing or minimising damage to the environment. The award may not exceed the salved value of the property.
  • Article 14 introduces special compensation: where the vessel or its cargo threatened damage to the environment and the salvor's Article 13 award is less than the expenses incurred, the salvor may recover those expenses from the shipowner, increased by up to 30 per cent - or exceptionally 100 per cent - where damage to the environment was actually prevented or minimised. This is the deliberate correction to the perverse incentive in pure no cure no pay.
  • Article 14 proved difficult in practice, because it turns on proving expenses and on a judgement about environmental benefit, and it generated expensive litigation. The market's response was the SCOPIC clause - Special Compensation P&I Club clause - which is not part of LOF but may be incorporated by the parties. It replaces the Article 14 mechanism with a pre-agreed tariff of daily rates for personnel, tugs and equipment, invoked at the salvor's option, backed by P&I club security, and supervised by a Special Casualty Representative appointed by the owner.

Why a Chief Engineer is asked this

Because the decision to sign is taken on board, at speed, and the engineering department supplies the facts it is taken on. Whether the ship can be refloated on her own power, what the condition of the machinery and the tanks is, and how long the ship can hold her position all bear directly on whether a salvage agreement is needed at all and on what the salvor will later be able to claim. The master signs; the Chief Engineer's report is part of what makes the signature defensible.

Study guide

The three sentences that carry the limb

In a short-note limb you have perhaps a third of a page. Spend it on: (1) what LOF is - a standard-form Lloyd's salvage contract, currently LOF 2020, no cure no pay, price deferred to London arbitration; (2) why that structure exists - it can be agreed in minutes in an emergency because only the price is left open; (3) how the environmental problem is handled - Salvage Convention 1989 Article 13 criteria including environmental effort, Article 14 special compensation, and SCOPIC as the tariff-based commercial alternative. Anything else is a bonus.

The distinction examiners test

LOF is a contract. The Salvage Convention 1989 is the law. General average is a different doctrine altogether - a sharing of sacrifice among the interests in the adventure, which may be engaged by the same casualty but answers a different question. Candidates who blur salvage into general average lose marks in both limbs, and this is a paper on which both limbs are commonly set together.
