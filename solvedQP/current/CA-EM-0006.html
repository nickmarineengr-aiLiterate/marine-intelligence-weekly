Coping with Stress-Affected Personnel, and Implementing It for Better Teamwork

Stress is one of the factors affecting the performance of an individual. What are the primary strategies that may be employed for coping with stress-affected personnel? How can these strategies best be implemented among a ship's personnel so as to motivate them for better teamwork?

Understand first

This question looks soft and is marked hard. The trap is that it invites sympathy - 'talk to the man, be understanding, encourage teamwork' - and sympathy is not a strategy. What turns it into a management answer is a HIERARCHY OF CONTROL, exactly as for any other hazard: remove the stressor if you can, control it by organisation if you cannot, support the individual only after those two, and treat the medical case as a medical case. A Chief Engineer who reorganises the work so that a man is not doing forty hours of unplanned maintenance on top of a watch has done more about stress than any amount of counselling. The second thing to hold onto is that the second half of the question is not a separate topic. It asks how 'these elements' - the coping strategies - are implemented so as to motivate for teamwork; the answer is that the measures that reduce stress are the same measures that build a team, because both come down to fair workload, clear authority, competence, being listened to, and being able to speak up without cost. Say that connection out loud. It is the sentence the question is fishing for.

Exam plan

Four sections, and put the hierarchy in writing

Head the answer What stress does to performance, The stressors on a ship, The strategies, in order of strength, and Implementation, and why it motivates a team. Naming the hierarchy explicitly - eliminate, control, support, treat - is what lifts this from a welfare essay to a management answer.

  • Open with the mechanism. One short paragraph on how stress degrades attention, judgement and risk perception, and therefore safety. That earns the right to everything after it.
  • Anchor it in instruments. ISM for the management duty, MLC 2006 as amended for the entitlements - including the 2022 amendments in force since 23 December 2024 - STCW for rest hours and for leadership competence, and the IMO fatigue guidelines. Candidates who write this question with no instrument in it lose the professional marks.
  • Separate fatigue from stress but link them. They are different mechanisms with a common cause in workload, and the examiner rewards the distinction.
  • Be concrete about implementation. Named actions a Chief Engineer takes - the work plan, the handover, the toolbox talk, the appraisal, the reporting culture - not aspirations.
  • Close on measurement. How you would know it had worked. Very few candidates do this and it is a strong finish.
Model answer — current framework

1. Why stress is a safety question, not a welfare question

Stress is the response to a demand that exceeds, or is felt to exceed, a person's capacity to meet it. A little arousal improves performance; beyond an optimum it degrades it, and the degradation is selective in the worst possible way. Attention narrows, so the scan of the plant becomes a stare at one gauge. Working memory shortens, so steps in a procedure are dropped. Judgement becomes short-term, so the quick fix beats the correct repair. Risk perception blunts, so a permit is skipped. Communication contracts, so the man who has spotted something says nothing.

Chronic stress adds physical and behavioural effects - disturbed sleep, illness, irritability, withdrawal, and increased use of alcohol or tobacco - and eventually anxiety and depression. The result is a person who is present but unreliable, which is more dangerous than an absent one, because the watch system assumes they are functioning. That is why casualty investigation so consistently finds the human element behind the technical cause, and it is why this is a Chief Engineer's problem and not the Company doctor's.

Distinguish stress from fatigue. Fatigue is the deficit left by insufficient rest or excessive duty; stress is the strain of demand exceeding capacity. They are separate mechanisms and they feed each other - stress spoils sleep, and lost sleep lowers the threshold at which demand becomes stress - so they usually have to be attacked together, and the same measures often serve both.

2. The stressors that are specific to a ship

  • Workload and manning - a reduced crew against a rising burden of planned maintenance, inspections, audits, vetting, port State control and reporting.
  • Watchkeeping and disturbed sleep - split rest, night calls, unmanned machinery space alarms, port work on top of a sea watch.
  • Confinement and isolation - long contracts, no shore leave, the same forty faces, and separation from family at exactly the moments that matter.
  • Poor connectivity - being unable to reach home, or reaching home and being unable to do anything about what you hear.
  • Multicultural and multilingual crews - miscommunication, differing norms of deference and of raising a concern, and hierarchy that discourages speaking up.
  • Inspection and blame pressure - the fear that a finding becomes a personal fault, and the fear of criminal exposure after a casualty or a pollution incident.
  • Job insecurity and money - contract renewal, promotion, wage delay, family debt.
  • Bullying, harassment and discrimination, including sexual harassment - now expressly prohibited under MLC 2006 as amended.
  • Exposure to danger - transit of a high-risk or conflict area, piracy, heavy weather, or the aftermath of a serious accident or a death on board.
  • Environmental load - noise, vibration, heat and motion, which raise the physiological cost of every task and are themselves regulated for exactly that reason.

3. The primary strategies, strongest first

This is the core of the answer. The order is the point: a strategy lower on the list is a mitigation of a stressor that a strategy higher up should have removed.

1. Eliminate or reduce the demandAttack the stressor itself. The two things not to do. Do not manage a stressed man by giving him less responsibility permanently and never discussing it - that reads as demotion and adds a stressor. And do not treat it as a discipline matter until you have established that it is not a capacity matter; disciplining a man for errors caused by a workload you set is both unjust and useless.

4. The framework this sits inside - and what it now requires

  • ISM Code. The safety management system must be resourced and manned, personnel must be familiarised and trained, and the Company must establish defined levels of authority and lines of communication and ensure personnel can communicate effectively in their duties. Reporting of non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences, and the Company's own review and evaluation, are the machinery through which a workload problem becomes a corrected problem.
  • MLC 2006, as amended. Hours of work and rest, minimum manning, accommodation, food and catering, medical care, shore leave, repatriation and access to welfare facilities. The 2022 amendments entered into force on 23 December 2024 and matter directly here: recreational facilities and amenities must include social connectivity, with reasonable access to ship-to-shore telephone and internet where available; there is a new obligation to ensure prompt disembarkation of seafarers needing immediate medical care and access to treatment ashore; the health and safety provisions now expressly prohibit shipboard violence, harassment, sexual harassment, bullying and assault, with preventive policies and safe reporting mechanisms required; and personal protective equipment must be of an appropriate size for the wearer.
  • STCW as amended. The minimum hours of rest and their exceptions; fitness for duty, including the alcohol limit; and, at management level, the competences in leadership and managerial skill and in engine-room resource management - which is the formal name for most of what this question is asking about.
  • IMO Guidelines on fatigue, MSC.1/Circ.1598 (24 January 2019). The current IMO guidance, addressed in separate modules to the seafarer, the master, the Company and the Administration, and expressly intended to be taken into account in developing and maintaining the safety management system, in training, and in casualty investigation. It is guidance, not a mandatory instrument, and should be named as such.

5. Implementing it - and why the same measures motivate a team

The second half of the question. The honest answer is that stress reduction and team motivation are not two programmes; they are the same actions seen from two sides. Every measure below does both jobs.

6. How you would know it worked

Finish with evidence, because a management answer that cannot be measured is an intention. Watch the near-miss and hazard-report rate (it should rise before anything else improves, because people have started speaking), rest-hour exceptions, overtime and deferred planned-maintenance backlog, the repeat-defect rate and unplanned work, sickness and medical repatriations, retention and re-joining rate, drill performance, and the pattern of findings at internal audit, port State control and vetting. Report the trend to the Company through the SMS review, so that the fix survives your relief.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Almost every candidate can list ways of being nice to a stressed shipmate. Very few produce a hierarchy of control, name the instruments, distinguish stress from fatigue, or answer the second half of the question as an implementation problem rather than a repeat of the first half. Those four things are where the marks are, and the last of them is where most answers simply stop.

The three traps

  • Answering with a motivation theory. The question is about coping with stress-affected personnel and then implementing those measures. Maslow and Herzberg are relevant background, but a question about stress answered as a lecture on the hierarchy of needs has changed the subject - and MIW's solved QP2512 Q4 already answers the Maslow question properly, so a candidate who wants that material should read it there.
  • Writing it with no regulation in it. This is a management-level examination. ISM, MLC as amended, STCW and the IMO fatigue guidelines all bear on it, and the 2022 MLC amendments in force since 23 December 2024 are the newest and most citable material available.
  • Treating discipline as a stress strategy. Until you have established that the errors are not caused by the workload you set, discipline is both unjust and ineffective. Say so - examiners notice it.

What this answer deliberately does not claim

  • It quotes no rest-hour figures in the body. They are in the critical-numbers card, sourced to a competent-authority restatement rather than to a text MIW holds under licence, so a candidate can see exactly how strong that claim is.
  • It gives no MLC Standard or Guideline numbers for the 2022 amendments beyond naming the subject matter of each. The substance - social connectivity, prompt medical disembarkation, the express prohibition of violence and harassment, appropriately sized PPE - is established and dated. MIW has not read the amendment instrument at source, and a mis-numbered Standard in an answer script is worse than a correctly described one with no number.
  • It does not claim MSC.1/Circ.1598 is mandatory. It is guidance, and it says so.

15-second recall
Stress narrows attention, shortens memory, shortens judgement and blunts risk perception - so it is a safety problem. Answer as a hierarchy: eliminate the demand (realistic PMS, manning, riding squads); control by organisation (rest patterns, planning, clear authority per ISM); build capacity (training, mentoring, ERM and leadership competence); detect early (know your people); support the individual (private conversation, reallocation, welfare and helplines, shore leave); treat the medical case medically (telemedical advice, prompt disembarkation under the 2022 MLC amendments); then review through the SMS. Implementation and motivation are the same actions: fair workload, briefed jobs, real delegation, protected and honest rest hours, no-blame reporting, drills, a working language, and defending welfare upward. Adopted but NOT yet in force — do not write these as current law
  • The comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code is in progress at IMO and NOTHING from it is adopted or in force. It is expected to reach an adoption meeting - an extended Maritime Safety Committee session or a diplomatic conference - around 2027, and among the workstreams carried forward is one on fatigue and hours of work and rest. A candidate may say that the review is under way and may not say that anything it produces is a requirement.
Superseded — do not cite
  • The IMO guidance on fatigue mitigation and management approved in 2001 was superseded by MSC.1/Circ.1598 of 24 January 2019. Older notes on this subject still cite the 2001 guidance and it should not be presented as current.
