Understand this first

A cargo does not become dangerous because it is reclassified; it was always dangerous, and the classification was wrong. Bauxite sat in the IMSBC Code as a Group C cargo, which meant it had no transportable moisture limit - so nobody had to certify its moisture and no master had a lawful basis to refuse it for being wet. The Bulk Jupiter was carried in full compliance with that schedule and was lost with 18 of her 19 crew. Everything that has happened since - the circulars, the research, the new Group A schedule for BAUXITE FINES - is the regulatory system correcting a classification, and the practical care the question asks about is simply what that corrected classification now requires.

Model written answer

1. What bauxite is, and why it was treated as safe

Bauxite is the principal ore of aluminium and one of the largest dry bulk trades in the world. For most of the history of the IMSBC Code it was listed as a Group C cargo - that is, a cargo which is neither liable to liquefy (Group A) nor possessed of a chemical hazard (Group B). The practical consequence is the important one: a Group C cargo has no transportable moisture limit, so no moisture certificate was required, no moisture limit applied, and the cargo could lawfully be loaded wet.

2. The casualty that forced the reassessment

The reassessment was forced by casualty, and principally by one. On 2 January 2015 the 10-year-old Bahamas-flag Supramax bulk carrier Bulk Jupiter was lost with 18 of her 19 crew, carrying bauxite. The cargo had been declared as a Group C cargo, and the IMO Sub-Committee on Carriage of Cargoes and Containers recorded that the loss may have been caused by liquefaction of the cargo. The characteristic of the casualty was its speed: the ship was lost so rapidly that there was almost no opportunity to respond.

That single loss exposed a structural gap rather than a single failure. The cargo had been carried in accordance with its schedule, and the schedule was wrong.

3. The mechanism - dynamic separation

The research that followed established that bauxite's moisture hazard is not classic liquefaction, and the distinction is the heart of this question.

  • Classic liquefaction - the cargo mass itself loses shear strength and behaves as a fluid. The whole body of cargo flows, and water and solids move together.
  • Dynamic separation - as the ship works, vibration and ship motion compact the cargo from underneath and moisture migrates upward. A liquid slurry of water and fine solids forms ABOVE the more solid material. The result is a free surface where the ship's stability calculation assumed a solid, trimmed cargo, with the loss of effective metacentric height that follows - and, if unchecked, capsize.

One consequence is a practical warning sign that a watchkeeper can actually use. Because the slurry lies on top and moves out of phase with the ship's roll period, the ship develops an atypical motion - a wobble - which is not the motion the loaded condition should produce. The Global Bauxite Working Group's observation was adopted by IMO in terms: an atypical motion of the ship (wobbling) may also be indicative of cargo instability, and the master should take appropriate action.

4. The regulatory response, in sequence

The sequence matters, because the interim measures have been overtaken and should not be quoted as current requirements.

  • CCC.1/Circ.2, 20 October 2015 - issued within months of the loss as an interim awareness measure. It advised masters not to accept the cargo unless the moisture content was certified below an indicative limit of 10% with the particle size distribution of the Group C schedule, or the cargo was declared Group A with TML and moisture declared, or a competent authority had assessed that it did not present Group A properties.
  • The Global Bauxite Working Group, with competent authorities, then researched the material's behaviour and established that bauxite presents a risk caused by moisture and that some bauxite cargoes should be treated as Group A.
  • CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1, 20 September 2017 - supersedes the 2015 circular, whose advice it records as having been superseded by the outcome of the research. The 10% indicative limit does not survive into Rev.1 and is not a current requirement. Rev.1 annexed the finalized Test Procedure for Determining the TML for Bauxite, the new Group A schedule with the Bulk Cargo Shipping Name BAUXITE FINES, and amendments to the Group C BAUXITE schedule.
  • Amendment 05-19, adopted by resolution MSC.462(101), brought all three into the Code and became mandatory on 1 January 2021.

5. What governs at this sitting, and which schedule applies

The IMSBC Code is mandatory under SOLAS chapters VI and VII, and the edition in force is amendment 07-23, mandatory since 1 January 2025. Bauxite now has two individual schedules, and everything else follows from which one the cargo falls into:

  • BAUXITE - Group C. The coarser material. No TML applies.
  • BAUXITE FINES - Group A, a cargo which may undergo dynamic separation. A TML applies and the moisture content must be kept below it.

The separation is by particle size distribution. The Test Procedure for Determining the TML for Bauxite applies to bauxite containing both more than 30% of fine particles less than 1 mm and more than 40% of particles less than 2.5 mm - the fine, drainage-poor material that holds moisture. The TML is determined by the Modified Proctor/Fagerberg test procedure for Bauxite, taken as the critical moisture content at 80% degree of saturation where the optimum moisture content occurs at saturation of 90% or above, and at 70% where it occurs below 90%.

The governing principle for the ship is unchanged and should be stated explicitly: the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its individual schedule govern - never the commodity name. "Bauxite" on a fixture note is not a classification.

6. Care and action before loading

  • Obtain the shipper's declaration required by SOLAS regulation VI/2 and section 4 of the Code, giving the BCSN, the group and the cargo's properties. Satisfy yourself that the BCSN matches what is actually being offered.
  • If the cargo is BAUXITE FINES or any Group A cargo, require the certificate of TML and the certificate of moisture content. Under section 4.5 the TML test must have been carried out within six months of loading, and the moisture content sampled and tested as near as practicable to loading and never more than seven days before it.
  • Check where and how the cargo has been stored - open stockpiles in a wet season are the single best predictor of trouble - and the loading port's recent and forecast weather.
  • Prepare the holds: clean and dry, bilge wells clear and tested, strainers and bilge covers in place and sealed so that fine material cannot blind the suctions, and sounding pipes clear. A bilge system that cannot be sounded or pumped is useless precisely when it matters.
  • Confirm the loading plan and stability, and for a bulk carrier the loading instrument and the requirements of SOLAS chapter XII, including water level detectors and alarms.

7. Care and action during loading

  • For a Group A cargo the rule is simple and absolute: the moisture content must be below the TML. If it is not, the cargo must not be loaded.
  • Inspect what is coming aboard. Visibly wet cargo, free water in the grab or on the conveyor, splattering, or material that flattens and glistens on the stow are all grounds to stop.
  • The can test is a complementary shipboard screening check, not a substitute for a TML determination. A cargo that fails it should be refused; a cargo that passes it has proved very little.
  • Where there has been significant rain or snow between testing and loading, check tests are required to confirm the moisture content is still below the TML.
  • The master is not bound by the shipper's declaration. If there is reason to doubt that the cargo is consistent with it, stop loading and require the shipper to verify the cargo's properties, seeking the advice of the competent authority of the port of loading if necessary. This is the express advice of the IMO circulars and it rests on SOLAS chapter VI.
  • Suspend loading in rain and close hatches; trim the cargo reasonably level to reduce the free surface available to any slurry and to limit shifting.

8. Care and action on passage, and if instability develops

  • Inspect the cargo surface daily where it is safe to do so - through hatch openings or access, without entering the space - and record what is seen. Look for free water, a slurry layer, flattening of the surface, or a change in the profile of the stow.
  • Sound the bilges at regular intervals, pump as necessary, and record the quantities. A rising trend is evidence, and the record is what makes it visible.
  • Treat atypical motion - the wobble - as a warning of cargo instability, not as a curiosity of the loaded condition.
  • If instability is suspected: reduce speed and alter course to ease the motion, avoid heavy weather and synchronous rolling, avoid ballasting or de-ballasting that would worsen stability, and inform owners, charterers, the flag Administration and the P&I club.
  • Consider seeking a port of refuge early. The Bulk Jupiter lesson is that once a slurry has formed and the ship is wobbling, the time available to act is very short.

The examiner's phrase is taken from the IMO circular itself, which asks that extreme care and appropriate action be taken, taking into account the provisions of relevant IMO instruments, when handling and carrying bauxite in bulk. The substance of that care is the sequence above: classify correctly, certify the moisture, refuse what is wet, watch the surface and the bilges, and act on the wobble.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints no mark allocation, and the stem contains two demands - causes and care and action - so give them roughly equal weight. The commonest failure is to spend the whole answer on a general account of liquefaction and never reach the practical care, which forfeits half the question. The second commonest is to give the care as an undifferentiated list of precautions. Answer the care limb in voyage order - before loading, during loading, on passage - because that is how it would actually be used and it is far easier to mark. Name dynamic separation early: it is the discriminating content, and an answer that never uses the term has not identified what makes bauxite different.

Where this question comes from

The printed stem is very nearly a quotation of an IMO circular. CCC.1/Circ.2/Rev.1 closes by inviting Member States to bring the information to the attention of the industry, "requesting that extreme care and appropriate action be taken, taking into account the provisions of relevant IMO instruments when handling and carrying Bauxite in bulk". The examiner has lifted that sentence almost word for word. Knowing this tells you what the answer is expected to contain: the casualty, the mechanism, the instruments, and the practical care - in that order.

The single idea that carries the answer

Bauxite was reclassified because of a casualty. Everything the question asks for follows from that one sentence: the causes are the reasons the old classification was wrong, and the care and action are the requirements the new classification imposes. If you open with that, the answer organises itself.

Dynamic separation is NOT liquefaction - and the distinction earns marks

This is the discriminating technical point and most candidates will blur it.

  • In liquefaction, the cargo mass loses shear strength and the whole body flows. Water and solids move together.
  • In dynamic separation, the cargo compacts from underneath under vibration and motion, moisture migrates upward, and a slurry forms on top of the solid cargo. The danger is a free surface that the stability calculation never allowed for.
  • The behavioural tell follows from the geometry: the slurry sloshes out of phase with the roll, producing the wobble. A liquefying cargo does not do that.

Why a Group C classification was so dangerous

Not because Group C is a lax standard, but because of what it switches off. A Group C cargo has no TML, so there is no moisture certificate, no moisture limit and no basis on which a master can lawfully refuse cargo for being wet. The cargo aboard the Bulk Jupiter was carried in accordance with its schedule. The schedule was the defect. That is worth one sentence in the answer, because it shows you understand that compliance and safety are not the same thing.

Do not quote the 10% limit as a current requirement

The indicative moisture limit of 10% belongs to CCC.1/Circ.2 of 2015, and it was an interim measure adopted while the research was running and before any Group A bauxite schedule existed. Rev.1 expressly supersedes that circular and records that its advice "has been superseded by the outcome of the research undertaken". Rev.1 carries no indicative moisture limit at all. Since 1 January 2021 the position is governed by the schedules and by a cargo-specific TML determined by test. Quote the 10% figure as history if you like - it shows you know the sequence - but never as the operative limit.

Numbers you can state, and numbers you cannot

  • Can state: the particle-size criteria - more than 30% of particles less than 1 mm and more than 40% less than 2.5 mm; the six month TML testing interval and the seven day moisture testing interval; the saturation figures 80% and 70% in the Modified Proctor/Fagerberg procedure for bauxite.
  • Cannot state: a TML value for bauxite. TML is cargo-specific and determined by test for each shipment. Any general figure is wrong by construction, and offering one suggests you think TML is a property of the commodity rather than of the consignment.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a generic liquefaction answer. Bauxite's mechanism is dynamic separation, and saying so is the point of the question.
  • Saying bauxite is a Group A cargo. BAUXITE is Group C; BAUXITE FINES is Group A. Which applies depends on particle size distribution, and the declared BCSN governs.
  • Quoting the 10% indicative limit as current. It is superseded.
  • Offering a TML figure for bauxite. TML is determined per shipment by test.
  • Treating the can test as a substitute for a TML certificate. It is a complementary screening check only.
  • Forgetting that the master may stop loading and require verification. The declaration is a starting point, not a defence.
  • Omitting the practical shipboard limb - daily surface inspection, bilge sounding and records, and the wobble.

Examiner traps

"The shipper hands you a Group C declaration for bauxite. Are you satisfied?" Not necessarily. Ask whether the material meets the Group C schedule's particle size distribution, how and where it has been stockpiled, and what the weather has been. If it is fine material that has been sitting in the open in the wet season, the declaration is questionable and you may stop loading and require verification.

"Your cargo passed the can test. Can you load?" The can test is a complementary screening check. Passing it does not establish that moisture content is below TML - only a certificate based on the proper test procedure does that.

"The ship has developed an odd wobble two days out. What do you do?" Treat it as evidence of cargo instability. Reduce speed and ease the motion, inspect the surface where safe, sound and record the bilges, avoid heavy weather, notify owners, charterers, flag and club, and consider a port of refuge early.

Uncertainty and currency

Both IMO circulars were read in full at source, and the particle-size criteria and the saturation figures are taken from the test procedure annexed to Rev.1. One limitation is declared: MIW holds no licensed IMSBC Code, so the individual schedules for BAUXITE and BAUXITE FINES have not been read in the Code itself. Their existence, their groups and the criteria separating them rest on the IMO circular and its annexes together with two independent authoritative sources - a strong position, but not the Code text, and the answer does not pretend otherwise. No TML value, stowage factor or angle of repose is quoted anywhere. Finally, amendment 08-25 was adopted at MSC 110 in June 2025, three months before this examination, but applies voluntarily only from 1 January 2026 and becomes mandatory on 1 January 2027 - at this sitting it had no application, and nothing from it is relied on.

Understand this first

CII is not a cap on emissions and not a trading scheme. It is an efficiency ratio - carbon per unit of transport work - turned into a public letter grade, and almost everything interesting about it follows from those two facts. Because it is a ratio, it can be improved by increasing the denominator rather than reducing emissions, which is where the shortcomings come from. Because it is a public grade attached to an asset, its real force is commercial rather than regulatory: the regulation itself imposes no fine, but a charterer, a bank and a buyer all read the letter. Keep those two ideas separate and both limbs of the question answer themselves.

Model written answer

1. a) What CII is, and where it sits in MARPOL

The Carbon Intensity Indicator is the operational half of the IMO's short-term GHG measure, imposed by MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28, 'Operational carbon intensity'. It applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above - the threshold of the IMO Data Collection System under regulation 27, on whose fuel data it runs. It must be distinguished from EEXI, a one-off design index applying from 400 gt: EEXI asks what the ship is capable of, CII what she actually did last year.

2. How attained and required CII are set, and how a ship is rated

  • The ship calculates an attained annual operational CII - CO2 per unit of transport work over the calendar year. For most cargo ship types this is the AER, CO2 divided by deadweight capacity multiplied by distance sailed; cruise and ro-pax types use a different metric.
  • The required annual operational CII is the 2019 reference line for that ship type and size reduced by a factor Z: required CII = reference value x (1 - Z/100). Z is 5% for 2023, 7% for 2024, 9% for 2025 and 11% for 2026, and was extended by resolution MEPC.400(83) of 11 April 2025 to 13.625%, 16.250%, 18.875% and 21.500% for 2027 to 2030.
  • The attained value is verified annually and the ship rated A to E, C being the required standard, the rating recorded with the Statement of Compliance issued under the DCS.
  • The factors were set so that, in combination with the rest of Annex VI, shipping reduces CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 compared with 2008.

3. What follows from a poor rating

There is no fine and no detention. The consequence is administrative: a ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E for one year, must develop a corrective action plan showing how the required index of C or above will be achieved. It is incorporated in SEEMP Part III and agreed before the Statement of Compliance is issued, so a ship that ignores it cannot keep her documentation in order. Incentives to A- and B-rated ships are encouraged.

4. The commercial impact on the shipping business

The commercial effect far exceeds the regulatory sanction, because the rating is a public, comparable letter grade on an asset:

  • Chartering. Charterers screen tonnage on rating and some will not fix D or E ships. Because a time charterer controls speed and routing - the very things that set the rating - CII clauses are now standard, notably the BIMCO CII Operations Clause 2022. Who bears a bad rating has become a negotiated term.
  • Operational control shifts. Owners reserve rights over speed, hull and propeller cleaning and voyage optimisation, so slow steaming becomes contractual - with the machinery consequences of prolonged low-load running that follow.
  • Finance. Lenders under the Poseidon Principles report portfolio climate alignment, so rating feeds into the cost and availability of debt.
  • Asset value. A persistent D or E shortens commercial life, depresses second-hand values and brings forward retrofit or scrapping decisions.
  • Cargo owners. Shippers reporting their own scope 3 emissions demand rating data, so pressure comes from the cargo side as well as the regulatory side.

5. b) The shortcomings the industry has identified

  • It rewards capacity, not cargo. The AER denominator is deadweight capacity times distance, not cargo carried, so a ship can improve her rating by sailing further with less on board while absolute emissions rise - a genuine perverse incentive.
  • Waiting is punished, sailing is not. Idling at anchor adds fuel but no distance. Port congestion and 'sail fast then wait' lie outside the ship's control, and the incentive to keep moving can increase emissions.
  • Trade patterns are penalised structurally. Short-sea, multi-parcel chemical and product, offshore and ice-class vessels and ships with long port stays are disadvantaged by a metric built around long laden passages, however efficiently run.
  • Responsibility and control are split. The rating attaches to the ship and the owner's documentation, but a time charterer determines speed, route, waiting and cargo intake.
  • It is an efficiency ratio used as a climate instrument. Being indexed rather than absolute, improving average CII does not itself guarantee a fall in total emissions.

6. The remedial measures adopted, and how far the review had got

The regulation anticipated this. Regulation 28.11 requires a review of the CII requirements to be completed by 1 January 2026, covering effectiveness, reinforced corrective action, enforcement, the data collection system, and revision of the Z factor and reference values.

  • Phase 1 was completed at MEPC 83 in April 2025: implementation experience was gathered and analysed and a list of challenges and gaps identified.
  • Resolution MEPC.400(83), adopted 11 April 2025, amended the G3 Guidelines to set the 2027 to 2030 reduction factors, aligning them with the 2023 GHG Strategy's carbon-intensity ambition.
  • The SEEMP Guidelines were also amended to allow other CII metrics to be developed at future sessions - the door through which a cargo-based metric would have to come.
  • MEPC 83 additionally approved draft amendments to regulations 20, 25 and 28, circulated by Circular Letter No.5005 of 11 April 2025 with a view to adoption at an extraordinary session in October 2025. At this sitting that session had not met: the amendments were approved but neither adopted nor in force.
  • The industry's own remedies have been contractual - the BIMCO clause - and operational, through just-in-time arrival and virtual arrival schemes aimed at waiting time.

The honest position at this sitting is therefore this. The shortcomings had been formally acknowledged and the review was under way but not complete, with its regulation 28.11 deadline of 1 January 2026 still ahead. What had actually been adopted was an extension of the reduction factors to 2030 and an amendment permitting alternative metrics to be developed; what had been approved and was awaiting adoption was the regulation 20, 25 and 28 package. The metric itself - the source of most of the criticism - had not been changed, and the remaining stages of the review were still to come.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) carries 10 of the 16 marks and two-thirds of candidates spend all of it on the formula. The four steps force the answer up the ladder from instrument to money, and the commercial half is where the differentiating marks are. Limb (b) is short and is best answered as mechanism then remedy, because a list of complaints without the mechanism that causes them reads as grumbling rather than analysis.

Why an indexed measure behaves this way

Any ratio can be improved by growing the denominator instead of shrinking the numerator. CII's denominator is transport work, and transport work was defined using deadweight capacity because capacity is a known, verifiable number that appears on every certificate, whereas cargo actually carried is commercially sensitive, unevenly recorded and open to manipulation. The choice was made for auditability, and the perverse incentives are the price of that choice. This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it explains why the fix is hard: a cargo-based metric would need a trustworthy, global, verifiable source of cargo data that does not currently exist.

CII, EEXI, EEDI and the proposed Net-Zero Framework - keeping them apart

  • EEDI - design index, new ships, regulation 22. What the ship was built to do.
  • EEXI - design index applied retrospectively to existing ships from 400 gt, regulation 23. A one-off calculation, often satisfied by engine or shaft power limitation.
  • CII - operational, annual, ships of 5,000 GT and above, regulation 28. What she actually did.
  • The Net-Zero Framework - a proposed new MARPOL Annex VI chapter, a fuel-intensity standard with a pricing mechanism. At this sitting it had been approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 and circulated for adoption at the extraordinary session MEPC/ES.2 (14 to 17 October 2025), which had not yet met. CII was in force and enforceable; the Net-Zero Framework was not adopted and not in force.

What the examiner means by 'remedial measures adopted'

This is the trap in limb (b), and it is a trap about verbs. The industry has asked for a great many things - a cargo-based metric, an exemption for waiting time, relief for short-sea trades. Almost none of that had been adopted. This sitting falls in a narrow and examinable gap. MEPC 83 met from 7 to 11 April 2025, five months before the paper. Common mistakes

  • Calling CII a cap on emissions or an emissions trading scheme. It is neither.

  • Quoting a reduction factor without its year - the figure changes annually and a bare percentage is unmarkable.
  • Answering limb (b) with what the industry has asked for rather than what was actually adopted.
  • Confusing the 5,000 GT CII threshold with the 400 gt EEXI threshold.
  • Treating the Net-Zero Framework, or the regulation 20/25/28 package, as adopted. At this sitting both were approved and awaiting an October 2025 session that had not met.

Model written answer

1. Particular Average

Particular Average is a partial loss of ship, cargo or freight caused accidentally by a peril insured against, which is not a general average loss. It is fortuitous rather than deliberate, and it falls solely on the owner of the property damaged - there is no right of contribution from the other interests. A sea striking in a forecastle door, or seawater damage to one parcel of cargo, is particular average.

2. General Average, and how it differs

General Average is the deliberate counterpart. Under Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules: "There is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure." The loss is then shared rateably by all the interests saved - ship, cargo and freight - in proportion to their contributory values. In Indian law a general average loss is defined at section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

3. When General Average may be declared

Every element of Rule A must be present:

  • a real peril to the common adventure - apprehension alone is not enough;
  • the act must be extraordinary - not something the ship is already bound to do under the contract of carriage;
  • it must be intentional and voluntary, not accidental;
  • it must be reasonable;
  • it must be for the common safety of the property in a common maritime adventure, not for one interest alone;
  • there must be a saving of property - if nothing is saved there is nothing to contribute to.

Typical acts: jettison of cargo, voluntary stranding, water damage from firefighting, machinery damage sustained in refloating, salvage and tug costs, and port-of-refuge expenses. The shipowner, normally through the Master, declares general average. The declaration is an operational step; it does not by itself establish anyone's liability or entitlement.

4. Average adjusters and their role

An average adjuster is an independent professional appointed by the shipowner to prepare the adjustment - the statement that apportions the loss. The adjuster does not declare general average and does not decide liability. The role is to:

  • collect GA security before cargo is released - an average bond from cargo interests and a GA guarantee from their underwriters, or a cash deposit;
  • determine which sacrifices and expenditures are allowable under the applicable Rules;
  • ascertain the contributory values of ship, cargo and freight at the termination of the adventure;
  • apportion the contributions rateably and issue the adjustment;
  • act impartially between the interests, notwithstanding that the shipowner appoints and pays him.

The security step is not administrative housekeeping. The shipowner has a lien on cargo for the general average contribution, and releasing the cargo destroys that lien - which is why cargo is held until the bond and guarantee are in place, and why a large general average can delay an entire container ship's cargo for months.

5. Which rules actually apply

The York-Antwerp Rules are not law and do not apply automatically. They are a contractual regime, effective only when incorporated into the bill of lading or charterparty - which is why those documents name the edition. YAR 2016 was adopted by the Comite Maritime International at New York in May 2016, and BIMCO agreed that its forms should refer to YAR 2016; older contracts still commonly incorporate YAR 1994 or 2004. Absent incorporation, general average falls back on the applicable national law - in India, the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

Why this structure scores

Four limbs, roughly four marks each. The commonest failure is a long, admiring essay on General Average that never reaches the average adjuster - forfeiting a quarter of the question. Answer under four headings and the marks look after themselves. The PA/GA contrast is worth a small table: it is fast to write and it demonstrates the distinction rather than asserting it.

The two traps

First: who declares? The shipowner declares general average, in practice through the Master. The average adjuster does not declare it - he prepares the adjustment afterwards. Candidates routinely say the adjuster declares GA, which inverts the relationship.

Second: the York-Antwerp Rules are not law. They are a contractual code. They bind only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them, and the incorporating clause names the edition. Saying so, and naming YAR 2016 as the current edition while noting that 1994 and 2004 remain in live contracts, is what separates a strong answer.

The right of contribution is the whole point

PA and GA differ in one commercially decisive respect: who pays. In particular average the loss lies where it falls. In general average it is redistributed across everyone whose property was saved. That is why the shipowner has a lien on cargo for the GA contribution, and why cargo is not released until security is in place - releasing it destroys the security.

Which Indian statute - and which one is NOT engaged

The Indian statutory reference for general average is section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. Two mistakes are available here and both are common. The first is citing the UK Marine Insurance Act 1906: it is the model for the Indian Act but is not the operative statute in an Indian examination. The second is reaching for the Merchant Shipping Act, which does not govern general average at all - general average is a matter of marine insurance and of the contract of carriage. At this sitting the operative Indian merchant shipping statute was in any event the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958; but the point is that neither Act is engaged by this question.

Examiner traps

"The Master jettisons cargo that was already on fire and unsalvageable. Is that general average?" Probably not - Rule A requires an extraordinary sacrifice of property that was still of value and at risk; sacrificing what is already lost saves nothing.

Model written answer

1. What a maritime lien is

A maritime lien is a privileged claim against the vessel herself rather than against any person. It is not created by agreement and it is not registered: it attaches to the ship by operation of law at the moment the claim arises, and it does so without notice to anyone. Until it is enforced it is inchoate - a right in the ship that lies dormant; it is crystallised by arrest and realised by forced sale. Its practical significance is its rank: a maritime lien takes priority over a registered mortgage, even one registered earlier and for a far larger sum.

That combination - secret, unregistered, automatic and senior - is what distinguishes it from an ordinary possessory lien, which depends on holding the goods and is lost when possession is given up.

2. How the lien travels with the property

This is the defining characteristic, and the Convention states it in one sentence. Article 8: the maritime liens follow the vessel, notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag.

  • The lien is attached to the res - the ship - and not to the owner, so selling the ship does not shed it.
  • It binds a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. A buyer may acquire a ship with unpaid crew wages or a salvage award still charged against her and have no means of discovering it from any register. This is why it is called a secret lien and why prudent buyers take indemnities and searches.
  • Reflagging and re-registration make no difference: the lien travels with the hull, not with the certificate.
  • It is defeated in only two ways - by a forced sale complying with article 12, which sweeps the ship clean of liens and encumbrances, or by lapse of time under article 9.

Under article 9 the article 4 liens are extinguished after one year unless, before then, the vessel has been arrested or seized and that arrest or seizure leads to a forced sale. Time runs from the claimant's discharge from the vessel for wage claims and from the moment the claim arises for the others, and it runs continuously, without suspension or interruption.

3. In rem and in personam

  • An action in personam is brought against a person - the owner, charterer, manager or operator. The claimant must find that person, establish jurisdiction over them, and then enforce against whatever assets they have. If the defendant is a single-ship company with no other assets, a judgment in personam may be worth nothing.
  • An action in rem is brought against the ship herself, which is named as defendant. The court's jurisdiction is founded on the presence of the res within the jurisdiction, and the ship may be arrested.

The connection between the two limbs of this question is the point the examiner is looking for: the maritime lien is what gives the claimant a right in rem against the ship. Because the lien attaches to the vessel and follows her, the claimant can proceed against the vessel wherever she is found and whoever now owns her.

Arrest is not usually an end in itself. It is a coercive step: it either compels the provision of security - a P&I club letter of undertaking or a bank guarantee - so that the ship may trade on, or, if no security is forthcoming, it produces a fund through forced sale out of which the claims are paid. The two forms of action are not alternatives and may be pursued together.

4. Which convention deals with maritime liens

The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993, adopted at Geneva on 6 May 1993 and in force from 5 September 2004. It is the successor to the earlier Brussels Conventions of 1926 and 1967 on the same subject, neither of which achieved wide acceptance. Its stated purpose is to improve the conditions for ship financing and the development of national merchant fleets - which explains its structure: it protects mortgagees by making mortgages recognisable and enforceable across States, and it does so by defining exhaustively the small class of claims that outrank them.

5. What the convention secures - article 4

Article 4(1) secures five claims, against the owner, demise charterer, manager or operator, by a maritime lien on the vessel:

  • (a) claims for wages and other sums due to the master, officers and other members of the vessel's complement in respect of their employment on the vessel, including costs of repatriation and social insurance contributions payable on their behalf;
  • (b) claims in respect of loss of life or personal injury, whether on land or on water, in direct connection with the operation of the vessel;
  • (c) claims for reward for the salvage of the vessel;
  • (d) claims for port, canal and other waterway dues and pilotage dues;
  • (e) claims based on tort arising out of physical loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel, other than loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects carried on the vessel.

Article 4(2) removes two categories from limbs (b) and (e): damage from the carriage of oil or other hazardous or noxious substances for which compensation is payable under regimes providing strict liability and compulsory insurance, and damage from the radioactive properties of nuclear fuel, products or waste. The reasoning is that those claimants already have a dedicated fund, and there is no need to give them a lien as well.

Note what is not a maritime lien: cargo claims, and the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers and agents. They may be maritime claims founding an action in rem, but they carry no lien under article 4.

6. How the liens rank among themselves - article 5

  • The article 4 liens take priority over registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges, and no other claim takes priority over either, subject to article 12(3) and (4).
  • They rank in the order listed in article 4 - so wages before personal injury, before salvage, before dues, before tort.
  • But salvage liens jump the queue: a lien securing a claim for salvage reward takes priority over all other maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The logic is obvious - the salvor preserved the very fund the others are claiming against.
  • Liens within each of (a), (b), (d) and (e) rank pari passu among themselves.
  • Salvage liens rank in the inverse order of time in which the claims accrued - the later salvage ranks first - each claim being deemed to accrue on the date the salvage operation terminated.

7. The order of settlement after forced sale - article 12

A forced sale in a State Party sweeps the vessel clean: all registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges, except those assumed by the purchaser with the holders' consent, and all liens and other encumbrances of whatsoever nature, cease to attach to the vessel, provided the ship was within that State's jurisdiction and the sale complied with its law and with articles 11 and 12. The proceeds are then distributed in this order:

  • 1. The costs and expenses of the arrest or seizure and of the subsequent sale, paid first out of the proceeds. These expressly include the costs of upkeep of the vessel and the crew, and the wages and other sums under article 4(1)(a) incurred from the time of the arrest or seizure.
  • 2. Wreck-removal costs, where a State Party has so provided. Article 12(3) permits a State to provide that, where a stranded or sunken vessel has been removed by a public authority in the interest of safe navigation or protection of the marine environment, those removal costs are paid before all other claims secured by a maritime lien. This is optional, not automatic.
  • 3. The article 4 maritime liens, in their article 5 order and subject to the salvage rules.
  • 4. The right of retention of a shipbuilder or shiprepairer under article 7. Article 12(4) requires him to surrender possession to the purchaser, but entitles him to be satisfied out of the proceeds after the article 4 lienholders.
  • 5. Registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges complying with article 1.
  • 6. Other maritime liens granted under national law under article 6, which rank after both the article 4 liens and the registered mortgages.
  • 7. The residue, if any, to the owner, and States Parties must ensure the proceeds are actually available and freely transferable.

The purchaser is then entitled to a certificate that the vessel is sold free of encumbrances, on production of which the registrar must delete the registered mortgages and charges and either register the vessel in the purchaser's name or issue a certificate of deregistration.

8. The Indian statutory position, kept separate

In India the subject is governed domestically by the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 (No. 22 of 2017, assented 9 August 2017), which consolidated admiralty jurisdiction and vested it in the High Courts. Its scheme closely follows the Convention, and the differences are worth knowing:

  • Section 9(1) lists the same five maritime liens in the same order, with two widenings: limb (c) covers salvage including special compensation, and limb (d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel.
  • Section 9(2) reproduces the travelling rule - the lien continues notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag - and the one-year extinction, but with a proviso giving TWO years for wage claims under limb (a), running from the date the wage, repatriation cost or social insurance contribution fell due. That is more generous to seafarers than the Convention.
  • Section 10 sets the order of priority of maritime claims in an admiralty proceeding as: (a) claims where there is a maritime lien; (b) registered mortgages and charges of the same nature; (c) all other claims - with claims in a single category ranking equally, and salvage claims in inverse order of time.

(The stem asks about the convention, and the answer above is given at convention level. The Indian Act is set out separately because it is the law an Indian court would apply, and it should not be conflated with the Convention.)

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains five separate demands in sixteen marks - define the lien, explain how it travels, explain in rem and in personam, name the convention, and give the order of settlement. That is roughly three marks each, so none of them deserves an essay and none may be skipped. The two that candidates most often lose are the last: they name the convention vaguely, or give a half-remembered priority list. The order of settlement is the single most concrete thing in the question and it is the easiest to prepare, so it should be written out as a numbered list, not narrated.

The idea that links the whole question

The lien and the action in rem are two halves of one mechanism. The maritime lien attaches to the ship rather than to a person; that is precisely what allows the claimant to sue the ship rather than a person. If you state that link explicitly - the lien is what gives the right in rem - the middle three demands of the question join up instead of reading as three unrelated definitions.

Why the lien is called secret, and why that matters commercially

There is no register of maritime liens. A buyer inspecting the ship's registry sees mortgages and charges but cannot see unpaid crew wages, an outstanding salvage award or a personal injury claim - and under article 8 all of those follow the vessel through a sale, a change of registry and a change of flag. So a purchaser can take delivery of a ship already encumbered by claims ranking ahead of the mortgage financing the purchase. This is why sale contracts carry indemnities and why a forced sale under article 12 is commercially valuable: it is the one transaction that delivers a genuinely clean ship, which is exactly why an arresting creditor's remedy has teeth.

The two salvage rules - state both

Salvage is the exception to almost every ranking rule in the Convention and both exceptions are examinable.

  • Salvage jumps ahead of earlier liens. A salvage lien takes priority over all maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The salvor preserved the fund everyone else is claiming against, so he is paid first out of it.
  • Salvage liens rank in inverse order of time. Where there is more than one salvage, the later ranks ahead of the earlier - each claim accruing on the date its salvage operation terminated. Every other category ranks pari passu.

What is NOT a maritime lien

A quick way to show command of article 4 is to say what it leaves out. Cargo claims are not secured by a maritime lien - article 4(1)(e) expressly excludes loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects. Nor are the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers, agents and chandlers. These may still be maritime claims supporting an action in rem and an arrest, but they carry no lien and therefore rank behind registered mortgages. The distinction between a maritime claim and a maritime lien is exactly what the priority list is about.

The Indian dimension - and the statement to avoid

The examiner asks about the convention, so answer at convention level. The Indian statute is the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, and giving it separately is a strength: its section 9 reproduces the five liens and their order, and its section 10 gives the three-tier priority - liens, then registered mortgages, then all other claims.

Two genuine Indian divergences are worth knowing. Section 9(1)(c) covers salvage including special compensation, and section 9(1)(d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel. Most importantly, section 9(2) gives seafarers two years for wage claims where the Convention gives one - more generous than the Convention.

What to avoid: do not assert whether or not India is a party to the 1993 Convention. It is not necessary to answer any part of this question, and a confident statement either way is a risk for no reward. Say what the Convention provides, say what the Indian Act provides, and let the two stand side by side.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a maritime claim with a maritime lien. Every lien is a claim; very few claims are liens.
  • Saying a maritime lien is lost on sale of the ship. It is not - article 8 is the whole point of the question's second limb.
  • Treating in rem and in personam as alternatives. They may be pursued together.
  • Putting mortgages ahead of maritime liens. Article 5 puts the liens first, which is the commercially surprising and therefore examinable point.
  • Forgetting that arrest and sale costs and post-arrest crew wages are paid before everything, including the liens.
  • Omitting the salvage inverse-order rule, or applying inverse order to all categories rather than only to salvage.
  • Giving a mortgage a rank ahead of the shipbuilder's or shiprepairer's right of retention. Article 12(4) puts retention after the article 4 liens but the Convention leaves mortgages behind both.

Examiner traps

"You buy a ship with a clean registry search. Can you still be caught?" Yes. Maritime liens are unregistered and follow the vessel through a change of ownership, registration and flag. Only a forced sale under article 12, or the lapse of time under article 9, clears them.

"Crew wages or the bank - who is paid first?" Crew. Article 4(1)(a) is the first maritime lien and article 5(1) puts all article 4 liens ahead of registered mortgages. And the wages incurred after arrest are paid even before that, as part of the article 12(2) costs.

"Two salvage services, one in January and one in June. Who ranks first?" June - salvage liens rank in inverse order of time.

"Is a bunker supplier's claim a maritime lien under the Convention?" No. It may be a maritime claim founding an action in rem, but article 4 does not secure it, so it ranks behind registered mortgages.

Model written answer

1. The Convention, and its status at this sitting

The instrument is the International Convention on Liability and Compensation for Damage in Connection with the Carriage of Hazardous and Noxious Substances by Sea - the 1996 Convention as amended by the 2010 Protocol, which by article 18 of that Protocol are 'read and interpreted together as one single instrument' and cited as the 2010 HNS Convention. It does for hazardous and noxious cargoes what the CLC and Fund Conventions do for persistent oil: strict liability on the owner, backed by compulsory insurance and a second tier financed by cargo interests. As the question states, it had not yet entered into force and was expected to do so shortly.

2. a) The substances covered - the seven categories of article 1.5

The Convention does not list commodities. Article 1.5(a) defines HNS as any substances, materials and articles carried on board a ship as cargo falling within seven categories, each defined by reference to an existing IMO instrument - so the HNS list updates automatically as those instruments are amended:

  • (i) Oils carried in bulk, as defined in regulation 1 of MARPOL Annex I - crude oil, fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil.
  • (ii) Noxious liquid substances carried in bulk, as defined in regulation 1.10 of MARPOL Annex II, together with substances provisionally categorised in pollution category X, Y or Z under regulation 6.3 - for example vegetable oils and phosphoric acid.
  • (iii) Dangerous liquid substances carried in bulk listed in chapter 17 of the IBC Code, and products for which preliminary carriage conditions have been prescribed under paragraph 1.1.6 - benzene, styrene monomer, methanol, sulphuric acid.
  • (iv) Dangerous, hazardous and harmful substances, materials and articles in packaged form covered by the IMDG Code - drummed and containerised chemicals, corrosives, explosives, flammable solids.
  • (v) Liquefied gases listed in chapter 19 of the IGC Code, and products prescribed under paragraph 1.1.6 - LNG, LPG, ammonia, ethylene, vinyl chloride monomer.
  • (vi) Liquid substances carried in bulk with a flashpoint not exceeding 60°C measured by closed-cup test - gasoline, naphtha, and similar light products.
  • (vii) Solid bulk materials possessing chemical hazards covered by the IMSBC Code, to the extent that they are also subject to the IMDG Code in effect in 1996 when carried in packaged form - ammonium nitrate and ammonium nitrate based fertilizers, seed cake.

Article 1.5(b) extends the definition to residues from the previous carriage in bulk of substances in (a)(i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii), so a tank that has been discharged but not cleaned still carries HNS for the purposes of the Convention.

3. a) Bulk HNS and packaged HNS - why the split matters

Article 1.5bis defines bulk HNS as categories (i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii) together with residues; article 1.5ter defines packaged HNS as category (iv) alone. The distinction is not academic: it sets the owner's limit of liability under article 9 and, in the second tier, packaged cargo does not contribute to the Fund in the way bulk cargo does.

4. b) The heads of damage - article 1.6

'Damage' is defined in article 1.6 and is significantly wider than pollution. It comprises four heads:

  • (a) Loss of life or personal injury, whether on board or outside the ship carrying the substances, caused by those substances.
  • (b) Loss of or damage to property outside the ship caused by those substances.
  • (c) Loss or damage by contamination of the environment - with the standard limitation that compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit from that impairment, is confined to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken.
  • (d) The costs of preventive measures, and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Two consequences follow, and they are where the marks are. First, because heads (a) and (b) are not qualified by the word contamination, the Convention reaches fire, explosion, toxicity and reactivity damage - the characteristic ways a chemical or gas cargo actually causes harm - and not merely spillage. That is the single most important structural difference between HNS and the oil-pollution conventions, which are contamination regimes. Secondly, economic loss consequential on contamination, of the kind suffered by fishing, mariculture and tourism interests, is recoverable through head (c). Where damage caused by the HNS cannot reasonably be separated from damage caused by other factors, the article deems the whole of it to be HNS damage.

5. b) What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it

  • CLC pollution damage. By article 4.3(a) the Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is actually payable under it - so the exclusion bites on the definition, not on the outcome of a claim.
  • Radioactive material of class 7 in the IMDG or IMSBC Codes, by article 4.3(b).
  • Claims arising out of a contract for the carriage of goods or passengers, by article 4.1 - cargo claims remain a matter for the bill of lading and the Hague-Visby or Hamburg regime.
  • Workers' compensation and social security, to the extent of incompatibility, by article 4.2; and warships and State non-commercial ships, by article 4.4.

One exclusion is commonly stated and is not in the text. Bunker oil is not excluded by any article of this Convention; the word does not appear in it. Bunkers fall outside because article 1.5(a) covers only substances carried as cargo. A ship's own fuel is not cargo, so it is never HNS in the first place - which is precisely why a separate Bunkers Convention was needed.

6. c) Why an oil products tanker needs certificates under all three conventions

Because the three regimes are mutually exclusive but jointly incomplete, and which of them applies to this ship changes with the parcel she happens to be carrying. Two variables do the work: what the substance is, and how the damage arises.

  • CLC 92 covers only persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil (article I.5) and only damage by contamination (article I.6). It covers such oil both as cargo and in the bunkers of such a ship. So on a voyage with a crude or fuel-oil parcel, the tanker is a CLC 'ship' and needs a CLC certificate - required by article VII once she carries more than 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo.
  • Bunkers 2001 covers contamination damage from the ship's own fuel (article 1.5), but article 4.1 disapplies it to anything that is CLC pollution damage. So it governs her bunkers precisely on the voyages when she is not a CLC ship - when she is carrying only non-persistent products such as gasoline or naphtha, or is clean of persistent residues. The certificate is required of a registered owner of a ship over 1,000 gross tonnage (article 7).
  • HNS covers the cargo in every case the other two do not reach. Non-persistent oil products are outside CLC's definition of oil but squarely inside HNS categories (i) and (vi). And even with a persistent oil cargo, fire and explosion damage is not 'pollution damage' under CLC, which is confined to contamination - so article 4.3(a) does not exclude it and HNS answers for it.

A products tanker trades across all of these states, often within a single charter: persistent one voyage, light distillate the next, in ballast with residues in between. No one certificate follows her through that cycle, and none of the three regimes will answer for a gap that belongs to another. She therefore carries all three so that, whatever she is loaded with and however the casualty develops, there is an identified insurer and a certificate to found a direct action against.

7. c) The certificate itself, and the two tiers behind it

Under article 12.1 the owner of a ship registered in a State Party and actually carrying HNS must maintain insurance or other financial security in the sums fixed by applying the article 9.1 limits. Article 12.2 requires a compulsory insurance certificate in the model form of Annex I, issued or certified by the State of the ship's registry, naming the ship, the owner, the IMO ship identification number, the type and duration of security and the insurer. It must be carried on board (12.4), it is mutually recognised by other States Parties (12.7), and a claim may be brought directly against the insurer (12.8).

Behind the certificate sits the two-tier structure. Tier one is the owner's strict liability under article 7, limited by article 9.1 to 100 million units of account for bulk HNS and 115 million where packaged HNS is or may be involved. Tier two is the HNS Fund, financed by receivers of contributing cargo, which under article 14 pays where no liability arises, where the owner is financially incapable, or where the damage exceeds his limit - the total of both tiers being capped at 250 million units of account for any one incident. Claims are time-barred under article 37 after three years from knowledge of the damage and in no case later than ten years from the incident.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The three limbs are worth 6, 5 and 5, and they are not equally hard. Limb (a) is recall and can be earned in full by anyone who has learned the seven categories with their parent instruments - it is the most reliably scoring six marks in the question. Limb (b) looks like recall and is not: the marks sit in noticing that the definition is wider than pollution. Limb (c) is the discriminating limb. It cannot be answered by describing three conventions in turn; it has to explain why one ship needs all three, which means identifying the variable each convention is drawn around.

The design idea behind article 1.5 - definition by reference

The Convention never says 'benzene' or 'LNG'. It says: whatever is in IBC chapter 17, whatever is in IGC chapter 19, whatever the IMDG Code covers in packaged form, and so on. This is deliberate and worth understanding rather than memorising. A closed commodity list written in 1996 would have been obsolete within a decade; a list of pointers updates itself every time those codes are amended. It also means a candidate who knows the codes already knows most of the HNS definition - the examiner is testing whether you can see the connection, not whether you have memorised a separate list.

The one phrase to underline in article 1.5(a) is 'carried on board a ship as cargo'. Almost every boundary question in this topic - bunkers, stores, residues, ballast - is answered by that phrase before any exclusion article is reached.

Why 'damage' is wider here than under CLC and Bunkers

Compare the three definitions side by side and the point becomes obvious. CLC article I.6(a): loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination. Bunkers article 1.9(a): loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination. HNS article 1.6: (a) loss of life or personal injury on board or outside the ship - no mention of contamination; (b) loss of or damage to property outside the ship - no mention of contamination; only head (c) speaks of contamination, and it is the environmental head.

That is not drafting untidiness. Oil pollution harms by coating; chemicals and gases harm by burning, exploding, reacting and poisoning, and they harm the ship's own crew as much as anyone ashore - which is why head (a) reaches injury on board, something neither oil convention does. If you write HNS as 'the chemical version of CLC' you have missed the whole of limb (b).

The bunkers point - an exclusion that is not there

Many summaries state that the HNS Convention 'does not apply to damage covered by the Bunkers Convention'. That proposition does not appear in the Convention. Article 4.3 contains exactly two exclusions - CLC pollution damage, and class 7 radioactive material - and the word 'bunker' appears nowhere in the instrument. Bunker oil is outside HNS for a more fundamental reason: article 1.5(a) covers only substances carried as cargo, and a ship's own fuel is not cargo. The distinction matters because it tells you what happens at the edges - residues of a previous cargo are expressly brought back in by article 1.5(b), whereas fuel never enters the definition at all.

Limb (c) worked through - the three variables

Set out the axis each convention is drawn around and the answer writes itself:

  • CLC 92 - drawn around a substance (persistent oil) and a mechanism (contamination). Note that it covers the tanker's own bunkers as well as her cargo, because article I.5 says 'whether carried on board a ship as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship'. And she is only a CLC 'ship' while actually carrying oil in bulk as cargo, or on a following voyage with residues aboard.
  • Bunkers 2001 - drawn around a function (oil used for the operation or propulsion of the ship) and the same mechanism (contamination). Article 4.1 stands it down wherever CLC pollution damage applies, so it fills the gap left when the tanker is not a CLC ship.
  • HNS - drawn around cargo and a much wider set of mechanisms. It picks up the two things the other two cannot reach: non-persistent oil products, which are outside CLC's definition of oil altogether, and non-contamination damage such as fire and explosion, which is outside CLC's definition of pollution damage even when the cargo is persistent.

So a products tanker on a rotation of crude, then gasoline, then a chemical parcel, moves between all three regimes without changing anything about herself. That is the answer to 'why all three', and it is worth stating explicitly in the script rather than leaving the examiner to infer it.

Common mistakes

  • Treating HNS as a pollution convention and confining limb (b) to spills and contamination.
  • Listing commodities instead of the seven categories with their parent instruments. The instrument reference is the mark.
  • Saying the Convention excludes bunker oil by an exclusion article. It does not - bunkers are simply not cargo.
  • Forgetting residues under article 1.5(b), which is the most commonly dropped part of limb (a).
  • Omitting that head (a) covers personal injury on board - a real difference from the oil conventions.
  • Answering limb (c) by describing three conventions separately instead of explaining why one ship needs all three.
  • Quoting a number of Contracting States, or an entry-into-force date. Neither was available at this sitting and the question does not ask for either.

Examiner traps

  • The stem's premise - 'is expected shortly to come into force' - is the examiner telling you the state of play. Answer inside it. An answer that corrects the examiner with a later date is answering a different paper.
  • 'List and give examples' in limb (a) means both. Categories without examples, or examples without categories, each lose half the limb.
  • 'Types of pollution and subsequent losses or damages' - the word 'pollution' in the stem is the trap. The Convention's own heading is damage, and it is wider than pollution. Answer what the Convention covers, not what the word in the stem suggests.
  • Limb (c) says 'probably require'. The examiner is inviting the trading-pattern reasoning, not a categorical assertion that every products tanker always carries all three.

Model written answer

1. Definition

Communication is the transfer of information, instructions and meaning from a sender to a receiver through a channel, understood by the receiver as the sender intended. It is complete not when the message is sent but when feedback confirms it was understood. That feedback loop is what distinguishes communication from broadcasting.

2. Types of communication

  • Verbal - face to face, telephone, UHF/VHF, public address: fast, immediate feedback, no record.
  • Written - work orders, permits, risk assessments, checklists, night orders, log books, handovers: slower but auditable, and what the safety management system relies on.
  • Non-verbal - hand signals, body language, and the visual and audible alarm system. In high noise this becomes the primary channel.
  • Formal and informal - the first recorded and following the organisational chart; the second quicker but unverified.
  • Vertical and horizontal - downward orders, upward reports and concerns, lateral between departments. Upward communication fails first, and safety depends on it.
  • Internal and external, and electronic - ship to shore, port, VTS and class; email, planned maintenance systems and alarm data.

3. Barriers to effective communication

  • Physical - engine room noise, distance, hearing protection, radio dead spots, intervening structure.
  • Language and semantic - multinational crews, differing fluency, jargon. Addressed by the working language required under SOLAS V/14.3 and ISM element 6.
  • Hierarchical and cultural - reluctance to question a senior. This is the barrier most often behind an accident everybody could see coming.
  • Physiological - fatigue, hearing loss, stress, time pressure, complacency on a routine job.
  • Organisational - unclear responsibility, two people each believing the other is in charge, poor handover.
  • Assumption - believing a message was understood because nobody asked. Silence is not confirmation.

4. Where communication failure becomes a hazard in this job

The defining feature is vertical separation with a shared machine between the two groups: each can alter the condition of the engine the other is working inside, and neither can see the other. Hence:

  • Turning gear operated with a person in the crankcase or under a piston - the most serious risk in the job.
  • Starting air admitted, or the main starting air valve left un-isolated, with the engine opened up.
  • Dropped objects - tools, nuts or debris falling from the cylinder head platform onto the group below.
  • Uncoordinated lifting of a piston, liner or head through the space while men work beneath the load path.
  • Hydraulic tensioning tools pressurised while another person handles the studs or nuts.
  • Systems restored by a third party - a standby pump starting on auto, or the control room restoring a system, releasing hot oil or water onto open work.
  • Enclosed space entry to crankcase or scavenge space without the other group knowing anyone is inside.
  • Shift handover - the incoming group not knowing what is isolated, what is slack, and where people are.

5. Risk mitigation - organise the job before it starts

  • A risk assessment and permit to work, with a separate enclosed space entry permit for crankcase or scavenge space.
  • One named person in overall charge and one leader per group. Nobody moves the engine except on that person's order.
  • A joint toolbox talk before starting: sequence, who is where, isolations, communication plan and the stop-work rule.
  • Lock-out and tag-out of turning gear supply, starting air, hydraulic power pack and standby pumps, tagged at the control room and at every local starter, with the key held by the person in charge.

6. Risk mitigation - the communication plan itself

  • A dedicated radio channel for the job, with headsets compatible with hearing protection, tested before work begins.
  • Closed-loop communication as the standing rule - instruction given, repeated back, execution confirmed. Turning the engine is announced, acknowledged by both groups, and only then carried out.
  • Agreed standard phrases and a hand-signal set for when radios fail or noise defeats them.
  • Confirm understanding by asking a person to state back what they are about to do - never by asking "do you understand?"
  • An explicit stop-work authority for everyone, stated at the toolbox talk so that using it is expected rather than brave.

7. Risk mitigation - physical and record controls

  • Avoid work directly above and below where the sequence allows; otherwise an exclusion zone, gratings or covers, and tethered tools.
  • A written handover at each shift change and a job log of isolations, work done and personnel inside the engine.
  • A personnel tally for crankcase and scavenge space entry, checked before any isolation is removed.
  • Formal permit close-out, confirming all persons and tools clear before turning gear is disengaged and the engine restored.

8. Conclusion

Here the machine is the message path: what one group does is felt immediately by the other. The controls that matter are specific, not exhortations - one person in charge, isolation that a misunderstanding cannot undo, closed-loop confirmation before the engine moves, and a written handover. Communication failure in this job does not cause inconvenience; it causes fatalities.

Why this structure scores

This is four questions in one, and the marks follow the work. The first three limbs are standard theory that every candidate can write, so they do not discriminate - keep them tight and complete. The scenario limb is where the marks are actually won, because it requires you to apply the theory to a specific engineering operation. Give roughly half the answer to limbs 4 to 7. A candidate who writes three pages on types and barriers and then two generic lines about 'good communication' has answered the easy part of the question and missed the point of it.

The word decarbonisation means the overhaul, not GHG

Read the scenario carefully. In this question "Main engine decarbonisation" means the traditional overhaul job - removing carbon deposits, pistons, liners, exhaust valves and heads - with men on the cylinder head platform above and on the bottom platform below. It does not mean greenhouse gas decarbonisation, even though Q2 of this same paper is about CII and GHG reduction. Writing about carbon intensity here would lose the entire second half of the question. The tell is the physical description of two working platforms.

The organising idea for the scenario limb

One sentence carries the whole limb: two groups, vertically separated, working on one machine that either of them can move or pressurise. Every hazard is a consequence - what falls from above, what moves in between, and what a third party can restore from the control room. If you state that principle first and then derive the hazards, the list writes itself and reads as analysis rather than recall.

Where the authority for this answer comes from - and where it honestly does not

Worth understanding, because it is the difference between a confident answer and an invented one. Some limbs here are governed and some are not. The working-language requirement is real and citable - SOLAS regulation V/14.3, supported by ISM Code element 6. Procedures for shipboard operations, which is where permits and isolations live, sit in ISM element 7, and the alarm and emergency response in element 8. Leadership and teamworking are STCW management-level competences.

But no convention prescribes a taxonomy of communication types, and none lists the hazards of a two-platform decarbonisation. Those come from established practice, the Code of Safe Working Practices - which is industry guidance, not a convention - and ordinary engineering risk assessment. Say so rather than inventing a regulation number. An answer that declares its basis honestly reads as competent; one that attaches a fabricated regulation to a good engineering point destroys the credibility of everything around it.

Common mistakes

  • Misreading 'decarbonisation' as GHG reduction and losing the scenario limb entirely.
  • Listing barriers without saying which ones bite in an engine room. Noise, hearing protection and radio dead spots are the specific ones; hierarchy is the dangerous one.
  • Giving mitigations that are slogans - 'improve communication', 'hold meetings'. Marks come from named controls: permit to work, lock-out/tag-out, closed-loop confirmation, exclusion zone, written handover.
  • Forgetting the third party. Many real accidents come from the engine control room or another watch restoring a system, not from either working group.
  • Defining communication without feedback. A definition that stops at 'transfer of information' misses the element every subsequent barrier attacks.
  • Attaching an invented regulation number to the communication theory. The theory limbs are not prescribed by any instrument, and saying so is stronger than pretending otherwise.

Model written answer

1. The background - the gap the Convention was written to close

By the 1990s the international liability architecture had a conspicuous hole in it. CLC 92 answered for oil pollution, but only from a ship constructed or adapted for the carriage of oil in bulk as cargo (article I.1) and only for persistent oil (article I.5). Every other ship - bulk carriers, container ships, car carriers, cruise ships - fell outside it entirely, and yet a large container ship or bulk carrier routinely bunkers several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel oil, more than the entire cargo of a small product tanker. A bunker spill from such a ship left claimants to whatever national law and ordinary tort principles offered: no strict liability, no compulsory insurance, no certificate, and often no solvent defendant. The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage was adopted on 23 March 2001 to close that gap and entered into force on 21 November 2008.

2. Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture

Its relevance is that it completes a three-part scheme in which each instrument is drawn around a different variable and the three are mutually exclusive: CLC 92 for persistent oil carried as cargo by a tanker, and that tanker's own bunkers; Bunkers 2001 for the fuel of every other ship; and the HNS Convention for hazardous and noxious cargoes. It deliberately borrows CLC's architecture - strict liability, limited liability, compulsory insurance, a certificate and direct action against the insurer - so that the same commercial machinery, and the same P&I 'blue card' practice, could be extended without a new institution. It has no cargo-financed second tier: there is no bunker equivalent of the IOPC Fund.

3. a) Pollution damage and exclusions

The two definitions are deliberately parallel. Under Bunkers article 1.9, and CLC article I.6, 'pollution damage' means loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination resulting from the escape or discharge of oil, with compensation for impairment of the environment other than loss of profit confined to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken, together with the costs of preventive measures and further loss or damage caused by them. Both are therefore contamination regimes, and neither reaches fire or explosion damage.

The differences are in the substance and the boundary:

  • What oil. CLC covers only persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil - crude, fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil - whether as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship. Bunkers covers any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and its residues, so it is not confined to persistent oil and picks up a marine gas oil spill that CLC would not.
  • The principal exclusion. By article 4.1 the Bunkers Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is actually payable under it. The two can never overlap, and the exclusion turns on the CLC definition, not on the outcome of a claim - so a claimant who fails under CLC cannot fall back on Bunkers.
  • Other exclusions. Warships, naval auxiliaries and State non-commercial ships (Bunkers article 4.2; CLC article XI), unless the State opts in.
  • Geographic scope. Both apply in the territory and territorial sea and in the exclusive economic zone of a State Party (Bunkers article 2), with preventive measures covered wherever taken.

4. b) Time limits for claims

Here the two are identical, and the symmetry is worth stating expressly. Under Bunkers article 8 and CLC article VIII, rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within three years from the date when the damage occurred; and in no case may an action be brought more than six years from the date of the incident which caused the damage. Where the incident consists of a series of occurrences, the six-year period runs from the first of them.

Two points earn the marks. First, these are extinction periods, not procedural limitation - the right itself ceases, so it cannot be revived by agreement or waiver in the way a domestic limitation defence sometimes can. Secondly, the three years run from when the damage occurred, not from when the claimant knew of it - which is stricter than the HNS Convention, where article 37 runs three years from knowledge and allows ten years absolute.

5. c) Liability of the shipowner

Both impose strict liability on the shipowner at the time of the incident - Bunkers article 3.1, CLC article III.1 - subject to the same three narrow defences, which the owner must prove: an act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; damage wholly caused by a third party's intentional act or omission; or damage wholly caused by the negligence or wrongful act of an authority responsible for the maintenance of navigational aids. In both, contributory intent or negligence of the claimant may exonerate the owner wholly or partly.

But who is liable is the single greatest difference between the two conventions, and it runs in opposite directions:

  • CLC channels liability inward. Liability attaches to the registered owner alone, and article III.4 expressly bars any claim against the crew, pilot, salvor, and against any charterer (howsoever described, including a bareboat charterer), manager or operator - unless they acted with intent or recklessly with knowledge. The claimant has one certain defendant and no more.
  • Bunkers spreads liability outward. Article 1.3 defines 'shipowner' to mean the owner including the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator, and article 3.2 makes their liability joint and several. The claimant may pursue any or all of them.

The practical consequence is real: under CLC the manager and the bareboat charterer are protected, and under the Bunkers Convention the very same parties are exposed. Where an incident involves two or more ships, the shipowners of all of them are jointly and severally liable for damage that is not reasonably separable (Bunkers article 5; CLC article IV).

6. c) Limitation of liability - the structural difference

  • CLC carries its own limits. By article V.1 the owner may limit to 4,510,000 units of account for a ship not exceeding 5,000 units of tonnage, plus 631 units of account for each additional ton, subject to an overall ceiling of 89,770,000 units of account. The fund is constituted with the court under article V.3, and once constituted the owner's other assets are protected and any arrested ship must be released (article VI). The right to limit is lost under article V.2 where the damage resulted from the owner's personal act or omission committed with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that such damage would probably result.
  • The Bunkers Convention carries no limits at all. Article 6 merely preserves the right to limit 'under any applicable national or international regime, such as the Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976, as amended'. There is no bunker-specific figure and no bunker-specific fund.

This is not a drafting convenience but the substantive weakness of the regime, and it is the point a management-level answer should make. Because limitation is left to the LLMC, a bunker-pollution claim competes in a general limitation fund shared with all other claims arising from the same incident - wreck removal, cargo, personal injury - rather than drawing on a dedicated pollution fund as it would under CLC. The available compensation is correspondingly less certain, it varies between States according to which limitation regime each has adopted, and there is no second tier to make up any shortfall.

7. d) Compulsory insurance and financial security

  • Who must insure, and the threshold. Under Bunkers article 7.1 the registered owner of a ship of more than 1,000 gross tonnage registered in a State Party must maintain insurance or other financial security covering the registered owner's liability, in an amount equal to the limits of the applicable limitation regime but not exceeding the LLMC figure. Under CLC article VII.1 the obligation arises instead from the cargo - an owner whose ship carries more than 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo must insure in the sums fixed by article V.1. So the two thresholds are measured in different units: ship size against cargo quantity.
  • A deliberate asymmetry. Liability under Bunkers article 3 attaches to the wide 'shipowner' group, but the insurance duty falls on the registered owner only. The bareboat charterer and manager may be sued and are not required to insure.
  • The certificate. In both regimes a certificate in the annexed model form is issued or certified by the flag State, naming the ship, the owner, the IMO ship identification number, the type and duration of security and the insurer. It must be carried on board (Bunkers article 7.5), and security may not lapse on less than three months' notice (article 7.6). A State Party may delegate issue to a recognised organisation (article 7.3). In practice the P&I club issues a 'blue card' evidencing cover, on which the State issues the certificate.
  • Direct action. Under Bunkers article 7.10 and CLC article VII.8 a claim may be brought directly against the insurer. The insurer may invoke the shipowner's defences other than bankruptcy, may plead the shipowner's wilful misconduct, and - importantly - may limit to the insured amount even if the shipowner has lost the right to limit.
  • Enforcement reach. A flag State may not permit a ship to operate without a certificate (article 7.11), and by article 7.12 every State Party must ensure that security is in force for any ship over 1,000 gt wherever registered entering or leaving its ports. That is why the certificate is carried by ships of non-party flags as a practical matter, and it is what gives the Convention near-universal effect at the port-State level.

CLC attaches to a cargo; the Bunkers Convention attaches to a ship's own fuel. Everything else follows. A cargo can be measured, so CLC's insurance threshold is 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo; fuel cannot usefully be, so Bunkers uses 1,000 gross tonnage. A tanker owner is a single identifiable defendant with predictable insurance, so CLC can channel liability to him and protect the charterer and manager; the operator of a general cargo ship may sit behind layers of management companies, so Bunkers casts the net wider. A tanker casualty is overwhelmingly a pollution event, so CLC can justify a dedicated limitation fund and a second tier; a bunker spill usually accompanies a grounding or collision generating many other claims, so Bunkers leaves limitation to the general LLMC regime.

The limitation point, which is where most answers go wrong

It is worth being precise, because this is the most commonly misstated proposition in the topic. The Bunkers Convention contains no limitation figures whatsoever. Article 6 reads that nothing in the Convention shall affect the right to limit 'under any applicable national or international regime, such as the Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976, as amended'. That is the entire limitation provision.

Three consequences follow, and stating them is what turns a recall answer into an analytical one. First, the amount available varies between States according to which limitation regime each has adopted, so compensation is not uniform in the way CLC compensation is. Secondly, the pollution claim is not ring-fenced: it shares a general limitation fund with wreck removal, cargo and personal injury claims arising from the same casualty, and may be substantially diluted. Thirdly, there is no second tier - no bunker equivalent of the IOPC Fund - so once the LLMC fund is exhausted there is nothing behind it. A major bunker spill from a very large non-tanker can therefore leave claimants materially worse off than the same spill from a laden tanker.

This answer does not quote an LLMC figure. That is deliberate: article 6 refers out rather than fixing a number, so a single figure would misdescribe the mechanism, and the comparison turns on the referral itself rather than on any particular amount.

Channelling - and the trap of getting it backwards

Under CLC article III.4 no claim may be made against the crew, the pilot, a salvor, or 'any charterer (howsoever described, including a bareboat charterer), manager or operator of the ship' unless they acted with intent or recklessly with knowledge. Liability is channelled to the registered owner. Under Bunkers article 1.3 the word 'shipowner' is defined to mean the owner 'including the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator', and article 3.2 makes them jointly and severally liable.

So the identical parties - bareboat charterer, manager, operator - are protected by one convention and exposed by the other. Candidates who have learned CLC well are the ones most likely to get this wrong, because they carry the channelling rule across. If you remember only one contrast from this question, make it this one.

What neither convention covers

Both are confined to damage by contamination. Neither reaches fire or explosion damage, and neither reaches damage from anything that is not oil. That is precisely the space the HNS Convention was written to occupy, and a sentence placing all three instruments together is a good way to close the answer - it shows the examiner you understand the architecture rather than two isolated conventions.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Bunkers Convention has its own limitation figures. It has none - article 6 refers out to the LLMC.
  • Applying CLC's channelling rule to the Bunkers Convention. It is the reverse: Bunkers spreads liability across owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator.
  • Saying bunker oil must be persistent. CLC's 'oil' is persistent; 'bunker oil' is any oil used or intended for the operation or propulsion of the ship.
  • Treating either convention as covering fire or explosion. Both are contamination regimes.
  • Giving different time limits for the two conventions. They are identical - three years and six years.
  • Describing the two conventions in turn instead of comparing them. The stem says 'comparing with'.
  • Saying there is a bunker equivalent of the IOPC Fund. There is no second tier.

Model written answer

1. a) The human element, and how IMO and ILO divide the work

The human element is not one topic but the recognition that the great majority of casualties originate in human performance rather than in equipment failure. IMO frames it through resolution A.947(23), Human Element Vision, Principles and Goals, adopted 27 November 2003, which treats it as a complex, multi-dimensional issue spanning ships' crews, shore management, regulators, recognized organizations, shipyards and legislators, all of whom must co-operate.

The division of labour between the two organisations should be stated at the outset, because it explains why two separate regimes exist. IMO regulates the seafarer as the operator of a ship - competence, watchkeeping, fitness for duty and safe operation. ILO regulates the seafarer as a worker - employment, wages, hours, accommodation, welfare and social protection. Neither is complete on its own, and the two interlock precisely where working conditions become safety conditions: hours of rest appear in both, because a seafarer's terms of employment and the safety of the ship are the same question asked twice.

2. The IMO regime - competence, safe operation and fitness for duty

  • STCW 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force 1 January 2012, is the competence instrument. Its Part A tables specify for each function and level the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods of demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard. Part A is mandatory, Part B is guidance. Manila added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking and, at management level, leadership and managerial skill, which is where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention.
  • STCW also regulates the physical human being: regulation I/9 medical fitness standards, regulation VIII/1 requiring Administrations, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and regulation VIII/2 governing watchkeeping arrangements. Manila also introduced alcohol limits.
  • Regulation I/14 places the duty on the company - valid certificates, familiarisation with the ship and her arrangements, and maintained documentation - so the human element is not left as the individual's problem. Regulations I/6, I/8 and I/11 govern training and assessment, quality standards systems and revalidation.
  • The ISM Code supplies the organisational half: element 6 resources and personnel, including qualification, familiarisation and a working language; element 8 emergency preparedness, drills and exercises; and element 9 reports and analysis of non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences - the reporting culture without which human-element failures are never learned from.
  • Manning is the point at which the two regimes meet most directly. SOLAS regulation V/14 requires ships to be sufficiently and efficiently manned and the flag State to issue a minimum safe manning document, the principles being set out in IMO resolution A.1047(27), Principles of Minimum Safe Manning, adopted 30 November 2011. MLC Regulation 2.7 imposes the same requirement from the labour side.

3. The ILO regime - MLC 2006, the fourth pillar

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 was adopted at the 94th (Maritime) Session of the International Labour Conference and consolidated some sixty-eight earlier ILO maritime instruments into one. It is universally described as the fourth pillar of the international regulatory regime, standing beside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW, and is often called the seafarers' bill of rights.

Its architecture is unusual and is worth a sentence. It has three parts: the Articles, setting out general obligations; the Regulations; and the Code. Article VI divides the Code in two - Part A contains Standards and is mandatory, while Part B contains Guidelines, not mandatory but to which Members must give due consideration. Article VI.3 adds substantial equivalence, allowing a Member to implement a Part A Standard differently provided the provision is substantially equivalent in achieving its general object and purpose. Article IV states the underlying rights: a safe and secure workplace, fair terms of employment, decent working and living conditions, and health protection, medical care, welfare measures and other forms of social protection.

The Regulations and Code are arranged in five Titles: 1 minimum requirements to work on a ship; 2 conditions of employment; 3 accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering; 4 health protection, medical care, welfare and social security protection; and 5 compliance and enforcement. The text operative at this sitting is the Convention as amended by the 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2022 amendments, the 2022 set having entered into force on 23 December 2024 - nine months before this examination, and therefore simply the law.

4. What the maritime labour and welfare regime actually requires

  • Title 1 - who may go to sea. Standard A1.1.1 prohibits the employment of any person under 16; night work and hazardous work by those under 18 are prohibited. A valid medical certificate is required, valid for a maximum of two years, or one year under 18. Seafarers must be trained and qualified, and recruitment and placement services regulated - no fees are borne by the seafarer.
  • Title 2 - conditions of employment. The seafarers' employment agreement under Standard A2.1.1 is signed by both parties, the seafarer having an opportunity to examine it and seek advice before signing; a copy is carried on board for the seafarer and for inspectors. Hours of work and rest under Standard A2.3.5 require each Member to fix either maximum hours of work - 14 in any 24 and 72 in any seven days - or minimum hours of rest - 10 in any 24 and 77 in any seven days. Wages are paid at intervals no greater than monthly with a means of transmitting allotments home; repatriation arises on expiry abroad, on termination for justified reasons and on inability to perform duties, with a maximum service period on board of less than twelve months, backed by financial security for abandonment evidenced by a certificate carried on board.
  • Title 3 - accommodation and catering. Safe and decent accommodation, with the construction standards applying to ships built on or after entry into force for that Member. Standard A3.1 paragraph 17 requires recreational facilities, amenities and services including social connectivity, the associated guidance addressing internet access at reasonable charges - connectivity is a welfare requirement of the Convention, not a commercial courtesy. Food and drinking water of appropriate quality, nutritional value and quantity are supplied free of charge.
  • Title 4 - health, welfare and social protection. Medical care comparable to that available ashore and provided free of charge, including access to a doctor or dentist in ports of call where practicable. Shipowners' liability for sickness and injury in service, with financial security for contractual claims arising from death or long-term disability. On health and safety, Standard A4.3 paragraph 1(b) requires the provision of all necessary appropriately-sized personal protective equipment; Standard A4.3 paragraph 2 with Guideline B4.3.1 paragraph 3 requires that engineering and design control and the substitution of processes - replacing the dangerous by the less dangerous - take precedence over personal protective equipment, which is the hierarchy of controls made express in the Convention. Standard A4.3 paragraph 5(a) requires all deaths of seafarers to be investigated, recorded and reported annually to the Director-General of the International Labour Office for publication in a global register. Shore-based welfare facilities and social security complete the Title.

5. Title 5 - what makes the labour regime enforceable

The flag State certifies. The maritime labour certificate is issued by the competent authority or by a recognized organization duly authorized, for a period not exceeding five years, subject to an intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates. It is accompanied by the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance: Part I, drawn by the competent authority, listing the national requirements; and Part II, drawn by the shipowner, setting out the measures adopted to ensure ongoing compliance between inspections. Appendix A5-I lists the sixteen matters that must be inspected and approved before certification.

In a foreign port, an authorized officer may inspect under Regulation 5.2.1. The certificate and DMLC are accepted as prima facie evidence of compliance and the inspection normally stops there; Standard A5.2.1.1 gives four triggers for a more detailed inspection - documents not produced, falsely maintained or invalid; clear grounds for believing conditions do not conform; reasonable grounds to believe the ship has changed flag to avoid compliance; or a complaint. Where conditions are clearly hazardous or the breach serious or repeated, the ship may be detained. Article V.7 supplies the commercial engine: ships of non-ratifying States must receive no more favourable treatment. Regulation 5.2.2 requires onshore complaint-handling procedures and Regulation 5.3 places duties on labour-supplying States such as India.

6. Where the two organisations act jointly, and the state of the regime at this sitting

  • The two organisations act together through joint ILO/IMO tripartite bodies, which produced the Guidelines on fair treatment of seafarers detained in connection with alleged crimes and the Guidelines on fair treatment of seafarers in the event of a maritime accident - the response to the criminalisation of seafarers - and the joint database on abandonment of seafarers, which sits alongside the MLC's financial-security requirement.
  • ILO Convention No. 185, Seafarers' Identity Documents, 2003, supports shore leave and transit, and is the labour-side counterpart to the FAL Convention's facilitation provisions.
  • The state of the MLC at this sitting must be stated in two parts. The 2022 amendments are in force, from 23 December 2024, and are integrated above. A further set of amendments was adopted on 6 June 2025 at the 113th Session of the International Labour Conference - three months before this examination - and was NOT in force, entry into force being expected in December 2027 under the Convention's tacit-acceptance mechanism. They comprise seven themed items touching Regulations 1.4, 2.4, 2.5, 3.1, 4.1, 4.3, 4.4 and 5.1: repatriation without discrimination; a new Standard A2.4.2 on shore leave; recognition of seafarers as key workers; fair treatment and marine casualties; the costs of repatriation the shipowner must bear; medical training; and violence and harassment, including sexual harassment, bullying and sexual assault. Naming them as adopted and awaiting entry into force is the correct answer at this sitting - they were neither absent from the regime nor yet part of the operative law.

7. B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The guidance is MSC.1/Circ.1598, 'Guidelines on fatigue', of 24 January 2019, approved at the 100th session of the Maritime Safety Committee and finalized by the HTW Sub-Committee. It supersedes MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, not mandatory - the binding rest-hour requirements remain in STCW chapter VIII and in MLC 2006. The Guidelines are arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party:

  • Module 1 - Fatigue: what it is and its causes, grouped as sleep and rest, the biological clock and circadian rhythm, health and stress, environmental factors such as noise, vibration and motion, sleep disorders and operational factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods.
  • Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company: assessing, mitigating and managing fatigue risk within the safety management system, in manning decisions and in scheduling.
  • Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer: practical guidance for the master, officers and ratings on sleep quality, diet and the limits of countermeasures - notably that caffeine is not a substitute for sleep.
  • Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training.
  • Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design: mitigation designed in - accommodation location, noise, vibration, lighting and habitability.
  • Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities: how regulators and inspectors should approach it.

8. How the guidance and the mandatory rules fit together

The Guidelines are explicit that hours of rest are set by a prescriptive formula. Under the Manila-amended section A-VIII/1 the minimum is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period; the daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive rest periods shall not exceed 14 hours. Authorised exceptions may not take weekly rest below 70 hours, may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and permit division into no more than three periods. MLC Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7 run the same way from the labour side.

The management-level point, and the one that separates a Class I answer from a recitation of numbers, is that complying with the formula is not the same as being rested. The Guidelines require managers to allow for circadian rhythm and accumulated sleep debt, and are explicit that rest means rest, not the substitution of a different form of work. A management-level engineer should therefore treat recorded compliance as the floor, and a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant as a warning rather than a reassurance - because the record is generated by the very person whose hours it records, under commercial pressure, on a ship manned to the minimum.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (a) as an STCW question and never reaching MLC 2006, when the stem names maritime labour and welfare.

Examiner traps

  • Is connectivity a legal requirement? Yes. Standard A3.1 paragraph 17 requires recreational facilities including social connectivity, with guidance on internet access at reasonable charges. It is not a commercial courtesy.
  • Does the MLC require PPE, or something more? More. Standard A4.3 paragraph 1(b) requires appropriately-sized PPE, and Guideline B4.3.1 paragraph 3 with Standard A4.3 paragraph 2 requires that control and substitution take precedence over PPE - the hierarchy of controls, express in the Convention.
  • Who draws each part of the DMLC? Part I the competent authority, listing national requirements; Part II the shipowner, setting out the measures for ongoing compliance. Reversing them is a giveaway.
  • Why does a non-ratifying flag not escape? Article V.7 - no more favourable treatment.
  • Is MSC.1/Circ.1598 mandatory? No. Its force comes from the mandatory instruments it sits beside, and from the ISM Code, under which a company that ignores known fatigue risk has a defective safety management system.

Model written answer

1. What a classification society is, and its two distinct roles

A classification society is a technical body that publishes its own rules for the design, construction and survey of ships, assigns class to a ship that meets them, and maintains that class by periodical survey. Its origin is commercial: underwriters needed an independent judgement of the ship they were asked to insure. Everything else follows from that.

The distinction that must be made at the outset is that a society wears two hats:

  • Classification - a private, contractual relationship with the owner, under the society's own rules. Class is not a government function, and the society does not act for the flag State when it assigns it.
  • Statutory work as a Recognized Organization - work delegated by the flag State under SOLAS regulation XI-1/1, in which the society surveys and certifies against the conventions on the Administration's behalf and subject to the Code for Recognized Organizations, resolutions MSC.349(92) and MEPC.237(65), mandatory since 1 January 2015. Here the society acts for the flag State, which retains responsibility.

2. How class rules are made

Rules are not written from theory alone. They are built from service experience - damage and defect returns from the surveyed fleet; from casualty investigation; from research and direct calculation, including finite element analysis and fatigue assessment; and from technical committees drawing on owners, builders, makers and the society's own surveyors. Because a ship trades worldwide and changes flag and society, divergence between societies is itself a hazard, so the members harmonise through IACS: Unified Requirements setting minimum standards each member must implement, Unified Interpretations of convention text, Procedural Requirements, and jointly developed rules - most importantly the Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers.

3. What the structural, mechanical and electrical rules cover

  • Structural - scantlings of plating, stiffeners and primary members; longitudinal and local strength; fatigue life; corrosion additions and coating requirements; loading conditions and hull girder limits; and the enhanced close-up survey requirements for bulk carriers and tankers.
  • Mechanical - main propulsion and essential auxiliary machinery; shafting, propellers and gearing; boilers and pressure vessels; piping systems; steering gear; and the type approval of components and materials.
  • Electrical - generation, distribution and protection, including discrimination and short-circuit capability; essential and emergency services and their sources; cable installation; high-voltage systems; and equipment for hazardous areas.

4. Implementation through the ship's life

  • Design - plan approval of structure, machinery and electrical installations against the rules, review of direct calculations, and type approval of equipment so that approved components need not be re-justified on each ship.
  • Construction - approval of materials and their manufacturers; certification of components; qualification of welding procedures and welders; witnessing of non-destructive testing and tank testing; survey during construction at the yard; and attendance at trials before the certificate is issued.
  • Maintenance in service - the periodical survey cycle that keeps class alive: annual, intermediate and renewal or special surveys, docking and in-water surveys, tailshaft and boiler surveys. Alternatives exist where they can be justified - continuous survey of hull or machinery spreading the items across the cycle, and an approved planned maintenance scheme allowing certain machinery items to be credited on the Chief Engineer's records. Where a defect is found, the society may impose a condition of class or recommendation with a date for rectification, and class may be suspended or withdrawn if it is not met.

5. Why compliance with class rules is now mentioned in SOLAS chapter II-1

The reference is SOLAS regulation II-1/3-1, "Structural, mechanical and electrical requirements for ships". It provides that ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society which is recognized by the Administration in accordance with the provisions of regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration which provide an equivalent level of safety.

The reason it is there is the gap it closes. SOLAS regulates subdivision and damage stability, machinery and electrical installations, fire protection and life-saving. But SOLAS never contained scantling rules - it does not state how thick a plate must be or what section modulus a hull girder must have. That had always been left to the classification societies as a matter of private contract between owner, society and underwriter. The consequence was that, as a matter of convention law, there was no requirement that a ship be built to any structural standard at all: class was commercially indispensable and legally optional.

Regulation II-1/3-1 converts that commercial arrangement into a statutory obligation, and four things follow:

  • compliance with a recognized society's rules becomes a SOLAS requirement, enforceable by the flag State and inspectable by port State control;
  • the obligation runs to maintenance, not only to design and construction - so loss or suspension of class becomes a SOLAS non-compliance, not merely a commercial and insurance problem;
  • the society whose rules are relied on must be recognized under XI-1/1 and must operate under the RO Code, so the standard is not simply whatever any body chooses to publish; and
  • an Administration may still use its own national standards, but only where they provide an equivalent level of safety - preserving flag State sovereignty without opening a loophole.

This also completes the picture begun by goal-based standards: for bulk carriers and oil tankers, the class rules relied on under II-1/3-1 must themselves have been verified at IMO as conforming to the goals and functional requirements under SOLAS II-1/3-10. SOLAS therefore requires compliance with class rules, and separately requires those rules to have been shown to be good enough.

(The printed question refers to "SOLAS ch.ll-1", using two lowercase letter L's. The reference intended is chapter II-1, and within it regulation 3-1.)

6. The harmonized system, and where these surveys sit

Before harmonization, each certificate carried its own survey dates, so a ship could be under survey almost continuously and a certificate could expire while the ship was at sea. The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification, introduced by the 1988 SOLAS and Load Lines Protocols and extended to MARPOL, aligns the certificates on a common five-year cycle with common anniversary dates and a common window of three months either side, so that surveys can be taken together. The guidance in force is the Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2023, resolution A.1186(33), adopted 6 December 2023, which revokes A.1156(32). The system uses initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal and additional surveys, plus inspection of the outside of the ship's bottom.

The heart of limb (a): what gap does II-1/3-1 close?

Most candidates will state that SOLAS II-1/3-1 requires compliance with class rules. Very few will say why that regulation had to exist, and that is the whole question. SOLAS has never contained scantling rules - it does not say how thick a plate must be. Structural standards were always a matter of private contract between owner, society and underwriter. So before II-1/3-1, a ship could in principle satisfy SOLAS while being built to no structural standard whatever, because class was commercially indispensable but legally optional. The regulation makes it legally compulsory, and extends the duty to maintenance - which is what turns loss of class from a commercial problem into a convention breach that port State control can act on.

The two-hats distinction is the other half of limb (a)

Classification is private and contractual; statutory certification as a Recognized Organization is delegated by the flag State under SOLAS XI-1/1 and governed by the RO Code. The same surveyor may do both on the same day, which is exactly why the distinction is worth stating. Note the resolution numbers carefully: the RO Code is MSC.349(92) with MEPC.237(65), mandatory from 1 January 2015. Getting the session number wrong here is an easy and visible slip.

Limb (b) has one fact almost nobody knows

The periodical survey is not simply "the survey that happens periodically". It attaches to only two certificates - Safety Equipment and Safety Radio - and it has a different frequency for each. For the Safety Equipment Certificate it falls at the second or third anniversary and replaces one of the annual surveys. For the Safety Radio Certificate it falls at every anniversary. A candidate who states both frequencies and notes that the radio survey is annual because distress communication cannot be allowed to degrade unnoticed has said something precise where most answers say something vague.

Why harmonization was needed at all

The point of HSSC is not the survey types; it is the alignment. Before it, certificates carried independent dates, so a ship might be under survey almost continuously and could still find a certificate expiring at sea. HSSC puts everything on a common five-year cycle with common anniversary dates and a common three-months-either-side window, so surveys can be combined into one attendance. If limb (b) explains the alignment rather than merely listing survey names, it has answered "relevance" as well as "difference".

Which edition of the Survey Guidelines - and why the answer here is NOT the one you will find today

The Survey Guidelines under the HSSC are re-adopted by the IMO Assembly roughly every two years, and each edition revokes the last. At the date of this examination the operative edition was resolution A.1186(33), the 2023 Guidelines, adopted 6 December 2023 and revoking A.1156(32).

Be careful with anything you read about this now. The 34th Assembly session adopted A.1207(34), the 2025 Guidelines, on 3 December 2025 - three months after this paper was sat. Current material will tell you that A.1186(33) has been revoked and that citing it is a mistake. For this sitting that is exactly backwards. The substance is unaffected either way: the annual and periodical survey definitions, frequencies and certificate attachments are the same in both editions. Only the resolution number and date differ - which is what makes this the most dangerous kind of error, because an answer citing the wrong edition reads as entirely correct.

Common mistakes

  • Treating classification and statutory certification as the same activity. One is private contract; the other is delegated flag State authority.
  • Saying SOLAS contains the structural rules. It does not - that is precisely why II-1/3-1 refers out to class rules.
  • Citing the RO Code as MSC.349(90). It is MSC.349(92), with MEPC.237(65).
  • Saying periodical surveys apply to all certificates. They apply only to the Cargo Ship Safety Equipment and Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificates.
  • Giving one frequency for the periodical survey. There are two, depending on the certificate.
  • Confusing the periodical survey with the intermediate survey. Both may fall at the second or third anniversary, but the intermediate attaches to different certificates and has a different scope.
  • Citing A.1207(34) as the Survey Guidelines for this sitting. That resolution was adopted on 3 December 2025, after this examination. The edition in force here is A.1186(33).
  • Forgetting that II-1/3-1 permits national standards of equivalent safety as an alternative to class rules.

Examiner traps

"Your ship loses class mid-voyage. Is that a SOLAS matter?" Yes. II-1/3-1 requires the ship to be maintained in compliance with the rules of a recognized society, so loss of class is a convention non-compliance and not merely a commercial and insurance issue.

"Must a ship be classed?" Strictly, it must comply with the requirements of a recognized society or with national standards of the Administration providing an equivalent level of safety. In practice class is universal, but the regulation offers the alternative and saying so shows the text has been read.

"When is your periodical survey due?" Ask which certificate. Safety Equipment: second or third anniversary, in place of an annual. Safety Radio: every anniversary.

"Who is responsible if the recognized organization gets it wrong?" The Administration. Recognition delegates the work, not the responsibility - which is the point of the RO Code.

16 marks		 The Convention, and its status at this sitting → a) The substances covered - the seven categories of article 1.5 → a) Bulk HNS and packaged HNS - why the split matters → b) The heads of damage - article 1.6 → b) What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it → c) Why an oil products tanker needs certificates under all three conventions → c) The certificate itself, and the two tiers behind it 2010 HNS Convention article 1.5 (what is HNS), article 1.6 (what is damage), article 4.3 (what is excluded), article 12 (compulsory insurance certificate) - read against 1992 CLC articles I.5 and I.6 and 2001 Bunkers Convention articles 1.9 and 4.1 for the boundary in limb (c).
Article 1.5(a) - seven categories, (i) to (vii); article 1.5(b) adds residues		 Answering as though HNS were another pollution convention. It is not. Article 1.6 heads (a) and (b) carry no reference to contamination, so fire, explosion, toxicity and reactivity - the ways chemical and gas cargoes actually kill and destroy - are squarely covered, and limb (b) asks for exactly that breadth. The second trap is stating that HNS excludes bunker oil by an exclusion: it does not, and the word 'bunker' appears nowhere in the Convention. Bunkers fall outside because article 1.5(a) covers only substances carried as cargo. The third trap is temporal and the examiner has already set it in the stem - the Convention was not in force at this sitting and the stem says it was 'expected shortly to come into force'. Write the answer in that posture. Do not import a later entry-into-force date, a later ratification, or a count of Contracting States.
Q6 Communication, Barriers, and Decarbonisation Work Hazards

Two groups, two platforms, one machine - the organising fact of the scenario		 Reading "decarbonisation" as GHG reduction rather than the main engine overhaul. The scenario's two working platforms are the tell, and misreading it loses the whole second half of the question - which is the half carrying the marks. The second trap is answering the mitigation limb with slogans rather than named controls; the third is attaching an invented regulation number to the communication theory, which no instrument prescribes.
Q7 Bunker Convention 2001 Compared with CLC 92
16 marks		 The background - the gap the Convention was written to close → Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture → a) Pollution damage and exclusions → b) Time limits for claims → c) Liability of the shipowner → c) Limitation of liability - the structural difference → d) Compulsory insurance and financial security Bunkers Convention 2001 articles 1.3, 1.5, 1.9, 3, 4.1, 6, 7 and 8, read against CLC 1992 articles I.5, I.6, III, V, VII and VIII. Note especially Bunkers article 6, which sets no limitation figure and refers out to the LLMC 1976 as amended.
Bunkers Convention adopted 23 March 2001, entered into force 21 November 2008		 Writing that the Bunkers Convention has its own limits of liability. It does not. Article 6 sets no figure at all and merely preserves the right to limit under any applicable regime, such as the LLMC 1976 as amended. That single fact is the substantive weakness of the whole regime - the claim competes in a general limitation fund shared with wreck removal, cargo and injury claims instead of a dedicated pollution fund, and there is no second tier to top it up. The second trap is getting the liable party backwards: CLC channels liability inward to the registered owner and expressly protects the charterer, manager and operator, whereas Bunkers spreads it outward to all of them jointly and severally. Candidates routinely state the CLC channelling rule and then apply it to the Bunkers Convention, which reverses the answer. The third trap is treating either convention as covering fire or explosion - both are confined to damage by contamination.
Q8 IMO and ILO Human Element Regimes, and Fatigue Guidance
16 marks		 a) The human element, and how IMO and ILO divide the work → The IMO regime - competence, safe operation and fitness for duty → The ILO regime - MLC 2006, the fourth pillar → What the maritime labour and welfare regime actually requires → Title 5 - what makes the labour regime enforceable → Where the two organisations act jointly, and the state of the regime at this sitting → B) The IMO guidance on fatigue → How the guidance and the mandatory rules fit together For limb (a): MLC 2006 Article VI, which divides the Code into mandatory Part A Standards and Part B Guidelines, read with the five Titles and with Title 5 enforcement. For limb B): MSC.1/Circ.1598, Guidelines on fatigue, 24 January 2019, read with STCW regulation VIII/1 and section A-VIII/1.
STCW Manila amendments in force 1 January 2012		 The two sets of MLC amendments, and the trap runs in both directions. The 2022 amendments entered into force on 23 December 2024, nine months before this sitting - they are not a recent development but the operative law, and belong integrated into the Titles they amend. The 2025 amendments were adopted on 6 June 2025, three months before the sitting, and were not in force. Omitting them is an error, because a question about the regimes is a question about the state of the regime and they had already been adopted; treating them as operative requirements is the opposite error. Adopted is not in force. The secondary trap is answering limb (a) as a second STCW question and never reaching MLC 2006, when the stem expressly names maritime labour and welfare.

RO Code - MSC.349(92) and MEPC.237(65), mandatory 1 January 2015		 Failing to explain WHY II-1/3-1 exists - that SOLAS never contained scantling rules, so structural standards were private contract until this regulation made them statutory - or stating a single frequency for the periodical survey when it differs between the Safety Equipment and Safety Radio certificates.
