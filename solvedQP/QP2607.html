Model written answer

(a) Application of formal safety assessment

1. Define FSA and cite the governing instrument

Formal Safety Assessment is defined in IMO MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 (9 April 2018) as a structured and systematic methodology for enhancing maritime safety by using risk analysis and cost-benefit assessment. After defining the problem it runs in five steps, applied here to the loading and carriage of iron ore pellets in bulk:

2. The five FSA steps applied to the cargo

FSA stepApplied to iron ore pellets in bulk
1. Identification of hazardsCargo not as declared or of a different Group; hull girder overstress; tank-top overloading by a dense, low-stowage-factor cargo; excessive GM and stiff rolling; water ingress; hatch cover leakage; loading rate beyond approval; dust exposure.
2. Risk analysisRank by likelihood and consequence. Structural overstress and undetected ingress dominate - the consequence extends to loss of the ship.
3. Risk control optionsVerify declaration and BCSN against the schedule; approved loading sequence run on the loading instrument; BLU Code ship/shore agreement; prove bilge systems and water level detectors; hatch cover weathertightness; trim level; weather routeing.
4. Cost-benefit assessmentCost per unit of risk reduced. A slower loading rate costs terminal time but removes a high-consequence structural risk. The test is ALARP - further reduction disproportionate to the benefit obtained.
5. Recommendations for decision-makingAdopt the retained controls into the loading plan, SMS procedures and standing orders. At IMO level this step produces amendments to mandatory instruments.

3. Where FSA applies, and the shipboard analogue

Strictly, FSA is a rule-development methodology applied by Member States and IMO Committees (para 1.3.1), not by a ship. IMO applied it to bulk carrier safety, producing SOLAS chapter XII; on board, the same logic is implemented as risk assessment under the SMS (ISM Code 1.2.2.2) plus compliance with the resulting instruments.

(b) Carriage and voyage monitoring requirements

4. Before loading

  • Obtain the shipper's cargo declaration required by SOLAS VI/2 and IMSBC section 4.2, stating the Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and Group, and read that BCSN's individual schedule. The declared BCSN governs, never the commodity name - IRON ORE, IRON ORE FINES, IRON ORE PELLETS and DIRECT REDUCED IRON (B) are different cargoes in different Groups, and pellets appears in two of those names.
  • Iron ore pellets are carried as Group C - neither liable to liquefy nor a chemical hazard - but establish that from the declared BCSN and its current individual schedule, noting that the fines schedule carries qualifying criteria under which fines may also be Group C.
  • Holds clean and dry; bilge wells clear; strainers, non-return valves and bilge lines proved; hold bilge suctions protected against ingress of fines.
  • Hatch covers weathertight-tested.

5. During loading

  • Load to an approved sequence, monitoring shear force and bending moment on the loading instrument required by SOLAS XII/11 for bulk carriers of 150 m and above, at every pour and every de-ballasting step.
  • The cargo is dense with a low stowage factor, so the approved loading manual limits - permissible mass per hold and tank-top loading - bind before cubic capacity does. (Engineering judgement; the loading manual is the governing document.)
  • Agree loading rate and sequence with the terminal under the BLU Code ship/shore checklist, to guard against structural damage from high-rate pouring.
  • Trim reasonably level in accordance with the Code's trimming procedures.
  • Watch GM - a heavy, low-stowed cargo gives an excessive GM and a stiff, violently rolling ship. Alternate-hold loading only if the ship is approved for it, and only within the loading manual limits.

6. Voyage monitoring

  • Hold bilge soundings and hold inspections at the SMS frequency, recorded.
  • SOLAS XII/12 hold, ballast and dry space water level detectors operational and tested - the principal safeguard against undetected ingress.
  • Weather routeing to limit slamming and rolling accelerations; monitor ship motions.
  • Hatch cover integrity, ventilator and air pipe closures maintained.
  • Enclosed-space entry controls before any hold entry.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question asks about the application of FSA to this cargo operation, so the marks are in the applying. Reciting the five steps earns a few; walking them through the real hazards of a dense bulk cargo earns the rest. Keep the methodological caveat to two lines in the answer - it shows you know what FSA formally is without spending the limb on it.

The distinction worth knowing, but not worth the whole answer

Under MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 para 1.3.1, FSA is applied by a Member State or consultative-status organisation proposing amendments to IMO instruments, or by an IMO Committee or subsidiary body. A ship does not formally conduct an FSA. IMO ran FSA studies on bulk carrier safety after the re-opened investigation into the loss of Derbyshire; MSC 76 (December 2002) approved the resulting package, including SOLAS chapter XII and regulation XII/12 water level detectors on all bulk carriers. The shipboard equivalent of the same reasoning is risk assessment under ISM Code 1.2.2.2 - assess all identified risks to its ships, personnel and the environment and establish appropriate safeguards - for which the Code prescribes no particular methodology.

Cargo identity - the real trap

Iron ore is not one cargo. IRON ORE, IRON ORE FINES, IRON ORE PELLETS and DIRECT REDUCED IRON (B) are separate schedules in different Groups, and pellets appears in the DRI (B) schedule name as well as in IRON ORE PELLETS. Nor is the fines/pellets split a clean binary: the iron ore fines schedule carries qualifying criteria - based on goethite content and particle size distribution - under which fines may be carried as a Group C cargo. IRON ORE FINES was introduced as a Group A schedule by resolution MSC.393(95), adopted June 2015 and mandatory from 1 January 2017, together with the Modified Proctor/Fagerberg test procedure. The safe rule for the exam and for the ship is the same: the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its individual schedule govern.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a liquefaction-and-TML answer. TML is a Group A concept and does not apply to a cargo correctly classified Group C. If someone offers you a TML for this cargo, question the classification rather than accept the figure.
  • Saying the ship or the Company carries out an FSA. It carries out risk assessment under ISM 1.2.2.2.
  • Calling Step 2 assessment of risks. The instrument calls it Risk Analysis.
  • Treating stowage factor as a stability issue only - for a dense cargo the loading manual limits usually bind first.
  • Forgetting that an excessive GM is a hazard, not a virtue.

Examiner traps

Expect a follow-up on what you would do if the cargo presented at the berth looks wetter or finer than the declaration says. Owners and masters are not bound by the shipper's declaration: you may stop loading, reject visibly wet cargo, require re-declaration and competent-authority certification, and demand TML and moisture certification. Saying so shows you treat the declaration as a starting point, not a defence.

  • Which IMSBC amendment is in force today?
  • What is ALARP, and which FSA step uses it?

Understand this first

An overflow is two emergencies at once, and candidates usually answer only the first. The physical one is obvious - oil is going over the side, and every minute of it enlarges the cleanup, the fine and the photograph. The second is jurisdictional: you are in someone else's waters, so a coastal State that never chose your ship is now entitled to investigate her, detain her and prosecute. That is why the order of your actions matters as much as their content. Stop the flow and contain it first, because everything that follows is judged against how quickly you did. Then fix the record while it is still contemporaneous - the oil record book, the bunker plan, the soundings - because from this moment every document you touch is evidence. Then report, early, to the coastal State, because a late report is an offence in its own right, separate from the spill. Then cooperate factually, and never volunteer a guess at cause.

Model written answer

The jurisdiction is unstated, so I would report through the coastal State's designated system and local contingency arrangements, using the SOPEP contact list.

1. Immediate action and containment

  1. Stop the transfer - emergency stop to the barge or terminal, shut the manifold valve; sound the alarm, muster the emergency team, inform the Master.
  2. Contain on deck: scuppers plugged, save-alls and drip trays, SOPEP locker deployed, absorbents to stop migration overside.
  3. Pump the spilled oil back inboard to a slack tank; sound all bunker tanks.
  4. If oil has reached the water, deploy boom where safe; no dispersants without coastal-State permission.
  5. Establish the cause - overfilled tank, failed high-level alarm, wrong line-up, blocked sounding pipe - before resuming.

2. Critical documentation

  • Oil Record Book Part I: code (G) accidental or other exceptional discharges of oil - time, position, approximate quantity and type, circumstances and reasons. Bunkering itself is code (H).
  • Bunker Delivery Note and sealed sample, retained under MARPOL Annex VI regulation 18.
  • Bunkering plan, pre-transfer checklist, agreed rates; soundings and ullages before, during and after.
  • Engine room logbook and bell book; alarm printouts, data logger records, high-level alarm test records.
  • Timed photographs and video; crew statements while memory is fresh; SOPEP and SMS records.

Preserve all of it. Never overwrite alarm logs, tidy records or backdate an entry - strike through, initial and date any correction.

3. Mandatory reporting

  • MARPOL article 8 and Protocol I: report without delay and to the fullest extent possible to the nearest coastal State, so counter-pollution action can follow. Format per resolution A.851(20).
  • Report via agents and the SOPEP contact list. MARPOL Annex I regulation 37 SOPEP is the plan under which this is executed; OPRC 1990 underpins the State's response.
  • Notify Company and DPA under ISM, flag State, class, P&I club and hull underwriters, charterers per the charterparty, and the bunker supplier.

4. Cooperation with investigators

  • Cooperate fully and factually with whichever authority attends - coastal or port State pollution and enforcement, flag State, the owner's P&I and insurer, and, only if the threshold is met, a marine safety investigation.
  • The Casualty Investigation Code (res. MSC.255(84), mandatory through SOLAS XI-1) requires investigation only of a very serious marine casualty - total loss, a death, or severe damage to the environment. A bunker overflow from an intact ship is usually a marine incident, where investigation is recommended practice, not a Code obligation.
  • Provide records, access and samples. Under MARPOL Annex I regulation 17 a master-certified copy of an ORB entry is admissible in juridical proceedings as evidence of the facts stated.
  • P&I and legal-protection practice, not a statutory duty: answer within my own knowledge; no speculation on cause, no admission of liability, nothing signed unread; Company or P&I correspondent involved where the interview carries legal consequences, subject to local law.
  • Never obstruct, conceal or alter a record. Res. LEG.3(91), promoted by A.1056(27), gives recommendatory guidelines on fair treatment of seafarers.

5. Liabilities to guard against

  • Which regime applies turns on facts the question omits. If she is a CLC ship - constructed or adapted to carry oil in bulk as cargo, and carrying it or on a following voyage - and the escaped oil is persistent, it is CLC damage and the Bunkers Convention excludes itself under Article 4(1). Otherwise the Bunkers Convention governs.
  • Bunkers Convention 2001 (in force 21 November 2008): Article 3 imposes liability without proof of fault on the shipowner - Article 1(3) defines that as the owner including registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator - jointly and severally, subject to the Article 3(3) defences. Article 7(1) separately places compulsory insurance on the registered owner alone, over 1,000 GT, with direct action against the insurer.
  • Administrative: detention, fines, refusal of clearance, PSC deficiencies, security or a letter of undertaking before sailing.
  • Criminal: depends on the coastal State and can attach personally to me. Regulation 17.6 makes the ORB entry evidence, so a false entry creates exposure separate from the spill.

6. Post-incident corrective action

Raise an incident report and non-conformity under ISM element 9, complete a root cause analysis and close out: alarm testing regime, revised bunkering checklist, overflow arrangements, soundings manned throughout transfer, refreshed drill under ISM element 8.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The command is state in systematic detail and the examiner has pre-numbered five components. Answer under the examiner's own headings, in order, and you cannot accidentally omit one. A well-written essay covering three of the five scores three-fifths, however good the prose.

The jurisdiction question

"Foreign waters" with no State named is deliberate. Candidates who invent a coast guard lose credibility. Say explicitly that you follow the coastal State's designated reporting system and local contingency arrangements, and that the SOPEP appendix carries the contact list. That one sentence turns an apparent gap into a demonstration that you know how the duty actually works.

CLC or Bunkers Convention - the distinction most candidates get backwards

The reflex answer is "CLC is for cargo oil, so a bunker spill must be the Bunkers Convention." That is wrong as a general statement. CLC 1992 defines oil as persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil "whether carried on board a ship as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship", and defines ship as a vessel constructed or adapted to carry oil in bulk as cargo (a combination carrier counting only while actually carrying oil in bulk, and on any following voyage unless no residues are proved). So a bunker spill from a laden tanker is CLC damage - and the Bunkers Convention then excludes itself under Article 4(1). Two facts decide it: is she a CLC ship, and is the oil persistent. The question gives neither, so the creditworthy answer is conditional.

Liability is not the same as the insurance duty

Under the Bunkers Convention, Article 1(3) defines shipowner broadly - owner, registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator - and Article 3 makes them liable jointly and severally. But Article 7(1) imposes the compulsory insurance obligation on the registered owner only, for ships over 1,000 GT. Merging the two is a common and costly error. The Article 3(3) defences are act of war or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; damage wholly caused by a third party's intentional act or omission; and damage wholly caused by the negligence of an authority responsible for navigational aids.

When is the Casualty Investigation Code actually engaged?

Only a very serious marine casualty - defined in the Code as one involving total loss of the ship, a death, or severe damage to the environment - attracts a mandatory safety investigation under Part II. Marine casualties short of that, and marine incidents, fall under Part III recommended practice. Note too that the Code's environmental limb requires severe damage "brought about by the damage of a ship" - an intact ship overfilling a tank does not obviously satisfy it. So the honest answer is that a bunker overflow is usually a marine incident. Saying so, rather than reciting the Code as automatically engaged, is the difference between knowing the instrument and knowing when it bites.

Common mistakes

  • Citing CLC 1992 as inapplicable to bunkers, or as applicable, without stating the two conditions.
  • Saying the Bunkers Convention makes the registered owner liable. It makes the shipowner liable; the registered owner insures.
  • Omitting the ORB code letters. (G) accidental discharge, (H) bunkering.
  • Presenting a Casualty Investigation Code investigation as automatic.
  • Presenting "no admission of liability" as a rule of international law. It is prudent P&I practice.
  • Forgetting the corrective-action limb - it is not in the examiner's five, but ISM 9 closure is what a Chief Engineer is judged on.

Examiner traps

"Would you delay reporting until you know the quantity?" No - the duty is to report without delay and to the fullest extent possible; an initial report goes immediately and is supplemented as facts firm up.

"The authority asks you to sign a statement admitting negligence." Decline politely, state the facts you know, ask for the Company and P&I correspondent. Cooperation is not self-incrimination - but note this is legal-protection practice, and what you may insist on depends on the local legal system.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which regulation requires the SOPEP, and for which ships? MARPOL Annex I reg 37; oil tankers 150 GT and above, other ships 400 GT and above.
  • Under which ORB code letter does an accidental discharge go? (G).
  • Who is liable under the Bunkers Convention, and who must insure? Shipowner as defined in Art 1(3); registered owner over 1,000 GT.
  • When must a flag State hold a safety investigation under the Casualty Investigation Code? For a very serious marine casualty.
  • Why is a false ORB entry so serious? A master-certified copy is admissible in juridical proceedings as evidence of the facts stated.

Understand this first

Two different things are easy to blur here, and they have been put in one question deliberately. A classification society writes its own private rules, and a shipowner contracts with it - that is class, and it is commercial. The same society may also be authorised by a flag State to issue statutory certificates on that State's behalf - that is a Recognized Organization, and it is public law. Hold that split and the rest falls out. The societies formed a club to stop their private rules drifting apart from one another, and its outputs differ by what each one binds: one changes what is physically built into your ship, one settles what a disputed convention word means, and one governs how the society itself must behave. An owner cares about the first because it arrives as steel, weight and cost.

(a) IACS structure, and UR / UI / PR

1. What IACS is and how it is structured

The International Association of Classification Societies is a non-governmental association of classification societies holding consultative status at IMO. Its structure:

  • Council - the supreme governance body, one Council Representative per Member. Sets strategy, approves the budget, admits Members and adopts Resolutions.
  • General Policy Group (GPG) - executive deputy to Council, made up of Members' Permanent Representatives, running the work programme between Council sessions.
  • Panels and Expert Groups - the technical bodies (hull, machinery, survey, environmental, safe decarbonisation, safe digital transformation, safety) which develop and revise the Resolutions.
  • Permanent Secretariat in London, with a Quality Committee overseeing Members' quality systems. Chairmanship rotates among Members.

2. UR, UI and PR - what each is and its effect

ResolutionWhat it isStatus and effect
UR — Unified RequirementA minimum technical requirement on matters directly connected with class rules and practices.Members must incorporate it into their own Rules, normally within one year of GPG approval. It reaches a ship through class rules, not directly.
UI — Unified InterpretationA common interpretation of an IMO convention provision that is vaguely worded or left to each Administration's satisfaction.Secures uniform application of IMO instruments across Members. It interprets; it does not create a class requirement.
PR — Procedural RequirementA requirement about the Members' own procedures — survey conduct, transfer of class, reporting.Incorporated into Members' practices within the period agreed by GPG. Governs how the society works, not the ship's technical standard.

3. Significance to owners and operators

A UR sets a floor below which no IACS Member's rules may fall, so an owner gets a predictable technical standard whichever Member classes the ship, and cannot shop for a materially softer rule. UIs reduce the risk of an arrangement accepted by one society being challenged elsewhere, which matters directly at port State control. PRs have commercial bite: the PR on transfer of class prevents an owner escaping outstanding recommendations by switching society.

(b) Recognized Organizations and the RO Code

4. What a Recognized Organization is

A Recognized Organization is an organization to which a flag Administration has delegated authority to carry out statutory certification and survey work on its behalf - most commonly, but not necessarily, a classification society. The delegation is of function, not responsibility: the Administration remains responsible for the statutory certification of its ships. A society acts in two distinct capacities - as a classification society under its own rules, and as an RO under delegated statutory authority.

5. The RO Code and its salient features

The RO Code was adopted by resolution MSC.349(92) (21 June 2013) and resolution MEPC.237(65). Salient features:

  • Part 1 - general provisions: purpose, definitions, application.
  • Part 2 - mandatory provisions for flag State and RO: competence and qualification of surveyors and auditors, quality management system, independence, the written authorization agreement, and reporting duties.
  • Part 3 - guidelines for oversight of ROs by flag States. Parts 1 and 2 are mandatory; Part 3 is guidance.
  • Made mandatory from 1 January 2015 by res. MSC.350(92), MSC.356(92) and MEPC.238(65), under SOLAS, MARPOL Annexes I and II, and the 1988 Load Line Protocol.
  • Purpose: a harmonised, transparent and independent standard for assessing, authorizing and overseeing ROs.

6. How Administrations monitor ROs

Through a written authorization agreement defining the scope and limits of delegated authority; initial assessment and periodic audit of the RO's quality system; verification of surveyor qualification; monitoring performance through port State control detentions and casualties attributable to the RO's certificates; vertical contract audits and witnessed surveys; requiring the RO to refer out-of-scope matters back to the Administration; and ultimately restricting or withdrawing the authorization. The flag State's own performance is auditable under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme against the III Code.

The distinction candidates get wrong

All three are IACS Resolutions, but they act on different things. A UR acts on the society's rules - it is a technical floor the Member must write into its own rulebook, which is why a UR reaches a ship only through class rules. A UI acts on an IMO convention - it says what an ambiguous SOLAS or MARPOL requirement should be taken to mean. A PR acts on the society itself - how it surveys, reports and transfers class. Ship / convention / society is the cleanest way to hold them apart.

Classification society is not a synonym for RO

A society classes ships under its own rules as a commercial matter. It becomes a Recognized Organization only when a flag Administration delegates statutory work to it, and only to the extent of that delegation. The same society may be an RO for one flag and not for another, and may hold different scopes from different flags. Saying "classification society = RO" is a real error - it also obscures the point that the Administration keeps the responsibility.

Understand this first

The rule is simple and the engineering is not. At the moment you cross the boundary, the fuel actually burning in the engines and boilers must already be inside the limit - not the fuel you have just begun pumping. That word already is the whole question, because a fuel system is not a tap. There is a service tank, a settling tank, pipework, filters, a viscometer and a hot engine full of residual oil, and the new fuel must displace all of it. So you work backwards from the line: how much volume has to be flushed, at what rate, and therefore how many hours beforehand you must start. Everything else is protecting the machinery while that happens. The compliant fuel is thinner and cooler, so viscosity, temperature and lubricity all move together - change them slowly, because thermal shock cracks fuel pumps and injectors, and a boiler that was atomising a heavy oil will not atomise a thin one at the same pressure.

Model written answer

I would brief the watchkeepers and issue written standing instructions covering the following sequence.

1. Establish the entry point and the applicable limit

  • Obtain from the bridge the time and position of ECA entry, and confirm which ECA. Inside a SOx ECA the fuel oil sulphur limit is 0.10% m/m, against the 0.50% m/m global cap outside.
  • Check any additional local or regional requirement. These are regional overlays, not MARPOL, and must be identified separately.

2. Calculate the changeover and start early enough

  • MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14.6 requires ships using separate fuel oils to carry a written changeover procedure, allowing sufficient time for the service system to be fully flushed so that compliant fuel is already being burnt on entry.
  • Calculate flushing time from the service-system volume - service and settling tanks, filters, heaters, pumps, injection pipework - against consumption rate. Justification: residual high-sulphur oil must be displaced, and dilution is asymptotic, so the changeover starts hours rather than minutes before entry.
  • Build in margin for a change of ETA or diversion.

3. Tank segregation and compatibility

  • Confirm segregated tanks, lines and transfer routes for compliant fuel, with valves identified so cross-contamination cannot occur.
  • Check compatibility before commingling. Justification: mixing incompatible residual fuels can precipitate asphaltenes, causing sludging, filter blockage and loss of propulsion at the worst moment. Where compatibility is uncertain, do not commingle.

4. Engines

  • Reduce fuel temperature gradually to bring the low-viscosity fuel to injection viscosity. Justification: rapid temperature change causes thermal shock and differential contraction in fuel pumps and injectors - scuffing, sticking and leakage.
  • Observe the maker's limits on rate of temperature change and minimum viscosity at the injectors. These are maker- and system-specific; I would work to our engine maker's stated limits, not a generic rule.
  • Hold viscosity within limits using the viscometer and cooler - low-viscosity distillate reduces lubricity and internal pump sealing, risking seizure and leakage.
  • Monitor fuel pump index, exhaust temperatures and leak-off alarms throughout.

5. Boilers

  • Change over auxiliary and composite boiler burners in step, adjusting atomising steam or air pressure and burner tip selection. Justification: a burner set for heated residual fuel atomises distillate badly - poor combustion, flame instability, sooting or flame failure.
  • Reduce or isolate fuel heating to the boiler; confirm stable flame and correct combustion after the change.
  • Watch for leakage at burner connections as the fuel cools and viscosity falls.

6. Records

  • Record in the logbook, as regulation 14.6 requires: the volume of low-sulphur fuel oil in each tank, and the date, time and position when the changeover was completed prior to entry and when commenced after exit.
  • Retain Bunker Delivery Notes and sealed representative samples, and keep the written changeover procedure available for inspection.

7. Contingency

If compliant fuel cannot be obtained, record the attempts made and submit a fuel oil non-availability report to the Administration and port State. It is an evidence obligation, not a licence to burn non-compliant fuel. An approved equivalent such as an exhaust gas cleaning system may be used where fitted and accepted.

Model written answer

1. Particular Average

Particular Average is a partial loss of ship, cargo or freight caused accidentally by a peril insured against, which is not a general average loss. It is fortuitous rather than deliberate, and it falls solely on the owner of the property damaged - there is no right of contribution from the other interests. A sea striking in a forecastle door, or seawater damage to one parcel of cargo, is particular average.

2. General Average, and how it differs

General Average is the deliberate counterpart. Under Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules: "There is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure." The loss is then shared rateably by all the interests saved - ship, cargo and freight - in proportion to their contributory values. In Indian law a general average loss is defined at section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

3. When General Average may be declared

Every element of Rule A must be present:

  • a real peril to the common adventure - apprehension alone is not enough;
  • the act must be extraordinary - not something the ship is already bound to do under the contract of carriage;
  • it must be intentional and voluntary, not accidental;
  • it must be reasonable;
  • it must be for the common safety of the property in a common maritime adventure, not for one interest alone;
  • there must be a saving of property - if nothing is saved there is nothing to contribute to.

Typical acts: jettison of cargo, voluntary stranding, water damage from firefighting, machinery damage sustained in refloating, salvage and tug costs, and port-of-refuge expenses. The shipowner, normally through the Master, declares general average. The declaration is an operational step; it does not by itself establish anyone's liability or entitlement.

4. Average adjusters and their role

An average adjuster is an independent professional appointed by the shipowner to prepare the adjustment - the statement that apportions the loss. The adjuster does not declare general average and does not decide liability. The role is to:

  • collect GA security before cargo is released - an average bond from cargo interests and a GA guarantee from their underwriters, or a cash deposit;
  • determine which sacrifices and expenditures are allowable under the applicable Rules;
  • ascertain the contributory values of ship, cargo and freight at the termination of the adventure;
  • apportion the contributions rateably and issue the adjustment;
  • act impartially between the interests, notwithstanding that the shipowner appoints and pays him.

5. Which rules actually apply

The York-Antwerp Rules are not law and do not apply automatically. They are a contractual regime, effective only when incorporated into the bill of lading or charterparty - which is why those documents name the edition. YAR 2016 was adopted by the Comite Maritime International at New York in May 2016, and BIMCO agreed that its forms should refer to YAR 2016; older contracts still commonly incorporate YAR 1994 or 2004. Absent incorporation, general average falls back on the applicable national law.

The two traps

First: who declares? The shipowner declares general average, in practice through the Master. The average adjuster does not declare it - he prepares the adjustment afterwards. Candidates routinely say the adjuster declares GA, which inverts the relationship.

Second: the York-Antwerp Rules are not law. They are a contractual code. They bind only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them, and the incorporating clause names the edition. Saying so, and naming YAR 2016 as the current edition while noting that 1994 and 2004 remain in live contracts, is what separates a strong answer.

The right of contribution is the whole point

PA and GA differ in one commercially decisive respect: who pays. In particular average the loss lies where it falls. In general average it is redistributed across everyone whose property was saved. That is why the shipowner has a lien on cargo for the GA contribution, and why cargo is not released until security is in place - releasing it destroys the security.

  • Treating the York-Antwerp Rules as an international convention.

  • Forgetting the requirement that property must actually be saved.

Model written answer

1. Qualify "zero-carbon" before evaluating

Ammonia (NH3) contains no carbon, so combustion produces no direct CO2. That is a statement about the molecule, not about climate performance. Whether ammonia is low-GHG depends on the production pathway: conventional ammonia uses fossil hydrogen and is carbon-intensive upstream, while green ammonia means renewable electrolytic hydrogen. The honest formulation is zero-carbon at the point of combustion, low-GHG well-to-wake only if made by a renewable pathway and certified as such. Combustion also produces N2O, a potent greenhouse gas which can undo much of the benefit.

2. Evaluation - the case for and against

In favour: no CO2, SOx or particulate matter from the fuel itself; liquefiable at modest conditions, so volumetrically far easier than hydrogen; and an existing production and seaborne trade base. Against: acute toxicity, poor combustion requiring a pilot fuel, low energy density needing roughly two to three times the tank volume of fuel oil, N2O and ammonia slip, material incompatibility, and immature regulation, training and bunkering. On balance a credible deep-sea candidate whose binding constraint is safety, not thermodynamics.

3. Safety, toxicity and storage

  • Toxicity dominates. Ammonia is acutely toxic by inhalation at low concentrations - a leak that would be a nuisance with hydrocarbon fuel is a life-safety event. Hence double-walled piping, gas-tight and ventilated fuel preparation spaces, gas detection, water spray for absorption, respiratory protection and citadel planning.
  • Corrosive to eyes and respiratory tract; readily absorbed in water, which is both a mitigation route and a discharge problem.
  • Flammable over a relatively narrow range with high ignition energy - a poorer fire risk than hydrocarbons, but not negligible.
  • Crew competence and drills are a genuine limiting factor; the hazard is unfamiliar to most engine crews.
  • Storage. Carried either refrigerated at about minus 33 degrees C at atmospheric pressure, or pressurised at ambient temperature, or semi-refrigerated - the same options as LPG, and the reason ammonia is far easier to store than hydrogen.
  • Low volumetric energy density means roughly two to three times the tank volume of equivalent fuel oil, costing cargo space and complicating arrangement.
  • Material compatibility is a real design constraint: ammonia attacks copper, copper alloys, brass, zinc and some plastics and elastomers, so fuel system materials must be selected accordingly. Stress corrosion cracking of certain steels is a known concern.
  • Boil-off management and a means of safe disposal or reliquefaction are required.

Section 15(1) extends eligibility to a citizen of India including a Non-Resident Indian or an Overseas Citizen of India, to a company or body established under a Central or State Act with its registered office or principal place of business in India, and to such other person or body as the Central Government may notify - in such proportion of ownership as may be notified. This directly enlarges the pool of capital that can flag tonnage in India.
  • Bareboat charter-cum-demise registration (section 16).   • Broader vessel coverage. The Act reaches craft the 1958 Act handled poorly or not at all, and consolidates the coasting-vessel regime by repealing the 1838 Act.
  • Temporary registration of a vessel sought to be recycled (section 17), supporting India's ship-recycling industry within a compliant framework.

3. Attracting foreign investment

The investment case rests on the same registration reforms plus procedural simplification: a wider ownership base, recognition of chartered-in tonnage, provisional and temporary registration routes, and a shift from prescriptive statutory detail toward rules made by the Central Government, so the regime can be updated administratively rather than by amending primary legislation. That cuts both ways - flexibility for the regulator is uncertainty for the investor until the rules are notified.

4. Alignment with global practice

  • The Act's long title states its purpose as ensuring compliance with India's obligations under the maritime treaties and international instruments to which India is a party. That is the hook on which convention alignment hangs, and the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is referred to expressly in the Act. Safety, security and marine-environment provisions are modernised across Parts VI to VIII.
  • Seafarer welfare: section 59 prohibits any person under sixteen years of age being engaged, employed or working on board any vessel, and the Act carries medical fitness, training and certification requirements aligned to international standards.
  • Strengthened provisions on maritime safety, pollution prevention and casualty response, and a consolidated framework for the maritime administration.

5. Assessment - law versus expectation

A precise answer separates three things. What the Act legally does: repeal and replace the 1958 Act, widen registration eligibility, permit bareboat charter-cum-demise registration, and give domestic effect to international obligations - all verifiable from the statute. What the Government expects it to achieve: tonnage growth, inward investment and improved ease of doing business - these are policy objectives, not statutory guarantees. What can reasonably be inferred: the registration reforms remove a genuine legal barrier to flagging tonnage in India, so the direction of effect is right; but the magnitude depends on fiscal treatment, the subordinate rules, and commercial factors the Act does not control. Legislation of this kind is a necessary condition for tonnage growth, not a sufficient one.

Model written answer

(a) Competence and performance

1. Competence defined

Competence is demonstrated ability to perform a task to a defined standard, assessed against stated criteria. Under STCW the standards of competence are set out in the Code's competence tables, and training and assessment must be administered, supervised and monitored in accordance with regulation I/6 and section A-I/6. Competence is proved at a moment in time and then certificated.

2. Performance defined

Performance is what the seafarer actually does in the real operational situation - at 0300, fatigued, with an incomplete alarm picture, in a team that may not share a first language. Competence is a property of the individual; performance is a property of the individual within a system.

3. Why competence does not guarantee performance

The gap is opened or closed by fatigue and hours of rest, workload and manning level, leadership and command climate, communication and language, team familiarity and crew turnover, stress and time pressure, situational awareness, and the quality of the procedures themselves. A certificate records that a person once met a standard; it does not establish that they will meet it tonight.

4. What ongoing training and drills do

Under ISM Code element 8 the Company must identify potential emergency shipboard situations and establish programmes of drills and exercises to prepare for them. Realistic, varied and honestly debriefed drills:

  • build the trained, near-automatic response that survives stress, when deliberate reasoning degrades first;
  • expose latent defects in procedures, equipment and manning before a real emergency does;
  • establish roles and closed-loop communication so that coordination need not be invented under pressure;
  • maintain rarely-used skills - emergency steering, blackout recovery, lifeboat launch, emergency fire pump - which decay fastest;
  • and, when debriefed rather than merely signed off, feed corrective action under ISM element 9.
  • ISM element 6 underpins all of this by requiring the Company to ensure personnel are qualified, medically fit and properly familiarised.

A drill recorded but not conducted is the clearest possible case of competence on paper with no performance behind it.

Model written answer

1. The governing statute

This is an Indian examination, so the governing statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. It closely follows the English Marine Insurance Act 1906, but the Indian Act is the one to cite.

2. The principle of Uberrimae Fidei

Section 19: a contract of marine insurance is a contract based upon the utmost good faith, and if utmost good faith is not observed by either party, the contract may be avoided by the other party. Two features matter. The duty is mutual - it binds the insurer as well as the assured. And it operates at the pre-contractual stage, because the underwriter must price a risk whose facts are known only to the proposer. Marine insurance is therefore held to a higher standard than an ordinary commercial contract, where the rule is broadly caveat emptor.

3. Disclosures the assured must make

Section 20(1): before the contract is concluded the assured must disclose every material circumstance which is known to him, and he is deemed to know every circumstance which in the ordinary course of business ought to be known to him. Section 20(2): a circumstance is material if it would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium or in determining whether he will take the risk. Section 20(5): "circumstance" includes any communication made to, or information received by, the assured.

The duty extends beyond the assured himself. Section 21 imposes it on an agent effecting the insurance, and section 22 requires representations made during negotiation of the contract to be true.

In practice, the material circumstances typically disclosed include: the vessel's age, type, flag and class, and her classification society standing; ownership and management; the intended voyage and trading limits, including war-risk or sanctioned areas; the nature, packing and stowage of the cargo; the loss and claims record; and known defects or outstanding conditions of class. (Industry practice - the Act states the test, not a list.)

4. Circumstances which need NOT be disclosed

Section 20(3) - in the absence of enquiry, four categories:

  1. any circumstance which diminishes the risk;
  2. any circumstance which is known or presumed to be known to the insurer - matters of common notoriety, and matters which an insurer in the ordinary course of his business ought to know;
  3. any circumstance as to which information is waived by the insurer;
  4. any circumstance which it is superfluous to disclose by reason of an express or implied warranty.

Note the qualifier "in the absence of enquiry": if the insurer asks, the exception falls away and the matter must be answered truthfully. Whether an undisclosed circumstance is material is a question of fact in each case (section 20(4)).

5. Effect of non-disclosure

Under section 20(1) the insurer may avoid the contract. Avoidance sets the contract aside ab initio - it is treated as never having existed, the premium is normally returnable, and no claim is payable, including a claim wholly unconnected with the undisclosed matter.

Three points a good answer adds. It is a right to avoid, not automatic nullity: the insurer may elect to affirm the contract, and may lose the right by waiver or by delay once he knows the facts. The failure need not be fraudulent - an innocent or negligent non-disclosure of a material circumstance is enough. And because section 19 is mutual, the assured may equally avoid for the insurer's want of good faith.

The English reform, and why it does NOT belong in your answer

English law on this subject was materially reformed by the Insurance Act 2015, which replaced the blunt all-or-nothing remedy of avoidance with a duty of fair presentation and a set of proportionate remedies - so that, for example, an innocent non-disclosure which would merely have raised the premium leads to a proportionate reduction in the claim rather than the loss of cover altogether. That reform is English and statutory. It did not amend the Indian Marine Insurance Act, 1963, under which avoidance remains the remedy. Knowing the contrast is worth mentioning in an oral; writing the English position as though it were the Indian one would be wrong.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the Marine Insurance Act 1906 in an Indian examination.
  • Importing the Insurance Act 2015 proportionate remedies into Indian law.
  • Saying the duty binds only the assured. Section 19 is expressly mutual.
  • Saying the contract is void. It is voidable at the option of the innocent party.
  • Omitting the words "in the absence of enquiry" from the section 20(3) exceptions - once asked, they must be answered.
  • Claiming non-disclosure must be fraudulent. It need not be.

Examiner traps

"The owner did not mention that the vessel had grounded two years ago, but the casualty was widely reported in the trade press. Must he disclose it?" Arguably not - section 20(3)(b) excepts circumstances known or presumed to be known to the insurer, including matters of common notoriety. But if asked, he must answer truthfully.

"The undisclosed fact had nothing to do with the loss. Can the insurer still avoid?" Under the Indian Act, yes - avoidance is not limited to matters causative of the loss. That is precisely the harshness the English 2015 Act was passed to soften.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which Act governs marine insurance in India, and from what year? The Marine Insurance Act, 1963.
  • State the test of materiality. Whether it would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium or determining whether to take the risk.
  • List the four circumstances which need not be disclosed.
  • Is the contract void or voidable? Voidable, at the option of the other party.
  • Does the duty bind the insurer too? Yes - section 19 says either party.

Memory framework

For the section 20(3) exceptions - D-K-W-S: Diminishes the risk, Known to the insurer, Waived, Superfluous by reason of warranty. And remember the gate on all four: in the absence of enquiry.
