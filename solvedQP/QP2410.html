Understand this first

The Act is doing two different jobs with one idea, and seeing that is the whole question. A criminal offence needs fault, a trial and proof, and it operates after the ship has sailed - which is no use whatever to the crew of a ship about to sail. An administrative detention power needs only a reasonable belief and can act within hours, but it punishes nobody. So section 334 attaches consequences to a decision already taken, and section 336 supplies the fast preventive lever, with the survey and the written grounds as the safeguards against its misuse. Section 335 then does a third thing again: it turns seaworthiness from something owed to cargo and underwriters into something owed to the people on board, by a term the owner cannot contract out of. Once you see three jobs rather than three definitions, the comparison limb writes itself.

Model written answer

(a) The meaning of an unseaworthy vessel — 5 marks

1. What the Act means by an unseaworthy vessel

The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 gives no definition of unseaworthiness in a definitions section. The description sits inside section 334 itself, which treats a ship as unseaworthy where the following are not such as to render her in every respect fit for the proposed voyage or service:

  • the materials of which she is made, and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, boilers and machinery.

Two features carry the marks in this limb. First, the test is relative. The Act does not ask whether the ship is in good condition in the abstract; it asks whether she is fit for the proposed voyage or service. A ship perfectly adequate for a sheltered coastal run may be unseaworthy for a North Atlantic passage in winter, and the same ship may be seaworthy on Monday and unseaworthy on Tuesday because the intended service has changed. Second, the concept is far wider than material condition. An undermanned ship is unseaworthy. A ship whose officers hold the wrong certificates is unseaworthy. A mechanically perfect ship with badly stowed cargo or ballast is unseaworthy. The Act puts people and stowage on the same footing as steel, and a candidate who treats unseaworthiness as a hull-and-machinery question has read only half the section.

2. The offence the section creates, and the defences it allows

  • Section 334(1) — every person who sends or attempts to send an Indian ship to sea from any port in India in such an unseaworthy state that the life of any person is likely to be thereby endangered is guilty of an offence, unless he proves that he used all reasonable means to insure her being sent to sea in a seaworthy state, or that sending her to sea in that state was, under the circumstances, reasonable and justifiable.
  • Section 334(2) — a master who knowingly takes such a ship to sea is likewise guilty, but with only the second defence open to him. The master cannot say he used all reasonable means; if he knew, he is answerable.
  • Section 334(3) permits a person charged to give evidence in his own defence.
  • Section 334(4) — no prosecution shall be instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government.

Liability is therefore not automatic, and the section is more carefully balanced than it first appears. The threshold is danger to life, not mere unfitness. The burden of the defence lies on the accused — he must prove it. But the standard demanded of him is reasonable means, not a guarantee of result, so a latent defect that diligence would not have found does not convict him. And the requirement of Central Government consent keeps the section from being used as a private weapon in a commercial dispute.

(b) The difference between an unseaworthy ship and an unsafe ship — 6 marks

This is the heaviest limb on the question, and it asks for a comparison, not for a second definition. Establish what an unsafe ship is first, because the difference cannot be stated until both concepts are on the page.

3. What an unsafe ship is, and the power it triggers

Section 336 is directed at a different problem altogether — stopping the ship rather than punishing anybody. A ship is unsafe where, by reason of the defective condition of her hull, equipment or machinery, or by reason of overloading or improper loading, she is unfit to proceed to sea without serious danger to human life, having regard to the nature of the service for which she is intended. The procedure is staged, and the stages are the safeguards:

  1. the Central Government, having reason to believe on complaint or otherwise that she is unsafe, may order her provisionally detained for the purpose of survey;
  2. a written statement of the grounds must be served forthwith on the master;
  3. the matter is referred to the court of survey for the port, or a competent person is appointed to survey the ship and report;
  4. on that report she is either released or finally detained.

Then close with the asymmetry, which is the observation that shows you have understood rather than memorised. A ship that is unsafe within section 336 will invariably also be unseaworthy within section 334 — but the converse does not hold. A ship one certificated engineer short of her safe manning document is unseaworthy under section 334, because crew qualifications are expressly part of the test; she is not unsafe under section 336, because that section's causes are confined to hull, equipment, machinery and loading. The two provisions overlap; they are not two names for one idea.

(c) The obligations of owners to crew with respect to seaworthiness — 5 marks

5. The owner's obligation to the crew

Section 335 converts seaworthiness from a matter between owner, cargo and underwriter into a duty owed to the people on board. In every contract of service, express or implied, between the owner of an Indian ship and the master or any seaman, and in every contract of apprenticeship, there is implied — notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary — an obligation that the owner, the master and every agent charged with the loading of the ship, or preparing her for sea, or sending her to sea shall use all reasonable means to ensure the seaworthiness of the ship for the voyage at the time when the voyage commences, and shall keep her in a seaworthy state during the voyage.

Three consequences follow, and they are the marks in this limb:

  • The duty is non-excludable. The words ‘notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary’ mean a term in the crew agreement purporting to waive it is void. A seafarer cannot sign away his right to a seaworthy ship.
  • It is a due-diligence standard, not a warranty of result. The obligation is to use all reasonable means. The owner does not guarantee that the ship is seaworthy; he guarantees that he tried properly to make her so.
  • It is continuing. The duty is owed at the commencement of the voyage and throughout it. It is not discharged at the pilot station, which is where the marine-insurance doctrine of a voyage-policy warranty would stop.

It also reaches beyond the registered owner. The section names the master and every agent charged with loading the ship or preparing or sending her to sea, so a shore manager or loading agent whose act renders her unseaworthy is inside the obligation, not outside it.

6. How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force on the day

Section 335(2) allows the Central Government, at the owner's request or otherwise, to arrange a survey of the hull, equipment or machinery by a surveyor. In practice the obligation is policed through the wider system rather than through that sub-section alone: classification and flag State statutory survey and certification, ISM auditing of the safety management system, and port State control inspection — with the section 336 detention power as the last resort when inspection finds a ship that should not sail.

One point of currency should be stated rather than assumed, because it is the standard way this question is lost. The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 was the operative statute on this examination date, and at this sitting it stood unopposed. There was no replacing Act, and there was not even a Bill before Parliament: the Merchant Shipping Bill, 2024 was introduced in the Lok Sabha only on 10 December 2024, two months after this paper was sat. Sections 334, 335 and 336 are therefore not merely the correct answer — at this sitting they are the only answer available.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has printed the structure for you, and the marks are not evenly split. 5 + 6 + 5 means the middle limb is the largest single block of marks on the question, and it is the limb candidates most often skip — they define unseaworthy, define unsafe, and never actually state the difference. Answer in the printed order, label the limbs (a), (b) and (c) so the marker can find them, and give limb (b) more paper than either of the others.

Within limb (b), do not settle for one sentence. A comparison stated on five axes — what each one is, what follows from it, which direction it looks in, how wide its causes are, and what threshold it sets — is five scoreable propositions where ‘one is an offence and the other is detention’ is one. Finish with the asymmetry, because it is the sentence that shows you have read both sections rather than remembered a slogan.

Why the Act splits one idea into two sections

A criminal offence needs fault, a trial and proof, and it operates after the event — which is no use at all to the crew of a ship that is about to sail. An administrative detention power needs only a reasonable belief and can act within hours, but it punishes nobody and it is open to abuse. The 1958 Act therefore does both: section 334 attaches consequences to a decision, and section 336 supplies the fast preventive lever, with the survey, the court of survey and the right to be told the grounds in writing as the safeguards against its misuse. Seeing that the two sections exist to solve different problems is the whole of the distinction, and it is worth more in limb (b) than any amount of re-quoting.

Seaworthiness has three different legal lives — keep them apart

  • Statutory and criminal — MSA 1958 s.334. A state of the ship, punishable when she is sent to sea in it, with a due-diligence defence. This is limb (a).
  • Contractual, owed to the crew — MSA 1958 s.335. Implied, non-excludable, and continuing through the voyage. This is limb (c).
  • Insurance and carriage — a warranty or a duty of due diligence depending on whether the policy is a voyage or a time policy and on the terms of the contract of carriage. Under Indian law that is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, not the English Act of 1906, and a breach affects recovery rather than attracting a penalty. This is not asked here — do not import it.

Most wrong answers to this question are right answers to the third bullet. The word ‘warranty’ is the tell.

Common mistakes

  • Defining both terms and never stating the difference. Limb (b) is six marks and it asks for one thing.
  • Treating unseaworthiness as a hull-and-machinery question. The section expressly includes the master's qualifications, the crew's number and qualifications, and the stowage of cargo and ballast.
  • Forgetting that the test is relative to the proposed voyage or service, and writing as though seaworthiness were an absolute condition.
  • Answering limb (c) with the English marine-insurance warranty of seaworthiness instead of section 335's implied contractual obligation.
  • Saying the owner guarantees a seaworthy ship. He does not: the standard is all reasonable means.
  • Stopping the section 335 duty at departure. It continues during the voyage, and those three words are worth a mark.
  • Naming the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. At this sitting it did not exist in any form.
  • Quoting a penalty figure for the section 334 offence. Get it wrong and you have volunteered an error; the section can be answered fully without one.

Examiner traps

‘Give me an example of a ship that is unseaworthy but not unsafe.’ A ship sailing one certificated engineer short, or with an officer holding the wrong certificate of competency. Crew qualifications are expressly inside section 334 and are outside the closed list of causes in section 336. This is the discriminating answer and it is the reason the asymmetry matters.

‘Who can prosecute under section 334?’ Nobody, without the Central Government. Section 334(4) provides that no prosecution shall be instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government.

‘Your owner tells you the charterer accepted the ship in her present condition and signed a waiver. Are you covered?’ Not as against the crew. Section 335 implies the obligation notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary, so a waiver in a crew agreement is void. And a charterer's acceptance is nothing to the point in a section 334 prosecution, where the threshold is danger to life.

‘Which Act applies?’ The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. At an October 2024 sitting there was no competing candidate at all — see the currency note below.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which section describes unseaworthiness, and why is it not in the definitions? Section 334; the Act describes the state inside the offence provision.
  • Name four things the section takes into account. Materials and construction; the master's qualifications; the crew's number, description and qualifications; the weight, description and stowage of cargo and ballast; the condition of hull, equipment, boilers and machinery.
  • What are the two defences to section 334(1), and which of them is open to the master? All reasonable means used, and reasonable and justifiable in the circumstances. Only the second is open to the master under s.334(2).
  • What are the causes of unsafety in section 336? Defective hull, equipment or machinery; overloading; improper loading.
  • Walk me through a detention under section 336. Provisional detention for survey; written grounds served forthwith on the master; reference to the court of survey or an appointed competent person; release or final detention on the report.
  • Can an owner contract out of section 335? No — it is implied notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary.
  • When does the section 335 duty end? It does not end at departure; it continues during the voyage.
  • Which Indian Act governs marine insurance, if this had been an insurance question? The Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

Understand this first

A charterparty is not a species of regulation and there is no convention behind it. It is a commercial bargain about a ship, and everything in it — the types, the clauses, the disputes — falls out of a single question: how much of the ship's operation has the owner sold?

Sell a single carriage and you have a voyage charter: the owner keeps the ship, the crew and the operation, pays for the bunkers and the port charges, and takes the risk that the voyage runs long — which is why he needs laytime and demurrage, a clock that starts when the ship is ready and charges the charterer once it runs out. Sell the ship's time and you have a time charter: the charterer now decides where she goes and therefore pays for the fuel and the ports, and the owner is paid a daily hire whatever she is doing — which is why the charterer needs off-hire, a clause that stops the clock when the ship cannot work. Sell possession itself and you have a bareboat charter: the charterer mans her, insures her and operates her, and the owner is close to a financier.

Hold that axis and you can derive the answer rather than recall it. And note where the Chief Engineer sits on it: the speed and consumption warranty, the off-hire clause and the bunker clauses are the three places where the engine room and the contract meet — and the evidence in every one of those disputes is engine-room evidence.

Model written answer

(a) Definition and types — 6 marks

1. What a charter party is, and what it is not

A charter party is a contract by which a shipowner agrees to place a ship, or part of her carrying capacity, at the disposal of a charterer for reward. It is a commercial contract and nothing else: no convention, code or resolution prescribes its content. Its terms are what the parties agree, subject to the general law of contract and to whatever law and arbitration forum the contract itself chooses. Saying that plainly is worth a mark, because it is the reason no carriage convention should be cited in answer to this question.

Three distinctions complete the definition:

  • It is not the contract of sale of the goods. That is a separate bargain between seller and buyer, and it is what decides who has to arrange and pay for the carriage in the first place.
  • It is not the same as a bill of lading. A bill of lading is a receipt for goods shipped, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title. Between owner and charterer the charterparty governs; but once a bill of lading passes into the hands of a third-party holder it operates as a contract in its own right and may attract a mandatory carriage regime that the charterparty, freely negotiated between commercial equals, does not.
  • It is almost always concluded on an amended standard form. The market uses printed forms — GENCON in dry-cargo voyage chartering, NYPE and BALTIME in time chartering, BARECON for bareboat, SHELLTIME and the common tanker forms in the oil trades — and then amends them with rider clauses, which prevail over the printed text where the two conflict. The printed form is the starting point; the rider clauses are where the negotiation actually is.

2. The types, and the one question that generates them

The types are not an arbitrary list. They fall out of a single question: how much of the ship's operation has the owner sold? Cost, time and control all follow the same axis, which is why the whole taxonomy can be reconstructed rather than memorised.

  • Voyage charter. The owner sells a single carriage. He keeps the ship, the crew and the whole operation, pays the voyage costs, and is paid freight — usually per tonne of cargo or as a lump sum. Because he is paying for the time, he needs a clock: hence laytime, demurrage and despatch.
  • Time charter. The owner sells the ship's time. He retains possession, the master and crew, and responsibility for navigation and maintenance; the charterer directs her commercial employment within agreed trading limits and pays hire, usually daily, whatever she is doing. Because the charterer is now paying for the time, he needs a clock that stops: hence off-hire.
  • Bareboat or demise charter. The charterer takes possession, mans, insures, maintains and operates the ship as disponent owner for the period. The registered owner is close to a financier. This is the type used in leasing and long-term financing structures.
  • Contract of affreightment. Not a charter of a named ship at all, but a commitment to carry stated quantities over a period, with ships nominated voyage by voyage. It suits a shipper with a continuous programme rather than a single parcel.
  • Intermediate forms exist and are worth naming: the trip time charter (a time charter for one voyage, so hire is paid but the charterer buys a passage), consecutive voyage charters, and the slot charter in liner trades, where what is bought is container capacity rather than a ship.

(b) The key clauses typically included — 5 marks

3. The key clauses, grouped so they can be recalled

The clause list varies with the form and the trade, so the useful answer describes the families by what they do. Six groups cover almost every charterparty:

  • The ship. The description clause — name, flag, class, tonnage, dimensions, cargo capacity, gear, and the speed and consumption warranty. Owners warrant the ship as described; a shortfall against the warranted speed or an excess over warranted consumption is what generates a performance claim.
  • The period or the voyage. For a time charter: place and time of delivery and redelivery, the period and any option, and the trading limits. For a voyage charter: the loading and discharging ports or ranges, and the laycan — the window within which the ship must present, with a cancelling date giving the charterer the right to cancel if she is late.
  • Money and time. Freight (voyage) or hire (time) and how, when and where it is paid; the owner's right of withdrawal for non-payment of hire; laytime, demurrage and despatch; off-hire; and the bunker clauses governing quantity, grade and price on delivery and on redelivery.
  • Risk and control. The safe port and safe berth warranty; the employment and indemnity clause, under which the master follows the charterer's employment orders and the charterer indemnifies the owner against the consequences; deviation and liberty clauses; excluded and dangerous cargoes; and the war risks, ice and strikes clauses.
  • Security and liability. The bills of lading clause, governing what the master may sign and on what terms; lien and cesser, giving the owner a lien on cargo and sub-freights for freight, hire, demurrage and general average contribution; general average and the New Jason clause; the both-to-blame collision clause; and insurance and P&I provisions.
  • Compliance and administration. ISM and ISPS clauses allocating responsibility for compliance and its costs; sanctions clauses; fuel-quality and emission-compliance provisions, which sit at the join between the charterer's obligation to supply fuel and the ship's obligation to comply; and the arbitration and choice of law clause, which decides where and under what law any of this is fought.

One general point outranks the list. A printed form is only the starting point. Rider clauses added in negotiation prevail over the printed text where they conflict, so a question about ‘typical’ clauses is really a question about what has to be settled — not about the contents page of any one form.

The organising point: under a time charter the risk of time moves to the charterer — except where the owner's own performance fails. That exception is the off-hire clause, which is the pivot of the whole relationship: hire ceases while the ship is prevented from working by a cause falling within it — typically breakdown of machinery, deficiency of crew, damage, drydocking or detention. Note also the division of authority: the charterer directs employment, but the owner and the master retain navigation and safety, and the master may refuse an order that would put the ship in danger.

Finally, and this is what makes the question a management-level one rather than a legal one: the Chief Engineer meets this contract in three specific places. The speed and consumption warranty, which turns the engine room's daily record into the evidence in a performance claim. The off-hire clause, where a machinery breakdown stops hire and the cause and duration have to be proved. And the bunker clauses, covering quantity and price on delivery and redelivery and the specification and quality of the fuel the charterer supplies — where a poor stem sample becomes simultaneously a commercial dispute and a compliance problem. In every one of those three, the evidence that decides the matter is engine-room evidence: noon reports, logbooks, bunker delivery notes and consumption records.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are 6 + 5 + 5, and the largest limb is the definition-and-types one. That is unusual and it is the planning fact to act on: six marks are not paid for a one-line definition, so the types must be developed properly — four principal types with who pays and who controls, plus the intermediate forms.

Limb (b) is five marks over a dozen or more clause families. That is roughly a line each, so group them. Six named groups, each with two or three examples, is readable and recallable; a random list of fifteen clause names is neither. Limb (c) is four cells, not two — owner and charterer, voyage and time — and a two-column table is the only way it fits in five marks. If you cannot draw a table, use four labelled sub-headings.

The single axis that generates the whole answer

Learn one question and you can derive limb (a) and most of limb (c) from it: how much of the ship's operation has the owner sold?

  • Sell a carriage → voyage charter. The owner keeps everything and pays the voyage costs, so he carries the risk of time — and needs laytime and demurrage to protect himself.
  • Sell the time → time charter. The charterer directs employment and pays for the fuel and the ports, so he carries the risk of time — and needs off-hire to protect himself.
  • Sell possession → bareboat charter. The charterer mans, insures and operates her; the owner is close to a financier.
  • Sell a quantity over a period → contract of affreightment. No named ship at all.

Notice that demurrage and off-hire are the same idea from opposite sides: each is the protection needed by whichever party is paying for time it is not getting the use of. A candidate who says that has understood the subject rather than memorised it.

Where the engine room meets the contract

This question is set in a management paper, so the examiner is entitled to expect the Chief Engineer's angle. There are exactly three places where the machinery and the charterparty touch, and knowing them converts a competent answer into a strong one:

  • The speed and consumption warranty in the description clause. An underperformance claim is decided on noon reports, weather data and consumption figures — that is, on the engine room's own record. Sloppy noon reporting loses defensible claims.
  • The off-hire clause. A breakdown moves money. How much depends on the wording (some clauses are ‘net loss of time’, others operate on a period basis) and on whether the cause and duration can be proved from the log and the planned-maintenance record.
  • The bunker clauses. Quantity, grade and price on delivery and redelivery; and the specification of the fuel the charterer supplies. A fuel that damages the engine, or that will not meet the ship's statutory compliance obligations, is simultaneously a commercial dispute and a regulatory one — and the bunker delivery note and the retained sample are the evidence in both.

Common mistakes

  • Citing a carriage convention as though it governed the charterparty. No instrument prescribes its terms.
  • Inventing a clause number, or attributing a term to a named form. Describe what the clause does; do not pretend to quote it.
  • Answering limb (c) as two descriptions instead of four cells. Both parties, both charter types.
  • Giving a one-line definition and then racing on. Limb (a) is the biggest limb.
  • Confusing freight with hire. Freight is for a carriage; hire is for time.
  • Confusing demurrage (voyage) with off-hire (time), or applying one to the wrong charter type.
  • Saying a time charterer controls the ship. He controls commercial employment; the owner and master retain navigation and safety.
  • Forgetting the bareboat charter and the contract of affreightment, which together are worth a mark or two in a six-mark limb.
  • Quoting a hire rate, demurrage rate or laytime allowance. All are negotiated case by case and any figure is invented.
  • Writing the whole answer as commercial law with no engineering angle at all, in a management paper.

Examiner traps

‘Your main engine breaks down for three days. Who pays?’ Under a time charter, hire ceases for the period the ship is prevented from working, if the cause falls within the off-hire clause — so the owner effectively pays. Under a voyage charter there is no off-hire at all: the owner simply loses the time, unless laytime was already running, in which case the position depends on whether the delay was at the charterer's risk. Naming both cases is the discriminating answer.

‘The charterer orders you to a port you consider unsafe. What happens?’ The charterer warrants the safety of the port he nominates, and the employment and indemnity clause obliges the master to follow employment orders with the charterer indemnifying the owner. But the master's responsibility for the safety of the ship survives, and he may refuse to enter. The warranty gives a remedy in damages; it does not oblige anyone to run the ship into danger.

‘Who pays for the lubricating oil?’ The owner, under a time charter — even though the charterer pays for the fuel. It is a favourite question precisely because it tests whether the candidate has learned the split or merely assumes the charterer pays for everything consumed.

‘Which prevails, the printed clause or the typed rider?’ The rider clause, where they conflict — the parties are taken to have meant what they specially negotiated.

‘What is the difference between a charterparty and a bill of lading?’ One is the contract for the use of the ship between owner and charterer; the other is a receipt, evidence of the contract of carriage, and a document of title — and in a third party's hands it may attract a mandatory regime the charterparty does not.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Define a charter party. A contract by which the shipowner places the ship, or part of her carrying capacity, at the disposal of the charterer for reward.
  • Name the principal types. Voyage, time, bareboat or demise, and contract of affreightment - plus trip time and consecutive voyage charters and, in liner trades, slot charters.
  • Who pays for bunkers under each? Owner under a voyage charter; charterer under a time charter; charterer under a bareboat charter.
  • What is laytime and what happens when it runs out? The time allowed for loading and discharging; beyond it the charterer pays demurrage.
  • What is despatch? Money payable by the owner where the operation is completed in less than the laytime allowed, commonly at half the demurrage rate.
  • What is off-hire? A clause under which hire ceases while the ship is prevented from working by a cause within the clause.
  • What does the description clause warrant that matters to you? Speed and consumption - and it is the basis of performance claims.
  • What is the employment and indemnity clause? The master follows the charterer's employment orders and the charterer indemnifies the owner against the consequences.
  • What does a safe port warranty promise? A port the ship can reach, use and leave without exposure to danger avoidable by good navigation and seamanship, absent an abnormal occurrence.
  • Who signs the bills of lading and on what terms? The master or an agent, under the bills of lading clause, which limits what may be signed and on what terms.

Uncertainty and the limits of this answer

There is no primary source for this question and none is invented. A charterparty is a contract; no convention, code or resolution prescribes its content, so the dominant provenance class here is settled commercial practice and that is stated rather than disguised. It would properly be reviewed by a chartering or shipping-law practitioner; a regulatory check would find nothing to check.

MIW holds no charterparty standard form. GENCON, NYPE, BALTIME, BARECON and SHELLTIME are named as forms — which is uncontroversial industry knowledge — but no clause number is asserted anywhere, no clause wording is quoted, and no term is attributed to any particular form. That is a deliberate discipline, because supplying a clause number from memory is the specific way this question goes wrong, and a wrong number is worse than none. The same applies to figures: no hire rate, demurrage rate, laytime allowance or performance tolerance is quoted, since all are negotiated case by case.

Two further boundaries. The interaction with mandatory carriage regimes is stated only at the level of principle — a charterparty is freely negotiated, a bill of lading in a third party's hands may not be — with no article of the Hague, Hague-Visby, Hamburg or Rotterdam Rules cited and no assertion about which applies to any given contract. And no decided case is cited: the propositions here are settled practice rather than contested law, and where a real dispute arises it turns on the wording of the particular contract, not on a general rule. Charterparty law is a genuine gap in the MIW corpus — no other solved question in twenty-eight papers treats it — and that is recorded so a future session knows this answer stands alone.

The time charterer pays hire continuously and is buying the ship's time, so without off-hire he would go on paying for a ship that cannot work.

To nominate a port and berth at which the ship can reach, use and return from without, in the absence of some abnormal occurrence, being exposed to danger which cannot be avoided by good navigation and seamanship. In three places. The speed and consumption warranty in the description clause, which generates performance claims settled on noon reports and weather data. Understand this first

The two limbs look unconnected and they are not, because both are about a compromise made at the design stage that the ship then lives with for twenty-five years.

In limb (A) the compromise is between holding a course and changing one. They pull in opposite directions, and the physics says why: a hull at a small angle of drift generates a sideways force whose centre lies forward of the ship's centre of gravity, so the drift tends to increase itself. Everything aft — the run of the after body, the skeg, the deadwood, the rudder — is there to supply the opposing moment. Add more of it and she holds her course beautifully and turns like a barn door; take it away and she turns in her own length and cannot be left alone for thirty seconds. Neither is right in the abstract. A VLCC on a long ocean passage and a harbour tug want opposite ends of that scale, and a modern ship is often deliberately placed on the marginally unstable side because the autopilot can close the loop and the manoeuvring gain is worth having.

In limb (B) the compromise is between the engine you have and the propeller you gave it. The engine can produce power along a field of speeds; the propeller demands power along a cubic curve, and where those two meet is where the ship actually runs. The essential insight is that the design point and the maximum rating are deliberately different, and that the propeller is deliberately made a little light when new — because the curve is going to march leftwards for the next five years as the hull fouls and the weather does its work, and it must have somewhere to march to. A propeller matched perfectly on trials is a propeller that is overloading the engine before the first drydocking.

Model written answer

(a) Directional stability and manoeuvring, and how they are provided — printed as limb (A), 8 marks

1. What directional stability is

Directional or course stability is a property of the hull, not of the steering gear, the autopilot or the helmsman. The test is simple and should be stated first, because it defines the whole term: disturb the ship from a straight course by a transient force — a gust, a wave, a moment of rudder — then return the rudder amidships and see what she does.

  • A directionally stable ship settles onto a new straight course. She ends up on a different heading, but she goes straight again without help.
  • A directionally unstable ship continues to turn. Rudder must be applied to stop her turning, and the helmsman or autopilot is permanently at work.

Note that stability is judged on the residual motion after the disturbance ceases, not on how far she deviated while it acted. A stable ship may swing a long way and still be stable.

Why instability arises at all. When a hull moves at a small angle of drift, it behaves as a low-aspect-ratio lifting surface and generates a lateral force. On most ship forms the centre of that force lies forward of the centre of gravity, so the moment it produces increases the drift angle — it is destabilising. The ship is directionally stable only where the restoring moment generated by the after body and its appendages exceeds that destabilising moment. That is why everything that provides course stability is aft, and why the answer to the second half of this limb is mostly about the stern.

2. The conflict - stability against manoeuvrability

The two attributes named in the question are not two virtues; they are opposed, and saying so is the substance of this limb rather than a refinement of it. Both depend on the same parameter — the longitudinal distribution of lateral area — and they want it in opposite places.

  • Trim matters as much as form. Trim by the stern moves lateral area aft and improves course stability; a ship in ballast trimmed by the head is markedly worse on course, which every watchkeeper has felt.
  • Draught and depth of water matter. Shallow water changes the pressure field around the hull and generally increases course stability while degrading turning ability.
  • The design is therefore a deliberate compromise chosen for the ship's service, and a modern ship is often placed slightly on the unstable side on purpose, accepting a small permanent workload on the steering gear in exchange for a real gain in turning ability — because the autopilot can close the loop and the fuel cost of continuous small corrections is less than the operational cost of a ship that cannot turn.

3. How the attributes are provided

Answer this in order of priority, because that order is itself worth marks: the property lives in the hull, and everything else modifies it.

  • Hull form — first and most important. Length-to-beam ratio, block coefficient, and the form of the run aft. A skeg is the single most direct device: it adds lateral area well aft of the centre of gravity and therefore adds restoring moment. Deadwood does the same. Full-form ships may use twin skegs, which also improve the wake field into two propellers.
  • The rudder — the primary control surface. What matters is area as a fraction of the ship's lateral plane, aspect ratio (a deeper, narrower rudder gives more lift for its area and stalls later), section, and type: a spade rudder for small and fast ships, a semi-balanced horn rudder where the stock must be supported on a large ship, and high-lift flap or fishtail rudders where a large side force is needed at low speed. A balanced rudder places part of its area forward of the stock to reduce steering torque.
  • The rudder works in the propeller race, and this is the operational point of the whole limb. Immersed in an accelerated slipstream, the rudder develops far more side force than the ship's speed through the water alone would produce. Stop the propeller and the rudder has only the free stream to work with, so steering effectiveness collapses even though the ship still has headway. That is why a ship loses steerage the moment the engine is stopped, and why a rudder behind a stopped propeller is nearly useless.
  • Low-speed devices. Bow and stern thrusters generate side force where the rudder has no flow at all, but their effectiveness falls away rapidly with headway as the ship's own flow sweeps the jet along the hull. Azimuth and podded units produce thrust in any direction and give a manoeuvring capability nothing else matches.
  • The control system. An autopilot with rate and counter-rudder terms anticipates the swing instead of chasing it, and an adaptive autopilot tunes itself to the loading and sea state. This is what makes a marginally unstable hull entirely usable in service, and it is the reason designers are willing to accept one.

Finally, the regulatory frame, stated at the level at which it can be stated honestly: IMO has adopted standards for ship manoeuvrability applying to ships above a stated size, assessed by defined trial manoeuvres — the turning circle, the zig-zag, the stopping test and initial turning ability — and classification societies set requirements for steering gear and may assign optional manoeuvrability notations. No resolution number and no numerical criterion is quoted here, because the standards are not held and an invented criterion would be worse than none.

(b) The engine power / engine speed diagram and the propeller demand curve — printed as limb (B), 8 marks

This limb asks you to use a diagram. Draw it. Power on the vertical axis, engine or shaft speed on the horizontal, both usually logarithmic so the curves become straight lines. Mark the engine's permitted field, lay the propeller cubic across it, and label MCR, the service point, and at least two propeller curves — trial condition and service condition. Everything below is what those labels mean.

4. The engine power versus engine speed diagram

  • The layout diagram is the field, bounded by the layout points, within which the particular engine may be optimised at the design stage — the manufacturer's offer, before a rating is chosen.
  • The load diagram is the operating field of the engine as actually rated, and it is the one that matters in service. Its boundaries each mean something physical:
  • above — the maximum mean effective pressure line, which is a limit on mechanical and thermal loading of the running gear and combustion chamber;
  • to the right — the maximum speed line, a limit on rotational speed and inertia loading;
  • to the left at low speed — the torque limit and the scavenge air pressure limit, which together prevent the engine being asked for high torque before the turbocharger can deliver the air to burn the fuel cleanly. This is the boundary that bites in service;
  • an overload region above the continuous field, permitted only for limited periods and normally only on trials.

MCR — the maximum continuous rating — is the top right corner of the permitted continuous field: the highest power the engine may develop continuously, at its corresponding speed. The service or continuous service rating is a point deliberately placed below and to the left of it, and that deliberate placement is the subject of step 6.

5. The propeller demand curve, and how it moves

For a fixed-pitch propeller in free-running conditions, the power the propeller demands varies approximately as the cube of shaft speed, and the torque as the square. The demand curve is therefore a cubic through the origin, and where it crosses the engine's field is where the ship actually runs.

Its position depends on two things: the propeller (pitch and diameter) and the resistance of the hull. The second is not constant, which is the whole point of this limb. Draw more than one curve, because the examiner's plural is deliberate:

  • the curve moves LEFT — more torque demanded at any given speed — with hull and propeller fouling, increased displacement, shallow water, heavy weather and head seas, ice, towing, and damage to a propeller blade;
  • the curve moves RIGHT when the ship is clean, light and in trial condition — the light-running case;
  • left is towards the torque limit. That single direction carries most of the operational content of the limb.

6. The relation the question asks for - the three margins

Now the printed question can be answered directly. The propeller is designed to absorb the SERVICE power at the SERVICE speed. The engine is rated ABOVE that point. The gap is deliberate, and it is made up of three margins.

  • Sea margin. Added power provided to overcome the added resistance of hull and propeller fouling and of weather accumulated over the docking cycle. Without it a ship could make her contract speed only on the day she left the builder's yard.
  • Engine or operating margin. The service point is kept below MCR so that the engine runs continuously at a load that is easier on it, more economical in specific fuel consumption, and leaves reserve power available.
  • Light-running margin. The propeller is deliberately made slightly light when the ship is new and clean — that is, it absorbs slightly less torque than the engine could give at that speed. This is the one candidates find counter-intuitive and it is the most important of the three: because the demand curve only ever migrates leftwards in service, it must be given somewhere to migrate to. A propeller matched exactly in trial condition is already heavy-running the day she leaves the dock.

So the answer to the question as printed is that the maximum rated output of the plant exceeds the power for which the propeller is designed by design, not by accident, and the difference is the sum of those three allowances. No percentage is given here for any of them: sea margin, engine margin and light-running margin are settled between owner, yard and engine builder in the newbuilding contract and vary with trade, hull form and docking interval.

7. What happens when the match goes wrong

Heavy running, or torque-rich operation. The demand curve has moved left of design — typically a foul hull, a foul or damaged propeller, deep draught, shallow water or persistent heavy weather. The engine now meets its torque limit before it reaches its rated speed, so it cannot develop rated power at all. The symptoms follow directly from that:

  • high thermal loading and high exhaust temperatures, because the engine is burning a large fuel quantity at a speed at which the turbocharger delivers less air;
  • turbocharger and gas-side fouling, which makes the air shortage worse and is self-reinforcing;
  • increased risk of scavenge fire and of exhaust-valve and piston-ring distress;
  • loss of speed and increased specific fuel consumption — and, as a commercial consequence, a worse operational carbon intensity for the year and a possible underperformance claim under the charterparty.

The remedies are in the same order as the causes: hull and propeller cleaning, correct trim and draught, and — if the condition persists after cleaning — a propeller re-pitch to move the curve back to the right.

Light running is the converse and is a real fault too, not merely a safe margin. If the propeller is too light, the engine reaches its speed limit before it is fully loaded: the ship never develops her design power, the engine is overspeed-limited, and the installation is not delivering what it was paid for.

In both cases the diagnosis is the same, and it is the practical point of the whole limb: plot the actual operating points — power, shaft speed, fuel index — on the engine's own load diagram, and see which boundary the ship is running into. A diagram used that way is a condition-monitoring tool, not a piece of design paperwork.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

This is two questions of eight marks joined by a staple. They share no content and they need no linking material — so treat them as two independent answers, label them (A) and (B), and give each half the paper. A candidate who is strong on one and weak on the other should still write both: eight marks are available in each, and half of a well-organised limb beats a rushed whole.

In limb (A), the eight marks are weighted towards the second half. The definition is one mark and the conflict is worth two or three; ‘how are these attributes provided in a ship’ is the biggest single block, and it wants hull first, rudder second, low-speed devices third, control system last. Answering it in that order is itself a scoring decision, because it shows you know where the property lives.

In limb (B) the stem says ‘Using typical engine power versus engine speed diagram and propeller demand curves’. Draw it. A written description of a diagram the examiner explicitly asked for is a weaker answer than the same words with a labelled sketch above them, and the sketch takes ninety seconds.

What to draw in limb (B), exactly

Axes: power vertical, engine or shaft speed horizontal, both on log scales so the curves come out as straight lines. Then, in this order:

  1. the load diagram as a four-sided field — mean effective pressure limit along the top, speed limit on the right, torque and scavenge-air limits sloping up from the left, and a small overload region above;
  2. MCR marked at the top right corner of the continuous field;
  3. the service point marked below and to the left of MCR — and the gap between them labelled with the three margins;
  4. the propeller demand curve in trial condition, passing through or just right of the service point;
  5. a second propeller curve to its left, labelled service or fouled condition, running into the torque limit — this is the one that makes the diagram tell a story;
  6. an arrow between them labelled fouling, weather, displacement.

Six elements. If you can draw those and label them, the prose almost writes itself and every mark in the limb is reachable.

The two ideas that carry the whole question

Limb (A): stability and manoeuvrability are opposed. Both depend on the longitudinal distribution of lateral area, and they want it in opposite places. Everything aft of the centre of gravity — a long run, a deep skeg, deadwood, trim by the stern — makes her hold a course and makes her reluctant to turn. There is no design that maximises both, so a ship is placed on the scale where her service needs her, and modern ships are often placed slightly unstable on purpose because the autopilot can carry the workload and the turning gain is worth having.

Limb (B): the design point and the maximum rating are different on purpose. The engine offers a field of power against speed; the propeller demands power along a cubic; and the gap between MCR and the propeller's design point is not slack but three deliberate allowances. The counter-intuitive one is the light-running margin: the propeller is made slightly light when new because the demand curve only ever moves leftwards in service. Give it nowhere to move and you have built in a heavy-running engine from the first voyage.

Common mistakes

  • Treating directional stability and manoeuvrability as the same virtue. They are opposed, and the question is about the compromise.
  • Answering limb (A) as ship handling. It asks how the attributes are provided in a ship — a design question.
  • Starting the ‘how provided’ answer at the rudder. The property lives in the hull; the rudder modifies it.
  • Forgetting that the rudder works in the propeller race, which is the reason steerage is lost when the engine stops.
  • Not drawing the diagram in limb (B) when the stem asks you to use one.
  • Drawing one propeller curve. The plural in the stem is deliberate — trial and service conditions.
  • Confusing MCR with the propeller design power. They are different points on purpose, and the difference between them is the answer.
  • Getting the direction wrong: fouling moves the curve LEFT, into the torque limit.
  • Saying the propeller should be matched exactly on trials. It should be slightly light.
  • Quoting a percentage for sea margin or light-running margin, or an IMO manoeuvring criterion. Neither can be defended from a held source.

Examiner traps

‘Is a directionally unstable ship unsafe?’ Not necessarily. Many large full-form ships are marginally unstable by design, and the autopilot with rate and counter-rudder terms handles it. It becomes a problem when the control system fails, when the ship is trimmed by the head in ballast, or when close-quarters manoeuvring demands more attention than the bridge team can give.

‘Why do you lose steering when the engine stops?’ The rudder works in the propeller race. Without the accelerated slipstream it has only the free-stream flow, so the side force collapses even with headway on. This is the single most practical consequence of limb (A).

‘Your engine will not make revolutions and the exhaust temperatures are high. What is it?’ Heavy running — the demand curve has moved left and the engine is on its torque limit. Confirm by plotting operating points on the load diagram; then hull and propeller cleaning, trim, and a re-pitch if it persists.

‘Why not match the propeller exactly at trials?’ Because the curve only moves one way afterwards. A perfectly matched propeller is heavy-running from the first month, and the engine cannot then reach rated power for the rest of the docking cycle.

‘What limits the engine at low speed?’ The torque and scavenge-air-pressure limits — you cannot demand high torque before the turbocharger can supply the air to burn the fuel. Naming the scavenge-air limit rather than just ‘the torque limit’ is the better answer.

  • What determines rudder effectiveness? Area as a fraction of the lateral plane, aspect ratio, section and type - and whether it is working in the propeller race.
  • When is a bow thruster useless? At any appreciable headway, as the ship's own flow sweeps the jet along the hull.
  • Name the IMO manoeuvring trials. Turning circle, zig-zag, stopping ability, initial turning ability.
  • How does propeller power vary with shaft speed? As the cube; torque as the square.
  • What bounds the load diagram? Mean effective pressure above, speed to the right, torque and scavenge air pressure at low speed, with an overload region above the continuous field.
  • Name the three margins between MCR and the propeller design point. Sea margin, engine or operating margin, light-running margin.
  • Which way does fouling move the demand curve, and what does that do? To the left - the engine meets its torque limit before rated speed and cannot develop rated power.

Uncertainty and the numbers deliberately withheld

Engineering judgement is effectively the sole provenance class on this question, and that is stated rather than disguised. No instrument prescribes hull form, appendage design, engine selection or the margins, so there is nothing regulatory to verify in either limb and nothing was invented to fill the space. The content is settled naval architecture and marine engineering explained by mechanism, and it would properly be reviewed by a naval architect and an engine specialist rather than by a regulatory check.

The one regulatory touch is deliberately held at regime level. IMO's standards for ship manoeuvrability and the class requirements for steering gear are real and are referred to by function — the trial manoeuvres are named because they are universally known by name — but MIW holds neither the standards nor any class rule, so no resolution number, no circular number, no application threshold and no numerical criterion is asserted anywhere. No advance, no tactical diameter, no overshoot angle, no track reach. An invented manoeuvring criterion would be a far worse defect than a declared gap, and this is exactly the kind of question where one is easy to invent.

Three sets of numbers are withheld on the same principle. No percentage for sea margin, engine margin or light-running margin — all three are contractual and installation-specific, settled between owner, yard and engine builder, and typical values vary with trade, hull form and docking interval. No numerical value for any load-diagram boundary, since those belong to a particular engine's project guide, which is not held. And no numerical course-stability criterion: the concept is explained and the index is not quantified. One further limitation is worth stating plainly — the stem asks the candidate to use a diagram, and this spec carries no figure, so the answer specifies exactly what should be drawn and labelled instead. A candidate in the examination room should draw it.

Three steps for eight marks in (A) and four in (B), which is right because (B) asks for a diagram and an explanation of a relationship rather than a description.

The property of a hull which, when disturbed from a straight course by a transient force and with the rudder then returned amidships, settles onto a new straight course rather than continuing to turn. It is judged on the residual motion after the disturbance ceases, not on the deviation during it - a directionally stable ship ends up on a different heading but going straight; an unstable one goes on turning until rudder is applied to stop her.

The rudder-amidships test is the whole definition, and stating it prevents the standard confusion with how well a ship steers under autopilot.

Both depend on the longitudinal distribution of lateral area. Lateral area aft of the centre of gravity - a long fine hull, a deep skeg, generous deadwood, trim by the stern - supplies the restoring moment that keeps her straight, and the same area resists the turning moment when you want her to turn. A short full hull with a cut-up after body turns readily and wanders. The design is a compromise chosen for the intended service.

Saying they are opposed, and why, is the substance of limb (A). An answer that praises both as virtues has not understood the question.

Hull first: proportions, after body form, skeg and deadwood, twin skegs on full-form ships, and trim. Rudder second: area as a fraction of the lateral plane, aspect ratio, section, and type - spade, semi-balanced horn, or high-lift flap - working in the propeller race. Low-speed devices third: bow and stern thrusters, azimuth or podded units, which produce side force where the rudder has no flow. And the control system last: an autopilot with rate and counter-rudder terms, which makes a marginally stable hull entirely usable.

The order matters. Candidates start at the rudder; the examiner wants the hull first, because that is where the property actually lives.

Because the rudder works in the propeller race. With the propeller turning, the rudder is immersed in an accelerated slipstream and generates far more side force than the ship's speed through the water alone would give it. Stop the propeller and the rudder has only the free-stream flow to work with, so effectiveness collapses even though the ship still has headway.

This is the single most practical consequence of the design in limb (A) and a favourite oral question, because it explains why a dead-ship drift is not merely a propulsion problem.

In free-running conditions, delivered power varies approximately as the cube of shaft speed and torque as the square. The demand curve is therefore a cubic through the origin, and its position depends on the propeller pitch and diameter and on the resistance of the hull.

The cube law is the reason a small speed reduction saves a large amount of fuel, and the reason a small increase in resistance produces a disproportionate torque demand.

Sea margin - added power to overcome the added resistance of hull and propeller fouling and of weather over the docking cycle. Engine or operating margin - the service rating is deliberately kept below the maximum continuous rating for reliability and fuel economy. Light-running margin - the propeller is deliberately made slightly light when new and clean, so that the demand curve can migrate left through the docking cycle without meeting the engine's torque limit.

This is the direct answer to the printed question: the maximum rated output exceeds the propeller design power by design, and these three margins are what fills the gap.

Because the demand curve only ever moves one way in service - to the left, towards more torque at a given speed - as the hull and propeller foul, displacement increases and the weather works on her. If the propeller were matched exactly in trial condition, it would already be heavy-running the day she leaves the dock, and the engine would meet its torque limit before its rated speed. The light-running margin gives the curve somewhere to migrate to.

This is the discriminating insight in limb (B) and shows the candidate understands the diagram as a lifetime operating envelope rather than a trial-day snapshot.

Heavy running, or torque-rich operation: the propeller demand curve has moved left of design, so the engine meets its torque limit before reaching rated speed and cannot develop rated power. The symptoms follow - high thermal loading, high exhaust temperatures, turbocharger fouling, risk of scavenge fire, loss of speed and increased specific consumption. Confirm by plotting actual operating points against the layout diagram; then hull and propeller cleaning, correct trim and draught, and if it persists a propeller re-pitch.

It joins the theory to the engine room, and the diagnosis - plot the points on the diagram - is the answer an examiner is waiting for.

Model written answer

1. The Convention, and exactly where it stood at this examination

The Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships, 2009 was adopted at a diplomatic conference in Hong Kong, China in May 2009. Begin the answer with its status, because the status governs the tense of everything that follows.

Article 17 makes entry into force conditional, and the condition is a triple one. The Convention enters into force twenty-four months after the date on which all three of the following are satisfied:

  1. not less than 15 States have signed it without reservation or deposited the requisite instrument of ratification, acceptance, approval or accession;
  2. the combined merchant fleets of those States constitute not less than 40 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; and
  3. their combined maximum annual ship recycling volume during the preceding ten years constitutes not less than 3 per cent of the combined gross tonnage of those same States.

The third condition is the interesting one, and it explains the sixteen-year delay better than any commentary. A tonnage threshold alone could have been met by flag States; the recycling-volume threshold made it impossible to bring the Convention into force over the objections of the recycling States themselves. The Convention could not begin until the countries whose domestic industry it regulates agreed to be regulated.

The position at this examination must be stated exactly. The Article 17 conditions were satisfied in June 2023 — which follows from Article 17's own twenty-four-month rule applied to the entry-into-force date. The Convention accordingly enters into force on 26 June 2025. That is eight months after this paper. So at this sitting the Convention is adopted, its conditions are met, its commencement date is fixed and publicly known — and it is not yet in force. No obligation in it binds any ship or any yard today. Everything below is what it will require, and the whole industry is in the final run-up to it.

2. What it will require of ships

Application. The Convention will apply to ships entitled to fly the flag of a Party or operating under its authority, and to recycling facilities under a Party's jurisdiction. Warships and government non-commercial ships are excluded, and under Article 3.3 it does not apply to ships of less than 500 GT, nor to ships operating throughout their life only in waters under the sovereignty or jurisdiction of their own flag State — Parties being required nonetheless to see that such ships act consistently with it so far as reasonable and practicable. Article 3.4 applies the familiar no more favourable treatment rule to ships of non-Parties.

  • An Inventory of Hazardous Materials, ship-specific and verified by the Administration or an organisation authorised by it. Part I identifies the listed hazardous materials in the ship's structure and equipment, with their location and approximate quantities, and is maintained and updated throughout the operational life.
  • Parts II and III added before recycling — Part II for operationally generated wastes and Part III for stores — and the whole then verified.
  • Control of listed hazardous materials. Appendix 1 prohibits or restricts asbestos (new installation prohibited on all ships), ozone-depleting substances, and polychlorinated biphenyls, and controls anti-fouling compounds and systems by reference to the AFS Convention as in force at the time of application — so later additions to the AFS list are caught automatically rather than being named here.
  • Existing ships must comply as far as practicable not later than five years after entry into force, or before going for recycling if that is earlier. Applied to 26 June 2025 that gives an inventory deadline of 26 June 2030, which is a date the industry can already plan to.

3. Survey, certification and the last voyage

The certification regime follows the familiar statutory pattern, with one addition at the end of the ship's life:

  • an initial survey before the ship is put in service or before the certificate is issued, verifying Part I of the Inventory;
  • a renewal survey at intervals set by the Administration not exceeding five years;
  • an additional survey, general or partial, after a change, replacement or significant repair;
  • and a final survey before the ship is taken out of service and before recycling starts. This one verifies three things: that the Inventory is complete in all three parts; that the Ship Recycling Plan properly reflects it and covers Safe-for-entry and Safe-for-hot work conditions; and that the yard where she is to be recycled holds a valid authorisation.

On the strength of the initial or renewal survey the ship carries an International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials; on the strength of the final survey she is certified as ready for recycling before any recycling activity takes place.

The last voyage itself is regulated. A ship destined for recycling must go only to an authorised facility, must minimise cargo residues, remaining fuel oil and wastes on board beforehand, and, if a tanker, must arrive with cargo tanks and pump rooms in a condition ready for certification as Safe-for-entry or Safe-for-hot work or both. She must give the yard all available information for the ship-specific Ship Recycling Plan, which the yard develops and the Competent Authority approves either explicitly or tacitly — tacit approval running a fourteen-day review period from a written acknowledgement issued within three working days.

4. What it will require of the recycling facility

This is the feature that makes the Convention unlike almost any other IMO instrument, and it deserves saying plainly: an IMO convention that reaches ashore and regulates a facility on land. It is also why the instrument took sixteen years to enter into force.

  • Each Party must legislate, establish an authorisation mechanism, and establish inspection, monitoring and enforcement provisions including powers of entry and sampling, and designate one or more Competent Authorities.
  • Authorisation of the yard by the Competent Authority, following verification of documentation and a site inspection, evidenced by a Document of Authorisation to conduct Ship Recycling and valid for a period not exceeding five years. If a yard refuses inspection, the authorisation is suspended or withdrawn.
  • A Ship Recycling Facility Plan, adopted by the board or governing body of the recycling company, covering worker safety and health policy, a system for implementation and continuous improvement, roles and responsibilities, worker information and training, an emergency preparedness and response plan, performance monitoring, record-keeping, and reporting of discharges, emissions, incidents, accidents and occupational disease.
  • Safe-for-entry and Safe-for-hot work conditions established, maintained and monitored throughout the recycling, in ship spaces including confined and enclosed spaces.
  • Hazardous materials identified, labelled, packaged and removed to the maximum extent possible prior to cutting, by properly trained and equipped workers — hazardous liquids and sediments, heavy metals, flammable or toxic coatings, asbestos, PCBs, CFCs and halons.
  • Controlled downstream management: wastes kept separate from recyclable material, labelled, stored safely, and transferred only to a waste management facility authorised to treat and dispose of them. This is where the real burden falls.

Enforcement is built in. Article 10 requires sanctions under national law, adequate in severity to discourage violations wherever they occur; Article 11 protects a ship against undue delay or detention, with compensation; and Article 12 requires each Party to report its list of authorised facilities and its competent-authority contact point to IMO for dissemination.

5. India's position and domestic implementation

  • India is one of the world's principal ship-recycling States, centred on the Alang-Sosiya yards in Gujarat. Published figures for its share of world recycling differ widely between sources and none is quoted here.
  • India acceded on 28 November 2019, the Director General of Shipping depositing the instrument at the thirty-first session of the IMO Assembly. That accession was decisive rather than symbolic: it took the count to the required fifteen States and contributed heavily to the recycling-volume condition, which was the condition no group of flag States could satisfy.
  • Domestically the Convention is given effect by the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 and the Recycling of Ships Rules, 2019, which apply Convention standards to ships recycled in India. India therefore legislated some six years ahead of entry into force, and its yards have been upgrading against that standard since.
  • The maritime administration is the competent authority, authorising yards and dealing with ship recycling plans.
  • Note the statutory architecture: Indian ship recycling is governed by its own separate Act, not by the general merchant shipping legislation. The general statute standing alongside it at this examination is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

6. Benefit, impact, and the balance

The stem says ‘benefit or be impacted’. That is not an invitation to choose; it is an instruction to argue both sides and reach a view.

How India stands to benefit

  • Legitimacy, and access to better tonnage. Owners who have avoided South Asian yards on reputational grounds will be able to send ships to an authorised Indian facility with a defensible answer, which should raise both the volume and the quality of tonnage arriving.
  • A level playing field. Once the Convention bites, competing recycling States must meet the same standard. The money Indian yards spent upgrading ahead of entry into force is then rewarded rather than punished on price.
  • Worker safety and environmental gain. Enforceable Safe-for-entry and Safe-for-hot work requirements, training, protective equipment and controlled waste handling address directly the fatality and contamination record that has defined the industry's reputation.
  • Downstream industry, investment and financeability. Formalisation supports the steel re-rolling and second-hand equipment trades, and a certified yard is easier to finance and to contract with on longer terms.
  • First-mover advantage. Because India legislated in 2019 and the Convention begins only in June 2025, Indian yards reach the start line with six years of compliance investment already made.

How it stands to be impacted

  • Cost of compliance. Impermeable floors, containment, lifting equipment, and hazardous-waste storage and treatment are capital-intensive. Smaller operators may not be able to afford them, so the industry consolidates and some yards close.
  • Downstream waste capacity is the binding constraint. The obligations do not end at the yard gate: treatment and disposal capacity for asbestos and other hazardous waste must exist, or compliance becomes documentary rather than real.
  • Price and leakage. Higher costs mean a lower price per light displacement tonne offered to owners, which creates an incentive to recycle elsewhere — including by changing flag before the last voyage to a State outside the Convention. Article 3.4's no-more-favourable-treatment rule limits this but does not close it.
  • Convention compliance is not European acceptance. The Convention does not expressly prohibit beaching; it regulates how recycling is done, not how the ship is brought ashore. The EU Ship Recycling Regulation maintains its own list of approved facilities on stricter terms, so an Indian yard may be fully Convention-compliant and still not be EU-listed.
  • Enforcement is the credibility test. Authorisation has to be inspected and enforced. A documentary-only regime would incur the cost and forfeit the reputational gain that is the main prize.

The balance. On the position as it stood at this examination, entry into force on 26 June 2025 is net positive for India. It converts an early and expensive investment into a competitive advantage, legitimises an industry that has been under external criticism for decades, and gives Indian yards a head start over competitors who have not yet spent the money. The gain is conditional, and honestly so: it depends on downstream waste infrastructure and on real enforcement keeping pace with certification. Without those, the cost is incurred and the reputational benefit is not.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three tasks are packed into one running stem and candidates routinely answer only the first. Budget the paper deliberately: roughly a third on the Convention and two thirds on India, split between benefit and impact. The command is evaluative, and evaluation questions are marked on whether a reasoned conclusion actually appears — so leave room for the final paragraph and do not let the machinery recital eat it.

One structural decision does more work than any other here: state the status in the first three lines. If the examiner reads ‘adopted May 2009, conditions met, enters into force 26 June 2025, not yet in force’ at the top of the answer, everything that follows is read as correctly tensed. If the status appears only at the end, or not at all, the whole script reads as though it were describing a live regime.

The date is the question — and this is the hardest kind of temporal trap

This topic has a hard boundary running through it, and at this sitting the boundary lies ahead rather than behind. Until 26 June 2025 the Convention was adopted but not in force: there were no operative obligations on ships or yards to describe, no certificates being issued under it, no yards authorised under it. From that date every one of those things can and must be stated.

The trap is that almost everything written about this Convention was written after it entered into force. A candidate revising from current material, or an author working from a later worked answer, will find the whole subject expressed in the present tense. Converting it back is not a matter of adding ‘will’ in a few places — it changes what the answer is: a description of a regime coming, with a fixed date and a preparation problem, rather than a description of a regime operating. That difference is worth several marks, and it is exactly what an October 2024 examiner was testing.

The corollary is a statistic to leave out. The figure of 24 Parties representing about 57 per cent of world tonnage is the position at entry into force. It is quoted everywhere, and at this sitting it is an anachronism. Say that the conditions were met and the date was fixed; do not quote a count.

The distinction that separates a good answer from a very good one

The Convention does not ban beaching. It requires safe and environmentally sound recycling, authorisation, planning and control — but it does not prescribe the method by which a ship is brought ashore. The EU Ship Recycling Regulation takes a stricter line and maintains its own list of approved facilities, which is why the acceptability of the Alang yards to European owners has been contested for years and is not settled by entry into force. So the accurate proposition is: Convention compliance is necessary for legitimacy but is not sufficient for European acceptance. A candidate who writes that the Convention has ended beaching, or that compliance automatically opens the EU market, has overstated it in exactly the way the examiner is watching for.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the whole answer in the present tense. At this sitting nothing in the Convention was in force. This is the single biggest error available.
  • Quoting the 24 Parties / 57 per cent figure, or any Party count. That is the position at entry into force, which had not happened.
  • Giving the adoption year as the operative date. Adopted 2009, in force 26 June 2025 — the gap is the interesting part and Article 17 explains it.
  • Reciting only the ship-side obligations and omitting the facility obligations, which are half the Convention and the half that made it hard to bring into force.
  • Confusing the ICIHM, held through service life, with certification as ready for recycling, which follows the final survey immediately before recycling.
  • Saying the Convention bans beaching, or that compliance automatically opens the EU market.
  • Attributing Indian ship recycling law to the merchant shipping statute of any year. It is the separate Recycling of Ships Act, 2019.
  • Naming the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. At this examination it did not exist even as a Bill.
  • Quoting a percentage share of world recycling for India. Published figures vary widely; name Alang-Sosiya instead.
  • Answering only the benefits when the stem says ‘benefit or be impacted’, and giving no concluding judgement.

Examiner traps

‘Is it in force?’ Not at this examination. Adopted May 2009; Article 17 conditions met; enters into force 26 June 2025, eight months after this paper. This is the discriminating answer and the examiner is asking it precisely because most candidates will have read post-2025 material.

‘Why did it take sixteen years?’ Article 17's three conditions, and specifically the 3 per cent recycling-volume threshold, which required the recycling States themselves to join. A tonnage threshold alone could have been satisfied by flag States.

‘Your ship goes for recycling next month. What must be in place?’ A completed Inventory in all three parts, a final survey, certification as ready for recycling, and a ship-specific Ship Recycling Plan approved by the Competent Authority for an authorised yard. Add that at this examination none of that was yet mandatory.

‘By when must an existing ship have an inventory?’ Five years after entry into force, or before going for recycling if earlier — so 26 June 2030. This is a legitimate future date to state even at this sitting, because the entry-into-force date was already fixed.

‘Which Indian statute governs ship recycling?’ The Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 with the Rules of 2019 — separate legislation with its own machinery. The general Act standing alongside it here is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

Model written answer

1. What the Convention is, and what it exists to do

The Convention on Facilitation of International Maritime Traffic, 1965 — the FAL Convention — was adopted on 9 April 1965 and entered into force on 5 March 1967. It is IMO's trade facilitation instrument, and it is the odd one out among the conventions a Chief Engineer meets. SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW tell you what the ship, her equipment and her people must be. FAL tells public authorities what they may demand of a ship when she arrives, stays and departs.

Its objectives, in the Organization's own words, are:

  1. to prevent unnecessary delays in maritime traffic;
  2. to aid co-operation between Governments; and
  3. to secure the highest practicable degree of uniformity in formalities and other procedures.

The problem it was written to solve was simple and expensive: every port demanded different documents, in a different format, in a different number of copies, and a ship could lose more time to paperwork than to cargo.

2. How it works

  • The obligations sit in an annex, expressed as Standards — measures necessary and practicable in order to facilitate international maritime traffic — and Recommended Practices, measures the application of which is desirable.
  • A Contracting Government that finds it impracticable to comply with a Standard, or that considers it necessary to adopt differing requirements, must notify the Secretary-General of the difference (article VIII). The obligation is therefore not absolute, but a departure must be declared rather than simply operated.
  • The annex prescribes a maximum, not a minimum. Under Standard 2.1bis public authorities shall not require more information than is provided for in the standardised declarations. This is the single most useful thing in the Convention from the ship's point of view.
  • Those declarations are the FAL Forms 1 to 7 — the General Declaration, Cargo Declaration, Ship's Stores Declaration, Crew's Effects Declaration, Crew List, Passenger List and Dangerous Goods manifest — together with the Declaration of Health and the documents required under the Universal Postal Convention.
  • The annex is amended by tacit acceptance: adopted under article VII(2)(a) and deemed accepted under article VII(2)(b) unless a stated proportion of Contracting Governments objects by a stated date. That mechanism is what makes the dates in step 4 matter.

3. Why it matters

  • Time is the cargo owner's money and the ship's cost. Port time is the least productive time a ship has; every hour saved at the interface is earned twice, once in charter hire and once in fuel.
  • Uniformity is what makes a ship tradeable worldwide. A master and crew cannot learn a different clearance regime for every port, and the FAL Forms mean they do not have to.
  • A ceiling on demands protects the ship from the port. Standard 2.1bis is a genuine legal protection, not an aspiration: it limits what an authority may lawfully require.
  • It is the foundation the digital layer was built on. Harmonising what is required had to come before automating how it is submitted, which is why the single window sits in this Convention and not in a new one.

4. The recent amendments - what is in force, and what is only adopted

This limb is relative to the examination date, and the distinction between adopted and in force is what the examiner is testing. State both, and say which is which.

Already mandatory before either amendment: the electronic exchange of information became mandatory from 9 April 2019, with a minimum twelve-month transitional period. That is the platform the single window was built onto.

IN FORCE at this sitting — resolution FAL.14(46). Adopted 13 May 2022 and, under the article VII(2)(b) tacit acceptance procedure, entered into force on 1 January 2024 — nine months before this paper. It is the amendment that matters, because it made the maritime single window mandatory:

  • Standard 1.3quin as amended — public authorities shall introduce arrangements enabling the provision of all information required under Standard 2.1 to a single window by electronic means, and shall consider the single window as the channel for communicating decisions.
  • The annex definition — a single window is ‘an environment that allows for the submission or provision of standardized and harmonized information and declarations to a single entry point, typically by electronic means’. Note standardized and harmonized: those two words are the test of whether a national portal is really a single window.

ADOPTED BUT NOT YET IN FORCE at this sitting — resolution FAL.15(47). Adopted 17 March 2023 under article VII(2)(a). Its operative paragraph determines, under article VII(2)(b), that the amendments enter into force on 1 January 2025 unless, prior to 1 October 2024, at least one third of Contracting Governments notify the Secretary-General in writing that they do not accept them. The amendments concern Recommended Practice 7.11, national facilitation programmes, taking into account the need to combat illicit activities.

So the position at this examination is unusually clean and worth stating in exactly these terms: one annex amendment recently in force, one adopted and waiting — with the waiting one's objection window closing at the very start of this examination month and its entry into force three months away.

One further distinction is worth a mark. Not every recent FAL resolution is an annex amendment. The Committee also adopts guidelines resolutions — for example the 2024 resolution on preventing wildlife smuggling. A guidelines resolution changes no Standard and no Recommended Practice, so however recently adopted it does not answer ‘recent amendments to the FAL Convention’. Approved is not adopted; adopted is not in force; and a guidelines resolution is not an annex amendment at all.

5. Impact - digitalization

  • One harmonised submission instead of many. Under the mandatory single window the ship, or her agent, submits the required information once, in an agreed format, to one entry point — and customs, immigration, port health, port authority and security all draw from it. The alternative, which is still the reality in some ports, is the same data typed several times into several systems.
  • A common data model underneath. The IMO Compendium on Facilitation and Electronic Business, developed with the World Customs Organization, UN/CEFACT and ISO, supplies the reference data model that makes ‘harmonized’ mean something in practice. Without it a single window is a single postbox for several incompatible letters.
  • Clearance comes off the critical path. Pre-arrival submission means the decisions are taken before the ship is on the berth, so the first hour alongside is a working hour. That is the operational point of the whole exercise.
  • But the duty falls on the public authority, and the ship's benefit is derivative. The obligation in Standard 1.3quin runs to Governments. Where a State implements well, the ship gains; where a State puts a web form in front of the same unharmonised demands, the ship gains almost nothing and cannot fix it.
  • And concentration has a cost. A single national entry point holding crew, cargo, security and commercial data is a high-value target and a single point of failure. Cyber resilience and a workable fallback procedure become part of port clearance planning rather than an IT afterthought.

6. Impact - sustainability

Be precise about the mechanism here, because this is the limb candidates handle worst. FAL sets no emission standard. It prescribes nothing about the ship, her fuel or her machinery. Its environmental benefit is indirect, and it runs through time.

  • Just-in-time arrival is the main effect. Reliable pre-arrival information exchange, and a clearance decision made before arrival, allow a ship to be given a firm berth time. She can then slow down on passage instead of steaming hard to an anchorage and waiting.
  • The saving is disproportionate to the speed reduction. Fuel consumption rises very steeply with speed, so a modest reduction over a long passage saves far more fuel than the same number of hours saved at the berth. Time saved in the port is converted into speed not made at sea, and that is where the carbon comes out.
  • Less congestion, less machinery running for no transport work. A ship at anchor waiting for a berth is burning fuel on generators, boilers and auxiliaries while performing no transport work at all — and, incidentally, scoring badly on any carbon-intensity metric that divides emissions by distance travelled.
  • The paper saving is real but small. Fewer printed sets, fewer courier movements, fewer launch trips to bring officials on board. Worth a sentence, not a paragraph — the fuel argument is the one that carries the marks.

7. The limits - what the Convention does not do

  • The benefit is conditional on implementation. A single window is an outcome, not a website. Where the same information is still demanded in several formats, the Convention has been complied with in form and defeated in substance.
  • Just-in-time needs the commercial contract to permit it. Laytime and demurrage terms frequently reward arriving early and waiting, and a charterer may be entitled to insist on utmost despatch. FAL removes a technical obstacle to slow steaming; it cannot remove a contractual one.
  • It imposes no requirement on the ship at all — no construction, equipment, manning or emission standard. It is not a safety or environmental convention and should not be described as one.
  • It does not override national customs, immigration, quarantine or security law. It harmonises the interface with those regimes; it does not replace them.
  • A Recommended Practice is not binding, and a notified difference is lawful. Uniformity under FAL is a strong presumption, not a guarantee, and a candidate who claims the Convention makes clearance identical worldwide has overstated it.

Four states, not two - approved, adopted, in force, and not-an-amendment

  • Approved — a Committee has agreed a text in principle. It binds nobody.
  • Adopted — the resolution exists and the tacit acceptance clock is running. It still binds nobody.
  • In force — the objection window has passed and the amendment has taken effect. Only now does it bind.
  • Not an annex amendment at all — a guidelines resolution, however recently adopted, changes no Standard and no Recommended Practice. This fourth category is the one candidates never think of, and naming it is worth a mark.

Common mistakes

  • Treating every amendment the candidate has heard of as being in force. At this sitting FAL.15(47) is not.
  • Describing FAL as a safety or environmental convention. It imposes no requirement on the ship at all.
  • Saying FAL reduces emissions. It sets no emission standard; the benefit runs through time saved and therefore through fuel.
  • Answering only digitalization and never reaching sustainability, when the stem names both.
  • Forgetting that Standard 2.1bis is a ceiling. Candidates routinely describe FAL as prescribing what must be submitted; it prescribes what may not be demanded.

Understand this first

Abandonment is not a shipwreck and it is not a strike. It is what happens when a commercial failure ashore leaves a crew stranded on an asset that nobody will pay for — and the reason it needed its own legal regime is that every ordinary remedy fails at once. The seafarer cannot sue an owner who has no money and often no traceable identity. He cannot go home, because he has no ticket, frequently no visa, and sometimes no passport. He cannot leave the ship, because leaving may forfeit the wage claim that is secured on her. And he cannot simply be rescued, because he is not in distress in any sense the salvage or safety conventions recognise: the ship is afloat, alongside, and perfectly safe. The crew are stranded rather than endangered, and no instrument written for danger reaches them.

That is the gap the 2014 amendments closed, and understanding what they did explains the whole shape of Standard A2.5.2. If the problem is that the owner has no money, then the money must come from somewhere other than the owner — hence compulsory financial security. If the problem is that the seafarer cannot enforce a claim, then he must be able to call on the security directly, without suing anybody — hence direct access. If the problem is delay, the assistance must be expedited and the certificate must be posted where the crew can read it, so they know who to call. Every element of the Standard is an answer to a specific way the old system failed, and an answer that shows that is worth far more than one that recites the paragraph.

Model written answer

(a) What abandonment is, and why the numbers keep rising — 8 marks

1. What abandonment means - the Convention's own definition

Abandonment is not a matter of impression. Since the 2014 amendments to the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006, which entered into force on 18 January 2017, it has had a hard legal definition. Standard A2.5.2, paragraph 2 provides that a seafarer shall be deemed to have been abandoned where, in violation of the requirements of the Convention or the terms of the seafarers' employment agreement, the shipowner:

  1. fails to cover the cost of the seafarer's repatriation; or
  2. has left the seafarer without the necessary maintenance and support; or
  3. has otherwise unilaterally severed their ties with the seafarer, including failure to pay contractual wages for a period of at least two months.

Two features of that definition are worth marks in themselves. First, ‘necessary maintenance and support’ is itself defined — it includes adequate food, accommodation, drinking water supplies, essential fuel for survival on board the ship, and necessary medical care. That last item shows the drafters understood the problem: a ship with no bunkers has no power, no cooling, no fresh water and no lighting. Second, the two-month wage test converts a subjective impression into a legal trigger. Before 2017 a crew could be visibly abandoned and have nothing to point to; now they have a date.

2. What the seafarer actually suffers, and why it shapes the regime

It is worth a short paragraph, because it explains why the remedy takes the form it does. An abandoned seafarer is stranded rather than endangered, and that is the whole difficulty:

  • he is unpaid for months, with a family dependent on remittances that have stopped;
  • he cannot go home — no ticket, frequently no visa, and sometimes no passport;
  • food, water, fuel and medical supplies run out on a ship with no operating budget;
  • he often cannot leave the ship even if he could get ashore, because his wage claim is secured on her and walking away may forfeit it;
  • and no safety instrument reaches him. The ship is afloat, alongside and perfectly safe. He is not in distress in any sense the safety or salvage conventions recognise.

Every ordinary remedy fails at the same moment. He cannot sue an owner who has no money and often no traceable identity; he cannot be rescued, because he is not in danger; and he cannot wait, because the food is running out. That is the gap the financial security regime was written to close, and it is why the Standard insists on direct access and expedited assistance rather than a claims process.

3. Why the numbers keep rising - the causes

The stem asks for causes, and they are commercial and structural rather than accidental:

  • Thin capitalisation in a cyclical trade. Marginal owners operate on narrow margins and short-term charters; one bad fixture, one long off-hire or one unexpected repair can end the business, and the ship is where the failure lands.
  • Single-ship company structures. Each vessel is owned by its own company, so a failure is ring-fenced: the loss stops at that ship and the beneficial owner's other assets are untouched. It is lawful, ordinary and it is also exactly what makes walking away cheap.
  • Registers with weak inspection, and opaque beneficial ownership. Where a flag State does not inspect and the real owner is hard to identify behind managers and nominees, there is little to deter abandonment and little to pursue afterwards.
  • Old tonnage at the end of its life. When a ship is worth little more than her scrap value, the cost of lay-up, repair or a proper recycling sale can exceed what she will fetch — and abandoning her becomes the cheapest option available to a failing owner.
  • Arrest, detention and sanctions. A ship arrested for debt, detained for deficiencies, or caught by sanctions can be frozen in place for months with the crew still aboard, and the owner may simply stop funding her.
  • Long charter and manning chains. Between the seafarer and whoever really controls the ship there may be a manning agent, a manager, a bareboat charterer and a time charterer. The crew's employer is often the entity with the least money in the chain.
  • War, sanctions and port closures stranding ships in places where no buyer, no charterer and no bank will engage.
  • And better reporting. Part of the rise is real and part of it is visibility: the joint IMO/ILO database records more cases because more are being looked for, reported and followed up. That does not diminish the problem, but an accurate answer says so.

(b) The role of the ILO and the ITF, and the provisions within the MLC — 8 marks

4. The MLC financial security regime - the operative answer

This is where the marks in limb (b) mostly are, because it is the part that is law. Regulation 2.5, paragraph 2 requires ships to carry financial security to assist seafarers in the event of abandonment, and Standard A2.5.2 sets out what that means.

  • Every flag State must ensure a system is in place for ships flying its flag. It may take the form of a social security scheme, insurance, a national fund or a similar arrangement, its form settled after consultation with shipowners' and seafarers' organisations. In practice the provider is usually a P&I club.
  • The system must provide direct access, sufficient coverage and expedited financial assistance. Those three words are the heart of it: the seafarer calls on the security himself, he does not have to sue anybody, and it must be quick.
  • A certificate or other documentary evidence of financial security must be carried on board, containing the information required by Appendix A2-I, in English or with an English translation, and a copy posted in a conspicuous place available to the seafarers. Where more than one provider gives cover, each provider's document is carried.
  • What it must cover: outstanding wages and other entitlements due under the employment agreement, the collective agreement or national law — limited to four months of wages and four months of entitlements; all expenses reasonably incurred by the seafarer, including repatriation; and essential needs — food, clothing where necessary, accommodation, drinking water, essential fuel for survival on board and medical care — from the act or omission constituting the abandonment until the seafarer's arrival at home.
  • Repatriation is by appropriate and expeditious means, normally by air, with food, accommodation, medical care, passage and transport of personal effects.
  • The security cannot simply lapse. It may not cease before the end of its period of validity unless the provider has given at least 30 days' prior notification to the competent authority of the flag State.
  • The provider is subrogated to the seafarer's rights up to the amount paid, and the regime is expressly not exclusive of any other rights, claims or remedies.
  • And it follows the flag, not the port. The provider must give the agreed assistance wherever the abandonment takes place, including in the territory of a State that has not ratified the Convention. That matters, because abandonment concentrates in exactly the ports least likely to be bound by it.

Behind that sits Standard A2.5 on repatriation itself, which supplies the backstop: where the shipowner fails, the flag State shall arrange the repatriation and may recover the cost from the shipowner — and a State which has paid may detain, or request the detention of, the ships of that shipowner until it is reimbursed. Two propositions in it are worth quoting: the expenses shall in no case be a charge upon the seafarer, and a Member shall not refuse the right of repatriation because of the financial circumstances of a shipowner or the shipowner's inability or unwillingness to replace a seafarer.

5. The role of the ILO

  • The ILO is the source of the law. The MLC is an ILO Convention, adopted by the International Labour Conference in 2006 and in force since 20 August 2013.
  • Its method is tripartite. Governments, shipowners and seafarers negotiate the text together, which is why the Convention carries the authority it does with the industry as well as with States.
  • The Special Tripartite Committee amends the Code by a simplified procedure — and the 2014 amendments, which created this entire abandonment regime, were its work. Before them the Convention did not directly address abandonment at all.
  • It supervises. Member States report; the ILO's supervisory machinery, including the Committee of Experts, examines those reports; and complaints procedures exist where a State is not applying a ratified Convention.
  • It works jointly with IMO on precisely this problem. The Joint IMO/ILO Ad Hoc Expert Working Group on Liability and Compensation regarding Claims for Death, Personal Injury and Abandonment of Seafarers is the body that identified the gap in the international legal regime in the first place, and the joint Guidelines on Provision of Financial Security in Cases of Abandonment of Seafarers — adopted by both the IMO Assembly and the ILO Governing Body — were the voluntary precursor of the mandatory regime that followed.

6. The role of the ITF

The International Transport Workers' Federation is a global federation of transport workers' trade unions. Be precise about what that means: it is industrial, not regulatory. It has no legal authority over a shipowner and it does not enforce the Convention. What it has is presence, money and leverage.

  • An inspectorate in ports worldwide. ITF inspectors board ships, talk to crews and check wages and conditions — and are very often the first people to identify an abandonment, because they are the only outsiders who routinely go aboard and ask.
  • Reporting. Cases found are reported into the machinery, including the joint database, which is part of why the recorded numbers have risen.
  • Collective bargaining agreements. The ITF negotiates agreements fixing wages and conditions, so there is an agreed figure against which arrears can be quantified — which matters when a claim has to be proved.
  • Pursuit of unpaid wages, including support for the arrest of the ship to secure the crew's claim, and legal assistance in doing it.
  • The flag-of-convenience campaign, which targets exactly the registers where abandonment concentrates.
  • Practical relief — food, water, funds, communications and welfare support while the formal machinery grinds. In a real abandonment this is often what actually keeps a crew fed in the first weeks.

7. What the regime still does not solve

An answer that stops at ‘there is now financial security’ has not finished. The regime is a large improvement and it is not complete:

  • The four-month cap can be less than what is owed. Where wages have been unpaid for eight months, four months of them are not covered.
  • It answers the wage and repatriation claim, not the wider loss — the career interrupted, the months of the seafarer's life, the family debt incurred meanwhile.
  • An uninsured or falsely certificated ship leaves the crew outside the system entirely. The certificate proves cover only if the cover is real.
  • Non-party flags and lapsed cover. A ship that falls out of class and out of insurance at the same time — which is a common pattern in a failing company — may have no security behind her at the moment she needs it.
  • Detention, arrest and sanctions can keep a crew aboard even when the money is available, because a port State may not release a ship or its crew until other matters are resolved.
  • Crews sometimes stay voluntarily, to protect a wage claim secured on the ship. A remedy the seafarer is reluctant to use is only a partial remedy.
  • And the flag State backstop is only as good as the flag State. The duty to repatriate falls on the same administrations whose weak oversight allowed the situation in the first place.

Define it, do not describe it

This is the single decision that separates a good script from an average one. An average answer says abandonment is when a crew is left on a ship without pay or supplies, which is true and worth almost nothing. A good answer gives Standard A2.5.2 paragraph 2: the shipowner fails to cover repatriation, or leaves the seafarer without necessary maintenance and support, or unilaterally severs ties — including failure to pay contractual wages for at least two months.

The two-month test is the detail to hold above all others. And add the definition-within-the-definition: necessary maintenance and support includes food, accommodation, drinking water, essential fuel for survival on board and medical care.

Examiner traps

‘Your wages are two months late. Are you abandoned?’ Not automatically. Limb (c) of the definition is unilateral severance of ties, including failure to pay wages for at least two months — the two months is the qualifying period for that limb, not a switch that operates by itself. But it is the point at which the question becomes a legal one, and at which the crew should be raising it formally.

‘Where is the certificate and what does it tell you?’ Carried on board and posted in a conspicuous place available to the seafarers, with the Appendix A2-I information, in English or with an English translation. It tells the crew who the provider is and how to contact them — which is the entire practical value of it.

‘The ship is abandoned in a State that has not ratified the MLC. Are you covered?’ Yes, if the flag State is a party and the security is in place. The obligation follows the flag, and the provider must assist wherever the abandonment takes place.

‘Who pays if there is no security at all?’ The flag State must arrange repatriation and may recover the cost from the shipowner, and a State which has paid may detain that owner's ships until reimbursed. The expenses shall in no case be a charge upon the seafarer.

‘What can the ITF actually do for you?’ Find you, report the case, quantify the arrears against a collective agreement, help arrest the ship to secure the claim, and feed you meanwhile. What it cannot do is order the owner to pay.

  • Which provision requires financial security? Regulation 2.5 paragraph 2, implemented by Standard A2.5.2.
  • When did it come into force? 18 January 2017, with the 2014 amendments.
  • What is the limit on wages covered? Four months of outstanding wages and four months of outstanding entitlements.

Model written answer

1. Why propeller efficiency is worth pursuing

The propeller is the final energy conversion in the propulsion chain and one of the least efficient links in it: a large proportion of the delivered power never becomes useful thrust. Because resistance rises steeply with speed, a modest improvement in propulsive efficiency releases a disproportionate saving in fuel and therefore in emissions.

The stem supplies its own framing — ‘improving efficiency of propellers is recognized as a growing means to improve energy efficiency of ships’ — and that framing is sound at this sitting. The 2021 revised MARPOL Annex VI has been in force since 1 November 2022, and it bites in two directions at once: a propeller change reduces the attained EEXI (regulation 23) against the required EEXI (regulation 25), which is a design-index requirement assessed once; and the same change improves the attained operational carbon intensity under regulation 28, which is assessed every year. Very few measures do both. And unlike reducing speed — the other large lever — a propeller change costs the operator no time.

2. Where the losses occur, and the ceiling on recovering them

A useful answer explains what each device is attacking before describing it. Overall propulsive efficiency is the product of hull efficiency, open-water (propeller) efficiency and relative rotative efficiency. The devices in this question act chiefly on the second, where the energy is lost in four ways:

  • Axial or slipstream loss. The propeller works by accelerating water aft; the kinetic energy left in that accelerated column is lost. It falls as the disc area is increased and the acceleration reduced.
  • Rotational loss. A single propeller leaves the race rotating. That swirl carries energy which does nothing to propel the ship, and it is the largest single recoverable loss in a conventional installation.
  • Frictional loss. Viscous drag over the blade surfaces — irreducible, and worsened by roughness and fouling.
  • Tip vortex loss. At the blade tip the pressure difference between face and back equalises around the edge, shedding a strong vortex which carries away energy and is a principal source of pressure pulses, hull vibration and cavitation erosion.

Cavitation sets the ceiling. Blade loading cannot simply be increased, because local pressure falling to vapour pressure produces cavities whose collapse causes erosion, vibration and radiated noise. Every device below is an attempt to recover one of these losses without breaching that ceiling: the duct attacks axial and tip loss, Kappel attacks tip vortex loss, contra-rotation attacks rotational loss, and azimuth propulsion attacks the quality of the water arriving at the disc.

3. Ducted propellers

Principle. The propeller runs inside an annular aerofoil — a nozzle, the classic form being the Kort nozzle. In an accelerating duct the internal section converges, so flow is accelerated into the disc; each blade then has to generate less thrust for the same total, and the duct itself, being a lifting surface with circulation, produces additional forward thrust. The close tip clearance also largely suppresses the tip vortex. A decelerating duct reverses the section to raise pressure at the disc and suppress cavitation and radiated noise — mainly a naval application.

The governing parameter is thrust loading. The duct earns its keep where the propeller is heavily loaded at low speed: tugs, trawlers, pushers, and slow full-form ships whose diameter is limited by draught.

  • Merits. Substantial thrust augmentation at high loading, which is why bollard-pull vessels are almost universally ducted; improved efficiency where the propeller would otherwise be overloaded; suppression of the tip vortex and its associated erosion; physical protection of the propeller from debris and quay contact; improved course stability with a fixed nozzle; and, with a steerable nozzle, a genuine manoeuvring gain that can remove the need for a separate rudder.
  • Demerits. The duct is an appendage with its own drag, and at light loading and higher speed that drag exceeds the benefit — a duct can make a fast, lightly loaded ship worse. It fouls and corrodes, and marine growth inside the nozzle degrades performance quickly; it can be damaged or blocked by debris or ice; internal cavitation erosion occurs on the inner surface and at the tip gap; clearances are critical and alignment must be maintained; it adds weight and structure; and it complicates survey, inspection and propeller removal in dry dock.

4. Kappel propellers

Principle. A Kappel propeller is a conventional-diameter propeller whose blade tips are curved smoothly towards the suction side, forming an integrated, continuous winglet rather than a bolted-on end plate. The curvature allows blade loading to be carried further outboard while the tip vortex is weakened and displaced away from the blade. The aeronautical analogy is exact: it is the marine equivalent of an aircraft winglet, and it recovers energy from the same mechanism.

  • Merits. It reduces tip vortex loss for the same diameter, which matters when draught or aperture fixes the diameter. There is no additional appendage, so no added drag, no added fouling surface and no new maintenance item. The weakened tip vortex produces a more uniform wake into the rudder, reducing pressure pulses, hull vibration and radiated noise — a real habitability and structural benefit as well as an efficiency one. And it is retrofittable as a straight propeller replacement at a scheduled dry docking.
  • Demerits. The gain is modest and is highly dependent on the hull wake field, so the blade must be designed for the specific ship — which means CFD and model testing, and a high design cost that is hard to justify on a single vessel. The curved tip geometry is complex to cast, dearer than a conventional blade, and more difficult to repair, straighten or re-pitch after damage; the tip is also relatively vulnerable. And the benefit is design-point dependent: a ship trading persistently away from the draught and speed for which the blade was designed may realise little of it.

5. Contra-rotating propellers

Principle. Two propellers are mounted coaxially on the same axis and turn in opposite directions. The forward propeller leaves a rotating race; the after propeller, turning the other way, is designed to recover that rotational energy and convert it into additional thrust. Because rotational loss is the largest single recoverable loss in a conventional installation, this attacks the biggest target directly. Mechanically it requires either concentric inner and outer shafting with epicyclic gearing, or a podded after unit driven electrically in the hybrid arrangement.

  • Merits. The highest theoretical efficiency gain of the four, because it recovers swirl rather than merely avoiding a loss. The total thrust is divided between two propellers, so loading per propeller falls — which reduces cavitation risk, pressure pulses and induced vibration, and permits a smaller diameter for a given thrust, valuable where draught constrains the aperture. Torque reaction is largely cancelled, improving course keeping and reducing rudder correction.
  • Demerits. The objection is mechanical, and it is serious. The arrangement demands concentric inner and outer shafting with complex reduction and reversing gearing, and the sealing of two concentric shafts at the stern tube is a recognised reliability weak point. Capital cost is high; alignment, maintenance and dry-dock withdrawal are all substantially harder; there are more components to fail in a system whose failure immobilises the ship. Service experience in the large merchant fleet remains limited, so spares support and operator confidence lag the theory — which, more than the engineering, is why adoption has been slow.

6. Azimuth propulsion

Principle — and note first that this item is a different kind of answer from the other three. Ducted, Kappel and contra-rotating are propeller or duct designs. Azimuth propulsion is a propulsion arrangement: a thruster unit rotatable through 360 degrees, producing thrust in any direction, in mechanical Z-drive or L-drive form with bevel gearing, or as an electrically driven pod with the motor inside the underwater housing. In the pulling or tractor configuration the propeller is ahead of its own housing and strut, so it works in undisturbed flow rather than in the disturbed wake behind them.

  • Merits. Inflow quality — the tractor arrangement gives a clean, uniform inflow, which raises open-water efficiency and reduces the wake non-uniformity that drives cavitation and pressure pulses. No shaft line, no rudder, no stern tube, which removes shafting losses and stern tube sealing, frees the after end for cargo or machinery, and allows the prime movers to be placed where they suit the ship. Outstanding manoeuvring: full thrust in any direction, so astern power without reversing the engine, and station-keeping and dynamic positioning capability that a shaft-and-rudder ship cannot approach. With diesel-electric drive the engines run at their own most efficient load independent of ship speed, and surplus sets can be shut down.
  • Demerits. The housing and strut are appendages with their own drag, which offsets part of the inflow gain, and the transmission or electrical conversion chain adds losses that a direct-coupled shaft line does not have. Capital cost is high and the unit is complex. Maintenance is the real objection: the whole unit generally must be withdrawn in dry dock, and the steering gear, slip rings, seals and thrust bearings inside the pod are new failure modes with limited redundancy. Fleet familiarity, spares support and repair capability are thinner than for conventional shafting, and on a straightforward deep-sea trade the manoeuvring advantage — which is the main prize — is worth very little.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four devices, sixteen marks, and a three-part demand on each — principle, merits, demerits. That is about four marks per device and it is tight. The structure that makes it fit is to establish the four loss mechanisms once, in a short block, and then open each device section with the loss it attacks. Done that way, each device needs only a compact principle and two short lists, and the answer is complete.

The alternative — a long theoretical preamble on propulsive efficiency followed by three devices in loving detail and the fourth in a sentence — is the commonest failure, and it is a planning failure rather than a knowledge failure. Decide the four-way split before you start writing.

The three words that change this question

An earlier sitting printed ‘Discuss any three of the following high-efficiency propellers’. This paper does not. It prints ‘Discuss the following’, and the three missing words move about four marks from optional to compulsory.

This matters for revision as much as for the examination room. A candidate working from a worked answer to the earlier sitting will have learned three devices thoroughly and will have seen azimuth propulsion demoted to an appendix — along with advice, entirely correct for that paper, that three treated properly beats four treated superficially. Read the stem, not the note. The general lesson is worth more than this one question: an examiner who recycles a stem often edits it, and the edit is where the marks move.

Why azimuth propulsion is the odd one out

Three of the four items are designs — changes to the blade or a duct around it. Azimuth propulsion is an arrangement, and its efficiency argument is correspondingly different. Very little of its gain comes from the blade: it comes from inflow quality in the tractor configuration, from deleting the shaft line, rudder and stern tube, and, with diesel-electric drive, from decoupling engine load from ship speed. Saying that explicitly shows the examiner you read the list rather than pattern-matched it, and it opens the manoeuvring and dynamic-positioning merits that no blade design can claim.

It also has the most honest set of demerits, and they are worth stating properly: the housing and strut are appendages with drag, the transmission chain adds losses, and the maintenance objection is real — the unit generally comes off in dry dock, and the seals, slip rings and steering gear inside it are failure modes a shaft line does not have.

Common mistakes

  • Answering only three devices. This sitting requires four.
  • Describing what each device looks like instead of naming the loss it recovers.
  • Giving merits only. The stem asks for merits and demerits, so half the marks are in the second list.
  • Treating a device as high-efficiency in the abstract. A duct makes a fast, lightly loaded ship worse.
  • Quoting percentage gains. They are vendor-sourced and hull-specific.
  • Naming manufacturers or products. Kappel is named only because the stem names it.
  • Forgetting cavitation as the ceiling. Without it, the answer implies blade loading could simply be increased.
  • Using the pre-2021 MARPOL Annex VI numbering in the framing paragraph. Regulation 21 is functional requirements; attained EEXI is 23, required EEXI 25 and operational CII 28.
  • Confusing contra-rotating (two propellers on one axis turning opposite ways) with twin screw (two independent shafts) or with a counter-rotating pod behind a conventional propeller.

Examiner traps

‘Would you fit a nozzle to a container ship?’ No. Thrust loading is low and speed is high, so the duct's own drag would exceed any benefit. This is the single best question in the topic because it tests whether the candidate understands the condition rather than the device.

‘Contra-rotating gives the biggest gain. Why is your ship not fitted with it?’ Because the gain is bought with concentric shafting, complex gearing and a stern tube seal on two concentric shafts — a recognised reliability weak point — at high capital cost, with harder alignment and dry-dock withdrawal and limited merchant service experience.

‘What does a winglet on a propeller actually do?’ It weakens and displaces the tip vortex, allowing load to be carried further outboard for the same diameter. The same physics as an aircraft winglet.

‘You have fitted a new propeller. What happens to your engine?’ The propeller demand curve moves, and the engine's operating point moves with it — which is why this question and the propeller-demand-curve question on the same paper are two halves of one topic. A propeller that absorbs more torque at a given speed makes the engine run heavy.

‘How much fuel will it save?’ The correct answer is that it depends on hull form, wake field, loading and trade, and that any single figure is manufacturer-sourced. Saying why the number cannot be given scores better than giving one.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What are the components of propulsive efficiency? Hull efficiency, open-water efficiency and relative rotative efficiency.
  • Which loss is the largest recoverable one? Rotational loss - the swirl left in the race.
  • What is a Kort nozzle, and what is the difference between an accelerating and a decelerating duct? An annular aerofoil around the propeller; the accelerating form converges to speed flow into the disc, the decelerating form raises pressure at the disc to suppress cavitation and noise.
  • On what parameter does the value of a duct depend? Thrust loading coefficient.
  • What is a Kappel propeller? A propeller with tips curved smoothly to the suction side as an integrated winglet, weakening the tip vortex.
  • How is a contra-rotating arrangement driven? Concentric inner and outer shafting with epicyclic gearing, or a podded after unit driven electrically.
  • What is the difference between a Z-drive and a pod? A Z-drive transmits mechanically through bevel gearing from an inboard engine; a pod carries the electric motor in the underwater housing.
  • What is the tractor configuration and why is it efficient? The propeller is forward of its own housing and strut, so it works in undisturbed flow.
  • Name one demerit of azimuth propulsion. Housing and strut drag; transmission or conversion losses; or the need to withdraw the unit in dry dock.
  • Why does cavitation matter to all of these? It sets the ceiling on blade loading, and its collapse causes erosion, vibration and noise.

Understand this first

The short-term measure is one policy with two halves, and almost every mark on this question comes from keeping them apart. EEXI is technical and it is assessed once: it asks what the ship is, on paper, against a reference line derived from ships built years ago. CII is operational and it is assessed every year: it asks what the ship actually did last year, and the target moves every January. A ship can pass EEXI comfortably and still be rated E, because a certificate about design says nothing about how she was traded.

The second thing to see is why most owners complied with EEXI by limiting power. The index uses a reference power in its numerator, so capping that power reduces the attained value arithmetically, immediately, and for the price of an engine-shop visit rather than a drydocking. It is a lawful and expressly contemplated route — and it makes the index better without making the ship more efficient. Once you have said that out loud, the criticism of AER in limb (b) is the same criticism wearing different clothes: a metric that can be improved without improving anything real is the recurring weakness of this whole regime, and a candidate who joins those two observations is answering at Class I level.

Model written answer

(a) Design features adopted to comply with EEXI — 8 marks

1. Where EEXI sits in law, and what the printed Phase 2 framing refers to

EEXI is the technical half of the IMO short-term GHG measure, introduced by the 2021 revised MARPOL Annex VI (resolution MEPC.328(76)), which was deemed accepted on 1 May 2022 and entered into force on 1 November 2022. Two regulations carry it:

  • Regulation 23 — attained EEXI. The attained value is calculated for each ship in the listed categories, is specific to the ship, is accompanied by an EEXI technical file containing the information necessary for the calculation and showing the process of calculation, and is verified on the basis of that file by the Administration or an organisation duly authorised by it. Where the ship already has a verified attained EEDI at or below the required EEXI, that value may be taken as the attained EEXI.
  • Regulation 25 — required EEXI. The requirement is Attained EEXI ≤ Required EEXI = (1 − Y/100) × EEDI reference line value, where Y is the reduction factor in Table 3.

Compliance is assessed once, against the design, at the first annual, intermediate or renewal IAPP survey on or after 1 January 2023, and the outcome is recorded on the International Energy Efficiency Certificate. It is a design index, not a performance measurement: the calculation uses reference conditions, not the ship's actual trading.

One point of precision on the printed stem. Read at source, EEXI is not phased. Regulation 25 Table 3 sets a single fixed reduction factor per ship type and size band — 20 per cent for a bulk carrier or tanker between 20,000 and 200,000 DWT and 15 per cent above that; 30 per cent for a gas carrier, general cargo ship or LNG carrier; 20 to 50 per cent for containerships by size; 5 per cent for ro-ro ships. The phases — Phase 0, 1, 2 and 3 on dated windows — belong to the required EEDI in regulation 24 Table 1. The two are connected, which is why the phase language attaches itself to EEXI in practice: the EEXI factors were derived from the EEDI reference lines at broadly phase 2 stringency, and for most merchant ship types they land in the 20 to 30 per cent band the question names. So the figure in the stem is right; the phase label belongs to EEDI.

2. The design measures that reduce the attained EEXI

The index is a ratio of CO2 emitted to transport work, so every measure either cuts the numerator or raises the denominator. Group them by where they act — a list organised by mechanism scores better than a list of product names.

  • Hull. Low-friction and foul-release coatings reduce frictional resistance, which is the dominant component for most full-form ships. Where a major conversion is being done anyway, bulbous bow replacement to suit the ship's actual operating draught and speed, and fairing of appendages, are available.
  • Propeller. Retrofit or redesign to the ship's real operating point, larger diameter turning more slowly where the aperture allows it, tip-modified blades, and propeller boss cap fins to recover hub vortex energy.
  • Energy saving devices. Pre-swirl ducts, stators and wake-equalising fins ahead of the propeller; rudder bulbs and twisted rudders behind it. Each conditions the flow into or out of the disc and each must be designed for the specific hull — none is universally worthwhile.
  • Machinery and heat. Waste heat recovery from exhaust gas; shaft generators to remove auxiliary engines from the load; variable-frequency drives on pumps and fans; efficient turbochargers and engine tuning to the new operating point.
  • Air lubrication where the hull form suits it, reducing frictional resistance by a carpet of microbubbles under the flat of bottom.
  • Wind assistance — rotor sails, rigid wing sails and kites — which the EEDI/EEXI calculation methodology expressly recognises through an availability factor.
  • Fuel. Because the numerator is CO2 and not fuel mass, moving to a fuel with a lower carbon conversion factor — LNG, methanol, a biofuel blend — reduces the attained value directly for the same energy delivered.

3. Power limitation - what most existing ships actually did

The honest answer to ‘what design features were adopted’ is that for the great majority of existing ships the compliance route was not a design change at all. The index is computed from a reference power, so capping that power reduces the attained EEXI arithmetically, immediately, and without a shipyard.

  • EPL — engine power limitation. A mechanical stop or a software limit on the fuel index or governor setting, restricting the maximum power the main engine can develop.
  • ShaPoLi — shaft power limitation. The limit is imposed and monitored on the shaft rather than on the engine, using a torsion meter, which suits electronically controlled and shaft-generator installations.
  • In both cases the limit is sealed and verified, and is overridable so that full power remains available for the safety of the ship in adverse conditions — with the override, its reason and its duration recorded and reported.

Its attractions are obvious: it is by far the cheapest route, needs no drydocking, and can be arranged between voyages. Its drawbacks are equally real and are what an examiner is looking for:

  • it caps sea speed, which is a commercial loss on trades where speed is sold;
  • it may conflict with charterparty speed and consumption warranties, so the limitation has to be disclosed and the description of the ship amended;
  • it reduces reserve power in heavy weather and, on some installations, the margin available for manoeuvring — which is precisely why the override exists;
  • and, most importantly, it improves the index without improving the ship. The vessel is no more efficient than she was; she is simply restrained. The regulation accepts that outcome deliberately, because the operational half of the measure is meant to pick up what the technical half cannot.

(b) The CII rating, AER and EEOI, and improving the rating — 8 marks

4. The CII rating - the mechanism

CII is regulation 28, the operational half. It applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above in the cargo and passenger categories listed in the regulation. Where EEXI is assessed once against the design, CII is assessed every year against how the ship was actually run.

  • Attained annual operational CII — calculated after the end of each calendar year over 1 January to 31 December, using the fuel oil consumption data already collected under regulation 27 (the IMO DCS, running since calendar year 2019), and reported to the Administration within three months of the year end.
  • Required annual operational CII — regulation 28.4: Required CII = (1 − Z/100) × CIIR, where CIIR is the reference value for the ship type on the 2019 reference line under the G2 guidelines.
  • Z, the annual reduction factor, is what makes the standard tighten. Under G3, resolution MEPC.338(76) Table 1, relative to the 2019 line:

Under regulation 28.6 the attained value is verified against the required value to give a rating of A, B, C, D or E — major superior, minor superior, moderate, minor inferior, inferior — and the middle point of rating level C is the required CII itself. The G4 guidelines place four boundaries around that midpoint, set on the 2019 fleet distribution so that the middle 30 per cent of a segment would have been C, the next 20 per cent either side B and D, and the outer 15 per cent either side A and E.

The consequences are in regulations 28.7 to 28.10. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E in any single year, must develop a plan of corrective actions; the SEEMP is reviewed to incorporate it and submitted for verification no later than one month after reporting the attained CII; and the ship must then duly undertake those actions. Administrations, port authorities and other stakeholders are encouraged to provide incentives to ships rated A or B. Note what the regulation does not do: there is no fine, no detention and no certificate withdrawn. The pressure is administrative, through the SEEMP, and commercial, through charterers, financiers and cargo owners who read the rating.

The position at this examination is worth stating. 2023 was the first calculation year and the attained value fell due for reporting by 31 March 2024, six months before this paper. The first CII ratings in the history of the measure have therefore already been assigned, and owners are now managing a real rating rather than an anticipated one. Regulation 28.11's review, due by 1 January 2026, had not reported at this sitting.

5. AER and EEOI critically compared

The G1 guidelines, resolution MEPC.352(78), draw the distinction the question is really about. A demand-based CII uses the actual mass or volume of cargo carried. A supply-based CII uses the ship's capacity as a proxy for cargo.

  • AER — the Annual Efficiency Ratio — is the supply-based CII using deadweight as the capacity: total CO2 for the year divided by (capacity × total distance travelled). The variant using gross tonnage is cgDIST, applied to cruise passenger ships, ro-ro cargo ships including vehicle carriers, and ro-ro passenger ships.
  • EEOI — the Energy Efficiency Operational Indicator — is demand-based: CO2 per tonne of cargo actually carried per mile, as defined in MEPC.1/Circ.684.

Their regulatory status is not equal, and this is the discriminating fact. AER (or cgDIST) is the metric regulation 28 is satisfied by. EEOI is not a regulatory metric at all: G1 section 5 lists it, with EEPI, cbDIST and clDIST, among indicators encouraged to be used for trial purposes. A candidate who presents them as two official alternatives has not read the guideline.

The critical case against AER is real and should be made:

  • It rewards distance, not cargo. A long ballast leg improves AER; days idle at anchor, burning fuel while travelling no distance, worsen it. Neither movement reflects transport efficiency.
  • It is blind to utilisation. Two identical ships, one full and one half empty, score identically if they burn the same fuel over the same distance — so the metric cannot reward the commercially efficient operator, and a truly efficient trade can be penalised.
  • It penalises short-sea and multi-port trades, where port time and manoeuvring are a large share of consumption and sea distance is small.
  • It can be gamed at the margin. Slow steaming with more ships lowers each ship's AER while the cargo moves no more efficiently overall — and, as with engine power limitation in limb (a), the index improves without the underlying efficiency improving.

The case for choosing it anyway is equally real, and stating it is what turns criticism into critical discussion. EEOI needs cargo mass data that the DCS does not collect, that is commercially confidential, that an Administration cannot verify in the way it verifies bunker and distance figures, and that is not comparable across trades — a car carrier, a container ship and a crude tanker cannot sensibly be ranked on tonne-miles. IMO therefore chose the metric that could be audited and enforced over the one that is conceptually correct. That trade-off — enforceability bought at the cost of validity — is the answer the examiner wants, and regulation 28.11's inclusion of the Z and CIIR values in its review is the Organization's own acknowledgement that the matter is not settled.

6. Measures to improve the rating, administered through the SEEMP

Because AER is fuel over capacity-distance, there are only two levers: burn less, or travel further for it. Operational measures dominate because they are available now and cost little.

  • Speed and voyage management. Consumption rises far faster than speed, so optimising speed to the required arrival time is the single largest lever. Just-in-time arrival converts waiting at anchor — which burns fuel and adds no distance — into a slower, cheaper passage.
  • Hull and propeller condition. In-water cleaning and propeller polishing scheduled against measured performance rather than against a calendar.
  • Trim, draught and ballast. Optimise trim against the ship's own trim tables or CFD-derived guidance, and carry no more ballast than is required.
  • Weather routeing to avoid added resistance, and autopilot tuning to reduce rudder-induced drag.
  • Auxiliary and port load. Shut down surplus generators, use the shaft generator, run pumps and fans on variable-frequency drives, manage cargo heating and inert gas use, control accommodation load, and take shore power where it is available.
  • Technical retrofit and fuel — the same measures as limb (a), which is the point at which the two halves of the measure meet: a device fitted for EEXI also improves the attained CII.
  • Data quality. The attained CII is only as good as the reported fuel and distance, so flowmeter calibration, consistent bunker figures and correct noon-report distances change the rating directly.
  • G5 voyage adjustments and correction factors, resolution MEPC.355(78), for defined cases such as ice conditions and ship-to-ship operations — used within their own stated limit, that their use should in no way undermine the goal of reducing carbon intensity.

All of it is administered through the SEEMP. Under regulation 26.3.1 an affected ship's SEEMP had to contain, on or before 1 January 2023, the CII calculation methodology and reporting process, the required CII for the next three years, an implementation plan documenting how it will be achieved, and a procedure for self-evaluation and improvement — and the SEEMP is subject to verification and company audit. Where a ship is rated D for three consecutive years or E, the corrective action plan goes into that same document. The examiner expects the measures above to live in the SEEMP, not in a loose list.

Understand this first

General average is older than marine insurance and is not insurance at all. It is a rule of contribution between the parties to one adventure: if the ship, the cargo and the freight are all in danger together and something belonging to one of them is deliberately given up to save them all, the loss is shared among all of them in proportion to what each had at stake. The idea is ancient and its logic is simple — nobody should be worse off for having been the one chosen to be sacrificed.

That is why the essential features are what they are, and why they are not really a list. Every one of them is a phrase of Rule A doing a job: extraordinary keeps ordinary voyage costs out; intentional separates a sacrifice from an accident; reasonable keeps panic out; peril keeps ordinary sea risk out; common ensures there is more than one interest to share; and preservation ensures there is something saved to contribute. Learn Rule A and you have the features.

Limb (b) then tests one thing only: can you tell a sacrifice from an accident? The engine damage is physically identical whether she was in danger or not. What changes the legal answer is the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked — and Rule VII says so in one sentence, with the converse attached.

Model written answer

(a) ‘General average is based on certain essential features’ — 8 marks

1. What a general average act is, and how the Rules apply

The statement in the question is correct, and the features are not a matter of opinion: they are drawn from the definition itself. Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules provides that there is a general average act ‘when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure’.

Note first how the Rules apply, because it is a point candidates routinely get wrong. They are not law. They take effect by incorporation into the contract of carriage — the bill of lading or the charterparty — and the Rule of Interpretation then makes them apply ‘to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith’. It follows that the version which governs any given adventure is the version the contract incorporates, which need not be the latest. In India the concept is separately recognised by statute, in the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 — not the English Act of 1906.

2. The essential features, drawn out of Rule A

Every phrase of Rule A is doing work, and each yields one essential feature. Present them that way and the list is defensible rather than remembered:

  1. Extraordinary. The sacrifice or expenditure must be outside the ordinary performance of the contract of carriage. Fuel burned on a normal passage, or wear on machinery in ordinary service, is not extraordinary however costly.
  2. Intentional and voluntary. The loss must be deliberately incurred. Cargo washed overboard by a sea is accidental and lies where it falls; cargo jettisoned by decision is a sacrifice. This is the single feature that most sharply separates general from particular average, and it is the one limb (b) turns on.
  3. Reasonable. Reinforced by the Rule Paramount, which overrides everything else: ‘In no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.’
  4. Peril. There must be real danger — not necessarily immediate, but genuine and substantial, and more than the ordinary risk of the sea.
  5. Common safety of a common adventure. The act must benefit the whole adventure, not one interest alone, and there must be more than one interest at risk. A ship in ballast with no cargo on board has no common adventure and can have no general average.
  6. Preservation of property. The purpose must be to preserve the property from that peril, and success matters: there must be something saved to contribute.

3. The lettered rules that qualify the definition

  • Rule C — only losses which are the direct consequence of the general average act are allowed. It expressly excludes demurrage, loss of market, loss caused by delay whether on the voyage or subsequently, and any indirect loss whatsoever, and excludes damage to the environment and loss in consequence of the escape or release of pollutant substances.
  • Rule D — rights to contribution are not affected even though the event was due to the fault of one of the parties to the adventure, without prejudice to any remedies or defences open in respect of that fault. So the adjustment proceeds first, and blame is fought separately.
  • Rule E — the onus of proof is on the party claiming, who must give notice in writing to the average adjuster and supply particulars of value, with a twelve-month period after which the adjuster may estimate.

4. How the loss is shared, and what general average is not

Under Rule A.2 the sacrifice is borne by the different contributing interests — ship, cargo and freight at risk — rateably in proportion to their values at the termination of the adventure. The adjustment is prepared by an average adjuster, and security is taken before cargo is released: normally an average bond from the receiver and an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter.

Two distinctions complete the picture and are worth a mark each:

  • General average is not particular average. Particular average is accidental damage; it falls on the interest that suffers it and is not shared.
  • General average is not insurance. It is a rule of contribution between the parties to the adventure, which predates marine insurance entirely — though in practice the contribution is recoverable under the policy.

(b) The refloating scenario — can the burnt bearings be included? — 8 marks

5. The rule written for exactly these facts

This situation is dealt with expressly, by a rule written for precisely these circumstances. Rule VII, ‘Damage to Machinery and Boilers’, provides:

‘Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.’

Rule VII is materially unchanged from the 1994 and 2004 texts, so the answer is the same whichever version the contract of carriage incorporates.

6. Applying it - the four conditions, the one that decides it, and the converse

Yes, the burnt bearings can be allowed in general average — provided four things are shown.

  1. The ship was ashore. She was aground, so the first condition is met on the facts given.
  2. She was in a position of peril. This must be established, not assumed — and the question does not say it. A vessel lying safely aground on soft mud in fine weather, in no danger and simply awaiting the tide, is not in peril; one working on a hard bottom, or exposed to weather, tide, swell or the risk of breaking her back, is. This is the condition on which the claim will actually turn, and an answer that does not identify it has missed what the examiner left out on purpose.
  3. The machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety. Running the main engine ahead and astern to shake her off the ground is precisely that — a deliberate act directed at the safety of the whole adventure, not a routine manoeuvre.
  4. The damage arose at the risk of such damage. The master must have accepted the risk of damaging the engine as the price of refloating. That acceptance is real and identifiable, because the engineering risk is well known: working an engine hard ahead and astern against a grounded hull applies sustained high torque at low or zero shaft speed with repeated reversals, so the hydrodynamic oil film in the main and crankpin bearings is degraded exactly when the load is highest — and if the sea suctions are silted, cooling is impaired at the same time. It is that knowing acceptance of a foreseeable risk that makes the loss a sacrifice rather than an accident.

The damage then satisfies Rule A as well: it is extraordinary (not ordinary service), intentional (the engine was deliberately worked in a way likely to damage it), reasonable if refloating was a sensible response to the situation, and directed at the common safety. It is also the direct consequence of the act, as Rule C requires. And under Rule D it makes no difference to the right of contribution that the grounding may have been somebody's fault — that is dealt with separately.

The converse must be stated, because it is the trap. Rule VII closes with an absolute prohibition: where the ship is afloat, damage from working the propelling machinery is ‘in no circumstances’ allowed. So if she had already come off and the bearings burnt while manoeuvring clear, or if she were merely touching the bottom and not in peril, the same physical damage would be particular average — the owner's own loss, recoverable if at all under the hull policy and not shared with cargo. The engine damage is identical; the legal answer is opposite; and what decides it is the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked.

Why this structure scores

The one thing the examiner has deliberately left out

Read the facts again: ‘A large vessel ran aground.’ That is all. It does not say she was in danger. Rule VII requires the ship to be ashore AND in a position of peril, and those are two conditions, not one.

So the correct answer is a qualified yes, and the qualification is where the marks are. A ship lying safely on soft mud in fine weather, waiting for the tide with tugs ordered, is aground but is not in peril; if her master works the engine and burns the bearings, that is his own particular average. A ship pounding on a hard bottom with a swell running, or one whose hull is taking the ground unevenly with the tide falling, plainly is. Say which facts you would need and why, and the justification the stem asks for writes itself.

Sacrifice or accident - the question underneath both limbs

The whole of general average turns on one distinction, and this paper tests it twice. In limb (a) it is the second essential feature. In limb (b) it is the entire analysis. Physically identical damage is a sacrifice or an accident depending on the circumstances and the intention — and Rule VII makes the point unusually cleanly, because it gives both answers in one sentence: allowed when she is ashore and in peril and the engine is worked knowing it may be damaged; never allowed when she is afloat.

If you can articulate that in the examination room, you can answer any general average scenario, including the ones this paper does not set — jettison, voluntary stranding, damage in extinguishing fire, port of refuge expenses.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (b) with a flat yes and no conditions.
  • Assuming peril because the ship went aground. Aground is not the same as in peril, and the question does not say.

  • Citing the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 in an Indian examination. The Indian statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.
  • Forgetting the converse in Rule VII — afloat, and it is in no circumstances allowed.

  • Forgetting that something must be saved. If the adventure is a total loss there is nothing to contribute.
