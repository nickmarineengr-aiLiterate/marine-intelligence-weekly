Model written answer

1. What big data means in a maritime setting

Big data describes datasets whose volume, velocity and variety exceed what conventional tools can store, process and analyse; veracity and value are usually added, because data that is unreliable or that supports no decision is a cost rather than an asset. In the maritime sector the point is not that the industry has recently begun generating data - ships have kept logs for centuries - but that a modern ship is in effect a distributed sensor network, continuously producing machinery, navigation, cargo and environmental data which can now be transmitted, pooled across a fleet and analysed ashore.

The distinction that matters is between data and information. Big data is only useful where analytics turn it into something a person can act on; volume without interpretation simply moves the problem.

One point of regulatory hard edge belongs at the start, because at this sitting digital data exchange is no longer voluntary. The 2022 amendments to the annex of the FAL Convention, resolution FAL.14(46) adopted on 13 May 2022, entered into force on 1 January 2024 under the tacit acceptance procedure. Standard 1.3quin now requires public authorities to introduce arrangements to enable the provision of all the information required under Standard 2.1 to a "single window" by electronic means, and to consider that single window as the channel through which they communicate decisions. The Convention defines a single window as "an environment that allows for the submission or provision of standardized and harmonized information and declarations to a single entry point, typically by electronic means". In the FAL Convention a Standard is mandatory, a Recommended Practice is not - so this is an obligation, and it took effect two months before this examination.

2. Where the data comes from

  • Machinery. Engine power, revolutions, fuel rack or index position, exhaust and scavenge temperatures and pressures, turbocharger speeds, torque and shaft power meters.
  • Condition monitoring. Vibration analysis, lubricating oil and scrape-down analysis, thermography, and the continuous record held by the alarm and monitoring system.
  • Navigation. AIS, ECDIS, GNSS, gyro, echo sounder, radar and the voyage data recorder.
  • Hull and voyage. Speed through water and over ground, draught and trim, rudder angle, weather routeing and metocean feeds, and hull and propeller condition.
  • Cargo. Reefer container monitoring, tank gauging, loading computer output, temperature and humidity, and increasingly IoT tags at package level.
  • Fuel and emissions. Mass flow meters and tank soundings, feeding the mandatory fuel oil consumption reporting regimes.
  • Shore side. Port call and terminal operating systems, customs and single-window declarations, classification society records, port State control databases, satellite imagery and AIS aggregation services.

The weakest element is the one the industry has relied on longest: the manually compiled noon report, which is low frequency, self-reported and inconsistent. Much of the value of automated acquisition lies simply in replacing it.

3. Shipping operations and fleet optimisation

  • Voyage optimisation. Weather routeing against measured ship response rather than assumed performance, and just-in-time arrival - slowing to a berth-ready time instead of steaming hard and waiting at anchor, which saves fuel that no engine modification can.
  • Trim and speed optimisation. Measured resistance across draught and trim combinations, so the ship is operated at the point the data shows rather than the point the trim table assumes.
  • Hull and propeller performance. Continuous monitoring separates the fouling penalty from weather and loading effects, which allows hull cleaning and propeller polishing to be timed on evidence and the payback of a coating or an energy-saving device to be measured rather than asserted.
  • Condition-based maintenance. The largest single change to the Chief Engineer's work. Vibration signatures, lube oil particle counts and temperature trends give warning of bearing, liner and turbocharger deterioration, so overhauls move from a calendar interval to a demonstrated need - reducing both unnecessary opening-up and unexpected failure.
  • Fuel management. Consumption verification against bunker delivery notes, bunker quality trending, and detection of loss or discrepancy.
  • Commercial performance. Charterparty speed and consumption warranties are now argued from continuously logged data rather than from noon reports.

This has acquired regulatory consequence. Ship energy efficiency is now assessed not only by design but by operational outcome, and an operational rating is computed from exactly the consumption and distance data described above. Data quality has therefore stopped being purely a commercial matter.

4. Port management, cargo tracking and safety

  • Port management. Berth allocation, ETA prediction, pilot and tug scheduling, yard and gate planning, and port call optimisation. The Maritime Single Window is the regulatory spine of this: mandatory from 1 January 2024, it replaces repeated submission of the same information to different authorities. The Convention's design principle is submit once and re-use to the maximum extent possible, and Standard 2.1bis caps what may be demanded - public authorities shall not require more than the information shown in appendix 1.
  • Cargo tracking. Container-level visibility across the chain, reefer and cold-chain integrity monitoring with alarm before spoilage rather than a claim after it, and control of demurrage and detention exposure.
  • Maritime safety. The shift is from investigating casualties one at a time to analysing them in aggregate. Casualty and near-miss databases expose recurring causal patterns; port State control deficiency data drives the risk-based targeting used by the regional memoranda; AIS traffic density analysis informs routeing measures, traffic separation and VTS design; hours-of-rest and fatigue analytics identify manning pressure; and machinery analytics turn alarms from reactive into predictive.

5. How it changes decision-making

The essential change is from reactive to anticipatory decision-making: acting on a trend rather than on a failure. It operates at three levels, and a good answer separates them.

  • Shipboard, immediate. A rising bearing temperature trend, a change in vibration signature, a fuel consumption step change - decisions in hours.
  • Shore technical and fleet, medium term. Maintenance planning, spares provisioning, dry-docking scope, hull cleaning intervals - decisions in weeks and months.
  • Commercial and strategic, long term. Retrofit business cases, fleet renewal, trade selection and compliance planning - decisions in years.

Two further effects are worth stating. Fleet benchmarking exposes the outlier ship that her own crew cannot see, because they have no comparator. And regulatory reporting has become a decision input rather than only an output - an operational efficiency rating affects how a ship can be traded, so the data feeds the commercial decision instead of merely recording it.

For a Chief Engineer the relationship is a specific one. You are simultaneously the largest producer of this data and the person best placed to interpret it. The analytics reports a trend; the engineer decides what it means and what to do. Preserving that division - and the competence that underpins it - is itself a management responsibility.

6. Technical and data barriers

  • Connectivity. Bandwidth at sea has improved greatly but remains variable and costly compared with shore, so systems must be designed to filter and aggregate on board and to tolerate long periods offline.
  • Data quality - the dominant problem. Uncalibrated or drifting sensors, manual entry, and inconsistent measurement bases, such as speed through water against speed over ground or fuel by mass against by volume. Analytics does not improve bad data; it launders it into confident-looking output, which is more dangerous than no output at all.
  • Standardisation and interoperability. There is no universally adopted ship data model. Equipment from many makers uses proprietary formats and closed interfaces, so integration is a bespoke project on each ship.
  • Legacy tonnage. Older ships were never instrumented for this. Retrofitting flow meters, torque meters and networked sensors costs real money on an asset that may have few years remaining.
  • IT and OT convergence. Extracting data from operational technology - the systems that actually control machinery - is technically harder and riskier than extracting it from administrative systems, and it is where the security problem begins.

7. Commercial, human and regulatory barriers

  • Cyber risk. The direct consequence of the previous point. Connecting operational technology to shore multiplies the attack surface, and a compromise reaches propulsion, steering or cargo systems rather than merely an office network. At this sitting the governing guidance is MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3, and resolution MSC.428(98) requires cyber risks to be addressed in the safety management system - so this is an ISM obligation, not an IT preference.
  • Data ownership and confidentiality. Owner, charterer, manager and equipment maker all claim rights in the same data stream, and contracts frequently do not say who owns it. Owners are reasonably wary that performance data will be used adversarially in a charterparty dispute.
  • Split incentives. On a time charter the owner buys the sensor and the charterer saves the fuel. Until the contract allocates the benefit, the investment case is weak for whoever must pay.
  • Industry structure. Shipping is fragmented into very many small owners, which slows the emergence of any common standard and prevents the scale that makes analytics cheap.
  • Cost and uncertain return. Benefits are real but hull-specific and hard to attribute, particularly on older tonnage with limited remaining life.
  • Skills and culture. Seagoing and superintendent staff need enough analytics literacy to challenge an output. Training and recruitment lag the technology, and a system nobody trusts is a system nobody uses.
  • Crew privacy. Where monitoring extends from machinery to people - hours worked, location, behaviour - there are legitimate data protection and industrial relations limits.
  • Regulatory fragmentation. Different reporting regimes demand differently shaped data for the same voyage, and port systems differ between and within States, which is precisely the problem the Single Window obligation is intended to reduce.
  • Automation bias. The subtlest barrier is one of judgement: decision support becoming, in practice, an unexamined decision maker. A model trained on normal operation is least reliable in exactly the abnormal conditions where a good decision matters most.

Understand this first

A bill of lading is three things at once, and almost every examinable point follows from which of the three is in play. As a RECEIPT it records what was shipped and in what apparent order and condition. As EVIDENCE OF THE CONTRACT it carries the carriage terms - but it is not the contract, which was made earlier. As a DOCUMENT OF TITLE it lets the goods be sold, financed and delivered against paper while the ship is still at sea, and that is why international trade works at all. Limb (c) then asks who owes what, and the answer is in two places: the Schedule to the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 2025 for the carriage obligations, and the Bills of Lading Act 2025 for how those rights and liabilities move to the consignee. THE DATE WARNING IS THE WHOLE OF THIS QUESTION: both Acts came into force on 10 September 2025, five weeks before this paper, replacing the 1856 and 1925 Acts. And the new Schedule is not simply Hague-Visby renumbered - it INCLUDES live animals and deck cargo in "goods", drops Article IV bis, and lets a court extend the one-year time bar by three months. Learn the Indian text, not the international one.

Model written answer

1. What a bill of lading is, and the law that governs it

A bill of lading is a document issued by or on behalf of the carrier to the shipper on receipt or shipment of goods. Neither Indian nor English law defines it by a single formula; it is defined by what it does, and the Indian Sale of Goods Act, 1930, section 2(4) reflects this by including a bill of lading within the expression "document of title to goods".

At this sitting the Indian statute book is new. The governing framework is the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 (Act 18 of 2025), which deals with the transfer of rights and liabilities under the bill, and the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 (Act 19 of 2025), whose Schedule carries the carriage regime. Both came into force on 10 September 2025 - about five weeks before this examination - repealing the Indian Bills of Lading Act, 1856 (s.6(1) of the 2025 Act) and the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 (s.12(1)) respectively.

The Act gives force of law to the Hague Rules as amended by the Protocols of 1968 and 1979 - the Hague-Visby regime - but expressly "as so amended and as set out with modifications in the Schedule". The Indian Schedule, not the international text, is the law here. Schedule Article I(c) is the gateway: the rules apply only to contracts of carriage covered by a bill of lading or any similar document of title. The Hamburg Rules 1978 are in force internationally but India is not a party; the Rotterdam Rules 2008 are not in force. Both are worth naming for completeness and must not be applied.

2. The three functions, and their commercial significance

  • A receipt for the goods. Article III(3) requires the carrier, master or agent, on the shipper's demand, to issue a bill showing (a) the leading marks necessary for identification, (b) either the number of packages or pieces, or the quantity, or weight, and (c) the apparent order and condition of the goods. The proviso matters in practice: no carrier is bound to state marks, number, quantity or weight which he has reasonable ground for suspecting not accurately to represent the goods, or which he has had no reasonable means of checking - the basis of the "said to contain" and "shipper's weight and count" reservations.
  • Evidence of the contract of carriage. The bill is not the contract itself - the contract is normally concluded when the space is booked, before any bill exists. Between carrier and shipper the bill is evidence of the terms; once it passes to a third-party holder its terms become the contract as between carrier and holder.
  • A document of title. Possession of the bill gives constructive possession of the goods, and an order bill is transferable by endorsement and delivery. This is the function that carries the commerce: it lets goods be sold and resold while at sea, lets a seller on CIF or FOB terms be paid against documents, and lets a bank take the bill as security under a documentary credit.

The evidential force strengthens on transfer. Article III(4) makes the bill prima facie evidence of receipt of the goods as described, but proof to the contrary is not admissible once the bill has been transferred to a third party acting in good faith - the Visby addition, carried into the 2025 Schedule, and the reason a buyer or bank can rely on the document.

One Indian enacting provision belongs here because it is unique to the statute and easy marks: section 5 requires that every bill of lading issued in India contain an express statement that it has effect subject to the applicable rules. That is the statutory clause paramount, and it means the regime cannot be quietly contracted out of at the document-drafting stage.

3. The bill issued to the ship, and its counterpart

A bill issued to the ship is one issued by the master, or by an agent signing "for the Master" on the shipowner's behalf - commonly called an owner's bill. Under it the shipowner is the contracting carrier and bears the Article III obligations personally.

Its counterpart is the charterer's bill, signed by or for the charterer, under which the charterer is the carrier and the shipowner is not a party to the contract of carriage at all. The Schedule expressly contemplates both: Article I(b) provides that "carrier" includes the owner or the charterer who enters into a contract of carriage with a shipper, and Article I(c) brings a bill issued under or pursuant to a charterparty within the rules from the moment at which it regulates the relations between a carrier and a holder - that is, once it is endorsed away from the charterer. Article V completes the picture: the rules do not apply to charterparties as such, but bills issued under one must comply.

The distinction is not academic. It determines whom the cargo claimant sues, whether the claim lies in contract or only in bailment or tort, and whose P&I cover responds. In practice it is resolved by reading the signature box, any identity of carrier clause and any demise clause. A third document must be kept separate: the house bill issued by a freight forwarder or NVOCC, which is not issued by or for the ship at all and under which the forwarder contracts as carrier.

4. The other principal types and their purposes

  • Shipped (on board) against received for shipment. Only the first states that the goods have actually been loaded. Documentary credits normally require an on-board bill, and a received-for-shipment bill is converted by an on-board notation. Article III(7) gives the shipper the right to demand a shipped bill.
  • Clean against claused or foul. A clean bill carries no clause expressly declaring a defective condition of goods or packaging, under Article III(3)(c). A claused bill is normally rejected under a letter of credit, which is exactly why shippers press for a clean bill against a letter of indemnity - a practice which is improper where the clausing would have been accurate, because it deceives the third-party holder.
  • Order, bearer and straight bills. An order bill ("to order" or "to order of X") is negotiable by endorsement; a bearer bill passes by delivery; a straight bill names a consignee and is not negotiable, so it cannot be used to resell the goods in transit.
  • Through and combined transport (multimodal) bills, covering carriage by more than one carrier or by more than one mode, with the issuer taking responsibility across the legs.
  • Sea waybill. A receipt and evidence of the contract, but NOT a document of title. It is non-negotiable, delivery is made to the named consignee on proof of identity, and no original need be presented - which is why it suits trades with no resale in transit and short voyages where the cargo outruns the paperwork.
  • Switch bills - a second set issued in place of the first, usually to conceal the true load port or shipper. Legitimate uses exist but the fraud risk is high and the first set must be surrendered. Stale bills are those tendered too late to be taken up under the credit.

5. The carrier's obligations under the Rules

Under the 2025 Schedule the carrier - whether shipowner or charterer - carries two positive duties and one duty to document.

  • Article III(1) - seaworthiness. The carrier is bound before and at the beginning of the voyage to exercise due diligence to (a) make the ship seaworthy; (b) properly man, equip and supply the ship; and (c) make the holds, refrigerating and cool chambers, and all other parts of the ship in which goods are carried, fit and safe for their reception, carriage and preservation. Two qualifications earn marks: it is a duty of due diligence, not an absolute warranty - and section 4 of the Act says so expressly, providing that there is no implied absolute undertaking of seaworthiness - and it attaches before and at the beginning of the voyage, so it is not a continuing obligation once the voyage has begun.
  • Article III(2) - care of cargo. Subject to Article IV, the carrier shall properly and carefully load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care for and discharge the goods. Seven verbs, and they are worth reproducing exactly.
  • Article III(3) and (7) - documentation. Issue the bill on demand, showing the three matters at §2 above, and a shipped bill on demand once the goods are loaded.

Two boundaries define the reach of those duties, and one of them is where Indian law now departs from the international text. Article I(a) - "carriage of goods" covers only the period from the time when the goods are loaded on to the time they are discharged, the tackle to tackle rule; that is unchanged.

Article I(d) is where the Indian text departs, and it runs in the cargo owner's favour. "Goods" INCLUDES any property, including live animals, as well as containers, pallets or similar articles of transport or packaging supplied by the consignor, irrespective of whether such property is to be or is carried on or under deck. Live animals and deck cargo are therefore inside the compulsory regime in India, and the carrier cannot take a cargo outside it by stating deck carriage on the face of the bill.

Against contracting out, Article III(8) is absolute: any clause relieving the carrier or the ship from liability for negligence, fault or failure in these duties, or lessening such liability otherwise than as provided in these rules, shall be null and void and of no effect - and by Article III(9) a benefit-of-insurance clause is deemed to be such a clause. Contracting to increase the carrier's liability remains permitted.

6. Immunities, the package limitation and the time bar

Against those duties the Schedule gives the carrier a defined set of protections. The balance between the two is the whole of the Hague-Visby compromise, and it survives in Indian law with the modifications noted.

  • Article IV(1). No liability for loss from unseaworthiness unless caused by want of due diligence - and the burden of proving due diligence is on the carrier.
  • Article IV(2) - the excepted perils, seventeen in all. Two matter most: (a) act, neglect or default of the master, mariner, pilot or the servants of the carrier in the navigation or in the management of the ship - the nautical fault defence, abolished by the Hamburg Rules and retained here - and (b) fire, unless caused by the actual fault or privity of the carrier. The rest run through perils of the sea, act of God, war, public enemies, arrest or restraint, quarantine, act or omission of the shipper, strikes, riots, saving life or property, inherent vice and wastage, insufficiency of packing or marks, latent defects not discoverable by due diligence, and the catch-all at (q), on which the carrier bears the burden.
  • Article IV(4) - deviation. Deviation in saving or attempting to save life or property at sea, and any reasonable deviation, is not a breach.
  • Article IV(5)(a) - package limitation. Unless the nature and value were declared by the shipper before shipment and inserted in the bill, liability is capped at 666.67 units of account per package or unit or 2 units of account per kilogramme of gross weight, whichever is the higher. The unit of account is the Special Drawing Right. Article IV(5)(b) is the container clause: where goods are consolidated, the number of packages enumerated in the bill is the number of packages, and otherwise the container itself is one package.
  • Article IV(5)(c) - loss of the limit, where the damage resulted from an act or omission done with intent to cause damage, or recklessly and with knowledge that damage would probably result. Note the lettering: this is (c) in the Indian Schedule, not the Hague-Visby (e).
  • Article IV(6) - dangerous goods, which the carrier may land, destroy or render innocuous, with the shipper liable for the consequences.
  • Article III(6) - the time bar, AND the Indian addition. Carrier and ship are discharged from all liability whatsoever unless suit is brought within ONE YEAR of delivery or of when the goods should have been delivered, extendable by agreement made after the cause of action has arisen. The Indian Schedule then adds a proviso the international text does not contain: a suit may be brought after that year within a further period of not more than THREE MONTHS as allowed by the court. That is a judicial extension, not a contractual one, and it is a real difference in the claimant's favour.

The Schedule runs Articles I to IX and contains no Article IV bis. There is therefore no statutory extension of the defences and limits to claims framed in tort, or to the carrier's servants and agents: protection for stevedores and sub-contractors depends on the Himalaya clause in the contract itself.

7. Shipowner, consignee, and the transfer of rights

Where the bill is an owner's bill the shipowner is the carrier and every duty in §5 is his. Where it is a charterer's bill he is not a party to the contract of carriage, but he is not immune: he holds the goods as bailee, and the Schedule consistently says "neither the carrier nor the ship" - the vessel herself is exposed, including to arrest in rem. The shipper also owes a duty: under Article III(5) he guarantees the accuracy of the marks, number, quantity and weight he furnishes and indemnifies the carrier against inaccuracy.

The consignee is brought into the contract by the Bills of Lading Act, 2025. Its three operative sections re-enact the scheme of the 1856 Act rather than reforming it, so the substance a candidate learned before September 2025 still holds - only the citation has moved:

  • Section 2 - every consignee named in the bill, and every endorsee to whom property passes upon or by reason of the consignment or endorsement, has all rights of suit transferred to and vested in him and is subject to the same liabilities as if the contract had been made with himself. Rights and burdens move together. (1856 Act s.1.)
  • Section 3 - the right of stoppage in transit and the shipowner's claim for freight against the original shipper or owner are preserved. (1856 Act s.2.)
  • Section 4(1) - the bill in the hands of a consignee or endorsee for valuable consideration is conclusive evidence of shipment as against the master or other person signing, even if the goods were never shipped - subject to a fraud proviso. It bites on the master personally. (1856 Act s.3.) Section 4(2) adds that it does not so operate where the holder had actual notice that the goods were not in fact laden.

Two Indian enacting provisions round off limb (c). Section 3 of the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 2025 fixes the geographical reach: the applicable rules have effect for carriage from any port in India to any other port, whether in or outside India - outward voyages, so an inward shipment is governed by the rules only if the contract so provides. And section 12(3) preserves section 331 and Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, so the shipowner's general limitation of liability sits alongside, and is not displaced by, the package limitation.

In performance the consignee must present an original bill to take delivery, pay freight where payable and give security for any general average contribution. The mirror obligation is the one most often broken: delivery without production of an original bill is at the carrier's own peril, and a letter of indemnity does not change that as against the true holder - it merely gives the carrier someone to sue.

THE TEMPORAL POINT - the Indian statute book changed five weeks before this paper

This is the most important currentness note in the paper, and it is the reason this question cannot be answered from an older model answer. At this sitting the governing Acts are the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 (18 of 2025) and the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 (19 of 2025), both in force from 10 September 2025. They repealed the Indian Bills of Lading Act, 1856 and the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 respectively. A candidate citing the 1856 and 1925 Acts as current law at October 2025 is citing repealed statutes.

The two Acts behave very differently, and it is worth knowing which is which. The Bills of Lading Act 2025 re-enacts: its sections 2, 3 and 4 carry the same three subjects as sections 1, 2 and 3 of the 1856 Act, so everything you already knew about the transfer of rights is still right and only the section numbers moved. The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 2025 does not: it gives the Hague-Visby regime force of law "with modifications", and four of those modifications matter.

The four modifications in the 2025 Schedule

  • 1. "Goods" now INCLUDES live animals and deck cargo - Article I(d). Hague-Visby Article I(c) excluded both. The Indian Schedule reverses this and adds the words "irrespective of whether such property is to be or is carried on or under deck". Deck cargo can no longer be taken outside the regime by a statement on the bill. If you learned the old exclusion, unlearn it for Indian carriage.
  • 2. Article IV bis is absent. The Schedule runs Articles I to IX and contains no "bis". The tort-channelling and servants-and-agents extension is simply not in the Indian text. Himalaya protection must come from the contract clause, not from the statute.
  • 3. A three-month judicial extension of the one-year time bar - Article III(6)(c) proviso. Under Hague-Visby the year can be extended only by agreement after the cause of action arose. In India a court may allow a further period of not more than three months. The one-year rule is still the rule; the escape valve is new.
  • 4. Article I and Article IV(5) are re-lettered. Carrier is now I(b), contract of carriage I(c), goods I(d), ship I(e), carriage of goods I(a); and loss of the limit is IV(5)(c), not IV(5)(e). Quoting the international lettering in an Indian answer is a small but avoidable error.

The substance of the carrier's duties and immunities is otherwise unchanged - Article III(1) and (2), the seventeen exceptions, 666.67 / 2 whichever higher, the one-year bar, Article III(8). This is why the route below is the same route that served the March 2024 sitting.

What "issued to the ship" means, and why the answer says so explicitly

The printed phrase is not a term of art, and part of the examiner's test is whether the candidate recognises what is being contrasted. Limb (c) asks for the obligations of the shipowner, the carrier and the consignee - naming shipowner and carrier separately. That is only meaningful if the two can be different people, which is exactly the owner's bill against charterer's bill distinction. The answer therefore reads "issued to the ship" as the owner's bill - issued by the master or "for the Master" - and says so in terms rather than assuming it.

If in the exam you are unsure, state your reading in one line and answer it. An examiner cannot penalise a clearly stated and reasonable construction; an examiner can penalise an answer that silently answers a different question.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the 1856 and 1925 Acts as current. Both were repealed with effect from 10 September 2025. Cite the 2025 Acts.
  • Saying "goods exclude live animals and deck cargo". True of Hague-Visby, and true of Indian law before 10 September 2025. False now - Article I(d) includes both.
  • Citing Article IV bis as Indian law. It is not in the Schedule.
  • Writing "a bill of lading is the contract of carriage". It is evidence of it. The contract predates the document.
  • Applying the Hamburg Rules to an Indian carriage. India is not a party. Name them, and the Rotterdam Rules, for status only.
  • Saying the seaworthiness obligation is absolute. It is due diligence, section 4 says so expressly, and it is not continuing.
  • Giving the package limitation as one figure. It is 666.67 SDR per package or unit OR 2 SDR per kilogramme, whichever is HIGHER.
  • Forgetting the one-year time bar, or quoting it without the Indian three-month judicial extension.
  • Treating a sea waybill as a bill of lading. It is not a document of title.
  • Answering limb (c) about the carrier only. Three parties were named.

Examiner traps

  • Deck cargo no longer needs the two-limb test in India. Under Hague-Visby you asked whether the cargo was both stated as deck cargo and so carried. Under Article I(d) the question does not arise - deck or under deck, it is "goods". Candidates who have memorised the old two-limb test will apply it confidently and be wrong.
  • Tackle to tackle survives. Article I(a) - the rules cover loading to discharge, not the port terminal periods either side. That boundary did not move.
  • Article III(8). Contracting out downward is void; contracting to increase the carrier's liability is not.
  • Article III(4). Prima facie against the shipper, conclusive against a good-faith third-party transferee. The change on transfer is the point.
  • Nautical fault. Article IV(2)(a) protects negligent navigation or management of the ship, not negligent care of the cargo. The line between "management of the ship" and "care of cargo" is the most litigated boundary in the regime.
  • Section 4(1) of the Bills of Lading Act 2025 bites on the master personally - a worthwhile point for a Chief Engineer to know about the person signing. Section 4(2) is the holder's-actual-notice escape.
  • Outward voyages. Section 3 applies the rules to carriage from an Indian port outwards. Inward carriage is caught only by contract.

Likely oral follow-up

  • "Name the three functions of a bill of lading." - receipt, evidence of the contract, document of title.
  • "Which Act governs carriage of goods by sea from India today?" - the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 2025, in force 10 September 2025, which repealed the 1925 Act.
  • "Is deck cargo covered by the rules?" - in India, yes. Article I(d) includes property carried on or under deck, and live animals.
  • "Cargo is damaged. How long has the receiver got to sue?" - one year under Article III(6), extendable by agreement after the cause of action arose, and the court may allow a further period of not more than three months.

Depth behind route steps 6 and 7

  • Why the new definition of "goods" matters commercially. Deck carriage was the standard way of putting a cargo outside the compulsory regime, so that the carrier could contract on his own terms - which is why the phrase "stated as carried on deck and so carried" appears on so many bills. Article I(d) closes that route in India. Containers carried on deck, which is most container carriage, are now squarely inside the regime, and the carrier's exposure is correspondingly wider. Live animals are in the same position.
  • Why the absence of Article IV bis is not the end of Himalaya protection. Article IV bis gave the defences statutory reach into tort and down to servants and agents. Without it, the carrier's stevedores and sub-contractors rely on the Himalaya clause drafted into the bill. The practical consequence is that the clause has to be there and has to be well drafted; the statute will not supply the protection.
  • Why "management of the ship" is the most litigated phrase in the regime. Article IV(2)(a) protects negligence in the navigation or management of the ship, but not negligence in the care of the cargo under Article III(2). The same act can fall either side: closing a valve to trim the vessel is management of the ship; closing it to protect a particular parcel is care of cargo. Where the purpose is mixed, the courts look at the dominant object of the act.
  • Why the limitation is expressed two ways. The package basis protects the carrier against a single high-value crate; the weight basis protects the cargo owner in bulk and heavy-lift trades where one "package" could be enormous. Taking whichever is higher means the claimant always gets the more favourable of the two.
  • The time bar is a true extinction, not a limitation period. Article III(6) says the carrier and the ship are discharged from all liability whatsoever. The Indian three-month judicial extension softens it but does not change its nature: outside the year, and outside any extension, the liability is gone rather than merely unenforceable.
  • Letters of indemnity in practice. Two situations recur and both are commercially normal and legally exposed: an LOI for a clean bill where clausing would have been accurate (which deceives the third-party holder and is generally unenforceable), and an LOI for delivery without the original bill (enforceable between the parties, but leaving the carrier liable to the true holder). Club forms exist for the second, usually requiring a bank counter-signature; the first is not a club-supported form.

Model written answer

1. What general average is, and where the law comes from

General average is the oldest rule in maritime commerce: where something is deliberately sacrificed, or extraordinary expense deliberately incurred, to save a venture in peril, the loss is shared by everyone whose property was saved rather than left with the party who happened to bear it.

Two bodies of law say so, and they are different in kind. The Indian Marine Insurance Act, 1963, section 66(1) defines a general average loss as one caused by or directly consequential on a general average act, and states that it includes a general average expenditure as well as a general average sacrifice. Section 66(3) creates the entitlement: the party on whom the loss falls is entitled, subject to the conditions imposed by maritime law, to a rateable contribution from the other parties interested - the general average contribution.

The York-Antwerp Rules are not a convention and not a statute but a contractual code, applying only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them. The current edition is the YAR 2016, adopted by the Comité Maritime International on 5 May 2016; the 1994 and 2004 Rules still govern contracts that name them, so which edition applies is a question of contract, not of date. Where incorporated, the Rule of Interpretation makes them apply to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith.

Contrast particular average, section 64(1): a partial loss caused by a peril insured against which is not a general average loss. It lies where it falls. That distinction - shared against borne alone - is what limb (a) turns on.

2. The elements of a general average act

Rule A.1 is emphatic in its drafting: there is a general average act "when, and only when" every one of the following is present.

  • Extraordinary. Ordinary running of the ship, ordinary wear, ordinary consumption of bunkers is not general average. The act must be outside the normal performance of the contract of carriage.
  • Intentional. A deliberate decision, not the casualty itself. Section 66(2) uses the word voluntarily. Cargo washed overboard is not a jettison.
  • Reasonable. Both in the decision and in the amount. The Rule Paramount repeats this as an independent override: "In no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred."
  • For the common safety, to preserve from peril. The purpose must be the safety of the whole venture, not of one interest. Section 66(2) says in time of peril.
  • A common maritime adventure. There must be more than one interest at risk - typically ship, cargo and freight. A ship in ballast on her owner's account cannot declare general average against herself. Rule B extends the idea to vessels towing or pushing one another, provided they are in commercial activity and not in a salvage operation.

Rule A.2 then states the consequence in one line: general average sacrifices and expenditures shall be borne by the different contributing interests on the basis the Rules go on to provide.

3. What may and may not be allowed

Establishing a general average act does not admit a claim. Four filters apply.

  • Rule C.1 - directness. Only losses, damages or expenses which are the direct consequence of the act.
  • Rule C.2 - environment. In no case an allowance for damage to the environment or for the escape or release of pollutant substances. Pollution liability belongs to the pollution regimes.
  • Rule C.3 - consequential loss. No demurrage, no loss of market, nothing for delay whether on the voyage or afterwards, and no indirect loss whatsoever.
  • Rule E.1 - proof. The onus is on the party claiming to show the loss is properly allowable.

Three Rules complete the picture. Rule D preserves the right to contribution even where the event was the fault of one of the parties, without prejudice to remedies or defences against that party - so unseaworthiness is fought separately and does not halt the adjustment. Rule F allows a substituted expense incurred in place of an allowable one, but only up to the amount of the general average expense avoided. Rule XIX deals with dishonesty: goods loaded without the shipowner's knowledge or wilfully misdescribed are not allowed yet still contribute if saved; goods declared below their real value are allowed on the declared value but contribute on the actual value.

4. Contribution among the parties

This is the heart of limb (a). The contributing interests are the ship, the cargo and the freight at risk, each contributing in proportion to its value at the end of the adventure. Rule G.1 fixes the moment: adjustment, as regards both loss and contribution, on values at the time and place when and where the common maritime adventure ends. Rule XVII sets the values:

  • XVII(a)(i) - contribution on the actual net values of the property at the termination of the common maritime adventure, except cargo, taken at its value at the time of discharge from the commercial invoice rendered to the receiver or, failing that, the shipped value.
  • XVII(a)(ii) - that value includes insurance and freight unless the freight is at others' risk, and deducts damage suffered before or at discharge. It carries the 2016 innovation: any cargo may be excluded from contributing where the adjuster considers the cost of including it disproportionate to its eventual contribution.
  • XVII(b) - the amount allowed as general average for property sacrificed is added back, so a party whose goods were sacrificed contributes as if they had been saved. Otherwise sacrifice would be rewarded.
  • XVII(d) - cargo sold short of destination contributes on the actual net proceeds of sale plus any amount allowed.
  • XVII(e) - mails, passengers' luggage, accompanied personal effects and accompanied private motor vehicles do not contribute at all.

Two Rules attach further money to the fund. Rule XX allows the capital loss on goods sold to raise funds for general average disbursements, and the cost of insuring those disbursements. Rule XXI allows interest on expenditure, sacrifices and allowances until three months after the date of issue of the adjustment, at the 12-month ICE LIBOR for the currency of the adjustment plus four percentage points - the 2016 Rules having abolished the old 2% commission in its favour.

5. Security, the adjustment and the time bar

General average is declared by the shipowner, who appoints an average adjuster. The adjuster is not an arbitrator: the adjuster collects values, applies the Rules and issues the adjustment, stating what each interest owes or is owed. The practical lever is cargo - the carrier withholds delivery until security is given, so the receiver signs an average bond and the cargo underwriter issues an average guarantee behind it; where cargo is uninsured a cash deposit is taken. Rule XXIII(a)(i) confirms their standing by referring expressly to rights to claim under general average bonds and guarantees.

  • Rule XXII - deposits. Sums are remitted forthwith to the adjuster, held in a special account in the adjuster's name, separate from the adjuster's own funds, in trust. Payments on account or refunds need the adjuster's written certificate and the depositor's approval, or ninety days; all are without prejudice to the ultimate liability of the parties.
  • Rules E.2 and E.3 - information. Every party supplies particulars of value and gives written notice of any claim as soon as possible. After twelve months the adjuster may estimate, and an estimate is challengeable only within two months and only as manifestly incorrect.
  • Rule XXIII - time bar. Rights to contribution, including under bonds and guarantees, are extinguished unless action is brought within one year of issue of the adjustment, and never more than six years from termination of the adventure. Extendable by agreement; inapplicable between the parties and their own insurers.

6. Scenarios in which general average is declared

The numbered Rules are, in effect, the examiner's list of examples. Each is a decided category, and quoting the Rule number turns a story into a legal answer.

  • Jettison of cargo (Rule I) - allowed only if that cargo was carried in accordance with the recognised custom of the trade.
  • Sacrifice for the common safety (Rule II) - including water going down hatches opened to make a jettison, so cargo never itself sacrificed is still allowed.
  • Extinguishing fire (Rule III) - water and other fire-fighting damage, including beaching or scuttling a burning ship; but no allowance for damage by smoke however caused, or by heat of the fire.
  • Voluntary stranding (Rule V) - a ship intentionally run ashore for the common safety, whether or not she might have been driven ashore anyway.
  • Machinery and boiler damage refloating (Rule VII) - allowed where the ship is ashore and in a position of peril and the damage arose from an actual intention to float her for the common safety at the risk of such damage. Where she is afloat, no such damage is allowed in any circumstances.
  • Lightening a ship when ashore (Rule VIII) - the extra cost of lightening, lighter hire and reshipping, and consequent damage.
  • Cargo, materials or stores burnt as fuel (Rule IX) - with credit to general average for the fuel that would otherwise have been consumed.
  • Port of refuge (Rules X and XI) - entry and departure expenses where the ship put in in consequence of accident, sacrifice or other extraordinary circumstances necessary for the common safety; plus handling, discharging, storage and reloading, and the wages and maintenance of master, officers and crew during the detention.
  • Salvage remuneration (Rule VI) - allowable where the operation preserved the property from peril; where the parties are separately liable to salvors it is allowed only if one of five conditions applies, and Rule VI(d) excludes article 14 special compensation and SCOPIC altogether.
  • And the counter-example, Rule IV - cutting away wreck already carried away or effectively lost by accident is not general average. There is nothing left to sacrifice.

7. Criteria and conditions in modern maritime commerce

Set against any scenario, six conditions decide whether general average applies.

  • Real peril - real and substantial, though not necessarily immediate. A precaution in fine weather is not general average.
  • A common adventure - two or more interests must stand to lose. This is why the question says "among parties involved in a maritime venture".
  • A voluntary act - the casualty is the peril; the response is the act.
  • Extraordinary and reasonable - separate gates, with the Rule Paramount making reasonableness an override on every allowance.
  • Success, a saved fund - contribution is levied on values at the end of the adventure (Rules G.1 and XVII). If nothing survives there is nothing to contribute from.
  • Incorporation - the Rules bind only because the contract of carriage says so; absent that, the position falls back on the general maritime law and, in India, the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

Modern commerce has changed the economics rather than the principle, and the 2016 Rules answer that directly. Containerisation means one casualty can involve thousands of consignees, making security collection disproportionate to the sums recovered - which is why Rule XVII(a)(ii) now lets the adjuster exclude low-value cargo and why Rule E.3 imposes a twelve-month cut-off backed by a power to estimate. Rule C.2 keeps the pollution regimes out of the adjustment, and Rule VI(b) stops salvage being adjusted twice where salvors have been paid separately.

Why this structure scores

The printed marks are 8 + 8, and the trap is that limb (a) names three things: the concept, the principles, and contribution among the parties. Most candidates write four hundred words on "what is general average" and then run out of time before contribution - which is the part the examiner has singled out and which no textbook paragraph covers. Route steps 1 to 3 handle the concept and principles briskly; steps 4 and 5 spend the marks where the question points.

Limb (b) asks for two things at once - examples and criteria and conditions. Splitting them into steps 6 and 7 means neither is dropped. Step 6 is a list of Rules; step 7 is the reasoning that governs all of them.

Statute, contract, convention - keep the three apart

General average is unusual in that the instrument students quote most - the York-Antwerp Rules - has no legislative force at all. It is a set of contractual terms produced by a private international body, the CMI, and binds only where a bill of lading or charterparty incorporates it. The Marine Insurance Act 1963 is the Indian statute, and it governs the insurance relationship - what the assured may recover from the underwriter - as much as the adjustment itself.

This matters practically. If asked "which York-Antwerp Rules apply to this casualty?", the answer is whichever edition the contract names. A 2025 casualty under a bill of lading referring to the YAR 1994 is adjusted on the 1994 Rules. Saying "the 2016 Rules, because they are the latest" is wrong.

What changed in 2016, and why it is worth knowing

  • Rule VI (salvage). The 2004 Rules had removed salvage from general average and were consequently almost never used. The 2016 Rules restore it, subject to the five gateways at VI(b) which prevent pointless re-adjustment where salvors have been paid separately.
  • Rules X, XI and XIV. The 1994 wording for port of refuge expenses, crew wages and temporary repairs was retained - the main reason shipowners accepted the 2016 Rules where they had rejected the 2004 Rules.
  • Rule XVII. The low-value cargo exclusion is new, and is the clearest example of the Rules bending to container economics.
  • Rules XX and XXI. The old 2% commission on general average disbursements was abolished, replaced by interest at 12-month ICE LIBOR + 4 percentage points.

BIMCO agreed in May 2016 that its charterparties and bills of lading should refer to the 2016 Rules, which is how the edition spread into standard forms.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the history of the Rhodian law and Lex Mercatoria. It earns nothing. The examiner asked for principles and contribution.
  • Answering limb (a) without ever reaching contribution. Half the limb is about who pays what.
  • Saying general average is "a convention" or "IMO law". It is neither.
  • Forgetting that sacrificed property still contributes (Rule XVII(b)). Candidates routinely say the jettisoned cargo owner "is paid and contributes nothing".
  • Treating fire damage as wholly allowable. Smoke and heat damage are excluded by Rule III - they are the peril, not the sacrifice.
  • Claiming machinery damage in general average for a ship that is afloat. Rule VII excludes it in any circumstances.
  • Saying fault defeats general average. Rule D says the opposite - the adjustment proceeds and fault is fought separately.

Examiner traps

  • "Only when". Rule A.1 uses those words. Every element must be present; the examiner is testing whether you know it is a conjunctive test.
  • Voluntary versus accidental. Cargo washed overboard is a particular average loss; cargo jettisoned is general average. Same cargo, same sea.
  • Rule IV. Cutting away a mast already carried away is not a sacrifice - a favourite oral question.
  • Rule XVII(e). Mails, passengers' luggage, accompanied personal effects and accompanied private motor vehicles do not contribute. Easy marks, rarely given.
  • The two time limits. Rule E.3's twelve months for particulars of value is not the same thing as Rule XXIII's one year from the adjustment, six years absolute.
  • SCOPIC. Rule VI(d) puts article 14 special compensation and SCOPIC outside general average.

Likely oral follow-up

  • "Your ship grounds and you damage the main engine trying to refloat. Is that general average?" - Rule VII: yes if she was ashore and in peril and the damage arose from an actual intention to refloat for the common safety; no if she was afloat.
  • "Who declares general average, and who pays the adjuster?" - the shipowner declares and appoints; the adjuster's fee is a general average disbursement borne by the fund.
  • "What is the difference between an average bond and an average guarantee?" - the bond is given by the cargo receiver, the guarantee by the cargo underwriter backing it.
  • "Can you refuse to deliver cargo until security is given?" - yes, that is the practical basis on which security is collected.
  • "The grounding was caused by our own negligent navigation. Can we still claim?" - Rule D: rights to contribution are unaffected, without prejudice to defences against us.
  • "Give three items that do not contribute." - Rule XVII(e).
  • "What is a substituted expense?" - Rule F, capped at the general average expense avoided; towage to destination in place of repairs at a port of refuge is the standard illustration.

Memory framework

For the elements, hold five words in Rule A order: EXTRAORDINARY, INTENTIONAL, REASONABLE, COMMON SAFETY, COMMON ADVENTURE - then add SAVED, because contribution is levied on what survives. Six gates, and every scenario in limb (b) is tested against them.

For contribution, remember the sequence VALUE - ADD BACK - EXCLUDE - APPORTION: take the actual net value at the end of the adventure (Rule XVII(a)); add back what was sacrificed (XVII(b)); exclude what does not contribute (XVII(e), and uneconomic cargo under XVII(a)(ii)); apportion the fund rateably (s.66(3), Rule A.2).

The scenarios in longer form - detail behind route step 6

The Model Answer states each category as a proposition plus its Rule number, which is what scores. The colour below is for the oral and for reading around the topic.

  • Jettison. The custom-of-the-trade condition in Rule I is aimed squarely at deck cargo. Containers stowed on deck under a modern trade where deck carriage is customary are within the Rule; goods stowed on deck against the custom of that trade are not, and their jettison earns no allowance.
  • Fire. Rule III draws the sharpest line in the Rules between peril damage and sacrifice damage. The water you put on the fire is a sacrifice; the smoke and the heat are the casualty. Beaching or scuttling a burning ship is expressly on the sacrifice side, which is why a deliberate flooding decision is worth recording carefully in the engine room log and the deck log alike.
  • Refloating. Rule VII is the one a Chief Engineer meets in practice. Working the main engine hard astern with the ship aground, knowing the risk to bearings, tailshaft, boilers or coolers, is a classic general average sacrifice - provided the ship was in a position of peril and the intention is evidenced. The engine-room record of the order, the times, the loads and the reason is what proves that intention months later, and its absence is the usual reason such a claim fails.
  • Port of refuge. This is the largest single head of general average expenditure in practice, because Rules X and XI between them pick up entry, discharge, storage, reloading, departure, and crew wages and maintenance for the whole detention. Rule X(a)(ii) extends the same treatment to a second port where repairs cannot be done at the first, including temporary repairs and towage.
  • Salvage. The five gateways at Rule VI(b) exist because, where every interest has already settled with the salvors under its own Lloyd's Open Form apportionment, putting salvage back through the general average adjustment usually moves very little money and costs a great deal. The gateways admit it only where it makes a real difference - for example where there are significant general average sacrifices, or where salved values are manifestly incorrect.

Model written answer

1. Why propeller efficiency is worth pursuing

The propeller is the final energy conversion in the propulsion chain and one of the least efficient links in it: a large proportion of the delivered power never becomes useful thrust. Because resistance rises steeply with speed, a modest improvement in propulsive efficiency releases a disproportionate saving in fuel and therefore in emissions.

The stem supplies its own framing - "improving efficiency of propellers is recognized as a growing means to improve energy efficiency of ships" - and that framing is sound at this sitting, because ship energy efficiency requirements, technical and operational, were already in force. A propeller change has a particular attraction in that context: it improves the ship's technical efficiency and her measured operational performance at the same time, and unlike reducing speed it costs the operator no time.

2. Where the losses occur

A useful answer explains what each device is attacking before describing it. Overall propulsive efficiency is the product of hull efficiency, open-water (propeller) efficiency and relative rotative efficiency. The devices in this question act chiefly on the second, where the energy is lost in four ways:

  • Axial or slipstream loss. The propeller works by accelerating water aft; the kinetic energy left in that accelerated column is lost. It falls as the disc area is increased and the acceleration reduced.
  • Rotational loss. A single propeller leaves the race rotating. That swirl carries energy which does nothing to propel the ship, and it is the largest single recoverable loss in a conventional installation.
  • Frictional loss. Viscous drag over the blade surfaces - irreducible, and worsened by roughness and fouling.
  • Tip vortex loss. At the blade tip the pressure difference between face and back equalises around the edge, shedding a strong vortex which carries away energy and is a principal source of pressure pulses, hull vibration and cavitation erosion.

Cavitation sets the ceiling. Blade loading cannot simply be increased, because local pressure falling to vapour pressure produces cavities whose collapse causes erosion, vibration and noise. Every device below is an attempt to recover one of these losses without breaching that ceiling: the duct attacks axial loss and tip loss, Kappel attacks tip vortex loss, and contra-rotation attacks rotational loss.

3. Ducted propellers

Principle. The propeller runs inside an annular aerofoil - a nozzle, the classic form being the Kort nozzle. In an accelerating duct the internal section converges, so flow is accelerated into the disc; each blade then has to generate less thrust for the same total, and the duct itself, being a lifting surface with circulation, produces additional forward thrust. The close tip clearance also largely suppresses the tip vortex. A decelerating duct reverses the section to raise pressure at the disc and suppress cavitation and radiated noise - mainly a naval application.

The governing parameter is thrust loading. The duct earns its keep where the propeller is heavily loaded at low speed: tugs, trawlers, pushers, and slow, full-form ships whose diameter is limited by draught.

  • Merits. Substantial thrust augmentation at high loading, which is why bollard pull vessels are almost universally ducted; improved efficiency where the propeller would otherwise be overloaded; suppression of tip vortex and its associated erosion; physical protection of the propeller from debris and quay contact; improved course stability with a fixed nozzle; and, with a steerable nozzle, a genuine manoeuvring gain that can remove the need for a separate rudder.
  • Demerits. The duct is an appendage with its own drag, and at light loading and higher speed that drag exceeds the benefit - a duct can make a fast, lightly loaded ship worse. It fouls and corrodes, and marine growth inside the nozzle degrades performance quickly; it can be damaged or blocked by debris or ice; internal cavitation erosion occurs on the inner surface and at the tip gap; clearances are critical and alignment must be maintained; it adds weight and structure; and it complicates survey, inspection and propeller removal in dry dock.

4. Kappel propellers

Principle. A Kappel propeller is a conventional-diameter propeller whose blade tips are curved smoothly towards the suction side, forming an integrated, continuous winglet rather than a bolted-on end plate. The curvature allows blade loading to be carried further outboard while the tip vortex is weakened and displaced away from the blade. The aeronautical analogy is exact: it is the marine equivalent of an aircraft winglet, and it recovers energy from the same mechanism.

  • Merits. It reduces tip vortex loss for the same diameter, which matters when draught or aperture fixes the diameter. There is no additional appendage, so no added drag, no added fouling surface and no new maintenance item. The weakened tip vortex produces a more uniform wake into the rudder, reducing pressure pulses, hull vibration and radiated noise - a real habitability and structural benefit as well as an efficiency one. And it is retrofittable as a straight propeller replacement at a scheduled dry docking.
  • Demerits. The gain is modest and is highly dependent on the hull wake field, so it must be designed for the specific ship - which means CFD and model testing, and a high design cost that is hard to justify on a single vessel. The curved tip geometry is complex to cast, dearer than a conventional blade, and more difficult to repair, straighten or re-pitch after damage; the tip is also relatively vulnerable. Finally, the benefit is design-point dependent - a ship trading persistently away from the draught and speed for which the blade was designed may realise little of it.

5. Contra-rotating propellers

Principle. Two propellers are mounted coaxially on the same axis and turn in opposite directions. The forward propeller leaves a rotating race; the after propeller, turning the other way, is designed to recover that rotational energy and convert it into additional thrust. Because rotational loss is the largest single recoverable loss in a conventional installation, this attacks the biggest target directly. Mechanically it requires either coaxial inner and outer shafting with epicyclic gearing, or a podded after unit driven electrically in the hybrid arrangement.

  • Merits. The highest theoretical efficiency gain of the three, because it recovers swirl rather than merely avoiding a loss. The total thrust is divided between two propellers, so loading per propeller falls - which reduces cavitation risk, pressure pulses and induced vibration, and permits a smaller diameter for a given thrust, valuable where draught constrains the aperture. Torque reaction is largely cancelled, improving course keeping and reducing rudder correction.
  • Demerits. The objection is mechanical, and it is serious. The arrangement demands concentric inner and outer shafting with complex reduction and reversing gearing, and the sealing of two concentric shafts at the stern tube is a recognised reliability weak point. Capital cost is high; alignment, maintenance, and dry-dock withdrawal are all substantially harder; there are more components to fail in a system whose failure immobilises the ship. Service experience in the large merchant fleet remains limited, so spares support and operator confidence lag the theory - which, more than the engineering, is why adoption has been slow.

6. Choosing between them, and the printed demand

The three are not competitors for the same ship. The choice follows the thrust loading coefficient and the operating profile:

  • Heavily loaded, low speed, high thrust - tug, trawler, pusher, slow full-form ship: the duct earns its drag several times over.
  • Moderately loaded single screw with a reasonably clean wake: a tip-modified blade such as Kappel gives a real gain with no new appendage and no new maintenance.
  • Where rotational loss is large and mechanical complexity is acceptable: contra-rotating offers the most, at the highest engineering and commercial risk.
  • Lightly loaded and fast: a duct is likely to make the ship worse, and this is the trap in the question - a device is not "high efficiency" in the abstract.

Two points on answering. The stem asks for any three of the four, with merits and demerits. Three treated properly earns more than four treated superficially, and an answer that gives only merits has answered half the question. And give no percentage figures: published gains are manufacturer-sourced and depend entirely on hull form, wake field, loading and trade. A correctly explained mechanism is worth more than a number you cannot defend.

The fourth option - azimuth propulsion

The Model Answer takes ducted, Kappel and contra-rotating, which is the printed demand of three. If you would rather substitute azimuth propulsion for one of them, here is the same treatment.

Principle. The propeller is carried in a steerable unit that rotates about a vertical axis, so thrust can be directed through a full circle. Drive is either mechanical through bevel gearing in a Z-drive, or electrical to a motor housed in a submerged pod. In a pulling (tractor) configuration the propeller runs ahead of the pod, in undisturbed inflow, which is the main hydrodynamic advantage.

  • Merits. The propeller works in a clean, uniform wake rather than in the hull's disturbed boundary layer, which improves efficiency and reduces cavitation and vibration; no rudder, no long shaft line, no stern tube and no separate reversing arrangement; outstanding manoeuvrability with full thrust in any direction and strong crash-stop performance; freed hull volume forward of the pod for cargo or machinery; and, with diesel-electric drive, the prime movers can be loaded efficiently and sited flexibly.
  • Demerits. High capital cost and dependence on the electrical chain, so a switchboard, converter or motor fault removes propulsion; the pod is a submerged, largely inaccessible unit, so significant repair usually means dry docking and specialist attendance; slewing bearings and seals are highly loaded and are the recognised failure mode; appendage drag of the pod and strut partly offsets the inflow benefit at higher speeds; conversion losses through the electrical chain; and crew familiarity and spares support are less widespread than for conventional shafting.

Common mistakes

  • Describing all four superficially when the stem asked for three. This is the single commonest error on this question.
  • Giving merits only. The stem says merits and demerits; half the marks are in the second half.
  • Quoting percentage gains. Vendor figures, hull-specific, indefensible under questioning.
  • Saying a duct always improves efficiency. It does not - at light loading its drag makes the ship worse. Loading is the whole story.
  • Confusing Kappel with a bolted-on end plate or a tip fin. It is an integrated blade geometry.
  • Treating contra-rotating as merely "two propellers". The point is recovery of rotational energy.
  • Omitting cavitation, which is the constraint that makes all of this difficult.

Examiner traps

  • Thrust loading decides everything about a duct. If you can state that, you have shown you understand rather than remember.
  • Accelerating against decelerating ducts. The second exists for cavitation and noise, not for efficiency.
  • The vibration benefit. Kappel and contra-rotation both reduce pressure pulses into the hull - an easy extra mark that connects to habitability and structural fatigue.
  • The stern tube seal on concentric shafting. The most credible practical objection to contra-rotating propellers, and a Chief Engineer is expected to raise it.
  • Design point. Every one of these devices is optimised for a condition; a ship trading away from it realises less.

Likely oral follow-up

  • "Why is a tug ducted and a container ship not?" - thrust loading. High loading at low speed is where the accelerating duct pays; at light loading its drag dominates.
  • "What is the largest recoverable loss in a conventional propeller?" - the rotational loss left in the race, which is what contra-rotation targets.
  • "How does a winglet on a propeller help?" - it weakens and displaces the tip vortex, reducing the energy shed at the tip and the pressure pulses into the hull.
  • "What limits how hard you can load a blade?" - cavitation, and its erosion, vibration and noise consequences.
  • "What would you inspect on a ducted propeller in dry dock?" - tip clearance and duct alignment, internal erosion at the tip gap, fouling and coating condition, structural attachment and any debris damage.
  • "You are offered a propeller retrofit. How would you justify it?" - measured hull and propeller performance data before and after, separating the effect from weather, loading and fouling. This links directly to Q1.

Model written answer

1. The instrument, and which revision applies

The question names MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3. The circular was originally approved jointly by the Facilitation Committee and the Maritime Safety Committee in 2017, superseding the interim guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1526. The edition in force at this examination is Rev.3, dated 4 April 2025, approved by the Maritime Safety Committee at its 108th session (15 to 24 May 2024) and the Facilitation Committee at its forty-ninth session (10 to 14 March 2025). Name the revision you are answering on - this circular has been revised three times and the content of three of the four limbs below depends on which edition you mean.

The Guidelines define maritime cyber risk as a measure of the extent to which a technology asset is threatened by a potential circumstance or event, which may result in shipping-related operational, safety or security failures as a consequence of information or systems being corrupted, lost or compromised (annex 1.1). Note the breadth: corrupted, lost or compromised - loss of availability counts, so a failure with no attacker at all is still a cyber risk.

Rev.3 also introduces the organising term used throughout the current text: a Computer Based System (CBS) is a programmable electronic device, or interoperable set of programmable electronic devices, and CBSs on board include both IT and OT systems. The Guidelines remain expressly recommendatory; section 7 below explains why that does not make them optional.

2. The onboard systems exposed to cyber risk

Annex 2.2.1 makes the point the question is quoting: cyber-technologies are essential to systems critical to safety, security and protection of the marine environment, but the vulnerabilities created by accessing, interconnecting or networking these systems create cyber risks. The Guidelines say vulnerable systems could include, but are not limited to - so this is a floor, not a closed list. Under Rev.3 there are nine categories:

  • Bridge systems - and Rev.3 spells out the examples: navigation systems, ship safety systems and communications systems. ECDIS, radar and ARPA, GNSS receivers, AIS, VDR, the integrated navigation system and the autopilot all sit here.
  • Cargo, bunkering, lubrication, ballast and other pumping handling and management systems - loading computers and stability software, tanker cargo control, container and reefer monitoring, level and valve control, and the ballast water management system.
  • Propulsion, fuel and machinery management and power control systems - main engine and auxiliary control, the alarm and monitoring system, the power management system, fuel-changeover control and remote OEM diagnostics.
  • Security, access control and surveillance systems - ship security alarm arrangements, CCTV, electronic door and gangway control.
  • Passenger and crew servicing and management systems - boarding, embarkation and property management systems holding personal and payment data for passengers and crew.
  • Passenger, crew and subcontracted service personnel facing public networks - passenger and crew Wi-Fi and entertainment, which are exposed by design, and the networks offered to riding squads and service technicians.
  • Administrative and crew welfare systems - the ship business network, planned maintenance and procurement, and crew internet.
  • Ship-port interfaces - the systems through which the ship exchanges data with the terminal and the port community.
  • Ship-to-shore integrated systems - for example remote control systems and Maritime Autonomous Surface Ship arrangements, where a shore station participates directly in controlling the ship.

One structural point is worth a line: the last two heads, ship-port interfaces and ship-to-shore integrated systems, extend the boundary of the problem beyond the ship's own hull, which is the direction the whole subject is moving.

3. What cyber risk management means, and where it starts

Annex 3.1 defines cyber risk management as the process of identifying, analysing, assessing, and communicating a cyber-related risk and accepting, avoiding, transferring or mitigating it to an acceptable level, considering costs and benefits of actions taken to stakeholders. Two halves: assess the risk, then take one of four decisions about it. The goal (3.2) is to support safe and secure shipping that is operationally resilient to cyber risks - resilience, not immunity.

Annex 3.3 places the starting point: effective cyber risk management should start at the senior management level, which should embed a culture of cyber risk awareness into all levels of the organization and ensure a regime that is holistic and flexible, in continuous operation and constantly evaluated through effective feedback mechanisms. Annex 3.4 gives the accepted method: compare the organization's current and desired postures, and address the gaps through a prioritized cyber risk management plan, so resources go where the risk is.

4. The six functional elements

Annex 3.5 sets out six functional elements, and states that they are not sequential - all should be concurrent and continuous in practice and incorporated appropriately in a risk management framework. Rev.3 adds that the controls listed under each element are the minimum controls, and more may be required depending on the risk evaluation:

  • GOVERN - establish and monitor the organization's cyber risk management strategy, expectations and policies; define personnel roles and responsibilities for cyber risk management; and ensure business continuity, such as backup management and disaster recovery, and crisis management.
  • Identify - determine the current cyber risk to ships and to ship/port interfaces, and identify the systems, assets, data and capabilities that, when disrupted, pose risks to ship operations.
  • Protect - implement risk control processes and measures to protect Computer Based Systems, together with contingency planning, so that shipping operations continue.
  • Detect - develop, implement and practise the activities necessary to detect a cyber-event in a timely manner.
  • Respond - develop, implement and practise activities and plans to provide resilience and to restore systems necessary for shipping operations or services impaired by a cyber-event.
  • Recover - identify and implement measures to restore onboard Computer Based Systems impacted by a cyber-event.

Govern is the element that is easiest to under-answer, and it is now first. It carries two named sub-controls: designate a person or entity accountable for the planning, resourcing and execution of cybersecurity activities, and ensure that person or entity has the necessary authority and support, and sufficient knowledge and expertise in cyber risk management. In other words the circular now asks, in terms, who owns this and can they actually do it - which is a management question rather than a technical one and belongs in an Engineering Management answer.

Note also the verb that Rev.3 repeats: practise. Detect and Respond both require the activities to be practised, not merely written. Annex 3.6 adds that together the elements constitute an ongoing process with effective feedback mechanisms, and 3.7 that awareness must be appropriate to each person's role. Writing the elements out as a six-step sequence to be worked through once is the commonest error on this limb.

5. Why cyber security matters in the maritime sector

The Guidelines give the reason directly: risk management has traditionally been focused on the physical domain, and greater reliance on digitization, integration, automation and network-based systems has created an increasing need for cyber risk management in shipping. Five features make the shipping case distinct:

  • A compromise becomes a safety event. Annex 2.2.5 says so - where vulnerabilities are exposed there are implications for the confidentiality, integrity and availability of data and for the safety and security of the ship, particularly where bridge navigation, main propulsion or cargo on/off-loading systems are compromised. Grounding, collision, loss of propulsion and pollution are cyber outcomes, not merely data outcomes.
  • IT and OT are connected, so an office problem becomes an engine-room problem. The mechanism is inappropriate connection between the two, or a procedural lapse such as the inappropriate use of removable media.
  • The threat need not be malicious. Software maintenance and user permissions sit alongside hacking and malware as sources of cyber risk.
  • The exposure runs down the supply chain. Annex 2.2.6 requires effective management to consider third-party vendors, embedded systems, and the software and hardware supply chains, and the maintenance devices brought aboard to service CBSs - the service laptop is itself an attack path.
  • The ship is hard to defend. Inadequacies in design, integration and maintenance and lapses in cyberdiscipline - weak passwords, absence of network segregation. Mixed-vendor equipment on long lifecycles, a small rotating crew and a constrained satellite link make patching, monitoring and specialist support all harder than ashore.

The management conclusion the Guidelines draw is that technologies and threats change too quickly to be addressed only through technical standards - which is why the answer is a risk-management regime placed inside existing safety and security management practice rather than beside it.

Rev.3 promotes both to formal definitions at annex 2.1, and supplies its own examples - which the limb expressly asks for:

Operational Technology (OT) - CBSs focused on the use of data to control or monitor physical processes, for example the main engine's oil temperature levels, which are forwarded to the control room.

7. How a recommendatory circular becomes a real obligation

The Guidelines themselves are recommendatory, but the obligation is not. Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted 16 June 2017, affirms that an approved safety management system should take cyber risk management into account in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code, and encourages Administrations to ensure that cyber risks are appropriately addressed in safety management systems no later than the first annual verification of the company's Document of Compliance after 1 January 2021.

That date is long past at this examination, so the position is: the guidance on how to manage cyber risk is recommendatory, but whether it is managed at all is an ISM Code matter - verified at DOC and SMC audit and capable of being written up as a non-conformity or a port State control deficiency. A second layer now applies as well: IACS unified requirements UR E26 (cyber resilience of ships) and UR E27 (cyber resilience of on-board systems and equipment) apply to ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024, so for newer tonnage cyber resilience is a class requirement and not only an SMS one. For the detail, the circular points outside the Organization to the industry Guidelines on Cyber Security Onboard Ships, IACS Rec 166, ISO/IEC 27001, the NIST Framework and the IAPH port guidelines - and requires the most current version of whatever is used.

THE SINGLE FACT THAT DATES THIS ANSWER - there are SIX elements, not five

The operative edition at this sitting is MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3 of 4 April 2025, and it sets out SIX functional elements: GOVERN, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover. Govern is new and it is placed first. Rev.3 was approved by MSC 108 in May 2024 and FAL 49 in March 2025, and issued in April 2025 - six months before this paper.

This is the highest-value currency point in the whole paper, because a very large amount of published material still says five. Rev.2 of 7 June 2022 listed Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover, and textbooks, training notes, industry guidance and older model answers written on Rev.2 remain in circulation. A candidate who has learned IPDRR and stops there is giving the previous edition's answer. The mnemonic to carry into this examination is G-IPDRR, and the wheel image is unchanged - the elements are still not sequential.

What else Rev.3 changed

  • The vulnerable-systems list went from eight to nine, and moved from annex 2.1.1 to 2.2.1. This is a restructure, not an append. Communication systems ceased to be a separate head and became an example under bridge systems; six of the seven surviving heads were widened - cargo now covers bunkering, lubrication, ballast and other pumping; propulsion now covers fuel; access control became security, access control and surveillance; passenger servicing became passenger and crew; passenger-facing networks became passenger, crew and subcontracted service personnel facing. Two genuinely new heads appeared: ship-port interfaces and ship-to-shore integrated systems including remote control and MASS.
  • IT and OT were promoted from description to definition, at annex 2.1, with examples - and the OT example is a marine-engineering one, the main engine's oil temperature forwarded to the control room. For this paper that is the single most useful sentence in the circular, because limb (D) expressly asks for suitable examples.
  • Computer Based System (CBS) is newly defined and becomes the organising concept of the whole circular. IT and OT are both CBSs.
  • Segregation became a stated duty at annex 2.2.2 - OT systems should be segmented from IT systems, protected from Internet-facing systems, and have appropriate protection tools.
  • Supply chain and maintenance devices were brought in at annex 2.2.6 - third-party vendors, embedded systems, software and hardware supply chains, and the devices used to maintain CBSs.
  • The listed controls are now expressly the MINIMUM, with more required depending on the risk evaluation.

What did NOT change is just as useful: the definition of maritime cyber risk at 1.1, the definition of cyber risk management at 3.1, the goal of operational resilience at 3.2, senior-management ownership at 3.3, the current-versus-desired posture method at 3.4, the not-sequential statement at 3.5, and the whole MSC.428(98) linkage. Four of the seven route steps carry across untouched.

Why the elements are not a sequence

Annex 3.5 says in terms that the functional elements are not sequential and that all should be concurrent and continuous. That is not a drafting nicety. A ship does not stop identifying while it is protecting; detection runs permanently; and recovery capability - backups, restore procedures, spare configuration - has to be built long before an event, which is impossible if recovery is treated as the last step. The addition of Govern at the front makes the point sharper rather than weaker: governance is not a phase that finishes, it is the frame the other five sit inside. Say this explicitly and the limb is answered at a management level rather than a checklist level.

IT and OT - the distinction that actually earns the mark

Almost every candidate can say 'IT is computers and OT is machinery'. The Guidelines distinguish them by what the data is for: information versus control or monitoring of a physical process. That formulation is better because it classifies the awkward cases correctly. A loading computer is IT when it produces a stability report and OT when it drives valve and pump control. An alarm and monitoring system is OT even though it controls nothing - it monitors a physical process. The follow-on point is the priority inversion: ashore, confidentiality usually leads; on the plant side, availability and safety lead, which is why you cannot simply apply office IT practice to the engine room. Under Rev.3 you can also quote the circular's own examples back at the examiner, which is worth doing.

Where this sits in the wider regulatory picture

Three layers, and keeping them apart is worth a mark. MSC.428(98) is the obligation - cyber risk must be addressed in the SMS. MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.3 is the recommendatory guidance on how. Industry and class material - the BIMCO-led Guidelines on Cyber Security Onboard Ships, IACS Rec 166, ISO/IEC 27001, NIST - is the detail, named precisely because the Organization does not intend to write it.

Class requirements ARE in application at this sitting, and that is a change worth knowing. IACS unified requirements UR E26 (cyber resilience of ships) and UR E27 (cyber resilience of on-board systems and equipment) apply to ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024 - fifteen months before this paper. For newbuildings the subject is now a class matter as well as an ISM matter. Mentioning this shows exactly the currency awareness the subject rewards.

Common mistakes

  • Giving five functional elements. Rev.2 had five; Rev.3 has six, and Govern comes first. This is the most likely single error on the paper.
  • Naming eight vulnerable systems, or naming the Rev.2 eight. There are nine, and the headings themselves were rewritten - communication systems is no longer a separate head.
  • Giving the functional elements as a sequence to be worked through. Annex 3.5 says they are not sequential.
  • Answering limb (C) with generic cyber-security material - hackers, ransomware, statistics - instead of the shipping-specific reasoning in the circular.
  • Treating cyber risk as a malicious-attack problem only. Software maintenance and user permissions are in scope.
  • Saying the Guidelines are mandatory. They are recommendatory; the obligation comes from MSC.428(98) through the ISM Code.
  • Forgetting that the list says 'could include, but are not limited to' - presenting the nine as a closed list.
  • Defining OT as 'machinery' rather than by function. It is the use of data to control or monitor a physical process.
  • Omitting the convergence point in limb (D), which is where the real risk lives - and missing that Rev.3 now states segregation as a duty at 2.2.2.

Examiner traps

"How many elements of cyber risk management are there?" Six, under the edition governing this paper - Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover - and they are not sequential. Name the circular and revision you are answering on and the answer cannot be marked wrong. Anyone who tells you five is quoting Rev.2.

"Is the ECDIS an IT or an OT system?" OT - it uses data to monitor and support control of a physical process, navigation. It is also connected for chart updating, which is exactly the convergence risk.

"Your ship has no internet. Do the Guidelines apply?" Yes. Unintended consequences of benign actions are in scope, and removable media are the classic propagation route. An air-gapped ship is infected by memory stick.

"Who owns cyber risk on board?" This is now the Govern element: designate a person or entity accountable for planning, resourcing and executing cybersecurity activities, and ensure they have the authority, support and expertise to do it. Annex 3.3 still places the start at senior management level. It is not the ETO's private problem.

"Which IMO instrument makes this mandatory?" None makes the Guidelines mandatory. MSC.428(98) makes cyber risk an ISM Code matter, verified at the first annual DOC verification after 1 January 2021.

Model written answer

1. The resolution, and what made electronic record books lawful

Resolution MEPC.312(74), adopted 17 May 2019, contains the Guidelines for the use of electronic record books under MARPOL. The Guidelines did not by themselves permit anything: at the same session the Committee adopted MEPC.314(74), MEPC.316(74) and MEPC.317(74), which amended MARPOL Annexes I, II, V and VI and the NOx Technical Code 2008 to enable the use of electronic record books. The amendments entered into force 1 October 2020.

The division of labour matters. MARPOL creates the permission; the resolution tells the Administration how to decide whether a particular system may be approved. An electronic record book is expressly an alternative method to a hard copy record book (annex 2.2), not a relaxation of what must be recorded.

2. The record books that may be kept electronically

Annex 2.1 lists seven record books and recording requirements, and the Guidelines apply only to these:

  • Oil Record Book, Parts I and II - MARPOL Annex I, regulations 17.1 and 36.1;
  • Cargo Record Book - MARPOL Annex II, regulation 15.1;
  • Garbage Record Book, Parts I and II - MARPOL Annex V, regulation 10.3;
  • Ozone-depleting Substances Record Book - MARPOL Annex VI, regulation 12.6;
  • recording of the tier and on/off status of marine diesel engines - MARPOL Annex VI, regulation 13.5.3;
  • Record of Fuel Oil Changeover - MARPOL Annex VI, regulation 14.6; and
  • Record Book of Engine Parameters - NOx Technical Code, paragraph 6.2.2.7.

Two boundaries are worth stating because they are commonly crossed. The Guidelines do not cover electronic access to, or electronic versions of, certificates and other documents that do not log continuous operations (annex 2.3); and they do not address ship-to-office transmission of the data, because that is not a MARPOL record book requirement at all (annex 2.4). The Ballast Water Record Book is not in the list - it is not a MARPOL instrument.

3. How the electronic log book meets the MARPOL requirements

Annex 4.1 is the answer to limb (b), and its principle is that the output must satisfy the requirements of all relevant regulations under MARPOL (4.1.1). Four mechanisms deliver that:

  • Complete entries only. The system should allow only a complete entry to be saved for the master's verification. The resolution's own example: for an Annex V discharge at sea, the entry cannot be saved without the latitude and longitude (4.1.2).
  • Protected automatic inputs. Where data can be captured automatically it should be, to ensure accuracy; those inputs must be protected against manipulation or falsification; manual entry is allowed on equipment failure but the change of data source must be recorded; and the system must automatically record any attempt to manipulate or falsify data (4.1.2).
  • The MARPOL form is preserved. Entry fields and data formats should be consistent with other IMO electronic reporting, and the record book should be presented in the form specified in the relevant MARPOL Annex (4.1.3).
  • Retention and hard copy. The system must retain records for the minimum period specified in each Annex, and must be able to produce a hard copy of verified records for the master to certify as a true copy on request (4.1.4).

4. Keeping pace with amendment

Annex 4.2 adds an obligation that a paper book never had: because MARPOL and its Annexes continue to evolve, approved systems must be reviewed and updated so that relevant amendments are incorporated, and the update must be completed before the amendment enters into force. Crucially, an update must not cause loss of existing records nor make them unreadable, and the system must continue to present all records in the MARPOL form. A software product therefore carries a continuing compliance burden that a printed book does not.

Annex 4.4 handles the physical side: the record book must form part of the Information Technology Business Continuity Plan, with backup, data recovery and alternate power supplies; automatic backup to offline storage updated every time an entry changes; the offline record protected by cryptography, held read-only, transferable, durable and printable; that offline record digitally signed by the master with title, full name, date and time of signing, PDF recommended; and the equipment installed in compliance with IEC 60092 and IEC 60533.

5. Security - access control and audit logging

Annex 4.3 is headed Security and accountability of the electronic record book and answers limb (c) directly. Security rests on two mechanisms:

  • Role Based Access Control is critical, and at a minimum every user must have a unique personal login identifier and password. The resolution states the purpose plainly: this is what makes the user making an entry accountable for any false entries or omissions (4.3.1).
  • Audit Logging replaces the officer's signature that MARPOL requires. The log must record a user code or identifying symbol against each entry, uniquely identifying the user and whether that user accessed or amended an entry (4.3.2). Electronic signatures must meet authentication standards adopted by the Administration (4.3.3).

Entries must be protected against unauthorized deletion, destruction or amendment. Once saved, the information must be secured against unauthorized or untraceable change; any change must be automatically recorded and made visible in the system and in any printed output; and - the load-bearing sentence - the system must retain both the original entry and every amendment (4.3.4). An amendment should carry a reason and the user identifier of the officer making it (4.3.5).

6. Accountability - verification and the status of an entry

MARPOL requires verification, not merely entry - Annex I regulation 17 requires each page of the Oil Record Book to be signed by the master. The Guidelines reproduce that in software:

  • The master's verification requires an additional authentication factor - further credentials supplied by the master at the time of verification (4.3.6).
  • The system logs when entries were made, amended or verified, which exposes the practice of entering days or weeks of operations at once (4.3.7).
  • Each entry carries a status field - 'pending' or 'awaiting verification', becoming 'verified' once the master signs (4.3.8).
  • If a verified entry is later amended, the system automatically returns it to pending or re-verification and notifies the master (4.3.9).
  • The system reminds the master that verification is due, ideally before arrival in port, and unverified entries must carry a comment giving the reason (4.3.10).
  • Receipts and endorsements - a waste reception facility receipt, a Cargo Record Book endorsement - may be identified or attached to the relevant entry (4.3.11).

7. Approval, the Declaration, and inspection

A system meeting the criteria is given written confirmation by the Administration, which must be carried on board for surveys and inspections - in practice the Declaration of MARPOL Electronic Record Book at the appendix, naming the ship, IMO number, flag, gross tonnage, the Annexes covered, and the manufacturer, supplier, installer, software name and version (annex 5.1). The Administration may delegate the assessment and the issue of the declaration to a recognized organization, at its discretion (5.2).

At inspection, the system must meet company audit requirements including integration with the ISM Code safety management system, all flag State and survey requirements, the MARPOL control provisions and the Organization's port State control procedures, and must support detection of violations under MARPOL article 6 (6.1.1). Using an electronic book in no way relieves the owner of the duty to maintain and produce records; if the ship can produce neither the system nor the declaration, the officer should ask for an alternative verified copy or a hard copy book (6.1.2). For prosecution, any entry or the whole book must be printable and each page physically signed by the master as a 'true copy', carrying the entering officer's title and full name, any changes made, the date and time of printing, the system name and version, and page numbering (6.3).

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Sixteen marks, three limbs, no printed split - so roughly five marks each, and limb (a) is the one that can be answered perfectly in six lines. It is a closed list of seven, and the marks are in naming them with their Annex and regulation. Do not spend a page on it. Limb (b) is the conceptual limb: the system meets MARPOL because it forces completeness, preserves the MARPOL form, retains for the statutory period and can produce a certified hard copy. Limb (c) is the richest and the resolution has a section with exactly that title - annex 4.3, Security and accountability - so a candidate who has read it can out-answer everyone else.

The list is seven, and it is not the list most people give

This is where the question is won or lost. Automated summaries of this very resolution get the list wrong - one consulted during research offered the Ballast Water Record Book (not a MARPOL instrument at all), 'engine maintenance and fuel consumption logs' (not a MARPOL record), and omitted the Cargo Record Book, the ODS Record Book and the NOx Technical Code engine parameter book entirely. The correct seven come from annex 2.1 and nowhere else. Notice the shape of the list: three classic record books (Oil, Cargo, Garbage), three Annex VI items (ODS, engine tier and on/off status, fuel oil changeover) and one from the NOx Technical Code.

Why the amendment obligation is the interesting part of limb (b)

A paper record book is compliant on the day it is printed and stays compliant because it cannot change. Software has the opposite property. Annex 4.2 therefore imposes something no paper book carries: the approved system must be updated before the relevant MARPOL amendment enters into force, without losing or corrupting the existing records. That converts a one-off approval into a continuing obligation shared between the supplier, the company and the Administration, and it is a genuinely management-level observation - exactly the register this paper is set in.

Security and accountability are two different things

Candidates blur them. Security is about keeping the record trustworthy - role based access control, unique credentials, cryptography on the offline copy, protection against unauthorized deletion or amendment. Accountability is about being able to say who did what and when - the audit log against each entry, the master's separate authentication factor, the pending/verified status, and above all the rule that both the original entry and every amendment are retained and visible. That last rule is what makes the electronic book stronger evidence than paper, where a neat line through an entry destroys the original.

Why this matters commercially

Oil Record Book falsification is the most heavily prosecuted MARPOL offence there is, and annex 6.3 is written for the courtroom: the printed page must be signed by the master as a true copy and must show any changes that were made, the software name and version, and the print date and time. A Chief Engineer should read that as follows - with an approved electronic book, an amendment is not a correction, it is a permanent record of a correction. That is a protection for an honest engineer and an exposure for a careless one.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the Ballast Water Record Book. It is a BWM Convention document, not MARPOL, and is not in annex 2.1.
  • Giving only the Oil and Garbage Record Books. There are seven items, including the Cargo Record Book, the ODS Record Book, engine tier and on/off status, fuel oil changeover, and the NOx Technical Code engine parameter book.
  • Saying MEPC.312(74) permits electronic record books. It does not - the MARPOL amendments adopted by MEPC.314(74), MEPC.316(74) and MEPC.317(74) do, in force 1 October 2020. The resolution is the approval guidance.
  • Treating the electronic book as a relaxation. Annex 2.2 calls it an alternative method; every MARPOL recording requirement still applies.
  • Forgetting the Declaration that must be carried on board, and that a ship which can produce neither system nor declaration will be asked for a hard copy.
  • Saying an incorrect entry is deleted or corrected. Both the original and the amendment are retained and visible.
  • Omitting the master's separate authentication factor - it is what replaces the signature MARPOL requires.
  • Claiming the Guidelines cover transmission of data to the office. Annex 2.4 says they do not.

Examiner traps

"Name all the record books that can be electronic." Seven, and the word all in the question means the examiner is counting.

"Can the Chief Engineer correct a wrong entry?" Yes, and the correction is permanent and visible: the amendment is recorded with reason and user identifier, the original is retained, and a verified entry returns to pending for the master to re-verify.

"The PSCO asks for the Oil Record Book and the tablet will not start." Annex 6.1.2 - the officer should ask for an alternative verified copy or a hard copy, and the duty to produce records is unaffected. This is why annex 4.4.1 requires backup, recovery and alternate power supplies.

"Who approves the system?" The Administration, which may delegate the assessment and the declaration to a recognized organization.

"What must appear on a printed page for a prosecution?" The entering officer's title and full name, any changes made, date and time of printing, software name and version, and page numbering - plus the master's physical signature certifying it a true copy.

Model written answer

1. The Code, and what made it mandatory

The IMO Instruments Implementation Code (III Code) is the annex to resolution A.1070(28), adopted by the Assembly on 4 December 2013. It revoked resolution A.1054(27), the Code for the Implementation of Mandatory IMO Instruments, 2011 (operative paragraph 3), which had been the basis of the voluntary audit scheme.

The Code is recommendatory in its own drafting - it says what a State "should" do - but its use was made mandatory by amendments to the treaty instruments themselves: A.1083(28), A.1084(28) and A.1085(28) amended Load Lines 1966, Tonnage 1969 and COLREG 1972; MSC.366(93), MSC.373(93), MSC.374(93) and MSC.375(93) amended SOLAS 1974, STCW 1978, the STCW Code and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol; and MEPC.246(66) and MEPC.247(66) amended MARPOL and its 1997 Protocol. That chain is what turns the Code into the standard against which a Member State is audited under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme.

The Code has four parts: Part 1 Common Areas, Part 2 Flag States, Part 3 Coastal States and Part 4 Port States. Each of Parts 2, 3 and 4 ends with an Evaluation and review section, which is where limb (b) lives.

2. The objective of the Code

Paragraph 1 states the objective in one sentence: to enhance global maritime safety and protection of the marine environment, and to assist States in the implementation of instruments of the Organization. The second half matters as much as the first - the Code is an assistance instrument, written because the Organization accepted that some States face real difficulty in complying fully.

Paragraph 2 qualifies it. Different States will view the Code according to their own circumstances and should be bound only for the implementation of those instruments to which they are Contracting Governments or Parties; by virtue of geography and circumstance some States have a greater role as a flag State, others as a coastal or port State. The Code is therefore not a uniform checklist applied identically everywhere.

Two general provisions frame everything that follows. Paragraph 4 makes States responsible for promulgating laws and regulations and taking all other steps necessary to give those instruments full and complete effect. Paragraph 5 requires that, in acting against pollution, States must not transfer damage or hazards from one area to another, nor transform one type of pollution into another.

3. The strategy recommended to the flag State

Paragraph 3 is the answer to the second half of limb (a). To meet the objective, a State is recommended to:

  • develop an overall strategy to ensure that its international obligations and responsibilities as a flag, port and coastal State are met (3.1);
  • establish a methodology to monitor and assess that the strategy ensures effective implementation and enforcement of relevant international mandatory instruments (3.2); and
  • continuously review the strategy to achieve, maintain and improve overall organizational performance and capability as a flag, port and coastal State (3.3).

Read together those three are a develop - monitor - review cycle, not a document to be written once. Paragraph 9 completes it: the State should communicate its strategy, including information on its national legislation, to all concerned.

Paragraph 7 tells the State what its legislation and administrative procedures must actually address - nine areas: jurisdiction; organization and authority; legislation, rules and regulations; promulgation of the applicable mandatory instruments; enforcement arrangements; control, survey, inspection, audit, verification, approval and certification functions; the selection, recognition, authorization, empowerment and monitoring of recognized organizations and nominated surveyors; investigations required to be reported to the Organization; and reporting to the Organization and other Administrations. Paragraph 8 adds the three initial actions a Government must be capable of when a new or amended instrument enters into force: the ability to promulgate laws giving effective jurisdiction and control, a legal basis for enforcement including investigative and penal processes, and sufficient personnel with maritime expertise.

4. Which IMO instruments the Code covers

This limb has two correct answers and they are not the same, which is where marks are usually lost.

First, the Code's own scope. Paragraph 6 says the Code addresses those aspects necessary to give full and complete effect to the applicable international instruments pertaining to six subject areas:

  • safety of life at sea;
  • prevention of pollution from ships;
  • standards of training, certification and watchkeeping for seafarers;
  • load lines;
  • tonnage measurement of ships; and
  • regulations for preventing collisions at sea.

Note what is absent: maritime security is not one of the six. The preamble to the resolution speaks of safety, security and protection of the marine environment, but the operative scope at paragraph 6 does not extend to the ISPS Code or SOLAS chapter XI-2.

Second, the treaty instruments under which use of the Code is mandatory, and which therefore define the audit scope. These are the instruments listed in resolution A.1187(33) (6 December 2023), the 2023 Non-exhaustive list of obligations under instruments relevant to the III Code: SOLAS 1974 with its 1978 and 1988 Protocols; MARPOL and its 1997 Protocol; STCW 1978 and the STCW Code; Load Lines 1966 and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol; Tonnage 1969; and COLREG 1972. A.1187(33) revoked the 2021 list, A.1157(32), so it is the current list at this sitting; its annexes divide the obligations into those of Contracting Governments generally and those specific to flag, coastal and port States.

5. Key performance indicators for flag State evaluation

Paragraph 42 requires a flag State to evaluate its performance on a periodic basis with respect to the administrative processes, procedures and resources needed to meet its obligations. Paragraph 43 then lists the measures, inter alia:

  • port State control detention rates;
  • flag State inspection results;
  • casualty statistics;
  • communication and information processes;
  • annual loss statistics, excluding constructive total losses (CTLs); and
  • such other performance indicators as may be appropriate.

The stated purpose is diagnostic, and quoting it earns the mark: the indicators exist in order to determine whether staffing, resources and administrative procedures are adequate to meet its flag State obligations.

Paragraph 44 then recommends ten areas for regular review: fleet loss and accident ratios, to identify trends over selected time periods; the number of verified cases of detained ships in relation to the size of the fleet; verified cases of incompetence or wrongdoing by holders of certificates or endorsements issued under its authority; responses to port State deficiency reports or interventions; investigations into very serious and serious casualties and the lessons learned; technical and other resources committed; results of inspections, surveys and controls of ships in the fleet; investigation of occupational accidents; the number of incidents and violations under the applicable international pollution prevention regulations; and the number of suspensions or withdrawals of certificates, endorsements or approvals.

6. Flag State responsibilities

Part 2 is the longest part of the Code, paragraphs 15 to 44. Its structure is implementation - delegation - enforcement - surveyors - investigations - review.

  • Implementation (15-17). Issue national legislation and guidance implementing the safety and pollution conventions, and assign responsibility for keeping it updated. Paragraph 16 requires a safety and environmental protection programme with five minimum elements, of which the load-bearing one is 16.2: compliance verified by an audit and inspection programme independent of any body issuing the certificates. Paragraph 17 requires ships to be sufficiently and efficiently manned, on the Principles of Safe Manning.
  • Delegation of authority (18-21). A flag State authorising a recognized organization must determine that it has adequate technical, managerial and research capability; base the authorisation on a formal written agreement; issue specific instructions for a ship found unfit to proceed to sea; supply its national law and interpretations; and require the organization to keep records. Paragraph 20 requires an oversight programme with supplementary surveys and competent supervising staff.
  • Enforcement (22-27). Prohibit a ship from sailing until it complies; inspect periodically to verify that the actual condition of ship and crew matches the certificates; verify seafarer familiarity and the complement's ability to coordinate in an emergency; provide penalties of adequate severity for ships and for certificate holders; and institute proceedings after investigation irrespective of where the violation occurred. Paragraphs 26 and 27 permit certification only after determining that all applicable requirements are met.
  • Surveyors and investigations (28-41). Surveyors must be qualified against a defined standard, worked under a documented qualification system, and issued an identification document. Marine safety investigations must be by impartial and objective investigators; casualties are investigated taking account of the Casualty Investigation Code and the report is forwarded to the Organization with the flag State's observations.

7. Coastal State and port State responsibilities

Parts 3 and 4 open with the same principle, at paragraphs 45 and 52: coastal and port States have rights as well as obligations, and when they exercise those rights they incur additional obligations. Both must implement policy through national legislation and guidance (46, 54) and ensure their legislation and procedures deliver consistent implementation and verification (47, 55).

Coastal State (Part 3). Paragraph 48 lists the rights, obligations and responsibilities, inter alia: radiocommunication services; meteorological services and warnings; search and rescue services; hydrographic services; ships' routeing; ship reporting systems; vessel traffic services; and aids to navigation - eight service areas, and note that they are services to shipping, not controls over ships. Paragraph 50 requires a control and monitoring programme to allocate statistical data for trend analysis, to establish mechanisms for timely response to pollution incidents in its waters, and to cooperate with flag and port States in casualty investigations.

Port State (Part 4). Paragraph 53 records that the port State role derives from a combination of treaties, conventions, national laws and in some cases bilateral and multilateral agreements. Paragraph 56 gives three obligations: provision of appropriate reception facilities or capability to accept all waste streams regulated under IMO instruments; port State control; and keeping a register of fuel oil suppliers. Paragraph 59 carries the no more favourable treatment principle - the port State must impose the conditions of those instruments on non-parties as well as parties. Paragraphs 60 and 61 require a PSC programme consistent with the relevant Assembly resolution, carried out only by authorized and qualified port State control officers; paragraph 62 requires those officers to be free from commercial, financial and other pressures, to hold no commercial interest in the port, the ships inspected, repair facilities or support services, and not to be employed by or work on behalf of recognized organizations or classification societies.

Both parts close as Part 2 does: paragraphs 51 and 63 require the coastal State and the port State to periodically evaluate their own performance in exercising their rights and meeting their obligations. That symmetry - implement, enforce, evaluate - is the single most useful thing to carry into the exam hall, because it lets you structure all three States from one remembered shape.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed marks are 6 + 5 + 5 and the question hides four tasks inside limb (a): objective, strategy, instruments, and the implied "what is this Code". Splitting (a) across route steps 2, 3 and 4 stops the six marks being spent on a single paragraph about the objective. Limb (b) is worth five marks here - as much as limb (c) - and it has a precise source in paragraphs 42, 43 and 44, so a candidate who names the indicators verbatim banks those marks quickly. Give paragraph 44's review areas as well as paragraph 43's measures; on this split, limb (b) is no longer the cheap limb.

Limb (c) covers three States in five marks. The Code makes that tractable because Parts 2, 3 and 4 share a skeleton: implementation, enforcement, evaluation and review. Answer all three on that skeleton and the examiner sees structure rather than a list.

The mandatory-application chain, and why it matters

Candidates often assert that "the III Code is mandatory" without being able to say why a recommendatory-sounding text binds anybody. The answer is that the Code itself was never redrafted into obligation language; instead the conventions were amended to require its use. That is why the Code still reads "should" throughout. Being able to say this in one sentence distinguishes a candidate who has read the instrument from one who has read about it.

The audit scheme is the practical consequence. The Code supplies the auditable areas; A.1187(33) supplies the obligation-by-obligation list that auditors and Administrations work from. A.1187(33) was adopted 6 December 2023, replacing the 2021 list, A.1157(32), and it is the current list at this sitting. Material citing the 2021 list is superseded. Note the opposite risk as well: the list is revised on a roughly biennial cycle, and the 2025 list was not adopted until 3 December 2025 - after this examination. At this sitting A.1187(33) is the list to cite.

The six-versus-instruments trap, in detail

The printed words are "Which all IMO instruments covered in the code". Two things are being asked at once and the safe answer gives both. The Code's scope at paragraph 6 is expressed as six subject areas, not as a list of conventions. The instruments under which the Code is mandatory are the SOLAS / MARPOL / STCW / Load Lines / Tonnage / COLREG family with their Protocols, listed in A.1187(33).

A count is risky. The audit scope is usually summarised as nine mandatory instruments - SOLAS 1974, SOLAS PROT 1988, MARPOL, MARPOL PROT 1997, STCW 1978, LL 1966, LL PROT 1988, TONNAGE 1969 and COLREG 1972 - but A.1187(33) annex 1 also carries a SOLAS PROT 1978 column, and neither the Code nor A.1187(33) states a number anywhere. List them; do not lead with a count. If a count is wanted, say "the SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, Load Lines, Tonnage and COLREG instruments together with their Protocols, commonly summarised as nine".

The absence of maritime security from paragraph 6 is a genuine examinable point, not a drafting slip: the preamble uses the phrase "safety, security and protection of the marine environment" while the operative scope does not extend to security instruments.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a general essay on flag State duties under UNCLOS article 94. That is a different question. The examiner has named a specific Code and wants its paragraphs.
  • Answering limb (a)'s "which instruments" with the six subject areas only, or with the convention list only. Give both and say why they differ.
  • Confusing the III Code with the ISM Code. The III Code binds States; the ISM Code binds companies and ships.
  • Treating the performance indicators as something the flag State reports to IMO. They are for the State's own periodic self-evaluation under paragraph 42.
  • Giving the coastal State a control-and-inspection role. Paragraph 48 is a list of services - SAR, hydrography, VTS, aids to navigation.
  • Spending half the answer on limb (c) because it is familiar. It is worth five marks - one less than limb (a), and no more than limb (b).

Examiner traps

  • Six subject areas versus the instrument list. The single most likely place to lose marks on this question.
  • "Should" throughout the Code. If asked whether the Code is mandatory, the answer is that its use is mandatory under the amended conventions, while the text remains recommendatory in form.
  • Excluding constructive total losses. Paragraph 43 says annual loss statistics excluding CTLs. Reproducing that qualifier shows the source has been read.
  • Independence. Two independence requirements appear, in different Parts and for different reasons - the flag State's audit programme must be independent of the certifying body (16.2), and PSCOs must be free of commercial interest and not work for recognized organizations (62). Do not merge them.
  • A.1054(27) was revoked. An answer that cites the 2011 Code as current is citing a revoked instrument.

Likely oral follow-up

  • "Is the III Code mandatory, and how?" - use the amendment chain.
  • "What is IMSAS and what does it audit against?" - the Code supplies the auditable areas; A.1187(33) supplies the obligations list.
  • "Your flag State has a rising detention rate. What does the Code expect it to do?" - paragraphs 43 and 44: treat it as a performance indicator, review detentions against fleet size, review responses to PSC deficiency reports, and test whether staffing and resources are adequate.
  • "What must a flag State do before authorising a class society?" - paragraph 18: capability, a formal written agreement, specific instructions for unfit ships, national law and interpretations, records.
  • "Name three port State obligations under the Code." - reception facilities, port State control, register of fuel oil suppliers.
  • "Which State investigates a casualty, and who gets the report?" - paragraphs 38 and 41: the flag State provides qualified investigators irrespective of casualty location; the report goes to the Organization with the flag State's observations.

Memory framework

Two shapes carry the whole question. The Code's shape is common areas, then flag, then coastal, then port. The shape inside each State part is implementation, enforcement, evaluation and review. Everything in limb (c) hangs on the second shape, and limb (b) is simply the "evaluation and review" box of the flag State part opened up.

For the coastal State's eight services, group them as talk, warn, rescue, chart (radiocommunication, meteorological warnings, SAR, hydrographic) and route, report, watch, mark (ships' routeing, ship reporting, VTS, aids to navigation).

Flag State surveyors and investigations - the detail behind route step 6

Limb (c) is five marks for three States, so the Model Answer gives the flag State the headline of paragraphs 28 to 41 rather than the whole regime. The detail below is worth holding for the oral, and for any future question that asks about surveyor competence directly.

  • Qualification (para 29). Either appropriate qualifications from a marine or nautical institution with relevant seagoing experience as a certificated officer holding or having held a valid management level certificate of competency, with maintained technical knowledge; or a degree or equivalent from a tertiary institution in a relevant field of engineering or science recognised by the flag State; or accreditation as a surveyor through a formalised training programme reaching the same standard.
  • Experience (paras 30-31). Personnel qualified under 29.1 should have served not less than three years at sea as a deck or engine officer; those qualified under 29.2 should have worked in a relevant capacity for at least three years.
  • Knowledge (para 36). The qualification should encompass knowledge of the applicable international and national rules; of survey, certification, control, investigative and oversight procedures; understanding of the goals of the instruments and of the processes both on board and ashore; professional competency; full safety awareness; and training or experience in the tasks performed.
  • Identification (para 37). The flag State issues an identification document for the surveyor to carry when performing his or her tasks.
  • Investigator expertise (para 39). Ready access is required to expertise in navigation and the Collision Regulations, flag State certificate-of-competency regulations, causes of marine pollution, interviewing techniques, evidence gathering, and evaluation of the effects of the human element.
  • Reporting threshold (para 40). Any accident involving personal injury necessitating absence from duty of three days or more, and any deaths resulting from occupational accidents and casualties, should be investigated and the results made public.

Uncertainty and instrument counting

The answer deliberately does not lead with a number of instruments. A.1070(28) states six subject areas and states no instrument count; A.1187(33) lists obligations by instrument without totalling them. The figure of nine is a widely used summary of the mandatory instruments and is offered as such, not as a quotation from either resolution. A candidate who writes "nine" will not be marked wrong, but a candidate who lists the instruments cannot be.

The Framework and Procedures for the Member State Audit Scheme was not re-read for this answer, so no claim is made here about audit periodicity or about the scheme's own procedural rules. The answer restricts itself to what the Code and the obligations list say.

Understand this first

This is a management-level incident report, not a fault-finding exercise, and the stem tells you its three parts: WHY it failed, what you did NOW for the rest of this voyage, and what must change so it NEVER happens again. The engineering insight the examiner is looking for is a distinction: losing bridge control is not the same as the engine being unable to go astern, and neither is the same as an astern order given too late to stop the ship. Those three have three different remedies, and the engine movement recorder tells you which one you had. The safety line you must not cross is resetting trips or bypassing interlocks to get moving before you know the cause.

Model written answer

1. The report, and the evidence behind it

The report is addressed to the Designated Person and the technical superintendent, copied to the master, and headed with ship, voyage, port, berth, date and time and the bare fact - that on approaching the dock gates the main engine did not respond to bridge control, ran ahead when astern was ordered, and the bow made contact with the dock wall.

It is structured as summary; sequence of events; damage sustained; cause; temporary measures instituted; permanent measures recommended. Two disciplines matter more than the format. Separate fact from opinion, and label the opinion as opinion - this report will be read by the classification society, by underwriters and quite possibly by an investigating authority, and a confident wrong diagnosis in the first report is very difficult to withdraw. And preserve the evidence before anything is reset or repaired.

Attached: the engine movement and telegraph logger record, the alarm and monitoring history, the control system fault log downloaded rather than cleared, the VDR data secured before it is overwritten, the bell book and engine room log, and the maintenance and test records for the remote control, control air and starting air systems, including the date of the last proven astern test.

2. Immediate action and the state of the ship

  • Control was taken at the local manoeuvring platform, the engine stopped and astern movement obtained locally; the master was informed immediately and the bridge advised to call for tug assistance.
  • After contact: forepeak, forward tanks and void spaces sounded, and an internal inspection made for set-in plating, fractured or cracked welds, and ingress. Forward draught, list and trim checked for change.
  • Pollution risk assessed - whether any bunker or oily tank lies in the affected area, and whether any boundary is compromised.
  • Machinery secured and not restarted on bridge control; the engine left available on local control only.
  • Notifications made to company, classification society, flag State and the port authority as the circumstances required, and the occurrence recorded as a reportable event under the safety management system.

3. Why the engines did not respond

The bridge control chain runs: bridge lever and telegraph → control transmitter → remote control system → safety and interlock logic → control air → starting air distributor and manoeuvring valves → reversing arrangement → fuel. A failure anywhere in that chain produces the same symptom on the bridge, which is why the report must work through it systematically rather than name a favourite cause.

  • Control signal. Bridge lever encoder or potentiometer fault; chafed, broken or water-ingressed control cable; loose terminal; failed input/output card; loss of control system power or a failed UPS battery that had never been load-tested.
  • Control logic and location. Control not correctly transferred to the bridge, or a take-over left in an intermediate state; a mismatch between lever position and actual demand; loss of shaft speed or direction feedback, so the system never confirmed the reversal and held the ahead order.
  • Control air. On pneumatic and hybrid systems this is the classic cause of an intermittent failure: low control air pressure, moisture, oil carry-over or ice in the lines, a sticking pilot valve, a leaking diaphragm, a blocked orifice, a dirty filter or reducing valve, or water carried over from an undrained receiver.
  • Starting air. Receiver pressure fallen after repeated manoeuvres; starting air distributor timing or leakage; a stuck cylinder starting valve; the main starting valve failing to open; line drains left shut.
  • Reversing arrangement. On a camshaft engine, low servo oil pressure or a sticking reversing servo so the camshaft failed to shift, worn linkage, or a reversing interlock never satisfied so fuel was never admitted in the astern direction. On an electronically controlled engine, an ECU or hydraulic control unit fault, or an angle encoder failure, with a latched alarm blocking the reversal.
  • Fuel side. A sticking fuel rack or index linkage, so the engine received the order but no fuel.
  • Interlocks and safety system. Turning gear interlock, a low lubricating oil pressure or other shutdown active, an unacknowledged alarm inhibiting a start - or, worse, an override left in place from earlier work.
  • Human and organisational. This must be stated honestly: the astern movement may have been ordered too late for the engine to absorb the headway, or the astern power may have been insufficient for the displacement and approach speed. It is also relevant whether a full astern trial was carried out before arrival and, if not, why not.

The most probable composite explanation, and the one the report should advance as a working hypothesis until proven, is a latent defect in the remote control or control air system which only manifested under the rapid, repeated manoeuvring of a dock approach, on a system whose astern function had not recently been proven under load. No protective trip should be reset, and no interlock bypassed, before the cause is established - resetting destroys the evidence and may re-create the hazard.

4. Lost control, incapable engine, or a late order

The report must distinguish three genuinely different failures, because the temporary and permanent measures differ completely and an incorrect classification sends the company down the wrong corrective path.

  • Loss of remote (bridge) control. The engine was capable of running astern but never received or executed the order. The remedy is immediate transfer to the engine control room or local station - which is precisely why those independent control positions are required - and then repair of the control system.
  • Mechanical inability to produce astern thrust. The engine could not start or run astern at all: starting air, reversing arrangement, or fuel. Transferring control would have achieved nothing; the remedy is mechanical.
  • An adequate response ordered too late. The engine did respond, but the headway could not be absorbed in the distance available. The remedy is navigational and procedural - notice of engines, approach speed, tug use - not engineering.

The engine movement recorder settles it: it shows what was ordered on the bridge, at what time, whether the engine received the order, and whether and when the shaft actually reversed. Until that record has been read, the report should attribute cause to neither the bridge nor the engine room. Saying so explicitly is a mark of a competent report rather than a defensive one.

5. Temporary measures for the remainder of the voyage

These were instituted immediately and are recorded as risk controls, not repairs.

  • All manoeuvring from the engine control room or local platform until the cause is proven and rectified, with a qualified engineer stationed at the manoeuvring platform for every arrival, departure and passage in confined waters.
  • Two independent, tested means of communication between bridge and engine room, with an agreed and rehearsed order-and-repeat protocol, and the engine movement book kept manually.
  • A full ahead and astern engine trial before every arrival, conducted at a safe distance from danger and recorded - the astern movement in particular, which is the function that failed.
  • Control air system: receivers and lines drained, filters renewed or dried, reducing valves checked, air dryer proved, and control air pressure and quality confirmed before each manoeuvring period.
  • Starting air: both receivers at full pressure before manoeuvring, compressors confirmed on automatic, and all drains proved clear.
  • Increased notice of engines, earlier stand-by, and a reduced approach speed; tug assistance for berthing and unberthing for the remainder of the voyage.
  • The master to brief pilots and port authorities of the restriction, and to consider daylight-only berthing.
  • No undocumented overrides, and no bypass or isolation without the agreement of class.
  • A written risk assessment and a toolbox talk with the engine room and bridge teams; class notified and attendance arranged at the next convenient port.

6. Permanent measures - technical

  • Root cause analysis with the maker's service engineer and with class in attendance, working from the downloaded fault log rather than from a cleared system. Repair or renew the proven defective component, not the suspected one.
  • If the control system is obsolete or no longer supported, plan a controlled upgrade or retrofit with assured spares support, rather than repeated repair of unobtainable parts.
  • Control air quality. Fit or renew a refrigerant or desiccant dryer and coalescing filters, fit automatic drains, use corrosion-resistant piping, and introduce periodic dew point checks. Fit a low control air pressure alarm if not already provided.
  • Verify genuine redundancy. Confirm that the bridge, engine control room and local positions are truly independent, with separate power supplies and a UPS with a tested battery and a defined replacement interval. Establish that no single point of failure is common to all three - which is the design question this incident raises.
  • Critical spares. Hold control cards, solenoid and pilot valves, pressure switches and position sensors on board as identified critical spares.
  • Fit independent shaft speed and direction of rotation indication on the bridge if it is not already provided, so that the bridge team can see whether the engine has actually reversed rather than inferring it from the telegraph.

7. Permanent measures - management and follow-up

  • Report and analyse the occurrence under the safety management system, with corrective action assigned, dated and verified. A propulsion failure resulting in contact damage is exactly the kind of hazardous occurrence the system exists to capture.
  • Identify the remote control system and the control air system as critical equipment. Critical equipment attracts specific measures to promote reliability, including regular testing of arrangements that are not in continuous use - and the astern function under bridge control is precisely such an arrangement.
  • Planned maintenance. Bring the remote control system, control air system, starting air distributor, reversing servo and interlock logic into the PMS with defined intervals and, critically, functional tests rather than visual inspections. A test that does not prove the astern function does not test what failed.
  • Contingency procedure. Write a documented loss of bridge control procedure into the SMS - who takes control, where, how the order is passed and acknowledged, and what the bridge does meanwhile - and drill it jointly with the bridge team, not as an engine room exercise.
  • Checklists. Amend the pre-arrival and manoeuvring checklists to require a recorded ahead and astern engine test at a stated time before arrival, and include propulsion status and any restriction in the master and pilot exchange.
  • Precursors. Review earlier logs and defect reports for intermittent faults on the same system, and state in the report whether any existed and, if so, why they were not escalated. If the answer is uncomfortable, that is the most valuable finding in the document.
  • Fleet action. Check sister ships and similar installations across the fleet, and review the company PMS templates. Close the loop by verifying effectiveness at the next internal audit rather than closing the non-conformity on the strength of the repair alone.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem is a single paragraph but it contains three explicit demands: why the engines failed, the temporary measure instituted to avoid further trouble on this voyage, and permanent measures to avoid repetition. Roughly a third of the marks each. The commonest failure is to write four hundred words of fault-finding and then two lines on permanent measures, throwing away a third of the question.

The second structural decision is route step 4. Separating loss of remote control from mechanical inability to go astern from a late order is what turns a list of possible faults into an engineer's analysis - and the examiner is looking for exactly that judgement, because the three lead to entirely different corrective action.

Answer it as a report, because it says so

The stem says "As Chief Engineer, report to head office". Use report form: a heading, short numbered sections, and factual language. Two things earn credit almost free - separating fact from opinion, and listing the attachments (movement recorder, alarm history, fault log, VDR, bell book, logs, maintenance records). They show you understand who will read the document and what it will be used for.

Do not use the report to allocate blame. A first report that blames the bridge, or the engine room, before the movement recorder has been read is a report that will have to be withdrawn.

The safety point that must not be got wrong

Never reset protective trips or bypass interlocks to get the engine running again before the cause is known. A trip that operated may have been doing its job; an interlock that blocked the reversal may have been protecting the engine. Resetting also destroys the evidence in the fault log. If an isolation or bypass is unavoidable to move the ship, it must be risk-assessed, documented, time-limited and agreed with class - never done quietly.

Common mistakes

  • Writing only about the engine and ignoring the control system, which is where this fault almost certainly lay.
  • Giving one cause confidently. The scenario does not state the engine or control architecture; a fault tree is the correct answer, and saying why is a strength.
  • Skipping temporary measures, which are a third of the marks and the part that shows operational judgement.
  • Recommending "repair the fault" as the permanent measure. The permanent measure is the system change that stops it recurring - testing regime, air quality, redundancy, PMS, critical spares.
  • Forgetting the ship. After contact with a dock wall, soundings, internal inspection and pollution assessment come before diagnosis.
  • Blaming the bridge team. It may be right, but not before the record is read.
  • Recommending resetting trips to prove the engine works.

Examiner traps

  • Loss of control is not inability to go astern. The single most valuable distinction in the answer.
  • Control air moisture. The classic cause of an intermittent failure that appears only under repeated manoeuvring - and a favourite oral question.
  • Repeated manoeuvring depletes starting air. A dock approach is exactly when receiver pressure falls and the distributor is worked hardest.
  • The astern function is rarely tested. Ahead running proves itself every day; astern is proved only when it is deliberately tested, which is why it is the function that fails when needed.
  • Critical equipment. Naming the remote control and control air systems as critical equipment, requiring regular testing of arrangements not in continuous use, is the management-level answer.
  • Precursors. Asking whether the fault had been seen before, and whether it was escalated, is what distinguishes a management-level report.

Likely oral follow-up

  • "The engine will not answer the bridge. What do you do first?" - take local control, get the engine responding from the platform, tell the bridge, and get tugs. Diagnose second.
  • "How would you know whether the bridge ordered astern?" - the engine movement and telegraph logger, and the VDR.
  • "Why does control air moisture cause an intermittent fault?" - water and oil collect in low points and pilot valves, and a valve that works in steady conditions sticks under rapid repeated operation, or ice forms on expansion.
  • "Would you reset the trip to get moving?" - no, not before the cause is known; and any unavoidable bypass must be risk-assessed, documented, time-limited and agreed with class.
  • "What is your permanent recommendation in one sentence?" - a proven astern test before every arrival, control air drying and filtration, verified redundancy with separate supplies, and both systems identified as critical equipment in the PMS.
  • "What do you check on the ship after contact?" - forepeak and forward tank soundings, internal structural inspection, draughts and trim, and pollution risk.

Memory framework

The report has three demands - WHY, NOW, NEVER AGAIN. For WHY, walk the chain in order: LEVER, SIGNAL, LOGIC, AIR, START, REVERSE, FUEL, INTERLOCK. For NOW, three words: LOCAL, TEST, TUGS - manoeuvre from local control, prove astern before every arrival, take tugs. For NEVER AGAIN, four words: CAUSE, AIR, REDUNDANCY, TESTING.

Regulation and source map

This is an operational question and no instrument is quoted. Two regimes frame the follow-up and are referred to at the level of their effect only:

  • The ISM Code - the obligations to report and analyse non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences; to maintain the ship and equipment including the identification of critical equipment and systems whose sudden failure may create a hazardous situation, with specific measures to promote reliability including regular testing of arrangements not in continuous use; and emergency preparedness through drills.
  • SOLAS chapter II-1 - the requirement that propulsion machinery be controllable from the navigation bridge, with an independent local means of control and indication of shaft speed and direction of thrust. That architecture is exactly why the temporary measure in route step 5 is available.

Uncertainty and the limits of this answer

The ISM Code and SOLAS chapter II-1 were not read in licensed consolidated editions this session. Both are therefore referred to at the level of their well-known effect, with no clause quoted and no regulation number asserted. This is the same posture already recorded for the ISM Code in Q5 of this paper, and no argument in the answer depends on precise wording.

The stem does not state the engine type, the control architecture, whether the installation is camshaft or electronically controlled, or whether a controllable pitch propeller is fitted. The answer therefore presents a fault tree across the plausible architectures and says so. That is the correct treatment of an under-specified scenario, not a gap in research - and in the examination, stating that the diagnosis depends on the installation is itself a mark of competence.

The report format used is a conventional company incident report. No company procedure was available and none is claimed.

The MLC is an ILO convention, not an IMO one, and that shapes everything about it. Model written answer

1. What the Convention is, and how it is built

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 was adopted at the 94th (Maritime) Session of the International Labour Conference. It consolidated some sixty-eight earlier ILO maritime instruments into one, and is universally described as the fourth pillar of the international regulatory regime, standing beside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW. It is often called the seafarers' bill of rights.

Its architecture is unusual and is worth a sentence in any answer. It has three parts: the Articles, which set out the general obligations; the Regulations; and the Code. Article VI then divides the Code in two - Part A contains Standards and is mandatory, while Part B contains Guidelines which are not mandatory but to which Members must give due consideration. Article VI.3 adds the flexibility mechanism of substantial equivalence: a Member may implement a Part A Standard differently provided the provision is substantially equivalent in achieving the general object and purpose.

The Regulations and Code are arranged in five Titles: Title 1 minimum requirements to work on a ship; Title 2 conditions of employment; Title 3 accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering; Title 4 health protection, medical care, welfare and social security protection; and Title 5 compliance and enforcement. Article IV states the underlying rights: a safe and secure workplace, fair terms of employment, decent working and living conditions, and health protection, medical care, welfare measures and other forms of social protection.

The text in force at this sitting is the Convention as amended by the 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2022 amendments, approved at the 103rd, 105th, 107th and 110th Sessions of the Conference respectively. The 2022 set is the most recent in force, and it adds requirements in three of the five Titles, noted in their place below.

2. Minimum requirements and the employment agreement

Title 1 controls who may go to sea.

  • Minimum age (Reg 1.1). Standard A1.1.1 - the employment, engagement or work on board of any person under the age of 16 is prohibited. A1.1.2 - night work by seafarers under 18 is prohibited, "night" covering a period of at least nine hours starting no later than midnight and ending no earlier than 5 a.m. Hazardous work by those under 18 is likewise prohibited.
  • Medical fitness (Reg 1.2). A valid medical certificate is required before beginning work. Standard A1.2.7 - it is valid for a maximum of two years, or one year if the seafarer is under 18, and a certification of colour vision for a maximum of six years. An STCW medical certificate is accepted.
  • Training and recruitment (Regs 1.3 and 1.4). Seafarers must be trained and qualified for their duties, and recruitment and placement services must be regulated - notably, no fees are to be borne by the seafarer.

The seafarers' employment agreement (Reg 2.1) is the document the question names first. Standard A2.1.1 requires an agreement signed by both the seafarer and the shipowner or a representative, providing decent working and living conditions; the seafarer must be given an opportunity to examine it and to seek advice before signing, so that the agreement is freely entered into. Both parties hold a signed original, a copy is carried on board and available to the seafarer and to inspectors, and the seafarer receives a record of employment which contains no statement as to the quality of the work or the wages.

3. Hours, wages, leave, repatriation and manning

Hours of work and rest (Reg 2.3) is the provision most tested in practice. Standard A2.3.3 sets the baseline at an eight-hour day with one day of rest per week and rest on public holidays. A2.3.5 then requires each Member to fix either a maximum number of hours of work or a minimum number of hours of rest:

  • maximum hours of work - not exceeding 14 hours in any 24-hour period and 72 hours in any seven-day period; or
  • minimum hours of rest - not less than ten hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any seven-day period.

Rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least six hours, with no more than fourteen hours between consecutive rest periods. A2.3.4 requires national standards to take account of the danger posed by fatigue, especially for those with navigational duties. A table of shipboard working arrangements is posted and records of daily hours are kept.

Wages (Reg 2.2) are paid at intervals no greater than monthly, with a monthly account and a means of transmitting allotments home. Leave (Reg 2.4) gives paid annual leave, and Reg 2.7 requires ships to be sufficiently and efficiently manned. Repatriation (Reg 2.5) is the seafarer's ultimate protection: Standard A2.5.1 gives entitlement when the agreement expires while abroad, when it is terminated by the shipowner or by the seafarer for justified reasons, and when the seafarer can no longer carry out the duties. A2.5.2(b) requires the maximum service period on board after which repatriation falls due to be less than twelve months. The shipowner must also maintain financial security for abandonment, evidenced by a certificate carried on board, and a seafarer's wages continue during captivity following piracy or armed robbery.

4. Accommodation, catering, health, welfare and social security

  • Accommodation and recreational facilities (Reg 3.1). Accommodation must be safe and decent, with detailed requirements for size, headroom, heating, ventilation, lighting, sanitary facilities and hospital accommodation. Note the application rule: the construction standards apply only to ships built on or after the Convention entered into force for that Member. Accommodation is inspected on board at frequent, recorded intervals. Standard A3.1 paragraph 17 requires appropriate recreational facilities, amenities and services including SOCIAL CONNECTIVITY, adapted to meet the special needs of seafarers who must live and work on ships - the associated guidance addressing internet access at reasonable charges. Connectivity is a welfare requirement of the Convention, not a commercial courtesy.
  • Food and catering (Reg 3.2). Food and drinking water of appropriate quality, nutritional value and quantity, supplied free of charge during the period of engagement; ships' cooks must be trained and qualified, and no seafarer under 18 may serve as ship's cook.
  • Medical care (Reg 4.1). Health protection and medical care comparable to that available ashore, provided free of charge, including the right to visit a qualified doctor or dentist without delay in ports of call, where practicable, a medicine chest and medical guide, and access to radio or satellite medical advice.
  • Shipowners' liability (Reg 4.2). The shipowner bears the cost of sickness and injury occurring in service, and must hold financial security for contractual claims arising from death or long-term disability. Appendix A4-I lists the particulars the certificate must carry, and paragraph (g) now requires the name of the shipowner or of the REGISTERED OWNER where different from the shipowner - a direct response to layered ownership defeating claims.
  • Health and safety (Reg 4.3). Occupational safety and health policies and programmes, risk assessment and accident reporting, and - Standard A4.3.2 - a ship's safety committee where there are five or more seafarers. Harassment and bullying are expressly addressed in the guidance. Three further requirements matter to an engineer: Standard A4.3.1(b) requires the provision of all necessary APPROPRIATELY-SIZED personal protective equipment - equipment that does not fit is not compliance; Standard A4.3.2 read with Guideline B4.3.1.3 establishes the hierarchy of controls, requiring account to be taken of engineering and design control, and substitution of processes and procedures, which have precedence over personal protective equipment; and Standard A4.3.5(a) requires the flag State to ensure that all deaths of seafarers on its ships are adequately investigated and recorded, and reported annually to the Director-General of the International Labour Office for publication in a GLOBAL REGISTER.
  • Welfare and social security (Regs 4.4 and 4.5). Access to shore-based welfare facilities, and social security protection which, on ratification, must cover at least three of the nine branches listed in Standard A4.5.

5. Flag State enforcement - certification and inspection

Title 5 is what makes the MLC enforceable rather than aspirational, and limb (b) cannot be answered without it.

The flag State certifies. Standard A5.1.3.1 - the maritime labour certificate is issued by the competent authority, or by a recognized organization duly authorized under Reg 5.1.2, for a period not exceeding five years, and is subject under A5.1.3.2 to an intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates. It is accompanied by the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance: Part I, drawn by the competent authority, listing the national requirements; and Part II, drawn by the shipowner, setting out the measures adopted to ensure ongoing compliance between inspections.

Appendix A5-I lists the working and living conditions that must be inspected and approved before certification. There are sixteen - minimum age, medical certification, qualifications, seafarers' employment agreements, use of licensed recruitment services, hours of work or rest, manning levels, accommodation, on-board recreational facilities, food and catering, health and safety and accident prevention, on-board medical care, on-board complaint procedures, payment of wages, financial security for repatriation and financial security relating to shipowners' liability. The last two were added by the 2014 amendments, which is why the list is sixteen and not the original fourteen. Beyond certification, Reg 5.1.4 requires a system of inspection and enforcement, Reg 5.1.5 requires on-board complaint procedures with safeguards against victimization and the right to complain directly to the master and to external authorities, and Reg 5.1.6 requires an official inquiry into any serious marine casualty.

6. Port State control and the labour-supplying State

Under Regulation 5.2.1 an authorized officer in a port of another Member may inspect. The certificate and DMLC are accepted as prima facie evidence of compliance, and the inspection normally stops there. Standard A5.2.1.1 gives four triggers for a more detailed inspection:

  • the required documents are not produced or maintained, are falsely maintained, lack the required information or are otherwise invalid;
  • there are clear grounds for believing that working and living conditions do not conform to the Convention;
  • there are reasonable grounds to believe the ship has changed flag to avoid compliance; or
  • there is a complaint alleging that specific conditions do not conform.

Appendix A5-III sets out the sixteen general areas open to such a detailed inspection - the same list as Appendix A5-I. Where conditions are clearly hazardous to safety, health or security, or the breach is serious or repeated, the ship may be detained until deficiencies are rectified. Article V.7 supplies the principle that makes this work commercially: ships flying the flag of a State that has not ratified must not receive more favourable treatment than those of a ratifying State. Two further limbs complete the architecture - Reg 5.2.2, which requires onshore seafarer complaint-handling procedures, and Reg 5.3, which places responsibilities on labour-supplying States such as India for recruitment, placement and social protection of their seafarers.

7. Challenges and limitations

The Convention's weaknesses are structural rather than textual, and limb (b) is asking you to name them.

  • Flag State capacity and delegation. Certification is almost universally delegated to recognized organizations under Reg 5.1.2, and the recognized organization is paid by the shipowner it inspects. Administrations with large registers and small staffs rely on that delegation entirely, so the quality of enforcement varies with the register rather than with the Convention.
  • Documentary compliance. Because the certificate and DMLC are prima facie evidence, a ship whose paperwork is in order will normally pass. The gap between a compliant file and a compliant ship is the central practical limitation.
  • Hours-of-rest records. The single most reported area of non-compliance, and structurally so: the record is generated by the person whose hours it records, under commercial pressure, on a ship manned to the minimum safe manning document. Port turnarounds, inspections and drills are absorbed by the crew and disappear from the record.
  • Uneven national implementation. Article VI.3 substantial equivalence and the non-mandatory status of Part B mean two ratifying States may impose materially different requirements. Seafarers of the same rank on the same trade can hold quite different entitlements.
  • The limits of port State control. An MLC inspection competes with SOLAS and MARPOL priorities during a short port stay; assessing wages, contracts and rest hours is a different skill from assessing equipment; and risk-based targeting under the regional memoranda means some ships are rarely inspected.
  • Who actually bears the sanction. Enforcement acts on the ship, through detention and delay. It does not reach the beneficial owner directly, and single-ship companies and layered ownership limit what a seafarer can recover. The financial security amendments were a direct response to exactly this problem in abandonment cases, and the requirement that the certificate now name the registered owner where different from the shipowner attacks the same weakness one layer further in.
  • Under-use of complaint procedures. Reg 5.1.5 gives the right and forbids victimization, but seafarers on short contracts in a competitive market fear blacklisting, so complaints are made late, ashore, or not at all.
  • Social security. Reg 4.5 is the weakest Title in practice: only three of nine branches are required at ratification, and portability between flag, nationality and manning-agent jurisdictions remains unresolved.
  • The absence of data - now being addressed. A recurring criticism has been that no one knows the true scale of seafarer death and injury, because reporting was fragmented across flags. Standard A4.3.5(a) now requires deaths to be investigated, recorded and reported annually to the ILO Director-General for a global register. It is a partial answer to the enforcement-data gap rather than a complete one - it measures the outcome rather than compelling the conditions - but it is the first Convention-level mechanism for counting.
  • External shocks. The COVID-19 crew change crisis demonstrated the ceiling: when States closed borders, seafarers served far beyond the repatriation limits and neither flag nor port States could give effect to Reg 2.5, notwithstanding repeated ILO and IMO appeals.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are 8 + 8, and the two limbs are very different tasks. Limb (a) is coverage: the examiner has named five areas - employment contracts, working hours, accommodation, health and safety, medical care and welfare - and expects each to be reached. Route steps 2, 3 and 4 walk the Titles in order so that none is missed, which is the commonest way marks are lost here.

Limb (b) is evaluation, and it is the harder half. It names three targets - flag State jurisdiction, port State control, and non-compliance by shipowners and operators - so an answer that merely describes Title 5 has answered the wrong question. Steps 5 and 6 establish the machinery briefly because you cannot criticise what you have not described; step 7 is where the marks are.

THE TEMPORAL POINT - the 2022 amendments ARE in force, the 2025 amendments are NOT

This question is dated at both ends, and both ends can catch you.

The 2022 amendments entered into force on 23 December 2024 - ten months before this sitting - and are simply the law here. They were approved at the 110th Session of the International Labour Conference in June 2022, and the operative consolidation at this examination is the Convention as amended in 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2022. An answer written from older notes will be short by five requirements, four of which fall squarely in Title 3 and Title 4 that the examiner has expressly named.

The 2025 amendments, adopted at the 113th Session in June 2025, are NOT in force and must not appear. They were adopted four months before this sitting, which makes them unusually dangerous: material published in the second half of 2025 and afterwards will describe them in the present tense, and current ILO pages fold them into "the MLC as amended". They are expected to take effect in 2027. Adoption is not entry into force. If you mention them at all, mention them as a future development and say so.

The five 2022 requirements, and where each belongs

These are worth learning individually, because each attaches to a Standard the examiner has already asked about - they are not a separate topic:

  • Social connectivity - Standard A3.1 paragraph 17. Recreational facilities, amenities and services must now include social connectivity, with the associated guidance addressing internet access at reasonable charges. This sits in Title 3, which limb (a) names. Connectivity is a welfare entitlement, not a perk.
  • Appropriately-sized PPE - Standard A4.3 paragraph 1(b). The precaution now expressly extends to providing all necessary appropriately-sized personal protective equipment. The word does real work: PPE issued in one size, which does not fit a substantial part of the crew, is not compliance.
  • The hierarchy of controls - Guideline B4.3.1 paragraph 3, with Standard A4.3 paragraph 2. Engineering and design control, and substitution of the dangerous by the less dangerous, have precedence over personal protective equipment. This is the standard occupational-hygiene hierarchy, now express in the Convention, and it is the most examinable of the five for an Engineering Management paper - it is a design and management duty, not a stores duty.
  • Deaths reported for a global register - Standard A4.3 paragraph 5(a). The flag State must ensure all seafarer deaths on its ships are adequately investigated and recorded, and reported annually to the Director-General of the ILO to be published in a global register. Use this twice: once in limb (a) as a flag-State duty, and once in limb (b), because it is a partial answer to the criticism that no one counts.
  • Registered owner on the financial security certificate - Appendix A4-I(g). The certificate must name the shipowner or the registered owner if different. It attacks layered ownership, which is one of limb (b)'s own complaints.

What did NOT change is the larger part of the answer, and it is worth knowing that too: the whole architecture of Articles, Regulations and Code, the five Titles, the hours limits, repatriation, the certificate and DMLC, the four PSC triggers, Article V.7, and the entire structural critique in limb (b). The route is identical to the March 2024 route; the content of two sections inside it grew.

A dating marker, and its limits

Appendix A5-I lists sixteen items, not the original fourteen - the two extra being the financial security items added in 2014. An answer that says sixteen has dated itself post-2017 and an answer that says fourteen is pre-2017. It is a useful check, but note what it does not do: it does not distinguish 2018 from 2022. It is necessary, not sufficient. The five provisions above are what date an answer to this sitting, and Standard A4.3.5(a) is the sharpest of them.

Common mistakes

  • Writing limb (b) as a second description of Title 5. The examiner asked for challenges and limitations.
  • Answering on the pre-2022 text and missing social connectivity, appropriately-sized PPE, the precedence of controls over PPE, the global register of deaths, and the registered owner on the security certificate.
  • Importing the 2025 amendments. Adopted June 2025, not in force at this sitting.
  • Saying the MLC is an IMO convention. It is an ILO convention.
  • Treating Part B Guidelines as mandatory. Article VI - Part A Standards bind; Part B must be given due consideration.
  • Giving only one of the two hours regimes. A Member fixes either maximum work or minimum rest, and you should give both sets of figures.
  • Confusing DMLC Part I (competent authority, national requirements) with Part II (shipowner, measures for ongoing compliance).
  • Forgetting Regulation 5.3, labour-supplying responsibilities - which is the limb that matters most to an Indian candidate.
  • Quoting a ratification count. Any figure you have read is almost certainly later than this sitting. Leave it out.

Examiner traps

  • The five named areas in limb (a). They are a checklist. Tick every one.
  • Accommodation applies by build date. Standard A3.1 construction requirements bite only on ships built on or after entry into force for that Member - a point candidates almost never make and which distinguishes a read answer from a remembered one. Note that paragraph 17 on social connectivity is not a construction standard and is not limited that way.
  • "Less than twelve months". Standard A2.5.2(b) says less than, not "up to". The eleven-month contract exists for this reason.
  • "Appropriately-sized" PPE. One word, and it is the whole point of the amendment.

Memory framework

The five Titles are the spine and they run in the order a seafarer experiences them: get on board (Title 1), terms of service (Title 2), live on board (Title 3), stay well (Title 4), who checks (Title 5). Route steps 2 to 4 are Titles 1 to 4; steps 5 to 7 are Title 5 and its shortfalls.

Uncertainty and currency

The answer was written from the ILO consolidated text including the Amendments of 2022, which is the edition operative at this sitting, and each of the five 2022 additions was located verbatim in that text and cited to its own Standard, Guideline or Appendix rather than to "the 2022 amendments" as a block.

The entry-into-force date of 23 December 2024 is taken from the corpus temporal record - itself verified against the ILO Compendium of maritime labour instruments, 4th revised edition - and from the ILO's own published notice, rather than from the consolidated text, which records the Conference session at which amendments were approved rather than the date they took effect. Two independent sources agree. The date matters because it places the amendments comfortably before this sitting.

The 2025 amendments were checked and deliberately excluded. The corpus holds them segregated from the in-force consolidation, so the exclusion was tested by probing the operative text rather than argued from memory. Their expected 2027 effective date is a projection and nothing in the Model Answer rests on it.

The number of ratifying States is not asserted anywhere. The figures available in the corpus are as at 2026 and post-date this sitting, so quoting them would be a currency error in the opposite direction. The COVID-19 crew change crisis is described qualitatively; no figure for seafarers affected is given, because no primary source for one was consulted.

Standard A4.3.1(b) requires reasonable precautions including the provision of all necessary APPROPRIATELY-SIZED personal protective equipment. Guideline B4.3.1 paragraph 3, read with Standard A4.3 paragraph 2, requires engineering and design control and the substitution of the dangerous by the less dangerous to have PRECEDENCE over PPE.

The hierarchy of controls is now express in the Convention - a design and management duty, not a stores duty.

Article VI - Part A contains Standards and is mandatory. Part B contains Guidelines which are not mandatory, but Members must give them due consideration. Article VI.3 also permits substantial equivalence, allowing a Member to implement a Part A Standard differently provided it is substantially equivalent in achieving the general object and purpose.

Treating Part B as binding is the most common structural error on this topic.

Standard A2.3.5 - a Member fixes either maximum hours of work, not exceeding 14 in any 24-hour period and 72 in any seven-day period; or minimum hours of rest, not less than ten in any 24-hour period and 77 in any seven-day period. Both regimes are needed - the Convention offers an either/or, not a single rule.

The Part I / Part II split is asked in almost every oral on this topic.

The certificate is prima facie evidence only, and these four triggers rebut it.

Standard A4.3.5(a) - the Member must ensure that all deaths of seafarers employed, engaged or working on board ships that fly its flag are adequately investigated and recorded, and reported on an annual basis to the Director-General of the International Labour Office to be published in a global register.

New with the 2022 amendments, and usable twice - as a limb (a) duty and as a limb (b) partial answer to the data gap.
