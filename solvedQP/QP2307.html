Model written answer

1. The Convention, and what the stem's facts have established

The governing instrument is the International Convention on Salvage, 1989, adopted at London and in force since 1996. It replaced the Brussels Convention of 1910 and, unlike its predecessor, is concerned with the marine environment as well as with property.

The facts in the stem matter and should be used, not skipped. A professional salvor has refloated a grounded ship and towed her to a place of safety. A useful result has been achieved, and that is the precondition for any payment at all. The stem is therefore correct that a right to an award has accrued.

Keep two articles apart from the first line, because the whole of limb (a) depends on it. Article 12 creates the entitlement - no useful result, no payment. Article 13 fixes the amount. A candidate who describes 'no cure, no pay' as an Article 13 principle has merged the two and will struggle to answer the rest cleanly. (The stem says award; the Convention says reward. They are the same thing and no mark turns on the word.)

(a) The Article 13 criteria

2. The scheme Article 13 sits inside

Article 12 retains the old bargain intact: salvage operations which have had a useful result give a right to a reward, and operations which have had no useful result give no payment. No cure, no pay survives into the modern treaty.

Article 13 then says how the reward is fixed, and it opens with its own purpose: the reward shall be fixed with a view to encouraging salvage operations. That purpose is worth one sentence in the answer, because it explains why the list contains things - promptness, readiness, equipment kept available - that have nothing to do with how hard this particular job was. The treaty is paying for a standing capability, not only for a service.

3. The ten Article 13 criteria

Taking them in the Convention's own order:

  • the salved value of the vessel and other property;
  • the skill and efforts of the salvors in preventing or minimising damage to the environment;
  • the measure of success obtained by the salvor;
  • the nature and degree of the danger;
  • the skill and efforts of the salvors in salving the vessel, other property and life;
  • the time used and the expenses and losses incurred by the salvors;
  • the risk of liability and other risks run by the salvors or their equipment;
  • the promptness of the services rendered;
  • the availability and use of vessels or other equipment intended for salvage operations;
  • the state of readiness and efficiency of the salvor's equipment and the value thereof.

Note where the environmental criterion sits - second, immediately after salved value. Under the 1910 Convention a salvor who prevented a spill but saved nothing earned nothing. Putting environmental effort near the top of the list is the 1989 Convention's answer to that, and it is the single most examinable feature of Article 13.

4. How the criteria are applied, and what can reduce the award

  • They are not listed in order of importance. The Convention says so expressly. A tribunal weighs all of them together; it does not work down a ranked list.
  • The reward may not exceed the salved value of the vessel and other property. This is the cap, and it is the reason a salvor of a low-value ship may recover far less than the operation cost him - which is the gap Article 14 special compensation was written to fill. Article 14 is not asked here and is not developed.
  • Payment is apportioned. The reward is paid by all the vessel and property interests in proportion to their respective salved values - so ship and cargo each contribute according to what each got back.
  • Article 18 - the salvor may be deprived of the whole or part of the payment where the salvage was made necessary or more difficult by his own fault or neglect, or where he has been guilty of fraud or other dishonest conduct.
  • Article 19 - services rendered notwithstanding the express and reasonable prohibition of the owner or master give rise to no payment.

(b) The duties of the parties

5. The salvor's duties

These are set out in Article 8. The salvor owes a duty to the owner of the vessel and of the property in danger:

  • to carry out the salvage operations with due care;
  • in performing that duty, to exercise due care to prevent or minimise damage to the environment;
  • whenever circumstances reasonably require, to seek assistance from other salvors;
  • to accept the intervention of other salvors when reasonably requested to do so by the owner or master of the vessel - though the amount of his own reward is not prejudiced should that request prove to have been unreasonable.

The last two exist because a salvor's commercial interest is to keep the job to himself, and the Convention will not let that interest run against the safety of the casualty.

6. The duties of the shipowner and master, and why they are mutual

Article 8 imposes duties on the other side too, and this is the half candidates omit. The owner and master of the vessel owe the salvor:

  • a duty to co-operate fully with him during the course of the salvage operations;
  • in doing so, a duty to exercise due care to prevent or minimise damage to the environment;
  • a duty to accept redelivery when the vessel or other property has been brought to a place of safety and redelivery is reasonably requested by the salvor.

The third of those is live on these facts. The stem has the ship towed to a place of safety, so the owner cannot leave her on the salvor's hands; once redelivery is reasonably requested he must take her back, and the salvage service ends there.

Two further points complete the limb. Article 6 gives the master authority to conclude a salvage contract on behalf of the owner of the vessel, and the master or owner authority to conclude one on behalf of the property on board - which is what makes a form signed at the height of a casualty binding on cargo interests who were never consulted. And the environmental duty is imposed on both sides in identical terms. That symmetry is the Convention's innovation, and saying so is what turns a list into an answer.

(c) LOF 2000, the Convention, and which governs

7. What LOF 2000 is, and what it is not

Lloyd's Open Form is a standard-form salvage contract. It is not an instrument, not a convention and not law. Getting that first sentence right is most of this limb.

  • It is open in the sense that matters here: the remuneration is deliberately left unfixed at the moment of agreement. Nobody prices the job in the middle of a casualty.
  • It is written on the no cure, no pay basis, the same principle Article 12 states.
  • It is short and signed in minutes, commonly by the master under his Article 6 authority, so that work begins at once instead of after a negotiation.
  • It carries its own machinery - arbitration, security and appeal - and it may be supplemented by the SCOPIC clause, which is likewise a commercial contract and not part of the Convention.

8. When the Convention applies, and when the LOF applies

The Convention applies by default. Where a Contracting State has given it force in its own law - India has, through the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 - its rules govern any salvage operation, whether or not a contract was made. It supplies the substantive law: the entitlement, the criteria, the duties, the cap and the apportionment.

But Article 6 makes the position more precise than 'the treaty always wins'. The Convention applies to any salvage operation save to the extent that a contract otherwise provides, expressly or by implication. So the parties may contract on their own terms - except that the Convention's protective provisions, in particular the duties in Article 8 and the environmental scheme, cannot be contracted out of.

The LOF applies where the parties agreed it, and on these facts they did. Within its scope it governs the relationship between them: it fixes the forum, the governing law, the provision of security, and the machinery by which the sum is determined. What it does not do is create the right to be paid. That comes from the Convention.

9. How a dispute is actually resolved, and the relationship stated as a rule

If a dispute arises under an LOF, it does not go to the courts of the place where the ship went aground:

  • it goes to arbitration in London under English law, under the form's own terms;
  • the salvor's claim is secured before the ship and cargo are released, so the owner cannot simply sail away from the argument;
  • the arbitrator fixes the amount by applying the Article 13 criteria - the same ten;
  • an appeal lies within the structure the form itself provides.

State the relationship as a rule and the limb is finished:

  • the contract governs procedure and terms; the Convention governs substance;
  • where the contract is silent, the Convention fills the gap;
  • where the contract would defeat the Convention's protective provisions, the Convention prevails.

That is why the arrangement exists at all. A casualty needs a decision in minutes and a valuation that takes months. The form lets the work start; the Convention makes sure the sum is afterwards fixed on principled criteria. Between them they are the reason a professional salvor sails towards a ship that nobody has yet agreed to pay for.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has set three tasks and paid 5, 5 and 6. The six is on the LOF limb, which is the analytical one. That allocation should visibly change what you write: the ten criteria are worth half a mark each and deserve a tight list, while limb (c) deserves prose and a stated rule.

The opening separation of Article 12 (entitlement) from Article 13 (amount) costs one sentence and protects the whole answer. Ending limb (c) on a three-line rule rather than on a description is what distinguishes a pass from a good mark, because it answers the question the examiner actually asked - which one governs - instead of describing each in turn.

Common mistakes

  • Answering only the salvor's duties in limb (b). Half the marks are on the owner and master. The stem even signals it by naming them.
  • Treating LOF 2000 as an instrument. It is a contract. A candidate who writes 'under LOF 2000 the salvor is entitled to...' as though it were law has missed the limb.
  • Ranking the Article 13 criteria. They are expressly not in order of importance.
  • Drifting into Article 14 special compensation. It is not asked. Mention the cap at salved value and move on.
  • Forgetting the cap and the apportionment. They are cheap marks in limb (a) and are routinely omitted.
  • Missing the redelivery duty, which is the one duty the stem's own facts trigger.

Examiner traps

  • 'Place of safety' is not 'place of refuge'. The first is the Convention's trigger for redelivery; the second belongs to the IMO guidelines on places of refuge and to a different question entirely.
  • 'The salvor has a right to award' is the paper's own wording, missing an article. It is not a hint that anything unusual is being asked.
  • The stem supplies LOF 2000 as a fact. Do not comment on whether a later edition of the form was in use - it is not asked, and it is not verifiable from the paper.
  • SCOPIC is a trap door. Name it in one line as a contractual supplement. A paragraph on it is a paragraph not spent on limb (c).
  • Indian law. India gave the Convention effect through the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. Say that; do not invent a section number.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What happens if the salvor saves nothing but prevents a major spill? (The Article 13 cap bites; Article 14 special compensation is the answer, and SCOPIC is the commercial fix.)
  • Who actually pays the award, and in what shares?
  • Can the master bind the cargo owner to an LOF he has never seen? (Article 6.)
  • Why arbitration in London and not the courts of the casualty's own country?
  • What is the difference between salvage and towage? (Danger, and voluntariness.)
  • Can a salvor be deprived of his reward? (Articles 18 and 19.)

Uncertainty and evidence limits

The Salvage Convention 1989 is not held by the True Source corpus. The corpus's liability-and-compensation family covers HNS, Nairobi, LLMC and the CLC/FUND/Bunkers group, and does not extend to Salvage. Every article identity and effect above is therefore authoritative secondary, carried through two MIW answers whose printed stems and content were read in full this session. No article is quoted, and no sub-paragraph below article level is asserted. The Article 13 criteria are given in the Convention's own order and count; the wording is paraphrase.

The contractual layer is deliberately under-specified. LOF and SCOPIC have each been revised more than once. LOF 2000 is named only because the stem supplies it, no other edition is named, no clause number is cited, and nothing is asserted about which form was in general commercial use in July 2023.

Model written answer

1. What this inspection is, and the authority for it

It is worth one sentence to say what kind of exercise this is, because everything after it follows. This is a familiarisation and takeover inspection, made under the Company's safety management system. It is not a statutory survey, it is not a class survey and it is not an ISM audit. Its purpose is that the incoming Chief Engineer knows the true condition of the plant before he becomes answerable for it.

The authority is the ISM Code, which requires the Company to establish procedures to ensure that personnel new to a ship are given proper familiarisation with their duties before the ship sails, and which makes the Company responsible for ensuring the ship is manned by people who are properly qualified and familiarised. STCW reinforces it with a separate on-board familiarisation requirement. The takeover itself is a documented SMS procedure and ends in a signed handover.

One idea governs the whole inspection. The certificates already assert that the equipment exists and was working when the surveyor saw it. The Chief Engineer is looking for something different: has it been operated recently, by someone on board, and is there a record of it? Existence is on the Record of Equipment. Condition is in the logs, and in the machine.

Before going below, take ten minutes with the paperwork: the Passenger Ship Safety Certificate and its Record of Equipment, the class status with any conditions of class or memoranda, the last port State control report, the standing defects list, and the outstanding items from the last survey. That tells you where to look hard.

2. Ship stability - the documents, the instrument and the lightship

On a passenger ship the stability case is not only about how she is loaded today; it is the foundation of the damage case in the next section. Inspect in this order:

The approved documents.

  • The Stability Information Booklet, approved by the Administration, in a language the officers understand - containing the intact and damage stability particulars, the approved loading conditions, the limiting KG or GM curve and the tank capacities and free-surface data.
  • The damage control information derived from the same calculations, which is what the next limb depends on.
  • The subdivision load lines and the certificate that assigns them - a passenger ship carries more than one, and the officer of the watch must know which applies.

The instrument. Verify that the approved loading and stability instrument is the one named in the approval document, that it is functioning, and that the approved test conditions have been run at the required intervals with the results recorded and within tolerance. Check that the crew can operate it, and that there is a documented alternative means of demonstrating compliance if it fails.

The standard she is held to. A passenger ship is subject to the probabilistic damage stability regime of SOLAS chapter II-1, not to a two-compartment rule of thumb, and to the intact criteria of the Intact Stability Code - which for passenger ships include the specific passenger-crowding and turning heeling criteria. Establish which regime her keel date puts her under; an older ship may be on the earlier deterministic standard and her booklet will say so.

The lightship. This is the passenger-ship point most often missed. The ship's whole stability case rests on her lightweight and centre of gravity, and accumulated modification changes both. A passenger ship must have a lightweight survey at periodical intervals not exceeding five years to check for deviation from the approved lightship data. Sight the date of the last one, the deviation recorded, and whether a re-inclining was required. Then check that recent modifications - a refit, new accommodation, added equipment - have been entered in the weight-change record.

Finally, look at the tanks: sounding and gauging arrangements accurate and calibrated, remote gauges cross-checked against manual soundings, and slack tanks minimised in the ballast plan, because free surface is the cheapest way to lose the margin the booklet assumes.

3. Damage control - plans, watertight boundaries and the means to use them

Damage control on a passenger ship is the practical half of the stability case: the ship is designed to survive flooding, but only if the boundaries are actually intact and the crew can act.

The plans and the booklet.

  • Damage control plans permanently exhibited for the guidance of the officer in charge, showing the watertight compartments, their openings and the means of closing them, and the arrangements for correcting list from flooding.
  • A damage control booklet available to the officers, in the ship's working language.
  • A duplicate set held ashore with the Company - verify it exists and is current, because it is what the emergency response service will work from.
  • The muster list and emergency organisation naming who does what, and the damage control training manual.

The watertight boundaries themselves. This is where an inspection earns its keep, because a plan is worth nothing if a door is chocked open:

  • Power-operated watertight sliding doors - test local operation both sides, remote closing from the bridge, and the audible and visual alarm that sounds before and during closure. Check the closing time and the hand-gear.
  • The indicator panel on the bridge showing every door open or closed, and every shell, bow, stern and side door - and confirm the indication is real rather than a lamp that has been jumpered.
  • The sea-going regime. Watertight doors are to be kept closed at sea; establish the ship's standing orders on the few that may be opened, the permission required, and the log entry that records it. Verify the record of the periodical operation and inspection of watertight doors.
  • Shell doors, bow and stern doors and their securing arrangements, with indicators and where fitted the leakage detection and surveillance - the direct legacy of the ro-ro passenger ship losses.
  • Cross-flooding arrangements - the valves, their accessibility, their marking, and the written instructions for their use, including the time to equalise.

The means to pump and to know. Inspect the bilge pumping and drainage system - pump capacities, the emergency bilge suction in the machinery space, non-return and isolating valves, strainers and level alarms - and confirm the onboard stability computer with a damage-stability capability, or the shore-based damage-stability support service, is available, current and has been tested. That facility is a passenger-ship requirement and is the tool that turns a flooding casualty into a calculable one.

Finally, ask when the ship last held a damage control drill, and read the record rather than the answer.

4. Firefighting - containment first, then the systems that fight it

Take structural containment before appliances. On a passenger ship the design intent is that a fire is confined to the space and the zone in which it starts; a candidate who begins with extinguishers has answered a cargo-ship question.

Containment.

  • Main vertical zones and the A-class boundaries that form them - inspect for penetrations made during refit and never made good, which is the commonest real defect on any ship.
  • Fire doors - self-closing, held on releases that drop them on the fire alarm, tested from the control station and locally; no wedges, no hooks.
  • Dampers and ventilation closures, remote stops for ventilation fans, and the closing arrangements for skylights and openings to machinery spaces.
  • Means of escape from every space, in particular the second means from the machinery spaces; escape routes clear, marked, and fitted with low-location lighting; emergency escape breathing devices at their marked stations.
  • Fire control plans permanently exhibited, with the duplicate set in a prominently marked weathertight enclosure outside the deckhouse for the shore fire service.

Detection and fixed systems.

  • Fixed fire detection and alarm throughout accommodation, service and control spaces, and the automatic sprinkler system required in those spaces on ships carrying more than 36 passengers - check the pressure tank, the pump auto-start on pressure drop, the section valves and the last section test.
  • The fixed fire-extinguishing system for the machinery spaces - identify the type installed and inspect its release station, the alarm that sounds before release, the ventilation and quick-closing valve interlocks, the bottle weighing or level check dates and the last servicing certificate.
  • Remote stops - fuel pumps, purifiers, ventilation fans - and the quick-closing valves on fuel and lubricating oil tanks, operated from their remote station and reset, which is the test most often skipped.
  • Fire pumps, their number and capacity, the emergency fire pump with its independent power and its isolating valve, hydrants, hoses and nozzles, and the international shore connection.
  • Firefighter's outfits and breathing apparatus - a passenger ship carries additional sets; check the counts against the Record of Equipment, the cylinder pressures, the recharging arrangement and the spare charges.

The safety centre. On a passenger ship the safety centre is the space from which the safety systems are controlled and monitored in an emergency. Go and sit in it. Verify that the indications work, that the controls operate, and that the officers can actually use them.

The paperwork that proves it. The maintenance and testing plan for the fire protection and fire-fighting systems, the servicing certificates for the fixed installations and the portable appliances, and the drill records - a passenger ship musters her crew weekly and musters embarking passengers within a short fixed period of departure. Read the records, and check the last drill included the machinery space.

5. Critical machinery - the list, the redundancy and the proof it has been run

Start from the definition, because the examiner is looking for it. The ISM Code requires the Company to identify equipment and technical systems the sudden operational failure of which may result in hazardous situations, to provide specific measures to promote their reliability, and to include testing at regular intervals of stand-by arrangements and equipment or systems not in continuous use. That last clause is the whole of limb (iv) in one line: the standby half of every pair is what fails, precisely because it is not running.

Step one - verify the list itself. Obtain the Company's critical equipment list for this ship and check that it is ship-specific rather than a fleet template, that it names systems and not just machines, and that each entry has a defined test interval and a record of the last test.

6. Recording the findings, and closing them out

  • Record findings in the safety management system as non-conformities or observations, with dates and owners - not in a private notebook, which leaves the Company no obligation and the next Chief Engineer no history.
  • Sign the handover document with the outgoing Chief Engineer, formally accepting the defect list, the conditions of class, the outstanding port State control items and the bunker and spares inventories.
  • Agree a priority plan with the Master and report it to the Company, distinguishing what must be corrected before sailing from what can be planned.
  • Anything that affects the ship's seaworthiness or her certificates is raised before departure, in writing. On a passenger ship that judgement is made against the number of people on board, not against the commercial schedule.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four heads are printed and the answer gives them four blocks in the printed order. That alone secures the structure marks. The framing section at the start and the close-out at the end are deliberately short - they are worth little individually, but without them the answer is a list of equipment rather than an account of an inspection, and the examiner has asked for an inspection.

Within each head the order is the same: documents, then physical checks, then the records and tests to be sighted. That repetition is what lets a candidate write four blocks quickly under time pressure without having to invent a shape for each one.

Common mistakes

  • Answering it as a cargo ship question. If nothing in the answer is specific to a passenger ship - no main vertical zones, no sprinklers, no lightweight survey interval, no safety centre, no safe return to port - the candidate has not answered this question.
  • Starting limb (iii) with extinguishers. Containment comes first on a passenger ship.
  • Producing a critical equipment list from memory instead of naming the ISM Code requirement that creates it. The definition is worth more than three extra items on the list.
  • Checking that equipment exists rather than that it has been operated. The Record of Equipment already establishes existence.
  • Omitting the lightweight survey in limb (i). It is the one stability item peculiar to passenger ships and it is regularly missed.
  • Writing an ISM audit. The question asks what you inspect, not how you verify the system.

Examiner traps

  • Safe return to port applies by date and size, not to every passenger ship. A candidate who asserts it for any passenger ship has over-claimed. State the thresholds and say the Chief Engineer establishes which side his ship falls.
  • The sprinkler requirement is tied to the number of passengers carried, and the threshold is more than 36. Do not state it as universal.
  • Damage stability for passenger ships is probabilistic under the current regime, but an older ship may be certificated on the earlier deterministic standard. The booklet settles it, not the candidate's assumption.
  • 'Critical machinery' is an ISM concept, not a SOLAS term. Say so.
  • Watertight doors are to be kept closed at sea. Describing a regime in which they are normally open, with the bridge closing them in an emergency, is the wrong way round.
  • The duplicate fire control plan lives outside the deckhouse, in a marked weathertight enclosure, for the shore fire brigade - not on the bridge.

Likely oral follow-up

  • You find a watertight door wedged open on passage. What do you do, and what do you write?
  • The emergency generator starts but will not take load. Is the ship seaworthy?
  • How would you satisfy yourself that the loading instrument is giving correct answers?
  • Why does a passenger ship need a lightweight survey when a cargo ship does not?
  • What is the difference between the safety centre and the engine control room?
  • Your predecessor has left twenty-two open defects. How do you take over?
  • Which of the fixed fire systems on board would you test first, and why not the others?

Memory framework

Work outward from the water, and the four heads fall into order on their own:

  • Does she float upright now? - stability: booklet, instrument, lightship.
  • Does she float upright after a hole? - damage control: plans, doors, cross-flooding, pumping.
  • Does a fire stay where it started? - firefighting: zones and boundaries first, then systems.
  • Will the machinery cause the emergency? - critical equipment: the list, the redundancy, the test record.

Then one sentence to open (what this inspection is) and one to close (write it down and hand it over).

Model written answer

1. Why NOx forms, and the three levers every measure pulls

Every measure in this question is an attack on one of three things, so it is worth thirty seconds establishing them before the limbs.

  • The nitrogen comes overwhelmingly from the air, not from the fuel. Residual fuel contains some fuel-bound nitrogen, but the great bulk of the oxides of nitrogen leaving a marine diesel engine is thermal NOx, formed from atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen in the flame. That is why NOx cannot be dealt with by buying a cleaner fuel, as sulphur can.
  • The formation rate rises very steeply with peak flame temperature, and requires available oxygen and time spent at that temperature.

So there are exactly three levers:

  • Peak temperature - add something that absorbs heat or raises the charge's specific heat;
  • Oxygen availability - displace part of the oxygen with inert gas;
  • Residence time - shorten the time at peak, through injection timing and rate shaping.

And one trade-off governs the whole subject: NOx and fuel consumption pull against each other. Cooling or slowing the burn reduces NOx and costs efficiency. That is why regulation sets a ceiling rather than demanding the cleanest possible engine, and why the strictest ceiling is applied only where it is most needed.

(a) Tier II and Tier III emission regulation on the main engine

2. The regulatory frame - regulation 13 and what fixes the tier

The controlling provision is MARPOL Annex VI regulation 13. At this sitting the applicable text is the revised Annex VI introduced by resolution MEPC.328(76), which entered into force on 1 November 2022.

  • Regulation 13 sets three tiers of permitted NOx emission for marine diesel engines above a defined power output.
  • Each tier's limit is not a single number - it is a function of the engine's rated speed, expressed in grammes per kilowatt-hour. A slow-speed two-stroke and a high-speed auxiliary are therefore held to different figures within the same tier. No limit value is quoted here.
  • Certification is under the NOx Technical Code 2008 as revised, on the test bed and by the approved methods; in-service compliance is demonstrated through the engine's NOx Technical File and its record book of parameter changes.
  • Certain engines are excluded - emergency engines, and engines fitted in lifeboats and survival craft.

3. Tier II and Tier III - what fixes each, and the two operating modes

Two different things fix the two tiers, and that is the substance of this limb.

  • The construction date of the ship fixes which tier the engine must be certified to. An engine on a ship built from the Tier II date onwards must meet Tier II; an engine on a ship built from the Tier III date onwards must additionally be capable of Tier III. It is the ship's date, not the engine's technology, that creates the obligation.
  • Geography fixes whether the strictest tier actually applies at any moment. Tier III is required only when the ship is operating inside a designated emission control area for NOx; outside it, the same engine may run to Tier II.

The practical consequence is that such a ship has two operating modes, and the change between them must be recorded - the date, the time and the ship's position on entering and on leaving. That log entry is the first thing a port State inspector asks for.

The engineering consequence is the point worth making. Tier II is reachable by combustion tuning and engine design - injection timing, chamber geometry, charging. Tier III is a step change and is not reachable by tuning alone. It requires either a heavy primary measure such as exhaust gas recirculation, or a secondary measure such as selective catalytic reduction. That is exactly why limbs (b), (c) and (d) of this question exist.

(b) Primary NOx reduction vis-a-vis secondary NOx reduction measures

4. Primary measures - preventing the NOx from forming

Definition first, because the definition is the mark. A primary measure acts on or before combustion, so that less NOx is ever created.

  • The combustion event - retarded injection timing, the simplest measure of all and the one that costs fuel most directly; injection pressure and rate shaping; common rail, which decouples timing and pressure from engine speed and load.
  • The charge - Miller timing combined with high-pressure turbocharging, so that the charge expands and cools before compression begins.
  • Design - combustion chamber and nozzle geometry, swirl, compression ratio: designed in, not added on.
  • Adding water - fuel-water emulsion, direct water injection, and scavenge air moistening (limb (d)).
  • Changing the charge itself - exhaust gas recirculation (limb (d)).

5. Secondary measures, and the comparison the limb asks for

A secondary measure leaves the combustion untouched and treats the exhaust. Selective catalytic reduction is the only mature secondary NOx measure in marine use - it is limb (c) and is dealt with there.

Three points finish the limb:

  • The dividing line is where the NOx goes, not where the equipment sits. Ask only whether the pollutant was prevented or removed.
  • Which is why exhaust gas recirculation is a PRIMARY measure. Its gas comes from the exhaust, so it is constantly misclassified - but it is returned to the cylinder to change the combustion, and the NOx is never formed. This is the single commonest error on this question.
  • An exhaust gas scrubber is not a NOx measure at all. It addresses sulphur oxides and particulates. Introducing it here loses marks rather than gaining them.
  • They are complements, not rivals. Primary measures are cumulative and are used together to meet the ordinary limit; a heavy primary measure or a secondary one is added to reach the strictest.

(c) SCR for NOx emission reduction

6. SCR - the reaction, and the arrangement that delivers it

Selective catalytic reduction converts the oxides of nitrogen already formed into harmless products before they leave the funnel.

  • A reagent - in marine practice an aqueous urea solution, or ammonia - is injected into the exhaust upstream of a catalyst.
  • Urea decomposes in the hot gas to ammonia, which over the catalyst reacts selectively with the oxides of nitrogen to give nitrogen and water vapour.
  • 'Selective' means the reagent reacts preferentially with NOx rather than being burnt by the oxygen also present in the exhaust - which is what makes the process work in a lean exhaust at all.
  • The installation is a reactor containing the catalyst elements, a dosing and injection system, a mixing length upstream of the reactor, a reagent storage tank and its supply and heating arrangements, plus soot blowing to keep the elements clear.

The reactor may sit before or after the turbocharger turbine, and which it is depends on the engine. A slow-speed two-stroke has a comparatively cool exhaust after the turbine, so the reactor is commonly placed on the high-pressure side, before the turbine, where the gas is hot enough. A four-stroke medium-speed engine generally runs hot enough after the turbine for a low-pressure arrangement, which is mechanically simpler.

7. SCR - the operating window, and what it costs

The whole difficulty of SCR is that it only works inside a window.

  • Temperature-sensitive. Below the window the reaction slows and stops; above it the catalyst degrades. This is precisely what decides the reactor's position relative to the turbine, and it is why low load, manoeuvring and port operation are the hard cases rather than full-away.
  • Sulphur-sensitive. Sulphur compounds in the exhaust form ammonium salts at low temperature that deposit on and blind the catalyst. A higher-sulphur fuel raises the minimum working temperature.
  • Dosing must be correct. Under-dose and NOx passes through untreated; over-dose and unreacted ammonia passes through as ammonia slip, which is itself a pollutant and is smelt long before it is measured.
  • It adds exhaust back pressure, which costs some engine efficiency.
  • It is a large installation with a consumable behind it - space and weight for the reactor and the urea tank, and a reagent bunkering chain that must be available wherever the ship trades. On a retrofit these are usually the binding constraints, not the chemistry.

Set against that: no fuel penalty, since the engine is untouched, and it is the route by which most large two-stroke engines meet the strictest tier.

(d) Scavenge Air Moisturizing and Exhaust Gas Recirculation for the main engine

8. Scavenge Air Moisturizing

The paper prints 'Moisturizing'; the industry term is scavenge air moistening, abbreviated SAM. It is the same system.

Water is evaporated into the scavenge air, downstream of the charge air cooler, so that the air entering the cylinder is saturated with water vapour.

  • How it works - one lever, pulled twice. The water absorbs latent heat as it evaporates, and the resulting water vapour has a higher specific heat than the air it replaces. Both effects lower the peak combustion temperature, and NOx falls steeply with it. SAM pulls the temperature lever only.
  • Arrangement. A humidification section after the air cooler, often in stages with re-heating between them so that the air can carry more vapour; a water supply, circulating pump and treatment plant; and controls linked to engine load.
  • The water must be fresh and clean. What reaches the cylinder must not carry salt or solids - sea water would be ruinous to the liner and the valves.
  • A water mist catcher is the safety-critical item. The cylinder must receive vapour, never droplets: liquid water reaching a hot liner causes thermal shock and washes away the oil film.
  • Costs. Corrosion in the air path, disturbance to cylinder lubrication and hence to the scrape-down oil condition, a continuous fresh-water demand, a small efficiency penalty and added control complexity.

It is a substantial primary measure, but it is normally part of a package rather than a complete answer to the strictest tier on its own.

9. Exhaust Gas Recirculation, and why both of these are primary

Part of the exhaust is cleaned, cooled and returned to the scavenge air.

  • How it works - two levers at once, which is why it reaches further than SAM. The returned gas has already had its oxygen consumed, so it reduces the oxygen concentration of the charge; and the carbon dioxide and water vapour it contains raise the charge's specific heat, lowering the peak temperature. Oxygen and temperature together.
  • Arrangement. A scrubber to wash soot and sulphur compounds out of the recirculated gas, a cooler, a water mist catcher, an EGR blower to overcome the pressure difference, and shut-off and change-over valves so the system can be cut in and out with the tier mode.
  • The turbocharging must be arranged to suit - cylinder cut-out, variable geometry or auxiliary blowers - because gas is being diverted away from the turbine.
  • The bleed-off water is a waste stream in its own right. It is acidic and carries soot and metals, and it must be treated, held and monitored against the discharge criteria before any release.
  • Costs. Fouling and acid corrosion in the gas path, soot carried into the cylinder, blower power, considerable complexity, and the waste-water handling above.

EGR is capable of reaching the strictest tier on its own, and it is the principal primary alternative to SCR for large two-strokes.

Close the limb on the classification, because it is where the marks are lost. Both SAM and EGR are PRIMARY measures. In both, the combustion itself is altered and the NOx is never formed. Neither treats the exhaust on its way out - EGR takes gas from the exhaust, but it sends it back into the engine to change the burn, and that is what puts it on the primary side of the line drawn in limb (b).

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four limbs at four marks each means four short, complete answers - not one long answer and three notes. The opening mechanism section is what makes that possible: once the three levers are established, each limb can be written in a few lines without becoming thin, because the reader already has the physics.

Within limb (b) the six-axis table does the comparison the word vis-a-vis asks for. A candidate who describes primary measures and then describes secondary measures, without ever comparing them, has answered a different question.

Common mistakes

  • Calling exhaust gas recirculation a secondary measure. The single most common error on this question. Its gas comes from the exhaust, but it goes back into the cylinder to change the combustion, so the NOx is never formed.
  • Bringing in the exhaust gas scrubber. That is a sulphur and particulate device. It is not a NOx measure.
  • Quoting tier limit values. They are a function of rated speed, so a single number is wrong. Explain the structure instead.
  • Saying Tier III applies from a date. The date fixes what the engine must be CAPABLE of; geography fixes when it must actually deliver it.
  • Writing limbs (b) and (d) at eight-mark length. Earlier sittings paid eight for that material; this one pays four.
  • Forgetting the water mist catcher on SAM, which is the item that keeps liquid water out of the cylinder.

Examiner traps

  • 'Tier 2 and 3' is printed in arabic numerals where the Annex uses roman. Nothing turns on it.
  • 'Moisturizing' is the paper's spelling. The system is scavenge air moistening. Note it once and move on; do not lecture the examiner.
  • 'Primary' does not mean 'first' or 'main'. It means the measure acts before or during combustion. A candidate who reads it as 'the principal measures' will build the whole limb wrongly.
  • SCR's position relative to the turbine is not a fixed fact - it depends on the exhaust temperature the engine delivers. Explain the dependence rather than asserting one arrangement.
  • Ammonia slip is a consequence of over-dosing, not of the catalyst failing.
  • The tier changeover must be logged with date, time and position. That operational detail is worth a mark and is regularly omitted.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Your SCR is fitted and the ship is manoeuvring in an ECA at low load. What is your concern?
  • Why does higher-sulphur fuel raise the minimum working temperature of an SCR?
  • What would happen if the water mist catcher on a SAM plant failed?

  • Why does retarding the injection reduce NOx, and what does it cost you?

  • Can you meet Tier III by tuning alone?

  • SAM - temperature only, by latent heat and specific heat.
  • EGR - temperature and oxygen. Two levers, so it reaches further.
  • Retarded timing and rate shaping - temperature and residence time.
  • SCR - no lever at all. It does not prevent the NOx; it removes it afterwards. That is the whole of the primary/secondary divide.

And for the tiers: the date says what the engine must be able to do; the chart says when it must do it.

Uncertainty and currency

MARPOL Annex VI is held by the True Source corpus, but its corpus classification is citation-ready only - it resolves to instrument identity and provenance and not to text. Regulation 13 is therefore given by identity and effect, and no tier limit value is quoted anywhere in this answer. That restriction is also a temporal safeguard: it makes it impossible to import a limit from a consolidated text later than this sitting.

The entry into force of the revised Annex VI is taken from the resolution itself, not from a secondary index. MEPC.328(76) was adopted on 17 June 2021; its own operative provisions deem the amendments accepted on 1 May 2022 and bring them into force on 1 November 2022 - eight months before this sitting, so the revised Annex is the applicable text here. The date matters: a reader who picked up 1 November 2023 from anywhere would place entry into force four months AFTER this sitting and force the answer onto a superseded Annex.

One later amendment is excluded deliberately. Resolution MEPC.385(81) was adopted in March 2024 and replaced the major-conversion provision of regulation 13. Any consolidated Annex VI text dated 1 May 2024 or later incorporates it. It is future by eight months at this sitting and is not relied on; and this is why the July 2024 question on the same subject was consumed for its technical content only, with its regulatory currency statement re-derived from the July 2023 position.

No emission control area is named and no area is asserted to have been designated for NOx at any particular date. The concept is used; the geography is not.

Model written answer

1. Which text applies, and how the Convention is built

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is an ILO instrument which entered into force on 20 August 2013 and is generally described as the fourth pillar of the international regulatory regime, alongside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW.

Say how it is built, because all four limbs of this question sit inside that structure:

  • Articles - the treaty obligations of the Member;
  • Regulations - the substantive requirements;
  • Code Part A - Standards, which are mandatory;
  • Code Part B - Guidelines, which are guidance to be given due consideration.
  • Five Titles, of which Title 5 is compliance and enforcement and divides into flag State, port State and labour-supplying responsibilities - the same three-way split this question follows.

One temporal point belongs at the top. At this sitting the operative text is the Convention as amended through the 2018 amendments. The amendments approved by the International Labour Conference at its 110th Session in 2022 are adopted but not yet in force; they enter into force on 23 December 2024. Nothing from that set is a present obligation.

(a) Flag State and port State responsibilities

2. Flag State and port State responsibilities

Flag State. The flag State must establish an effective system of inspection and certification of labour conditions, ensuring that ships flying its flag comply.

  • It may authorise recognized organizations to carry out inspection and certification, but it remains fully responsible for the work done on its behalf, must satisfy itself of their competence and independence, and must make the list public.
  • Ships of 500 gross tonnage and over on international voyages, or operating from or between foreign ports, must carry a Maritime Labour Certificate and a Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance; others may be certified on request.
  • The DMLC is in two parts, and the split is the classic examination point. Part I is drawn up by the competent authority and identifies the national requirements; Part II is drawn up by the shipowner and sets out the measures adopted on the ship to ensure ongoing compliance.
  • The certificate is valid for five years, with an intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates; an interim certificate may be issued for six months.
  • The interval between inspections shall in no case exceed three years. Inspectors must be qualified and independent, must report to the authority and the master, and have power to require rectification and to prohibit the ship from sailing until deficiencies are put right.

Port State. Any foreign ship calling in the port of a Member may be inspected to determine compliance.

  • But the certificate and the DMLC must be accepted as prima facie evidence of compliance, so the ordinary inspection is a document review. Without that presumption the certificate would be worth nothing and every port would re-audit every ship.
  • The port State inspection is limited to the Articles, the Regulations and Part A of the Code - the mandatory material. Part B Guidelines are not the measure of compliance.
  • No more favourable treatment. A ship flying the flag of a State that has not ratified the Convention is entitled to no better treatment than one that has - which is what stops non-ratification from becoming a commercial advantage.
  • Where more is needed, the officer proceeds to a more detailed inspection, which is limb (c).

(b) On-board and on-shore complaint procedures

3. The on-board complaint procedure

Every ship must carry on-board procedures for the fair, effective and expeditious handling of seafarer complaints alleging breaches of the Convention.

  • The design preference is resolution at the lowest level - the complaint is raised with the head of department and worked upward.
  • But that preference is expressly without prejudice to the seafarer's right to complain directly to the master, and to complain directly to external authorities - the flag State administration, or an authorized officer in a port of call. A procedure that required him to exhaust the chain of command first would not comply.
  • A copy of the procedures must be provided to every seafarer, together with contact information for the competent authority of the flag State and of the seafarer's country of residence, and the name of a person on board who can advise him impartially and otherwise assist him.
  • The seafarer has the right to be accompanied or represented during the procedure.
  • Victimization for filing a complaint is prohibited and must be penalized. This is the provision that makes everything else usable: without it the machinery exists and nobody dares use it.

4. The onshore complaint-handling procedure

A seafarer may bring a complaint to an authorized officer in the port at which the ship has called. The procedure is an escalation with three rungs:

  • Ship level first. The officer makes an initial investigation, and where the complaint can be resolved at ship level he seeks that resolution - the on-board procedure is used or reinstated, and where the complaint concerns matters within a more detailed inspection he proceeds accordingly.
  • Flag State next. If it cannot be resolved on board, the officer notifies the flag State, seeking advice and a corrective plan of action, with a deadline. A copy of the report goes to the shipowners' and seafarers' organisations in the port State.
  • The ILO last. Where the matter remains unresolved, the port State sends a copy of the report, with the flag State's response, to the Director-General of the International Labour Office, so that the failure is recorded internationally.
  • The source of the complaint is kept confidential at every stage, and the officer must take appropriate steps to safeguard it - including giving no intimation to the master or the owner that an inspection was made on account of a complaint.

(c) Detainable deficiencies

5. Detainable deficiencies under the Convention

Begin with what the Convention does not do. Unlike the regional port State control memoranda, the MLC publishes no list of detainable deficiencies. It states two grounds, and a candidate who recites a memorandum's list has answered from the wrong instrument.

The two grounds. A ship may be detained where the conditions on board are shown, following a more detailed inspection, to be:

  • clearly hazardous to the safety, health or security of seafarers; or
  • a serious or repeated breach of the requirements of the Convention, including seafarers' rights.

The route to them matters as much as the grounds themselves. Detention is reached only through a more detailed inspection, and that inspection may be carried out only where one of four triggers is present:

  • the required documents are not produced, or are not maintained, or are falsely maintained, or do not contain the information required;
  • there are clear grounds for believing the working and living conditions do not conform;
  • there are reasonable grounds to believe the ship changed flag to avoid compliance;
  • there is a complaint alleging that specific conditions do not conform.

What follows a finding.

  • The deficiencies are to be rectified; or, where rectification cannot be completed in that port, the officer may accept a plan of action with a timetable.
  • The ship is not released until the hazard is removed or the plan accepted.
  • The flag State is notified forthwith, and the shipowners' and seafarers' organisations in the port State are informed.
  • Compensation is payable for any loss or damage suffered where a ship is unduly detained or delayed. The Convention places the burden of proof in each case on the complainant, and that provision is the discipline on the inspector.

(d) Grievance redressal mechanisms for Indian seafarers

6. Grievance redressal for Indian seafarers

Answer this in three layers, because a single list would hide the point.

Layer 1 - the Convention's own route, which every Indian seafarer already has. India is a ratifying Member, so the whole of the machinery in limbs (b) and (c) is available to him: the on-board procedure with its impartial adviser and its protection from victimization; direct complaint to the master; direct complaint to the flag State administration; complaint to an authorized officer in any port of a ratifying Member; and, at the end, the Director-General of the ILO.

Layer 2 - what India can actually reach, and this is the substance of the limb.

  • For an Indian-flag ship, India is the flag State and the full inspection, certification and enforcement apparatus of Title 5 is Indian.
  • But most Indian seafarers serve on foreign-flag ships, where India is neither the flag State nor, usually, the port State. The operative head is then India's labour-supplying responsibility, which is the third limb of Title 5 and is the one that exists precisely for large seafarer-supplying countries.
  • Under it, and under the recruitment and placement provisions, India licenses and regulates crewing agents, requires registers to be kept and employment agreements to be verified, enforces the prohibition on charging recruitment fees to the seafarer - save for the statutory medical certificate, the seafarer's book and a passport - and requires a system of protection to compensate the seafarer for monetary loss arising from the agent's failure to meet its obligations.
  • That is where the practical remedy lies. The foreign owner is beyond India's reach; the agent who put the man on board is not.

Layer 3 - the domestic frame.

  • The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the statute in force, and is the vehicle through which India gives effect to its flag State, port State and labour-supplying obligations.
  • The shipping master is the officer concerned with engagement and discharge of seafarers and with disputes over wages arising from them.
  • The Directorate General of Shipping is the maritime administration and the competent authority for the purposes of the Convention.
  • Practical actors, but not law: the seafarers' unions, and the ITF inspectorate, which in practice recovers a great deal of unpaid wage.

No circular number, named cell, named office, forum or tribunal is asserted here, and no section of the 1958 Act is cited by number. The machinery above is stated at the level at which it is verifiable.

7. The thread that joins the four limbs

Close by connecting them, because the connection is the understanding the examiner is testing.

The flag State certifies and the port State verifies - and both can be entirely satisfied while a seafarer sits unpaid on a properly certified ship. Certification catches systems; it does not catch individual wrongs. What closes that gap is the complaint - and that is exactly why a complaint is not merely a welfare mechanism but one of the four triggers that entitles an officer to stop treating the certificate as conclusive, carry out a more detailed inspection, and if necessary detain the ship.

Limb (d) is the same thread followed to its Indian end. India cannot inspect a foreign ship in a foreign port; what it can do is regulate the agent who is within its reach, and make that agent answerable for the engagement it arranged.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four limbs at four marks each. The framing section is short and buys structure for the whole answer by establishing the Articles / Regulations / Part A / Part B architecture, which every limb afterwards uses without re-explaining.

Limb (b) is given two numbered sections because the on-board and onshore procedures are separate machinery with separate rules. Running them together is the commonest way to lose half that limb. The closing section is worth writing even under time pressure: it is where the candidate shows he understands why the pieces are arranged as they are, and it takes four lines.

Common mistakes

  • Reciting the Paris MoU list of detainable deficiencies. That is a different instrument. The MLC publishes no list and gives two grounds.
  • Saying Part I of the DMLC is drawn up by the shipowner. Part I is the competent authority's; Part II is the shipowner's.
  • Describing port State inspection as a full audit. The certificate is prima facie evidence; the ordinary inspection is a document review.
  • Treating the on-board procedure as compulsory before any external complaint. The lowest-level preference is expressly without prejudice to direct complaint to the master or to external authorities.
  • Answering limb (d) as though India were the flag State. For most Indian seafarers it is not; the operative head is labour-supplying responsibility.
  • Citing the 2022 amendments as current obligations. They enter into force 23 December 2024, seventeen months after this sitting.
  • Inventing an Indian forum. No cell, tribunal, circular or section number should appear.

Examiner traps

  • Part B is guidance, not the measure of compliance. A port State inspection is limited to the Articles, the Regulations and Part A.
  • The MLC is amended by the ILO's own tripartite machinery, not by IMO tacit acceptance. Candidates habitually import the IMO procedure.
  • 'Detainable deficiency' is port State control vocabulary. The examiner has used it; answer it with the MLC grounds and say so.
  • Compensation for undue detention is a real provision and is the check on the inspector. It is regularly omitted.
  • Confidentiality of the source runs through the whole complaint machinery, including that the master is not to be told an inspection arose from a complaint.
  • Victimization is prohibited AND penalized. Both words matter - a prohibition without a penalty would be decorative.
  • The administration at this sitting is DG Shipping. The later renaming belongs to a statute that is years away from this paper.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Who signs Part II of the DMLC, and what is in it?
  • A seafarer tells you he has not been paid for three months. Walk me through what happens.
  • On what grounds can a port State detain a ship under the MLC, and who pays if you are wrong?
  • Your ship changes flag in the middle of a wage dispute. Does that help the owner?
  • What is the difference between the MLC route to detention and the SOLAS route?
  • An Indian seafarer on a foreign-flag ship has been charged a fee by his agent. What can India do?
  • Why must the certificate be accepted as prima facie evidence at all?

Memory framework

Follow the seafarer outward, one rung at a time, and the four limbs come in the printed order:

  • Who certifies, and who checks? - flag State and port State (limb a).
  • How does he complain on board? - lowest level, but direct access preserved, and no victimization (limb b, first half).
  • How does he complain ashore? - authorized officer, then flag State, then the ILO (limb b, second half).
  • When is the ship stopped? - two grounds, four triggers, one more detailed inspection (limb c).
  • What can India reach? - not the foreign owner; the agent (limb d).

One sentence ties it: certificates catch systems, complaints catch wrongs.

Model written answer

1. The legal frame - which instruments govern a collision claim

Three bodies of law meet in a collision claim and it is worth naming them before the limbs, because each limb draws on a different one.

  • The Collision Convention of 1910 - the Brussels convention for the unification of certain rules of law with respect to collisions between vessels - is the source of the modern doctrine on apportionment and on presumptions. It is unamended, and it is the instrument all four limbs of this question are really about.
  • The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the Indian statute through which those rules have effect, and which carries the master's duties after a collision.
  • The COLREGs supply the standard of conduct. They do not themselves allocate loss; breach of them is the usual way in which fault is established.

One principle governs the whole subject: liability in collision is FAULT-BASED, not strict. If the collision is accidental, or is caused by force majeure, or if the cause of it is left in doubt on the evidence, the damages are borne by those who have suffered them - the loss lies where it falls. That is the baseline against which limbs (A), (B) and (D) all operate.

2. (A) Apportionment of damages - the rule, and what it replaced

The rule. Where a collision is caused by the fault of two or more vessels, the liability of each vessel is in proportion to the degree of the fault respectively committed.

What it replaced, and why it is worth a line. Before the Convention, admiralty applied the rule of equal division: once both vessels were shown to be at fault to any extent at all, the loss was simply halved. A ship overwhelmingly to blame paid the same as one barely careless. The rule was easy to administer and plainly unjust, and the Convention's proportionate rule exists to replace it.

The fallback. The old rule survives only as a residue. Where the degrees of fault cannot be established from the evidence, or where the respective faults are equal, the liability is apportioned equally. So equal division is now the answer to an evidential failure, not the general rule.

3. (A) How the shares are applied - and the split that matters most

Once the percentages are fixed, they are applied differently according to what was lost. This distinction carries most of the marks in limb (A).

What was damaged or lostHow liability operatesPractical consequence
The vessels, their cargoes, and effects on boardSeveral - not joint. Each vessel answers only for her own proportionA cargo owner must pursue both ships, and recovers only the relevant share from each. If one is insolvent or beyond reach, he bears that part of the loss
Loss of life and personal injuryJoint and several. Either vessel may be made to answer for the whole claimThe claimant sues whichever ship he can reach; that ship then has a right of recourse against the other for its proportion

The reason for the difference is a policy one and is worth stating: the law is content to make commercial parties chase their proportions between themselves, but it will not require a dead seafarer's family to sue two shipowners in two jurisdictions to be made whole.

Two practical points close the limb:

  • Single liability or cross liabilities. In practice the claims are commonly settled on a single liability basis - the two claims are set against each other and only the net balance is paid. Marine policies may instead respond on a cross-liabilities basis, each claim being treated as paid in full, which can produce a different recovery for the assured.
  • The Both-to-Blame Collision Clause. Because cargo on the carrying ship can recover its share from the other vessel, and that vessel can pass part of that back to the carrying ship, the carrier ends up indirectly bearing a cargo liability from which the contract of carriage exempts him. The clause in the bill of lading requires the cargo owner to indemnify the carrier against that circularity.

4. (B) Proportionate fault - what the degree actually measures

Fault here means a failure in the duty of good seamanship owed by each vessel to the other. In practice it is most often a breach of the COLREGs - a wrong or late alteration, an improper speed in restricted visibility, a failure to keep a proper lookout by sight, hearing and all available means, a failure to use radar and plotting properly - but it also covers negligent navigation generally, defective equipment, poor bridge or engine-room manning, and unseaworthiness.

The examinable proposition is what 'degree' measures, because it is not one thing but two:

  • Blameworthiness - the culpability of the conduct. How far short of the standard of a prudent seaman did this ship fall? A deliberate or reckless breach weighs heavily; a momentary error of judgement in the agony of the moment weighs lightly, and may not be treated as fault at all where it was induced by the other ship's wrong.
  • Causative potency - how much the conduct actually contributed to the collision. A grave breach that made no difference to the outcome counts for little; a small lapse at exactly the decisive moment can carry the greater share.

Both are weighed together, and neither alone settles the share. A candidate who defines proportionate fault only as 'who was more to blame' has given half the answer.

5. (B) How the degree is fixed, and where it cannot be

  • The court assesses in broad percentages on the whole of the evidence - a judgement about the relative responsibility of the two ships, not an arithmetic. Apportionments are expressed in round terms, and no attempt at precision is made or expected.
  • Where more than two vessels are in fault, each bears her own proportion. A vessel not at fault bears nothing at all, whatever the others do between themselves.
  • Compulsory pilotage is no longer an answer. The presence of a compulsorily engaged pilot does not exonerate the ship from liability for negligent navigation.
  • Where the degrees cannot be established, the shares are equal - the fallback in section 2.
  • And where fault is not proved at all, there is no liability. Inevitable accident is a complete answer, not a mitigating factor.

6. (C) Collision liabilities - the heads of claim

The phrase carries two questions - what may be claimed, and who finally bears it. Take them in that order.

Claims by and against the other vessel:

  • Cost of repairs, together with drydocking, cleaning, gas-freeing, class and damage surveys;
  • Detention - loss of earnings or loss of hire while the ship is repaired, and the cost of any substitute tonnage;
  • Salvage rendered as a consequence of the collision, and any general average sacrifice or expenditure arising from it;
  • Total loss, where she is lost or is a constructive total loss.

Claims by others:

  • Cargo on either ship, subject to the several / joint and several distinction in section 3;
  • Loss of life and personal injury - to crew, to passengers and to third parties;
  • Wreck removal and marking, which on a modern casualty is frequently the largest single head;
  • Damage to fixed and floating objects - berths, jetties, cranes, buoys, cables and aids to navigation;
  • Pure economic loss, which as a general rule is not recoverable in tort where it is unaccompanied by physical damage - the closed berth and the delayed third party usually recover nothing.

Pollution is a separate and stricter regime and must not be folded into the fault analysis. Where oil escapes, the oil pollution and bunkers conventions impose liability on the registered owner without proof of fault, channelled to him and backed by compulsory insurance with a right of direct action. So one casualty can generate a fault-based, apportioned claim between the ships and a strict-liability claim against one of them at the same time, and the two are answered by different rules.

7. (C) Limitation, and where the liability finally lands

  • Limitation. The shipowner may limit his aggregate liability for most collision claims by reference to the ship's tonnage, under the limitation convention, with separate funds for claims for loss of life and personal injury and for property claims. The right is lost where the loss resulted from his personal act or omission committed with intent to cause it, or recklessly and with knowledge that it would probably result. No limitation figure is quoted.
  • Hull and machinery insurance covers the ship's own damage, and through the collision liability clause - the running down clause - it covers three-fourths of the shipowner's liability to the other vessel, subject to the sum insured.
  • The P&I club carries the remaining one-fourth of the collision liability, and in addition the heads the hull policy does not touch at all: cargo, pollution, wreck removal, loss of life and personal injury, and damage to fixed and floating objects.
  • Time bar. Claims arising from a collision must be brought within two years of the casualty, with a further period for claims of contribution between the vessels. Preserving that limit by agreement or by issuing proceedings is a first step, not a last one.

8. (D) Presumptions of fault - the abolition, and the one that survives

This limb has a counter-intuitive answer and that is why it is set.

The Convention abolished all legal presumptions of fault. Fault must be PROVED. The fact that a collision has occurred raises no presumption against either vessel.

What was abolished:

  • the statutory presumption, formerly in English law and carried into the law of jurisdictions following it, that a ship found in breach of the collision regulations was deemed to have caused the collision unless she proved that the breach could not have contributed;
  • the presumptions once attaching to a vessel under way as against one at anchor or moored, and the various presumptions of the older admiralty practice.

What survives, and the distinction matters:

  • Evidential inferences of fact. A vessel under way which strikes a properly anchored or moored vessel plainly calls for an explanation, and in the absence of one the court will readily find her at fault. That is an inference drawn from the evidence, not a rule of law that shifts the legal burden, and it is available to the tribunal in the ordinary way.
  • Breach of the COLREGs remains powerful evidence of fault - and is very often decisive. What it no longer does is prove causation automatically.
  • One genuine statutory presumption survives, and it is about conduct AFTER the collision. The master's duty is to stand by, render assistance to the other vessel and to those on board, and to exchange the identifying particulars of his ship. Where he fails in that duty without reasonable cause, the collision is presumed to have been caused by his wrongful act, neglect or default unless the contrary is shown - quite apart from the offence he commits by failing.
  • The important contrast: the United States is not a party to the Convention and retains the Pennsylvania Rule, under which a vessel shown to be in breach of a statutory rule intended to prevent collisions bears the burden of proving that her breach could not have been a cause. That is the direct opposite of the Convention position and is not the Indian position. Knowing the difference, and saying which applies, is what distinguishes a good answer here.

9. How the four fit together in a single claim

The four limbs are one process, and the paper prints them in almost the reverse of the order a court works in:

  • First (D) - was anyone at fault? Nothing is presumed; it must be proved.
  • Then (B) - how much fault, weighing blameworthiness against causative potency.
  • Then (A) - apply the shares: severally for property, jointly and severally for life and injury.
  • Then (C) - to what? The heads of claim, subject to limitation, and landing between hull underwriters and the club.

The abolition of presumptions is what makes the rest of it work. Apportioning a loss by degree is only a meaningful exercise if fault has been established on evidence rather than assumed from the fact of contact. That is also why the engineering evidence matters: the movement book, the voyage data recorder, the alarm and event printouts, the machinery and steering-gear logs and the maintenance records are the material from which a DEGREE - and not merely a fact - is afterwards established. Preserving them is a real duty on a Chief Engineer after a collision, and it is the point at which this doctrine touches his own job.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four terms at four marks each, answered in the printed order, with two sections each on (A), (B) and (C) because each of those contains a rule plus the qualification that carries the mark. The framing section costs three lines and lets every limb afterwards refer to 'the Convention' without re-explaining it.

The closing section is where the marks above a bare pass are found. It shows that the candidate sees the four as one process, and it takes six lines. The link at the end to the engineering evidence is worth making in an oral even if it is cut in the written paper.

Common mistakes

  • Saying that breach of the COLREGs raises a presumption of fault. It is powerful evidence and it is not a presumption. This is the biggest single error available on this question.
  • Applying the Pennsylvania Rule as though it were the general law. It is United States law and the United States is not a party to the Convention.
  • Making all collision liability joint and several, or all of it several. Property is several; loss of life and personal injury is joint and several.
  • Defining proportionate fault as blameworthiness alone. Causative potency is the other half.
  • Writing an essay on limitation of liability under limb (C). It is one bullet of a four-mark limb.
  • Treating pollution as part of the apportionment. It is a strict-liability regime running alongside, not inside, the fault analysis.
  • Forgetting that equal division survives as the fallback where degrees cannot be established.

Examiner traps

  • 'The damage lies where it falls' - inevitable accident, force majeure or an unproved cause produce NO liability. Candidates instinctively look for someone to pay.
  • Compulsory pilotage is not a defence. The old exoneration is gone.
  • The surviving statutory presumption is about failing to stand by, which is conduct after the collision - not about the navigation that caused it.
  • Cargo cannot recover its whole loss from the other ship. Several liability means it recovers only that ship's proportion, which is exactly the circularity the Both-to-Blame Collision Clause addresses.
  • Pure economic loss is generally irrecoverable. The berth that lost a week's business usually gets nothing.
  • Three-fourths, not all, of the collision liability sits on the hull policy. The last quarter is the club's, and so is everything else.
  • Two years is the collision time bar, not the general commercial period.

Model written answer

1. What FSA is, and its objectives

Formal Safety Assessment is a structured and systematic methodology aimed at enhancing maritime safety - including protection of life, health, the marine environment and property - by using risk analysis and cost-benefit assessment. It is set out in the Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment for use in the IMO rule-making process, MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, issued 9 April 2018 - five years before this sitting and unrevised since.

Say what kind of thing it is before anything else, because the whole answer follows from it: FSA is a tool for making RULES, not for running a ship. The Company's obligation under the ISM Code to assess risks to its ships and personnel and to establish safeguards is a different exercise - a different body, a different scale, a different output. Confusing the two is the framing error on this question.

Its objectives are:

  • to provide a rational and systematic process for assessing the risks associated with shipping activity, and for evaluating the costs and benefits of options for reducing those risks;
  • to provide a means of evaluating new regulations, or of comparing existing regulations with proposed ones, so that safety and environmental benefit can be weighed against cost;
  • to assist in demonstrating to decision-makers that a proposed measure delivers a benefit in proportion to what it costs;
  • to support equitable and consistent regulatory change, and to aid the reaching of consensus among Member States by making the reasoning visible rather than assertive.

One honest qualification belongs here: the Guidelines do not claim that FSA is applied in all circumstances. It is a tool for the questions that need it, not a mandatory precondition for every amendment.

2. The characteristics that define it

  • Proactive, not reactive. It argues about a rule before the rule exists, rather than after a casualty. This is the single most important characteristic and belongs first.
  • Structured and systematic, so that two studies of the same problem can be compared and the reasoning followed.
  • Transparent, documented and auditable - every assumption, data source and judgement is recorded, because the output is meant to persuade a committee of States rather than to satisfy its own author.
  • Generic rather than ship-specific. It is applied to a generic model of a ship type, system or operation, not to one vessel. That is what makes the result usable as a rule.
  • Proportionate in depth. The work is screened so that effort goes where the risk is, instead of being spread uniformly.
  • Iterative. The steps are not a one-way corridor - findings at a later step send you back to an earlier one, and step 5 draws on all of them.
  • Explicitly cost-aware. Alone among the safety methods in common use, it requires the cost of a proposed measure to be estimated and compared with the risk it removes.

3. The process - and the step that is not one of the five

Before step 1, the decision-makers define the problem: the ship type, system or operation to be studied, the scope and boundaries, the operating conditions assumed, and the regulations already in force. This is preparatory and it is NOT one of the five steps. Calling it step 1 and pushing hazard identification to step 2 is the commonest structural error on this question.

Two definitions are needed before going further:

  • a hazard is a potential to threaten human life, health, property or the environment;
  • risk is the combination of the frequency of an occurrence and the severity of its consequence.

The five steps are then:

StepNameQuestion it answers
1Hazard IdentificationWhat could go wrong?
2Risk AnalysisHow likely is it, and how bad?
3Risk Control OptionsWhat could be done about it?
4Cost Benefit AssessmentWhat would that cost, against what it saves?
5Recommendations for decision-makingWhat should therefore be required?

4. Step 1 - Hazard Identification

Purpose: to produce a prioritised list of hazards and of the accident scenarios they lead to, specific to the problem defined.

  • The creative element - a structured group review by a balanced team of people with practical experience: seagoing officers, designers, operators, surveyors and risk specialists, using techniques such as structured what-if and hazard and operability review. Its purpose is to find hazards that the data do not contain because they have not happened yet.
  • The analytical element - casualty and incident data, port State control and class defect records, published studies and previous FSA work. Its purpose is to make sure the known hazards are not overlooked while the group is being imaginative.
  • Both are necessary. Data alone will miss the novel hazard; imagination alone will miss the common one. A candidate who gives only one of the two has given half the step.
  • A coarse analysis of causes and consequences is carried out for each hazard, sufficient only to rank it - not to analyse it, which is step 2's job.
  • Ranking on a risk matrix of frequency against severity, so that the small number of scenarios carrying most of the risk are carried into step 2 and the remainder are screened out. This is where the proportionality characteristic is actually applied.
  • Output: a ranked list of hazards and scenarios, with the initiating events and the accident categories they belong to.

5. Step 2 - Risk Analysis

Purpose: to examine in detail the causes and consequences of the scenarios carried forward from step 1, and to quantify the risk so that contributors can be compared with one another.

  • Fault tree analysis works backwards from an undesired event to the combinations of failures and conditions that could produce it, and yields an estimate of its frequency.
  • Event tree analysis works forwards from an initiating event, through each barrier or intervention that may or may not succeed, and yields the spread of consequences and their probabilities.
  • Together they form the risk contribution tree - causes on one side, consequences on the other, the accident event in the middle. That structure is the whole of step 2 in one picture.
  • Results are expressed as individual risk - the risk to a particular person, typically a crew member - and as societal risk, the risk to the whole exposed population, commonly presented on an FN curve plotting frequency against number of fatalities.
  • The results are judged against the ALARP framework: a region of risk that is intolerable and must be reduced whatever the cost; a middle region that is tolerable only if the risk is as low as reasonably practicable, where cost may properly be weighed; and a region so small as to be broadly acceptable. The middle region is where step 4 does its work.
  • The output that matters is not a single number but which contributors dominate - which causes and which consequences carry most of the risk - because those are the only places a control option can usefully be aimed.

6. Step 3 - Risk Control Options

Purpose: to devise measures that reduce the dominant contributors identified in step 2, and to assemble them into options a regulator could actually adopt.

  • Individual risk control measures are generated against the dominant contributors, and are classified two ways: whether they are preventive - reducing the frequency of the event - or mitigating, reducing its consequence; and whether they are engineering, procedural or organisational.
  • Measures sharing common risk-reducing attributes are then grouped into risk control OPTIONS. This is the step's distinctive move and it is regularly missed: a regulation is adopted as a coherent package, not as a single bolt or a single procedure, so the assessment must evaluate packages.
  • Each option is examined for practicability, cost, effectiveness and confidence - and for whether it introduces new hazards of its own, which is why the process is iterative.
  • The risk reduction attributable to each option is then re-evaluated through the step 2 model, giving the change in risk that step 4 will divide the cost by.
  • Output: a list of risk control options with the risk reduction each achieves.

7. Step 4 - Cost Benefit Assessment

Purpose: to determine the cost-effectiveness of each risk control option - what it costs set against the risk it removes.

The assessment is made over the whole life cycle and across the whole affected fleet, not for one ship in one year. That scope is what makes the result usable as a rule.

  • Costs include design and construction, equipment and retrofit, installation and downtime, training, survey and certification, operating and maintenance cost, and any loss of earning capacity - lost deadweight, lost cargo space, lost speed.
  • Benefits include fatalities and injuries averted, casualties and pollution incidents avoided, property and cargo preserved, reduced insurance and clean-up cost, and any operational saving the measure itself produces.

These are then expressed as cost-effectiveness indices, and naming them is what turns a vague weighing into an answer:

  • the gross cost of averting a fatality - the additional cost of the option divided by the reduction in risk it achieves, expressed per fatality averted;
  • the net cost of averting a fatality - the same calculation after subtracting the economic benefit the measure itself produces. Where a measure pays for itself in avoided loss, the net figure can be negative, which is the strongest possible case for adopting it.

The indices are compared against a criterion of what society is prepared to pay to avert a fatality, and are used to rank the options. No criterion value is quoted here: the figure is a matter for the decision-makers, is periodically revisited, and asserting one would be to state as fact something the Guidelines leave open.

Two honest qualifications close the step. The benefits are dominated by items that are genuinely hard to value, and the indices are sensitive to the assumptions behind them - which is why step 5 requires the assumptions to be exposed. And cost-effectiveness is a ranking tool, not a decision rule: a measure may be adopted although it is expensive, or rejected although it is cheap, for reasons the calculation does not contain.

8. Step 5 - Recommendations, and what FSA does not settle

Purpose: to present decision-makers with ranked and justified recommendations drawn from steps 1 to 4.

  • The recommendations set out which options are recommended, in what order, with the risk reduction and the cost-effectiveness of each.
  • The assumptions, the quality of the data and the uncertainties are exposed alongside them, not buried. A recommendation whose sensitivity to a doubtful assumption is hidden is worse than no recommendation.
  • Where the analysis cannot support a conclusion, that is itself reported.
  • The study is submitted to the relevant IMO Committee and is subject to independent expert review before it is relied on - which is the transparency characteristic made operational rather than merely asserted.

Close on the limits of the method, because an answer that presents FSA as a machine for producing correct rules has misunderstood it:

  • FSA does not decide. It informs a judgement that remains with the Member States, and that judgement is properly political as well as technical.
  • FSA does not make a ship safe. It helps produce a better rule; the rule is then implemented, surveyed and lived with by people on ships.
  • FSA cannot be better than its data. Casualty data are incomplete and under-reported, and a generic model is by definition an abstraction from every real ship it covers.
  • And it is only as honest as its assumptions - which is precisely why transparency, documentation and independent review are not decoration but the conditions on which the method works at all.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem names five things - objectives, characteristics, processes, and then three of the five steps by name. The answer gives all five in the order asked, and the mark is secured by giving the whole process and then going deeper where directed, rather than answering only the three steps the examiner happened to list.

Section 3 is the load-bearing one. Separating 'define the problem' from the five steps, and defining hazard and risk before describing them, costs about six lines and prevents the structural error that most damages this answer.

Common mistakes

  • Calling 'define the problem' step 1. It is preparatory. There are five steps and hazard identification is the first of them.
  • Answering only the three named steps. The question asks for the objectives, characteristics and processes first, with specific explanation of three. Both parts are required.
  • Confusing FSA with ISM risk assessment. One makes rules; the other runs a ship.
  • Giving hazard identification as a data exercise only, or as a brainstorm only. It is both, and the reason both are needed is a mark in itself.
  • Describing cost-benefit assessment without naming the cost-effectiveness indices. The gross and net cost of averting a fatality are what make the step concrete.
  • Presenting FSA as deciding anything. It recommends; the Committee decides.
  • Forgetting that risk control MEASURES are grouped into risk control OPTIONS.

Examiner traps

  • Risk is frequency AND severity. An answer that treats risk as probability alone cannot explain why a rare catastrophe is treated differently from a frequent nuisance.
  • ALARP has three regions, not two. The middle one is where cost may properly be weighed - and it is the only place step 4 is legitimate.
  • Fault tree backwards, event tree forwards. Candidates reverse them.
  • Preventive reduces frequency; mitigating reduces consequence. Simple, and regularly muddled.
  • Do not quote a figure for what society will pay to avert a fatality. It is periodically revisited and the Guidelines leave it to the decision-makers.
  • FSA is generic, not ship-specific. If the answer describes a study of one vessel, it has described the wrong thing.
  • The method is iterative. Step 3 can send you back to step 2, and a new hazard introduced by a control option goes back to step 1.

Model written answer

1. The physical basis common to all three limbs

Open on the physics, because all three limbs are the same physics seen in different places, and an answer that begins with paint has given away the framing.

  • A ship's resistance divides into frictional and residuary components, and at merchant-ship speeds frictional resistance dominates. The condition of the wetted surface is therefore a fuel matter, not a cosmetic one.
  • Roughness thickens and disorders the turbulent boundary layer, and raises skin friction.
  • The penalty depends on the texture and spacing of the roughness as much as on its height. This is why a thin, even film of slime can cost more than a physical roughness of the same measured thickness - a fine, dense, disordered surface disturbs the flow far more efficiently than an occasional weld seam.
  • The consequence is the same in both places: more shaft power for the same speed, or speed lost at constant power. On the propeller it appears additionally as more torque for the same revolutions - the engine runs heavy, which the engineer sees in the torsionmeter, the fuel index and the exhaust temperatures long before anyone calculates a fuel penalty.

(a) Hull Roughness Management

2. Hull roughness - what is being managed, and the cycle that manages it

Begin by dividing the roughness, because the two halves behave completely differently:

  • Physical roughness - plate waviness, weld seams, corrosion pitting, mechanical and ice damage, coating breakdown and the accumulated layers of old paint. It is largely irrecoverable and it ratchets upward over the ship's life. Each drydocking can slow the rise; none reverses it.
  • Biological roughness - slime, weed and hard fouling. It is recoverable, and it is where in-service effort is aimed.

Management is then a cycle, and giving it as a cycle is what makes this a management answer rather than a paint answer:

  • MEASURE. Average hull roughness taken with a gauge at drydocking, over a defined pattern of readings; and in-service hull and propeller performance monitoring, comparing delivered power against speed. The monitoring is only worth anything if it is normalised for draught, trim, weather, sea state and current - otherwise the trend is noise and every conclusion drawn from it is an accident.
  • SPECIFY. Surface preparation standard, dry film thickness, and application quality and conditions. A poor application is itself a roughness decision - overspray, dry spray and runs create a rough surface on day one that no amount of later cleaning will recover.
  • CHOOSE. A coating system matched to the ship's actual trade: self-polishing copolymer, controlled depletion, or foul-release. The choice turns on service speed, activity level and idle time - foul-release systems depend on movement through the water to shed settlement, so a ship that lies idle for long periods is a poor candidate for one.
  • INTERVENE. Planned inspection, including the niche areas - sea chests and gratings, bow thruster tunnels, bilge keels, rudder and stern arrangements, cathodic protection anodes - which carry a disproportionate share of hard fouling and are routinely skipped. Prefer proactive grooming, which removes settlement gently and often, to reactive cleaning, which removes established fouling aggressively and late.
  • VERIFY. Check the result against the performance trend, not against the diver's report alone, and feed the outcome back into the next specification.

Two constraints belong in the answer because they bite in practice. Aggressive cleaning damages the coating and so accelerates the next fouling cycle - the cure worsening the disease. And in-water cleaning requires the permission of the port or coastal State, which is not automatic and in some places is refused, because of the release of biocides and the transfer of invasive species.

3. Why it has become a duty rather than a preference

Hull condition used to be a matter for the owner's own economics. It is now also a regulatory and commercial obligation, and naming the driver is worth a mark.

  • The ship energy efficiency management plan, required under the revised MARPOL Annex VI which entered into force on 1 November 2022. Hull and propeller measures are among the measures planned, implemented, monitored and evaluated within it, so the work must be documented and auditable rather than merely done.
  • The operational carbon intensity regime, operative from 1 January 2023 and therefore in its first year at this sitting. It makes in-service hull and propeller condition commercially visible - a fouled ship is a worse-rated ship, and charterers read the rating.
  • The IMO biofouling guidance, under which a ship keeps a biofouling management plan and a biofouling record book, and pays specific attention to niche areas and to in-water inspection and cleaning. This is guidance and not law, and saying so is itself a mark - though several States and ports have made parts of it a condition of entry.
  • The Anti-Fouling Systems Convention, under which organotin compounds acting as biocides are prohibited, and under which the control on cybutryne took effect on 1 January 2023 - six months before this sitting, and therefore operative.

(b) Condition Based Propeller Maintenance

4. Condition-based propeller maintenance - the measurement that sets the interval

Define it first, because the definition is the whole limb. Condition-based propeller maintenance means the propeller is worked on when measured condition says it needs to be, and not when the calendar says so. The output of the system is a trigger, not a date.

Three independent sources of evidence feed it, and a good answer names all three:

SourceWhat it tells you
The ship saysThe power-speed-revolution relationship against the baseline; heavy running - the engine making its power at lower revolutions; slip; and torsionmeter readings of shaft power and torque. All normalised for draught, trim, weather and current
The diver seesFouling and calcareous deposit; cavitation erosion, characteristically on the suction face near the leading edge and towards the tip; impingement attack and pitting; edge damage; and the quality of the previous polish, including coarse directional scratching left behind
The machinery tellsStern tube bearing wear-down, stern tube oil condition and consumption, seal performance, and the blade-rate vibration signature, which changes as blade condition and wake inflow change

The system then works as follows:

  • A baseline is established, ideally from sea trials or immediately after a dock polish.
  • The normalised trend is watched, and an agreed threshold is set - a percentage loss of performance, or an inspection grade.
  • Intervention is triggered when the trend or the inspection grade crosses that threshold, and is scheduled to the next convenient port with the necessary permissions.
  • The result is verified against the trend, which both proves the work and re-establishes the baseline.

The contrast with calendar-based polishing is the point of the limb. A fixed interval either sends divers to a clean propeller - money spent for nothing - or leaves a fouled propeller turning for months while the ship burns the difference. Neither is visible without measurement, which is why the calendar survives on ships that do not measure.

Its limits are real and should be stated. It requires normalised data of decent quality and a baseline to compare against; it requires a threshold that someone has actually agreed and will act on; and it requires access - port permission for in-water work, adequate water clarity, tolerable current, and conditions in which it is safe to put a diver over the side.

(c) Propeller Coating and Polishing

5. Propeller coating - purpose and real limits

  • Purpose: to resist fouling and corrosion and to hold a smooth surface for longer, so that the interval between polishes lengthens and the average roughness across the cycle falls.
  • Type: typically a biocide-free foul-release system - silicone or fluoropolymer - presenting a low-surface-energy, low-adhesion finish from which settlement is shed by the water flow rather than killed.
  • Application is the whole of it. Full cleaning, blasting and profiling of the blade, controlled temperature and humidity, and correct film thickness. A badly applied coating is worse than no coating: it raises roughness immediately and then detaches unevenly.
  • Benefits: slower fouling and easier subsequent cleaning, a lower average roughness across the docking cycle, and some protection against corrosion and impingement.

The limits are what distinguish a good answer, and they are specific:

  • Coatings wear preferentially at the leading edge and at the tip - the highest relative velocity, and precisely the areas whose finish matters most.
  • Cavitation removes them locally, so a blade with an existing cavitation problem will strip its coating in exactly the region that is already suffering.
  • A coating is therefore a life-extender between polishes, not a substitute for polishing. A ship that coats and stops measuring has bought a longer interval and lost the ability to know when it has expired.

6. Propeller polishing - method, grades, and why it pays

Polishing is the controlled removal of fouling, calcareous deposit and surface roughness to a specified finish. It is done in water by divers, or in dock, where a higher and more consistent standard is achievable.

  • Cover the whole surface. Both the pressure and suction faces, and also the boss and the rope guard - the areas skipped in a hurried in-water job and the ones that quietly re-seed the blades.
  • Work through progressively finer grades, and never leave coarse directional scratching: a badly polished blade can be rougher than the fouled one it replaced, because the scratch pattern is regular and closely spaced, which is exactly the texture that costs most.
  • The leading edge and the outer radii are specified to a finer finish than the rest of the blade, because the relative velocity there is highest and they do most of the work.
  • Record the finish grade achieved, blade by blade, and verify against the performance trend - which closes the loop back to limb (b) and turns the polish from an event into a measurement.

Draw one boundary firmly, because crossing it is an operational error and not merely an examination one. Polishing does not reshape the blade. Dressing out edge damage, correcting pitch, welding and blending cavitation damage, and any removal of material that alters the section are propeller repair. They require approved procedures, a qualified repairer and class involvement, and a diver with a grinder must never be allowed to drift from one into the other.

Why it pays, in one paragraph. The propeller is a small area operating at the highest relative water velocity on the ship, and it sits at the end of the whole propulsion chain - so a few per cent lost there is a few per cent lost on everything upstream of it. Polishing is cheap, quick and can be done in water without taking the ship out of service, and its effect is felt immediately as a lighter-running engine. Of every energy-efficiency measure available to a ship in service, it has the best ratio of benefit to cost - and it is really a measurement discipline wearing the clothes of a cleaning job.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three limbs and no printed marks, so the answer gives three roughly equal blocks. The opening physics section is what allows the three to be short: once skin friction, the boundary layer and the texture-and-spacing point are established, each limb can be written tightly without becoming thin.

Limb (a) is given two sections because it contains two different things - the maintenance cycle, and the regulatory driver that has turned it into a duty. Limb (c) is given two because the printed limb names two different activities, and a candidate who runs coating and polishing together will describe neither properly.

Common mistakes

  • Turning it into a paint question. The examiner asked about management, monitoring and maintenance.
  • Forgetting that physical roughness is irrecoverable. It is the reason a twenty-year-old hull cannot be returned to newbuild condition by cleaning.
  • Describing performance monitoring without normalisation. Unnormalised data is noise, and conclusions drawn from it are worse than no conclusions.
  • Defining condition-based maintenance as 'maintenance based on condition' and stopping. The content is the three evidence sources and the agreed threshold.
  • Treating a propeller coating as a permanent fix. It wears first at the leading edge and tip.
  • Blurring polishing into repair. Reshaping a blade is a class matter.
  • Drifting into a general decarbonisation essay. Name the SEEMP and the carbon intensity regime and get back to the hull.

Examiner traps

  • Slime costs more than you expect. Texture and spacing matter as much as height, so a soft even film can outperform a weld seam as a source of drag.
  • Niche areas. Sea chests, thruster tunnels and bilge keels carry disproportionate hard fouling and are the classic omission.
  • Foul-release coatings are activity-dependent. They are a poor choice for a ship that lies idle.
  • In-water cleaning needs permission, and it is not always given.
  • Aggressive cleaning damages the coating and shortens the next cycle. The fix can make it worse.
  • A bad polish can be worse than no polish if it leaves regular coarse scratching.
  • The biofouling guidance is guidance, not law - though ports increasingly make parts of it a condition of entry.
  • Do not quote fuel-saving percentages. They are quoted freely in the trade press, vary enormously with ship and trade, and are exactly the kind of figure an examiner will ask you to justify.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Your performance monitoring shows a five per cent power increase. How do you know it is the hull and not the weather?
  • Why is a foul-release coating a bad idea for a ship that spends months at anchor?
  • Where would you expect to find cavitation erosion on a blade, and why there?
  • You have a diver down and he reports edge damage. What do you authorise him to do?
  • What is in a biofouling record book, and is it mandatory?
  • How does a fouled propeller show itself in the engine room?
  • Why polish the boss and the rope guard?

Model written answer

1. What has actually happened - three events in one casualty

Read the printed facts before reaching for the statute, because they decide which powers are engaged. This casualty contains three events:

  • A collision - engaging the duties of both masters, and in due course the apportionment of fault between the vessels;
  • Loss of life - which makes this a very serious marine casualty, and that classification is what makes the safety investigation mandatory rather than discretionary;
  • Damage to the vessels - raising seaworthiness, survey, class, detention and possibly salvage.

And one event that has NOT occurred: there is no oil spill. The stem says only that lives were lost and the vessel damaged. That absence is load-bearing. The stem nevertheless still asks about the marine environment, so the environmental limb survives - but it survives as a duty to prevent a discharge from a damaged ship that threatens one, not as a response to pollution that has happened. An answer describing booms, skimmers and shoreline clean-up has answered a different casualty.

The governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and it is worth noticing what kind of statute it is: as much a grant of powers as a list of prohibitions. Almost every step below is somebody exercising a power the Act confers - to detain, to direct, to inquire, to act and recover the cost.

2. Immediate steps on board - the Master, and the order matters

The master initiates all of this, because he is the only person present. The order is itself examinable.

  • Muster, account for all persons on board, and search for the missing. Lives come before the ship. This is the overriding first duty and it precedes damage assessment and reporting.
  • Render assistance to the other vessel, her master, crew and passengers, so far as he can do so without serious danger to his own ship and those on board; stand by; and exchange the identifying particulars of his own ship - her name, port of registry and the ports she is coming from and going to. Failure to comply with this duty is an offence, and it also raises a presumption that the collision was caused by his wrongful act, neglect or default.
  • Assess the ship. Sound all tanks, bilges and compartments; establish the extent and location of the damage; contain flooding and close watertight boundaries; and assess stability and residual longitudinal strength before moving the ship at all - a damaged ship put on a course or a speed she can no longer take is how a survivable casualty becomes a total loss.
  • Prevent a discharge. Isolate the threatened tank, transfer oil internally to sound tanks where it is safe to do so, and implement the shipboard oil pollution emergency plan against the probability of a release rather than against one that has happened.
  • Broadcast and report. A distress or urgency message as the situation requires; and a danger message where the casualty endangers navigation.
  • Preserve the evidence. Secure the voyage data recorder before it overwrites; preserve charts, the bridge and engine room log books, the movement book, course and engine order recorders, alarm and event printouts, and note the position and time. This is a first-hour duty, not an afterthought, and on the engineering side it is the Chief Engineer's.

4. Steps for the safety of the ships

Both ships, not only the Indian-flag one.

  • Damage survey by the classification society and by the Administration's surveyor. Repairs, or an approved temporary repair, before the ship proceeds, with conditions of class imposed and a voyage plan agreed where she is permitted to move to a repair port.
  • Detention. Where a ship is unsafe by reason of damage, defective equipment, undermanning or overloading, the Director-General or an authorised surveyor may provisionally detain her. This is the power that converts an opinion about seaworthiness into an enforceable prohibition on sailing.
  • Salvage and towage where the ship cannot proceed under her own power - commonly engaged on Lloyd's Open Form, under the salvage provisions of the Act and the rules made under it.
  • A place of refuge. Where a damaged ship needs sheltered water, the decision is the coastal State's alone and there is no right to one. It is made under the IMO Assembly guidelines on places of refuge then operative, read with the resolution on maritime assistance services, balancing the risk to the ship against the risk to the coast.
  • Directions. The Act empowers the Central Government to give directions to the owner, the master or the salvor as to what shall or shall not be done. This is the power that makes a course of action compellable rather than merely recommended, and it is the answer to 'who should initiate' when the owner will not.
  • Navigational safety - marking the casualty, navigational warnings to shipping, and an exclusion zone around the site where necessary.

5. Steps for the marine environment - preventive, because nothing has yet escaped

The whole of this section is contingent, and saying so is what makes it correct on these facts.

  • The ship acts first. The shipboard oil pollution emergency plan is implemented; the threatened tank is isolated; oil is transferred internally where it is safe; leaks are plugged and patched; and the ship rigs her own containment as far as she is able.
  • The risk is assessed and declared honestly - the quantity and grade of oil in the threatened spaces, the condition of the boundaries retaining it, and the realistic worst case. Understating it to avoid a detention is the single worst decision available at this point.
  • The Indian Coast Guard and the national oil spill contingency plan are placed on READINESS, not on response. Equipment and personnel are mobilised to a state of preparedness; nothing is deployed against a spill that has not occurred.
  • The legal engine is the Part of the Act empowering the Central Government, where a casualty creates a risk of pollution, to give directions, to take such action itself as it considers necessary, and to recover the cost of doing so. The power reaches a THREATENED escape and not only an actual one - which is precisely why it applies on facts where nothing has yet been spilt.
  • Co-ordination with the State pollution control board, the fisheries and environment authorities and the port authority.
  • Funding is already in place - through the P&I club and, where the ship carries the appropriate certificate, through the compulsory pollution insurance with a right of direct action against the insurer.

6. Investigation and inquiry - two systems, and both are engaged

There are two, they run in parallel, and confusing them is the classic error.

The national system, under the Act:

  • the report of the casualty by the master or owner;
  • a preliminary inquiry by a person appointed for the purpose - a surveyor or other officer - to establish what happened and whether more is needed;
  • a formal investigation before a court with assessors, which may find fault and which may cancel or suspend the certificate of a master, mate or engineer. It is therefore partly disciplinary.

The international system:

  • the Casualty Investigation Code, made mandatory through SOLAS chapter XI-1;
  • mandatory here, because a death makes this a very serious marine casualty. Note that the environmental trigger for the same obligation requires severe damage to the environment, and there has been none - so it is the loss of life alone that compels the investigation;
  • India, as flag State, is the marine safety investigating State; the other vessel's flag State, if foreign, is a substantially interested State and may participate; and the report is submitted to IMO.
  • Its purpose is the prevention of recurrence, and it is expressly NOT the apportionment of blame or the determination of liability.

Hold the two apart in the answer. One can take a man's certificate away; the other must not apportion blame at all. A candidate who merges them has misdescribed both.

7. Liability, and a direct answer to the examiner's 'who'

  • Collision liability is apportioned by degree of fault, on the COLREG breaches established by the evidence. The doctrine of apportionment is the subject of a separate question on this paper and is not repeated here.
  • Claims lie in the admiralty jurisdiction of the High Courts under the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 - in rem against the ship, with arrest and the provision of security, or in personam against the owner.
  • Claims for loss of life and personal injury carry a maritime lien, ranking second in the priority of liens, after crew wages.
  • Limitation. The owner may limit his liability by tonnage under the limitation convention as amended, unless the loss resulted from his personal act or omission committed with intent or recklessly and with knowledge.
  • Pollution liability arises only if a discharge follows, and is stated conditionally for that reason: strict, channelled to the registered owner, and backed by compulsory insurance.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question asks two things - what steps and who initiates them - and an answer that gives only the first loses half of it. The 'who' is therefore answered inside every section and again in a table at the close, which costs four lines and directly answers the printed words.

Section 1 is the section that makes the rest correct. Identifying the three events, and expressly identifying the fourth that has not happened, is what stops the answer sliding into a spill-response essay - and it takes six lines.

Common mistakes

  • Inventing an oil spill. The stem says lives lost and vessel damaged. Nothing has escaped. The environmental duty here is preventive.
  • Giving the master's duties out of order. Muster and search for the missing come first.
  • Merging the two investigations. The national formal investigation may cancel a certificate; the international safety investigation must not apportion blame.
  • Saying a damaged ship has a right to a place of refuge. She does not. The coastal State decides.
  • Forgetting the other ship. The question says 'the ships', plural.
  • Omitting evidence preservation, and in particular the VDR, which overwrites.
  • Answering 'who' with a single authority. The statute distributes the initiative deliberately.

Examiner traps

  • Loss of life is what makes the safety investigation mandatory. The environmental trigger requires SEVERE damage to the environment, which has not occurred - so do not reach for it.
  • The MARPOL Protocol I report is triggered by a PROBABILITY of discharge. No actual escape is needed, which is exactly the point on these facts.
  • The statutory environmental power reaches prevention, not only containment. A candidate who thinks the power only bites after a spill will conclude, wrongly, that there is nothing to say.
  • Failure to stand by is both an offence and the trigger for a presumption of fault. Two consequences, not one.
  • Detention is provisional and is exercised by the Director-General or an authorised surveyor - not by the port, not by class, and not by the coast guard.
  • Loss of life claims rank SECOND among maritime liens, after crew wages.
  • The administration at this sitting is DG Shipping. The later renaming belongs to a statute that is years away from this paper.
  • Assess stability and residual strength BEFORE moving. The instinct to make for port is how damaged ships are lost.

Uncertainty and jurisdiction notes

Model written answer

1. What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship

Begin with an honest statement about the Act's structure, because it is itself a mark. The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 carries no separate definitions entry for 'unseaworthy'. The meaning is drawn from the section that creates the offence of sending an unseaworthy ship to sea, which describes the condition by listing what fitness is measured against.

A ship is unseaworthy where, by reason of any of the following, she is not fit to proceed to sea:

  • the materials of which she is made, and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including the officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, and of her boilers and machinery.

And the closing formula is the test itself: she must be fit in every respect to proceed on the proposed voyage or service without serious danger to human life.

Two features of that test are what the examiner is looking for.

  • It reaches far beyond steel. The crew and the stowage are in the list. A structurally perfect ship with a full set of certificates can be unseaworthy because she is undermanned, or because her officers hold the wrong certificates for the trade, or because her cargo has been stowed so as to make her unstable or overstressed. Candidates who describe only hull and machinery have given a third of the definition.
  • It is relative, not absolute. Fitness is judged against the proposed voyage or service. The same ship may be entirely fit for a short coastal passage in fine weather and unfit for a winter ocean crossing, without anything about her having changed. Seaworthiness is a relationship between a ship and a job, and saying so is what turns a list into an answer.

2. The offence, and the defences the Act allows

The definition exists because the Act builds an offence on it:

  • it is an offence for a person to send or attempt to send an Indian ship to sea in an unseaworthy state;
  • and it is a separate offence for a master who knowingly takes her to sea in that state.
  • The threshold in both cases is that the life of any person is likely to be thereby endangered. Not every unfitness is criminal - the unfitness must endanger life.

The Act allows two defences, and knowing them is what distinguishes a precise answer:

  • that he used all reasonable means to ensure her being sent to sea in a seaworthy state; or
  • that sending or taking her to sea in that state was, under the circumstances, reasonable and justifiable - and the Act places the burden of proving that on him.

There is also a procedural protection: no prosecution is instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government. That is not a technicality. It exists because a criminal charge against an owner or master over a matter of seaworthiness is a serious step, and the Act reserves the decision to take it.

3. What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her

'Unsafe' is a different provision doing a different job. A ship is unsafe where, by reason of:

  • the defective condition of her hull, equipment or machinery; or
  • overloading or improper loading,

she is unfit to proceed to sea without serious danger to human life, having regard to the nature of the service for which she is intended. Note that the same relative test appears - fitness is measured against the intended service, not in the abstract.

The consequence is a power, and its shape matters:

  • the ship may be provisionally detained for the purpose of survey;
  • a written statement of the grounds is served on the master forthwith, so that the owner knows the case he has to meet;
  • a survey follows, by a surveyor or a competent person appointed for the purpose;
  • on the report, the ship is either released or finally detained until the defects are made good to the satisfaction of the Administration;
  • and there is provision for compensation where a ship has been detained without sufficient reason - the discipline on the power, and the counterpart of the fact that it can be exercised on an opinion rather than on proof.

The relationship between them, stated as a rule: every unsafe ship is unseaworthy, but not every unseaworthy ship is unsafe within the meaning of the detention power. A ship that is structurally sound and correctly loaded but seriously undermanned, or whose chief engineer holds no valid certificate for her, is unseaworthy - yet she does not fall within the listed causes of the detention provision. In practice the gap is closed by other machinery: the safe manning document, the certificates themselves, and port State control.

5. The owner's obligation to the crew

This is the most quietly forceful provision in the area, and its force is in how it operates rather than in what it requires.

  • It is IMPLIED. The obligation is implied into every contract of service between the owner and the master or a seaman, and into every contract of apprenticeship to the sea service. Nobody has to write it in, and its absence from the agreement means nothing.
  • It operates NOTWITHSTANDING ANY AGREEMENT TO THE CONTRARY. It cannot be excluded, limited or waived - not by the contract, and not by the seafarer himself. This is the heart of it: the person the obligation protects is the person with least power to refuse to sail, so the law removes his capacity to give the protection away.
  • It binds more than the owner. The obligation lies on the owner, on the master, and on every agent charged with the loading of the ship or the preparing of her for sea or the sending of her to sea - so responsibility follows the function and cannot be arranged out of existence by interposing a manager or an agent.
  • Its content is twofold. To use all reasonable means to ensure the seaworthiness of the ship for the voyage at the time when the voyage commences; and to keep her in a seaworthy condition during the voyage.

The second half of that is the part candidates omit, and it is the operationally important one. The duty does not discharge itself at the moment of departure. It is a continuing obligation throughout the voyage - which is precisely why a defect arising at sea, ignored, is a breach, and why the planned maintenance system, the defect list and the reporting of defects to the company are the machinery by which an owner actually performs it.

Note also what the standard is: all reasonable means, not a guarantee. The Act does not make the owner an insurer of the ship's condition; it requires him to have done everything reasonable. That is the same standard as the first defence to the offence, and the two provisions read together.

6. How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force at this sitting

  • Under the Act itself, the Central Government may cause a ship to be surveyed where a complaint is made that she is unseaworthy, and the detention machinery in section 3 above stands behind it.
  • In practice the obligation is policed by the classification society at periodical and occasional survey, by flag State survey and certification, by ISM internal and external audit of the safety management system, and by port State control in every port the ship enters.
  • And by the seafarer himself - through the on-board complaint procedure, through complaint to the flag State administration, and through complaint to an authorized officer in a port of call. The statutory obligation and the labour-convention machinery reinforce one another: the Act says the ship must be kept seaworthy, and the complaint machinery is how the man on board says that she is not.

Close on the statute, because the question says 'as amended' and invites the point. The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the operative statute at this sitting, and there is nothing else in the field. The Act that eventually replaced it was years away: the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 received the assent of the President on 18 August 2025 and commenced on 15 March 2026, roughly thirty-two months after this sitting. Every proposition above is given on the 1958 Act as it then stood.

Common mistakes

  • Confining unseaworthiness to hull and machinery. The crew and the stowage are in the statutory list, and they are the half that is forgotten.
  • Treating the two terms as synonyms, or as differing only in degree. One is a state carrying an offence; the other is a trigger for a power.
  • Getting the overlap backwards. Every unsafe ship is unseaworthy; the converse does not hold.
  • Forgetting that the obligation to the crew is IMPLIED and cannot be contracted out of. That is the whole point of it.
  • Stopping the owner's obligation at departure. It continues throughout the voyage.
  • Omitting the defences to the offence, and the requirement of Central Government consent to prosecute.
  • Describing detention as final. It is provisional, pending survey.
  • Answering on the wrong statute. The 1958 Act governs.

Memory framework

One phrase carries the definition: fit in every respect to proceed on the proposed voyage or service. Hang the five heads off it - materials and construction, master, crew, cargo and ballast, hull and machinery - and note that two of the five are about people and cargo rather than about the ship.

Then two words and two mechanisms:

  • UNSEAWORTHY = STATE + OFFENCE. Wide, retrospective, needs a prosecution.
  • UNSAFE = LISTED CAUSE + POWER. Narrow, preventive, needs an opinion and a survey.
  • The overlap runs one way: every unsafe ship is unseaworthy; not the converse.

And for the last part, one sentence: implied into every contract, cannot be signed away, binds the agent too, and continues throughout the voyage.

Critical regulation
Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 - the unseaworthy-ship provision creating the offence and supplying the meaning; the provision implying the obligation of seaworthiness into every contract of service and apprenticeship notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary; and the unsafe-ship provision conferring the power of provisional detention.
Major trap
Treating 'unseaworthy' and 'unsafe' as the same idea in different words. They differ in KIND: unseaworthiness is a STATE, defined widely enough to include the crew and the stowage, and the law's response is an OFFENCE punished after the fact; an unsafe ship is a TRIGGER FOR A POWER, defined narrowly by listed causes, and the response is DETENTION before the fact. The overlap runs one way only - every unsafe ship is unseaworthy, but a sound ship that is seriously undermanned is unseaworthy without falling within the detention provision. The second trap is stopping the owner's obligation at departure: it is implied, unwaivable, binds the loading agent too, and CONTINUES THROUGHOUT THE VOYAGE.
