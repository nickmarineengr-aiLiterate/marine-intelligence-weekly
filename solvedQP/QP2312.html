Understand this first

Rules used to tell a builder exactly what to do - how thick a plate, how big a scantling. That has two problems: it locks in the design practice of whenever it was written, and it gives no credit to a designer who reaches the same safety a better way. Goal-based standards turn it round. The regulator says what the ship must achieve and for how long she must keep achieving it, and leaves the detailed rule-writing to the people who have always done it - the classification societies. The regulator's job becomes checking that those detailed rules really do deliver the stated outcome. That is why the structure comes in layers: a plain statement of what a safe ship is at the top, the things she must functionally be able to do underneath it, and the detailed rule book below that - with an audit in between to join them up. And because the goal is about the whole of the ship's life rather than the day she is handed over, the assumptions behind the design have to be written down and handed over with her.

Model written answer

1. What goal-based standards are, and the problem they solve

A prescriptive rule tells the builder what to do - this plate thickness, that scantling. It has two defects. It freezes the design practice of the year it was written, and it gives a designer no credit at all for a better idea that reaches the same safety by another route.

Goal-based standards invert that. The regulator states the outcome the ship must achieve, and for how long she must go on achieving it. Somebody else - the classification society, or the Administration through national standards - decides how. The regulator's job then becomes checking that the detailed rules genuinely deliver the stated outcome.

2. Where the requirement bites

SOLAS regulation II-1/3-10 applies to oil tankers of 150 m in length and above and to bulk carriers of 150 m in length and above constructed with single deck, top-side tanks and hopper side tanks in cargo spaces, excluding ore carriers and combination carriers. It catches a ship by any one of three thresholds:

  • building contract placed on or after 1 July 2016; or
  • in the absence of a contract, keel laid or similar stage of construction on or after 1 July 2017; or
  • delivery on or after 1 July 2020.

The standards themselves were adopted by resolution MSC.287(87) and made mandatory through this regulation. Naming the two ship types is worth a mark: they were chosen because bulk carriers and oil tankers dominated the serious structural-failure and pollution casualty record.

3. The tier structure - and how many tiers the instrument contains

The framework is described in five tiers, but the adopted instrument contains only the top three. Getting this right separates a real answer from a memorised diagram:

TierWhat it isWho owns it
Tier IThe goals - what a safe and environmentally friendly ship isIMO
Tier IIThe functional requirements the ship must meet to achieve the goalsIMO
Tier IIIVerification of conformity - how it is proved that Tier IV rules deliver Tiers I and IIIMO
Tier IVThe detailed class rules and national standardsClassification societies / Administrations
Tier VIndustry standards, codes of practice and quality systemsIndustry

Tiers IV and V are not in the instrument. IMO deliberately did not write scantling rules; it wrote the goals and the machinery for checking somebody else's rules against them.

4. Tier I and Tier II - the goal, and what it demands

The regulation states the goal directly: ships shall be designed and constructed for a specified design life to be safe and environmentally friendly, when properly operated and maintained under the specified operating and environmental conditions, in intact and specified damage conditions, throughout their life. It then defines the terms:

  • Safe and environmentally friendly means adequate strength, integrity and stability to minimise the risk of loss of the ship, or of pollution, due to structural failure including collapse resulting in flooding or loss of watertight integrity.
  • Environmentally friendly also means the ship is constructed of materials for environmentally acceptable recycling.
  • Safety also includes structure, fittings and arrangements providing for safe access, escape, inspection and proper maintenance, and facilitating safe operation.
  • Specified operating and environmental conditions are defined by the intended operating area throughout the ship's life, including intermediate conditions from cargo and ballast operations in port, in waterways and at sea.
  • Specified design life is the nominal period the ship is assumed to be exposed to those conditions and to the corrosive environment - the period used to select design parameters. The actual service life may be longer or shorter.

Beneath the goal, Tier II sets fifteen functional requirements grouped by lifecycle phase - design, construction, in-service, and recycling. Two of their assumptions carry most of the engineering weight: a design life of not less than 25 years, and North Atlantic environmental conditions as the design environment regardless of the trade actually intended.

5. Why this enhances safety and environmental protection

This is limb (a)'s real question and it should be answered as a set of consequences, not as praise.

  • The goal is whole-life, not delivery-day. The words throughout their life and the 25-year design life force fatigue strength, corrosion additions and residual strength after damage into the design, rather than leaving them to be discovered in service.
  • Structural redundancy and residual strength become explicit objectives. A ship is required to survive specified damage, not merely to be strong when intact.
  • Accessibility and inspectability are written into the goal. A structure that cannot be reached cannot be surveyed, and a structure that is not surveyed fails quietly. This is the direct link to safe access and to the enhanced survey regime.
  • Corrosion control is pollution control. The dominant loss mechanism on both ship types is corrosion and fatigue leading to hull or tank breach; the goal that targets it protects the environment and the ship by the same measure.
  • Recycling is inside the goal, not bolted on. Requiring construction of materials for environmentally acceptable recycling reaches the end of the ship's life at the moment she is designed.
  • Transparency. The regime forces the design assumptions to be recorded and handed over, so that the people who maintain the ship know what she was designed to withstand.

6. Tier III - how conformity is verified

This opens limb (b), and it is the part most candidates omit. The rules are audited, not self-certified.

  • The regulation requires the goal to be achieved through the applicable structural requirements of an organization recognized by the Administration under regulation XI-1/1, or through national standards of the Administration, conforming to the functional requirements of the goal-based standards.
  • A classification society wishing its rules to be used submits them to IMO for verification of conformity, carried out under the Revised guidelines for verification of conformity, resolution MSC.454(100).
  • An audit team appointed by the Secretary-General, not the society itself, examines the rules against Tiers I and II and reports to the Maritime Safety Committee, which decides.
  • Verification is maintained, not granted once: when a society amends its rules the amendments are notified and the conformity has to be kept good.

7. Who does what - the stakeholders

PartyWhat it actually does

The examiner's phrase various stakeholders is asking for exactly this division. The mark is for showing that each party's obligation is created by a different instrument - the society's by the verification guidelines, the Administration's by SOLAS XI-1/1, the builder's by the Ship Construction File requirement.

8. The Ship Construction File - the part that survives delivery

SOLAS II-1/3-10 paragraph 4 requires that a Ship Construction File, with specific information on how the functional requirements of the goal-based standards have been applied in the design and construction, be provided upon delivery of a new ship, kept on board and/or ashore, and updated as appropriate throughout the ship's service. Its contents must at least conform to the guidelines developed by the Organization, at MSC.1/Circ.1343.

This is the mechanism that makes the whole regime operational rather than documentary. It carries the design life, the corrosion margins, the assumed loading conditions and the access arrangements forward to the people who will survey and repair the ship in twenty years - and it is the Chief Engineer's route to knowing what his ship was designed to withstand.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Eight marks each way, and the two limbs ask genuinely different things. Limb (a) is concept plus significance; limb (b) is process plus people. The commonest failure is to write one long essay on what goal-based standards are and then produce three lines on stakeholders, because the concept material is easier to recall. Steps 6, 7 and 8 are the whole of limb (b) and carry half the question.

The single highest-value discrimination on the page is knowing that the adopted instrument contains three tiers, not five. Tiers IV and V describe where class rules and industry practice sit in the framework; they are not part of what IMO adopted.

The idea underneath the question

The regulator stopped writing the rules and started auditing them. Every other feature follows from that one move: the tiers exist because somebody has to say what the goal is and somebody else has to write the detail; the verification audit exists because the two are different people; the Ship Construction File exists because the design assumptions have to outlive the designer.

Common mistakes

  • Saying goal-based standards apply to all ships. They apply to bulk carriers and oil tankers of 150 m and above, with ore carriers and combination carriers excluded.
  • Giving the application date as the adoption date. The standards were adopted in 2010 and the regulation entered into force in 2012, but the requirement bites on a building contract on or after 1 July 2016.
  • Presenting five tiers as the content of the instrument.
  • Describing verification as the society certifying its own rules. It is an IMO audit reporting to MSC.
  • Omitting the Ship Construction File, or calling it a set of as-built drawings. It is a record of how the functional requirements were applied.
  • Answering limb (b) as a second description of the concept rather than as process and stakeholders.

Examiner traps

  • 'Which ships?' Bulk carriers and oil tankers, 150 m and above, single deck with top-side and hopper side tanks; ore carriers and combination carriers excluded.
  • 'What design life?' Not less than 25 years, in North Atlantic environmental conditions - regardless of the intended trade.
  • 'Who verifies the class rules?' An audit team appointed by the Secretary-General, reporting to MSC. Not the society, not the flag State.
  • 'Is recycling part of a construction standard?' Yes - the goal requires construction of materials for environmentally acceptable recycling.
  • 'Where does the Ship Construction File live?' Provided on delivery, kept on board and/or ashore, updated throughout service.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the difference between a prescriptive and a goal-based rule? Prescriptive states the means; goal-based states the outcome and audits the means somebody else chooses.
  • Why were bulk carriers and oil tankers chosen? They dominated the serious structural-failure and pollution casualty record.
  • What does the regulation actually require of the builder? Structural requirements of a recognized organization conforming to the functional requirements, plus delivery of the Ship Construction File.
  • What happens if a society changes its rules after verification? The conformity has to be maintained; amendments are notified and re-examined.
  • How does this relate to regulation II-1/3-1? II-1/3-1 requires every ship to be designed, constructed and maintained to recognized class rules; II-1/3-10 adds, for these two ship types, that those rules must themselves have been verified against goals set by IMO.

Uncertainty, and what does not apply at this sitting

This is an unusually stable subject, which is why the December 2023 position can be stated with confidence. Every instrument this answer needs - the standards of 2010, the regulation in force since 2012, the 2016 contract threshold, the 2018 verification guidelines and the Ship Construction File circular - pre-dates this sitting by years. What does not apply at this date: any 34th Assembly instrument; the Merchant Shipping Act 2025; and the later work on extending goal-based standards to other ship types, which is an unfinished agenda item and is not law at this sitting or at any other. One citation to get right: the Ship Construction File sits at paragraph 4 of the regulation, not at paragraph 2.1 - the paragraph number is an easy slip and an examiner will notice it. The tier count, the number of functional requirements, the 25-year design life and the North Atlantic assumption are the content of the adopted standards themselves, which MIW does not hold; they are given as established instrument content and no provision has been invented to support them.

Understand this first

Two different things are going on in this question and mixing them is what loses the marks. The first is a question about authority: a port State is stopping and inspecting a ship that belongs to somebody else's register, and the mark is for knowing that it is allowed to do that because each of the great conventions contains its own clause saying so. The published inspection manual is guidance on how to use that power; it is not the source of it. The second is practical and personal. If the ship is held and you think the reasons are unfair, there are two separate jobs and they are not yours to combine. Getting the ship to sea is engineering: put the defect right, satisfy the officer, have the ship released. Arguing that holding her was wrong is legal, it belongs to the company rather than to you, and it will not move tomorrow's sailing by an hour - the manual says in one line that appealing does not lift the detention. What you can do, and what matters most, is preserve the evidence before you repair anything, because once the defect is fixed the proof that it was never dangerous has gone.

Model written answer

1. What port State control is - and what it is not

  • The inspection of foreign ships in national ports to verify that the condition of the ship and her equipment, the competence and certification of her crew, and the manner in which she is operated, comply with the requirements of the international conventions.
  • It is a safety net beneath the flag State, not a substitute for it. It does not transfer the flag State's responsibility; the flag State remains the primary regulator and is notified of what is found.
  • It operates in practice through regional memoranda of understanding, which coordinate targeting, share inspection data and avoid the same ship being inspected repeatedly in one region.
  • It is not a survey, and not a commercial vetting inspection. Its purpose is to identify a substandard ship and to see her corrected.

2. The legal framework - where the power actually comes from

This is the heart of limb (a) and the mark is for knowing that the power is conferred by the conventions themselves, each of which contains its own control article. The IMO procedures are guidance on how to exercise that power; they do not create it.

IMO publishes a consolidated set of Procedures for Port State Control, revised periodically by the Assembly. At this sitting the edition in circulation is the one adopted by the 32nd Assembly in 2021. The 33rd Assembly adopted a 2023 revision on 6 December 2023 - within the month of this examination; this paper prints no day and no claim is made about which side of that date it fell. Nothing in this answer depends on the point, for a reason worth stating in the exam:

  • The Procedures are recommendatory. The resolution adopts them and invites Governments to implement them. It confers no power and removes none - the power is in the conventions above.
  • The structure the question asks about is unchanged across the two editions: initial inspection, clear grounds, more detailed inspection, detention, notification to the flag State.

The operational layer is regional. Port States coordinate through memoranda of understanding - Paris, Tokyo, Indian Ocean, Mediterranean, Black Sea, Caribbean, Vina del Mar, Abuja and Riyadh. India is a member of the Indian Ocean MOU, and inspections in Indian ports are carried out by surveyors of DG Shipping under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 and the rules made under it.

4. No more favourable treatment

A short paragraph that earns its marks. The conventions provide that ships of States which are not Parties, and ships below convention size, shall be given no more favourable treatment than ships entitled to fly the flag of a Party. The effect is that not ratifying buys no advantage, which is what makes the whole system workable.

5. How an inspection becomes a detention

  • Initial inspection - the certificates and documents are examined and the officer forms a general impression of condition, including engine room and accommodation.
  • Clear grounds - evidence that the ship, her equipment or her crew does not correspond substantially with the certificates, or that the crew is unfamiliar with essential shipboard procedures. This is the legal trigger, and it must exist before the inspection goes further.
  • More detailed inspection - directed at the areas where the clear grounds arose.
  • Detention - where deficiencies are clearly hazardous to safety, health or the environment, the ship is detained until they are rectified or until she may proceed without unreasonable danger.
  • Notification - the detention is reported to the flag State in sufficient detail for the severity to be assessed, and to the recognized organization that issued the certificates.
  • Proceeding to a repair port - where the deficiency cannot be remedied at the port of inspection, the officer may allow the ship to proceed to another port on conditions, notifying the next port and the flag State.

6. The right of appeal - what it is, and what it does not do

This opens limb (b), and three points carry it.

  • The right is the Company's, not the Chief Engineer's. The Procedures state that the company or its representative have a right of appeal against a detention taken by the authority of a port State. The Chief Engineer, as such, does not hold it - which is the discrimination the printed question is actually testing.
  • The appeal does not suspend the detention. The ship stays detained while the appeal is heard. Arguing is not a way to sail.
  • The officer must inform the master of the right of appeal.

The appeal is then pursued at two levels in practice: to the port State authority itself under its national administrative procedure, and through the regional MOU's review mechanism, which can have a detention recorded as unjustified and removed from the ship's and the company's record. The flag State is the company's most effective ally: it may take the matter up with the port State directly, and where the certificates were issued by a recognized organization that organization is drawn in too.

7. What the Chief Engineer should actually do

The question addresses the Chief Engineer directly, so the answer must separate his job from the Company's.

  • Record the evidence before anything is changed. Photographs, readings, alarm and event logs, planned maintenance records, the certificates as presented. Once the defect is rectified the evidence that it was not hazardous is gone.
  • Ask, politely and in writing, which requirement each deficiency rests on. A deficiency has to be traceable to a provision. This is the single most useful thing he can do, and it is not an argument.
  • Notify immediately - master, Company and Designated Person, flag State Administration, and the recognized organization that issued the certificate.
  • Rectify and contest in parallel, never in sequence. Getting the ship to sea is an engineering job; challenging the detention is a legal one belonging to the Company. Doing the second instead of the first keeps the ship alongside.
  • Keep it professional. The officer is exercising a treaty power. The record made in the first hour is what the Company's appeal will be built on.

8. Undue delay and compensation - a different remedy

Where a ship is unduly detained or delayed, the conventions provide a remedy in damages: MARPOL article 7(2) and the corresponding provisions of SOLAS regulation I/19 and LOAD LINES article 21 require that every effort be made to avoid undue detention, and entitle the ship to compensation for loss or damage suffered.

Two traps live here. First, compensation is a claim, not a release - it is pursued afterwards and does nothing to get the ship to sea. Second, it is a different remedy from the appeal: the appeal attacks the detention, the compensation claim attacks the loss it caused, and they are pursued in different places. A candidate who runs them together loses the discrimination the limb is testing.

Understand this first

Two ideas sit under this question and both run against the ordinary meaning of the words. The first is that general average is not compensation for damage at all. It is the sharing out of a loss that somebody chose to suffer on purpose - throwing cargo over the side, deliberately running a sinking ship aground, flooding a hold to put out a fire - because doing so saved the whole venture. Everyone whose property was saved therefore pays a share. That single idea separates it from ordinary damage, which the sea inflicts on you and which you bear alone. The second is that a warranty here is not the small print. In ordinary contracts a warranty is the minor promise and breaking it earns damages. In marine insurance it is the severe one: it has to be kept exactly, whether or not it had anything to do with what went wrong, and breaking it ends the insurer's liability from that moment onward. Notice also that some of these promises are written into the policy and some are put there by law whether anyone writes them or not - and the ones the law implies do not all apply to every kind of policy.

Model written answer

1. The types of loss in marine insurance

Losses divide first into total and partial. Two further heads - salvage charges and particular charges - are recoverable but are not, strictly, losses of the subject matter, and saying so is worth a mark.

The pivot of the whole classification is deliberateness. Particular average is what the sea did to you; general average is what you chose to do about it.

2. The general average act, as the 1994 Rules define it

The examiner has named the York-Antwerp Rules 1994, and Rule A is where the definition lives. In substance it provides that there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety, for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure; and that general average sacrifices and expenditures are borne by the several contributing interests on the basis set out in the Rules.

Five words in that sentence each carry a condition, and the next step unpacks them. The wording of Rule A is unchanged in the 2016 edition, so a candidate who learns it once has it for either.

3. The circumstances in which general average can be declared

Set these out as separate conditions - the examiner is counting them.

  • There must be a real peril, present and substantial, threatening the common adventure. Apprehension of a danger that has not arisen is not enough.
  • The act must be extraordinary. Doing what the ship is contractually bound to do, or what ordinary good seamanship requires, is not a general average act however expensive it proves.
  • It must be intentional and voluntary - a decision, not an accident. Cargo swept overboard by the sea is particular average; cargo jettisoned to lighten the ship is general average.
  • It must be reasonable. The Rule Paramount - in these terms in the 1994 edition - provides that no allowance shall be made unless the sacrifice or expenditure was reasonably made or incurred.
  • It must be for the common safety, that is for the benefit of the whole adventure and not of one interest alone. Sacrificing cargo to save only the ship is not general average.
  • There must be a common maritime adventure - at least two interests at risk, typically ship, cargo and freight.
  • Property must in fact be saved. There must be a fund to contribute; if everything is lost there is nothing to share.
  • Fault does not bar the claim. Rule D provides that rights to contribution are not affected even though the event which gave rise to the sacrifice or expenditure may have been due to the fault of one of the parties to the adventure - but this is without prejudice to any remedies or defences which may be open against or to that party in respect of that fault. General average is therefore declared and adjusted first, and the question of fault is fought separately; an allegation of unseaworthiness or negligence does not stop the adjustment being drawn up.
  • The declaration is made by the shipowner, through the Master, who appoints an average adjuster; general average security - a general average bond from cargo interests and a general average guarantee from their underwriters - is taken before cargo is released.

Classic examples: jettison of cargo; voluntary stranding to prevent foundering; damage from extinguishing a fire aboard, including water damage to sound cargo; engine damage caused deliberately in working a stranded ship off; and port of refuge expenses - putting in, discharging to effect repairs, and the associated wages and maintenance.

4. Why the 1994 edition matters, since the examiner named it

  • The Rules are contractual, not law. They bind only because the contract of carriage and the policy incorporate them, and the edition is whichever the contract names. That is the first mark in this step.
  • Rule XX (1994) allows a commission of 2 per cent on general average disbursements other than crew wages and maintenance and fuel and stores not replaced.
  • Rule XXI (1994) allows interest at 7 per cent per annum on expenditure, sacrifices and allowances until three months after the adjustment is issued. Later editions replaced that fixed rate with an annually determined one.
  • The 1994 Rules end at Rule XXII and contain no time bar. A time-bar rule appears only in later editions.
  • The 2016 edition is the newer text a modern contract may incorporate. It is not a superseding law and it does not amend the 1994 Rules; if the contract says 1994, the 1994 Rules apply.

5. Warranties in marine insurance - and what breach does

Limb (b) turns on a word that means something different here than in ordinary contract law. In ordinary contract law a warranty is the minor term. In marine insurance it is the severe one.

  • A marine-insurance warranty is a promissory warranty: the assured undertakes that some thing shall or shall not be done, that some condition shall be fulfilled, or affirms or negatives the existence of a particular state of facts.
  • It must be exactly complied with, whether it is material to the risk or not. Substantial compliance is not compliance.
  • Under the Indian Act, as under the English Act of 1906 on which it is modelled, breach discharges the insurer from liability as from the date of the breach. It does not avoid the policy from inception, and the insurer remains liable for anything that happened before.
  • Breach is excused only narrowly - where circumstances change so the warranty ceases to be applicable, or where compliance is made unlawful by a subsequent law. A remedy of the breach before loss does not, of itself, restore cover under this rule.
  • The English Insurance Act 2015 changed the English position, converting breach into a suspension of liability that revives on remedy, and preventing an insurer relying on a breach irrelevant to the loss. That is English law. It is not the Indian rule, and saying so is a high-value discrimination.

6. Express warranties, with a hull and machinery example

  • An express warranty is one written into or upon the policy, or contained in a document incorporated into it by reference. No particular form of words is required - what matters is that the intention to warrant is clear.
  • It does not exclude an implied warranty unless it is inconsistent with it.

Hull and machinery examples, any one of which answers the question:

  • Classification warranty - warranted classed and class maintained with a named or approved society, with the assured undertaking to report to underwriters any change, suspension, withdrawal or expiry of class. This is the commonest and best example, and it links directly to the classification regime.
  • Navigation and trading limits - warranted no Baltic winter trading, or trading confined to defined institute limits.
  • Lay-up warranty - a return of premium warranted only for a laid-up and out-of-commission period in a safe port.
  • Warranted no towage or salvage services, save as customary or under standard terms.

7. Implied warranties, and the twist that catches candidates

  • An implied warranty is imposed by law and is in the policy without being written in it.
  • Legality of the adventure - a warranty that the adventure is lawful and, so far as the assured can control it, will be carried out lawfully. This one cannot be excluded by agreement.
  • Seaworthiness in a voyage policy - a warranty that the ship shall be seaworthy at the commencement of the voyage for the purpose of that particular adventure, including being properly manned, equipped, fuelled and supplied.
  • Here is the twist that decides the mark in a hull and machinery question: there is NO implied warranty of seaworthiness in a TIME policy, and a hull and machinery policy is normally written for a period of time. There is also no implied warranty as to the nationality of the ship.

But unseaworthiness is not irrelevant to a time policy. Where, with the privity of the assured, the ship is sent to sea in an unseaworthy state, the insurer is not liable for any loss attributable to that unseaworthiness. That is a narrower and different rule from a warranty: it requires the owner's knowledge or blind-eye awareness, and it defeats only the loss the unseaworthiness caused - not the whole policy.

The idea underneath the question

Two ideas, and both are counter-intuitive. First, general average is not compensation for damage - it is the sharing out of a loss somebody chose to suffer on purpose to save the whole venture, which is why everyone who benefited pays a share. Second, in marine insurance a warranty is the severe term, not the minor one: it must be kept exactly, whether or not it has anything to do with the loss, and breaking it ends the insurer's liability from that moment.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing particular average with general average. Particular average is accidental and borne alone; general average is deliberate and shared.
  • Omitting the notice of abandonment from constructive total loss. Without it the loss is treated as partial.
  • Reciting Rule A without unpacking the conditions. The circumstances are a separate task and are separately marked.
  • Saying the York Antwerp Rules are law. They are contractual and bind by incorporation.
  • Answering to the 2016 edition when the question names 1994.
  • Saying breach of warranty avoids the policy from inception. It discharges the insurer from the date of breach.
  • Stating an implied warranty of seaworthiness in a hull and machinery time policy. There is none - the classic error on this limb.
  • Applying the English 2015 reform as though it were the Indian rule.

Examiner traps

  • 'Cargo washed overboard in heavy weather - general average?' No. Accidental, therefore particular average.
  • 'Cargo jettisoned to lighten a grounded ship?' Yes - deliberate, extraordinary, for the common safety.
  • 'Nothing was saved. Can general average be declared?' No. There must be a contributing fund.
  • 'Your hull policy is a time policy. Is there an implied warranty of seaworthiness?' No - but if the ship went to sea unseaworthy with the owner's privity, a loss attributable to that unseaworthiness is not covered.
  • 'You breached a warranty but fixed it before the casualty. Are you covered?' Under the Indian rule, no - remedy before loss does not restore cover. Under the English Act of 2015 it would. Say which system you are answering in.
  • 'Which edition of the Rules applies to your voyage?' Whichever the contract of carriage and the policy name.

Model written answer

1. Why decarbonisation changes what an engineer must be competent at

The important claim is not that engineers must work harder at what they already know. It is that the hazard has changed in kind. Heavy fuel oil hurt you by burning. The fuels now arriving hurt you in ways the existing training does not reach:

  • one is poisonous well before it is flammable;
  • one burns with a flame you cannot see in daylight;
  • one escapes through joints that would hold oil, and embrittles the steel it escapes through;
  • one stores its energy chemically, so you cannot make it safe by shutting a valve.

A competence framework built around a hot, oily, flammable liquid does not transfer to any of those by degrees. That is the case for upskilling, and it should be the opening sentence of the answer.

2. The driver, as it stands at this sitting

  • The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy was adopted at MEPC 80 on 7 July 2023, replacing the Initial Strategy of 2018. It sets the trajectory - uptake of zero or near-zero GHG emission technologies, fuels and energy sources, with indicative checkpoints on the way. It is policy, not law, and saying so is a mark.
  • What is law, and already running: EEXI and the operational carbon intensity indicator under MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4 have applied since 1 January 2023. 2023 is the first CII data year, so the first ratings will not be assigned until 2024 - the regime is being implemented at this sitting, not yet mature.
  • No market-based measure has been adopted. There is no IMO carbon price and no fuel levy. The candidate who says otherwise is describing a debate, not an instrument.
  • The strategy's effect on manpower is direct: a fleet that must change fuel needs engineers who can run the new plant, and the ships are ordered years before the training framework catches up.

3. Where the mandatory training framework reaches - and where it does not

This is the sharpest point available on limb (a) and few candidates make it.

  • STCW regulation V/3 and section A-V/3 set mandatory minimum training, at basic and advanced level, for seafarers on ships subject to the IGF Code. That is the framework, and it works.
  • The IGF Code was built around gas and other low-flashpoint fuels, with LNG as the worked case. Ships burning methanol operate under interim guidelines rather than a matured Code chapter, and ammonia and hydrogen have no adopted IMO safety guidelines at all at this date - the work is under development.
  • The consequence: the training obligation is thinnest exactly where the hazard is newest. Competence for ammonia and hydrogen is being supplied by makers, owners and training providers, not by the Convention.
  • A comprehensive review of STCW is under way but has produced nothing yet. It is the right long-term route and it will not arrive in time for the ships already on order.
  • The practical answer at this sitting is therefore company-led: fuel-specific familiarisation beyond the Convention minimum, maker training, simulator work, and a risk assessment done by the ship's own staff.

5. The competences that are not about fuel at all

  • Efficiency operation and data integrity. The CII regime turns operational data into a compliance outcome. The engineer must understand what he is reporting and why an error in it is now a compliance failure and not just a bad number.
  • Dual-fuel changeover at sea, and the judgement of when not to attempt it.
  • Risk assessment for a plant nobody has operated for twenty years. Where there is no accumulated fleet experience, the ship's own hazard identification is the control.
  • Emergency response written for the new hazard - a toxic release drill is not a fire drill.
  • Leadership and change management, because the crew has to be brought through a change of fuel without losing the safety culture built around the old one.

6. Why communication is a causal factor, not a contributory nicety

Limb (b) opens with the word the examiner used. Causal means it is a link in the chain, not a regrettable feature of the background.

The recurring pattern in casualty investigation is that the information existed on board and did not arrive where the decision was taken. Somebody knew the pump had been isolated, that the alarm had been inhibited, that the tank was not empty - and the person who acted did not. Four failure shapes cover most of it:

  • Vertical - a junior officer or rating who sees the problem and does not challenge the senior. The information is present and suppressed.
  • Horizontal - between departments. The classic is engine and deck operating on different assumptions during manoeuvring, bunkering, cargo work or a blackout.
  • Handover - watch to watch, or joiner to leaver. What was temporary becomes permanent because the reason for it was never passed on.
  • Ship to shore - the office and the ship holding different pictures of the same situation, each believing the other has understood.

7. What the instruments actually require

  • ISM Code 1.4.2 - the safety management system must define and document levels of authority and lines of communication between shore and shipboard personnel and among themselves.
  • ISM Code 6.6 - the Company shall establish procedures to ensure that ship's personnel receive relevant information on the safety management system in a working language understood by them.
  • ISM Code 6.7 - the Company shall ensure that ship's personnel are able to communicate effectively in the execution of their duties.
  • SOLAS regulation V/14 - a working language shall be established and recorded in the log book, and English is required on the bridge for safety communications where the working language is not a common one.
  • STCW Code section A-VIII/2 - watchkeeping arrangements, including bridge and engine-room resource management, which is where challenge, closed-loop communication and shared mental models are actually taught.
  • The Standard Marine Communication Phrases - a fixed vocabulary for exactly the situations in which improvised English fails.

Note that the requirement is capability, not declaration. The Code says personnel must be able to communicate effectively. A language written in a manual and not shared in practice does not satisfy it.

8. Why present-day crewing makes this harder

  • Multinational, multilingual crews are the norm. A declared working language is not the same as a shared one, and competence in a second language degrades fastest under stress - which is precisely when it is needed.
  • Short contracts and high turnover erode the informal knowledge that used to carry a ship - the things nobody wrote down because everybody had been there two years.
  • Power distance. Crews drawn from cultures with steep hierarchy, working under a Master or Chief from another, produce a real and measurable reluctance to challenge. Resource-management training exists for this reason.
  • Reduced manning and higher workload mean fewer people, each doing more, with less overlap in which to notice that a message did not land.
  • Fatigue degrades communication before it degrades anything else - the read-back is the first thing to go.
  • Shore intensity. Continuous connectivity gives the office a near-real-time picture and a stronger voice, which is useful, and which can also displace the judgement of the people who can actually see the ship.
  • And the two limbs meet here. A crew operating a fuel nobody aboard has run before is a crew whose cost of an ambiguous message has gone up. Poor communication and unfamiliar plant are not two separate problems; they multiply.

Understand this first

Every rule of maritime safety written before this one told you something about the ship: how thick the steel, how many boats, how the bulkheads were arranged, what the fire pumps must deliver. You could inspect the ship and know whether the rule had been obeyed. But ships kept being lost that satisfied every one of those requirements, and when the losses were investigated the failure was repeatedly found not in the hardware, and often not even entirely on board. It lay in how the operation was run - nobody had written down who was responsible for what, the people ashore were pressing commercially and nobody could say no, and the lesson from the last near miss had never reached anyone who could act on it. So this Code regulates the running of the ship instead of the building of her. It does not write the procedures, because the right procedure differs from trade to trade; it requires the company to have a documented system, to say who does what, and then to keep checking and correcting that system itself. That last part is what makes it work, and it is also why the Code has hardly changed its wording in thirty years while the things it has to cover - new technology, computers that can be attacked, environmental performance - have changed completely. A rule about a process can absorb a new hazard. A rule about a hazard has to be rewritten for each one.

Model written answer

1. What the Code is, and how it binds

  • The International Management Code for the Safe Operation of Ships and for Pollution Prevention, adopted by resolution A.741(18) and made mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX.
  • The operative text is the Code as amended through MSC.353(92), whose amendments entered into force on 1 January 2015. There has been no amendment to the Code since.
  • It regulates management, not construction or equipment. Every other safety instrument tells you something about the ship; this one tells you how the operation is to be run.

2. What a safety management system is, and why the Code works through one

A safety management system is a structured and documented system enabling Company personnel to implement effectively the Company's safety and environmental protection policy. The Code sets objectives and requires a system; it does not write the procedures - because the right procedure for a product tanker is not the right procedure for a bulk carrier.

That is the answer to limb (a)'s opening question. The system is not a by-product of the Code; it is the instrument through which the Code operates. Without it the Code is a statement of intent with nothing to inspect.

3. The six functional requirements

Element 1.4 requires every safety management system to include:

  • a safety and environmental protection policy;
  • instructions and procedures to ensure safe operation of ships and protection of the environment, in compliance with relevant international and flag State legislation;
  • defined levels of authority and lines of communication between and among shore and shipboard personnel;
  • procedures for reporting accidents and non-conformities with the provisions of the Code;
  • procedures to prepare for and respond to emergency situations;
  • procedures for internal audits and management reviews.

Six is a countable list and the examiner will count it. Note that the last two are the continuous-improvement machinery appearing already at the functional level.

Part B is the certification layer - elements 13 to 16: certification and periodical verification, interim certification, verification, and the forms of certificates.

  • Document of Compliance to the Company, valid up to five years, verified annually within three months of the anniversary date.
  • Safety Management Certificate to the ship, valid up to five years, with an intermediate verification between the second and third anniversary.
  • Interim certification for a new company, a new ship type or a newly delivered ship.
  • A major non-conformity requires immediate corrective action and can lead to withdrawal of the certificate - which is what gives the Code its teeth.

5. The role the system plays in implementing the Code

  • It converts objectives into procedures that a named person performs. Element 3 requires the Company to define and document responsibility, authority and interrelation of all personnel.
  • It creates the shore-to-ship link through the Designated Person, who has direct access to the highest level of management - the single structural innovation of the Code.
  • It preserves the Master's overriding authority to make decisions for safety and pollution prevention and to request the Company's assistance, so that the system can never be used to override his judgement.
  • It generates the evidence on which compliance is verified. Audit is possible only because the system produces records.

6. Continuous improvement - the mechanism, and why it is the significant part

The examiner has asked specifically for the significance of continuous improvement, so name the machinery and then say why it matters.

  • Element 9 - procedures for reporting non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences, their investigation and analysis, and corrective action to prevent recurrence. Near-miss reporting lives here, and it is the input that keeps the system honest.
  • Element 12 - the Company carries out internal safety audits at intervals not exceeding twelve months, periodically evaluates the effectiveness of the system, and requires timely corrective action by personnel responsible.
  • Element 5 - the Master's own periodic review of the system and his reporting of deficiencies to shore management.
  • Management review at Company level closes the loop, because a defect the ship cannot fix has to reach somebody who can.

Why it is the significant part: without it a safety management system becomes a static manual describing a ship nobody operates. Continuous improvement is what makes the system self-correcting rather than merely documented - and it is the difference between a certified company and a safe one. It is also the reason the Code has needed so few amendments: a system required to examine and correct itself absorbs change that a prescriptive rule would have to be rewritten to accommodate.

7. Why the Code exists, and the shift it represents

Limb (b) begins properly with the reason, because every later evolution follows from it. The Code came out of a major ro-ro passenger casualty and an inquiry that found the fault ashore as much as aboard - in how the operation was managed rather than in how the ship was built.

  • From regulating hardware to regulating management.
  • From compliance demonstrated by inspection of the ship to compliance demonstrated by an auditable system.
  • From the ship alone to the Company ashore, which is why the Document of Compliance is issued to the Company and not to the vessel.

8. The evolution - five stages

  • From recommendation to mandatory instrument. Adopted as an Assembly resolution, then made mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX and phased in by ship type. This is the single largest change in its history.
  • The amendment chain. MSC.104(73), MSC.179(79), MSC.195(80), MSC.273(85) and MSC.353(92) - successively tightening company responsibility, the handling of non-conformities, risk assessment wording and internal audit. The current text has been in force since 1 January 2015 and is the operative one at this examination.
  • Technological advancement. Integrated bridge and engine control, remote monitoring and diagnostics, condition-based maintenance and electronic record-keeping. The Code accommodated all of it without amendment, through elements 6, 7 and 10 - resources and personnel, shipboard operations, and maintenance. Familiarisation and competence, not the technology, become the binding constraint.
  • Cyber risk. Resolution MSC.428(98), adopted 16 June 2017, affirms that an approved safety management system should take cyber risk into account, and requires it to be appropriately addressed no later than the first annual verification of the Document of Compliance after 1 January 2021 - so at this examination it is a mature requirement, verified for nearly three years, not a novelty. No new element was added to the Code. The existing risk-assessment and procedures machinery was used, which is a demonstration of the Code's method rather than an exception to it.
  • Environmental sustainability - and the honest limit. Environmental protection is in the Code's title and in its objectives from the beginning, and element 7 procedures cover pollution-prevention operations. But the instruments that actually drive environmental performance sit outside the Code, in MARPOL Annex VI - the ship energy efficiency management plan and the carbon-intensity requirements that have applied since 1 January this year. The Code adapts by generality, which is both its strength and its weakness: it can absorb a new risk without amendment, and it therefore says nothing specific about any of them.

The closing judgement, which is what 'examine' is asking for: the Code has evolved far less in its text than in what it is asked to cover, and it has survived because it regulates a process rather than a hazard. A process requirement absorbs new hazards; a hazard-specific rule has to be rewritten for each one.

That single test tells you which costs continue when the ship stops earning, and therefore which ones the Chief Engineer can actually influence. Owning the ship costs money whether or not she moves; keeping her manned and fit to trade costs money every day she exists; performing a voyage costs money only while the voyage is being performed. Once you see that, the question of where an engineer has leverage answers itself - and the honest answer includes admitting where he has none. Inventory control is the same idea applied to the storeroom: decide what each item deserves before deciding how much of it to hold, because the cheapest item on the shelf may be the one whose absence stops the ship.

Model written answer

1. The three cost families, and what distinguishes them

The families are separated by what makes the money go out - not by how large they are. That single test is what lets the Chief Engineer see where he has leverage:

FamilyTriggered byBehaves like
CapitalOwning the ship at allFixed, and committed before she ever traded
OperatingKeeping her manned, maintained and fit to tradeLargely fixed per day, incurred whether or not she is employed
VoyagePerforming a particular voyageVariable - it stops when the voyage stops

2. What falls into each family

  • Capital - the newbuilding or purchase price, debt service and interest, depreciation, and the return the owner requires on his equity. A major capital improvement belongs here, not in maintenance.
  • Operating - crew wages, victualling and travel; stores and consumables including lubricating oil and chemicals; spares; repairs and maintenance; drydocking and survey costs; hull and machinery insurance and protection and indemnity cover; registration and flag fees; and shore management and administration.
  • Voyage - bunker fuel, which normally dominates; port dues and charges; canal transit dues; and pilotage and towage. Cargo-handling costs are treated by some texts as a fourth family; they are taken here as voyage-related, and the convention is stated rather than assumed.

Bunkers are a voyage cost, not an operating cost. They are triggered by performing the voyage and they cease when she stops. Misclassifying them is the commonest single error on this question.

3. Where the Chief Engineer has leverage - and where he has none

Being candid about this is what separates a management answer from a list.

  • Capital - effectively no short-run influence. The price was paid and the loan signed before he joined. His effect is indirect and long-term: through the condition in which the ship is kept, her residual value, and the technical advice he gives on a major upgrade.
  • Voyage - his largest lever, because fuel dominates the voyage account, and fuel consumption is a direct function of hull and machinery condition and of how the plant is operated.
  • Operating - shared with the office. Crew cost, insurance and flag fees are set ashore. Maintenance, spares, stores, lubricating oil and the drydock specification are substantially his.

4. Optimising voyage cost

  • Hull and propeller condition. Fouling raises the power needed for a given speed and is usually the largest single recoverable loss on a ship. Systematic performance monitoring normalised for draught, weather and speed; timely propeller polishing and hull cleaning; and a coating specification matched to the actual trade.
  • Machinery condition and performance. Indicator and pressure-rise data, turbocharger condition, air and gas differential pressures, cooler and economiser fouling - each degradation shows up as fuel before it shows up as a defect.
  • Correct operation for the loading in use. Slow steaming saves fuel but moves the engine into a region it was not optimised for; running there without the appropriate measures costs more in wear than it saves in fuel.
  • Fuel management. Bunker quantity survey and sampling at delivery, correct treatment and settling, avoidance of commingling incompatible fuels, and minimising unpumpable residues.
  • Auxiliary and hotel load. Generator loading, boiler and steam management, shaft generator use where fitted, and load reduction in port.
  • The regulatory overlay - and what it is not. Under MARPOL Annex VI regulations 23, 25 and 28, EEXI and the operational carbon intensity indicator have applied since 1 January this year, so efficiency is now a compliance outcome and a data-integrity obligation and not merely a cost. 2023 is the first data year, so no ship has yet been assigned a rating. There is no carbon price of any kind reaching this voyage account - no IMO market-based measure has been adopted, and the European emissions trading scheme does not extend to shipping until the first of next month.

5. Optimising operating cost

  • Maintenance strategy. Breakdown maintenance is the most expensive way to run machinery; time-based maintenance overhauls items that were still serviceable; condition-based maintenance spends the money when the evidence says it is needed. The real contribution is choosing the right strategy for each item rather than applying one to all.
  • Off-hire avoidance. A day off-hire usually costs more than the repair that caused it, because the daily operating cost continues while the revenue stops. Ranking maintenance by the consequence of failure rather than by the price of the part follows directly from that.
  • Lubricating oil management. Feed-rate optimisation, the correct base number for the fuel in use, and onboard and laboratory analysis to catch wear early.
  • Drydock specification. The docking is the single largest operating-cost event the Chief Engineer materially controls, and a specification written from real condition data rather than repeated from last time is where the money is saved or lost.
  • Spares and stores control - which is the third task in the question.

6. Inventory control - classifying the stock

Modern inventory control begins by recognising that not every item deserves the same control. The techniques are ways of deciding which item gets which treatment.

TechniqueClassifies byUsed for
ABC analysisAnnual consumption valueConcentrating control effort on the few items carrying most of the money
VED analysisCriticality - Vital, Essential, DesirableDeciding what must never be out of stock, regardless of price
FSN analysisMovement - Fast, Slow, Non-movingFinding dead stock and obsolescence
SDE analysisProcurement difficulty - Scarce, Difficult, EasySetting safety stock by how hard the item is to obtain

The important move is to combine them. ABC alone will under-protect a cheap but vital item; VED alone will over-protect an expensive but non-critical one. The practical shipboard method is the ABC-VED matrix, and the criticality axis is anchored in ISM Code element 10.3, which requires the Company to identify equipment and technical systems whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations, and to provide specific measures promoting their reliability. That identification is the ship's own authoritative definition of a 'Vital' item.

7. Inventory control - ordering, and the limits of just-in-time at sea

  • Economic order quantity balances the cost of ordering against the cost of holding and gives the order size at which their sum is least. A useful idea, but it assumes steady demand and a stable lead time.
  • Reorder level and minimum-maximum. Reorder level is average consumption over the lead time plus a safety stock. On a ship the lead time is the hard variable, because it depends on where the ship will be, not on how quickly the supplier can act.
  • Safety stock buffers variability in both demand and lead time, and should be set by criticality rather than uniformly.
  • Lead-time and voyage-plan integration. Ordering is planned against the trading pattern so deliveries fall where the ship will actually be, and against the maintenance plan so parts arrive before the overhaul rather than after it.
  • Standardisation and fleet pooling. Common makers and models across a fleet cut the number of distinct items held and let stock be shared between ships.
  • Vendor-managed inventory and framework agreements with makers and service providers, which move some holding cost and obsolescence risk onto the supplier.
  • Digital stock control integrated with the planned maintenance system, so that consumption, criticality and the maintenance schedule are read from one record rather than three.

Just-in-time deserves a qualification, not a recommendation. Minimising holding is sound for consumables and for non-critical items obtainable worldwide. It is wrong for critical spares: a ship is not a factory beside a motorway, the lead time is measured in ports rather than hours, and the consequence of a stock-out is an unseaworthy ship rather than an idle production line. The correct answer is selective - just-in-time where the item is easy and the failure tolerable, and protected stock where element 10.3 says the failure is not.

Why this structure scores

Three tasks in one unlimbed question, roughly a third of the marks each. The failure mode is predictable: candidates write at length on the cost families, moderately on the Chief Engineer's role, and then produce two lines on inventory control because the time has gone. Steps 6 and 7 are a full third of the question and the route is built so they are reached with time in hand.

Step 3 - being candid about where the Chief Engineer has no influence - is what makes this read as a management answer rather than a recitation. Saying plainly that capital cost is beyond his short-run reach earns more credit than inventing influence he does not have.

The idea underneath the question

Classify by trigger, not by size. Capital is triggered by owning, operating by keeping her fit to trade, voyage by performing the voyage. Once costs are classified that way you can see immediately which ones stop when the ship stops - and therefore which ones the person running the machinery can actually move. Inventory control is the same idea applied to stock: decide what each item deserves before deciding how much of it to hold.

Common mistakes

  • Classing bunkers as an operating cost. They are a voyage cost.
  • Listing the three families and never answering the second and third tasks.
  • Claiming the Chief Engineer optimises capital cost. He does not, in any short-run sense.
  • Naming ABC and VED without saying what each classifies by, or how they are used together.
  • Recommending just-in-time for critical spares without qualification.
  • Treating drydock cost as unavoidable. The specification is where it is decided.
  • Forgetting that cargo-handling costs are treated differently by different texts - say which convention you are using.

Examiner traps

  • 'Is fuel a voyage or an operating cost?' Voyage. The commonest single trap on the question.
  • 'Would you apply just-in-time on board?' Selectively - and never to items identified under element 10.3.
  • 'ABC says this item is class C. Do you let it run out?' Not if VED says it is Vital. That is exactly why the two are combined.
  • 'What is the largest recoverable fuel loss on a ship?' Usually hull and propeller fouling.
  • 'What happens to costs when the ship goes off-hire?' The daily operating cost continues while the revenue stops - which is why off-hire avoidance dominates the arithmetic.
  • 'Does a carbon cost appear in your voyage account?' Not at this date. No market-based measure has been adopted internationally, and the European scheme does not reach shipping until the first of next month.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which costs continue when the ship is laid up? Capital and most operating costs; voyage costs cease.
  • How would you set the safety stock for a turbocharger spare? By criticality under VED and procurement difficulty under SDE - not by its price.
  • What does element 10.3 require? Identification of equipment whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations, with specific measures to promote reliability.
  • Name three maintenance strategies and say when each is right. Breakdown, time-based and condition-based - chosen per item by the consequence of failure.
  • How do you demonstrate that a fuel saving is real? Systematic performance monitoring normalised for draught, weather and speed.

Model written answer

1. The accurate starting point - critical habitat, but not a designated area

The wording of the question invites an error, and the first mark is for not making it. Both island groups are unquestionably critical habitat - coral atolls and reefs, seagrass beds, mangrove, turtle nesting beaches and endemic species - and both carry substantial protection under Indian domestic law.

But neither group is a designated IMO Particularly Sensitive Sea Area. Designations worldwide are few, the first being the Great Barrier Reef in 1990, and none of them is in Indian waters. No Indian sea area is a MARPOL Special Area and none is an Emission Control Area either.

That is precisely why the stem asks how these islands could be protected. The answer is properly a programme of measures - some already in place domestically, some India would have to seek internationally - and the discrimination this limb rewards is between an environmentally sensitive area and a formally designated area under an international instrument.

2. The Indian statutory and administrative layer

  • Ship-source pollution - the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, which carries the prevention and containment of pollution of the sea by oil, with the Merchant Shipping Rules as the working detail, administered by DG Shipping.
  • Coastal and island zoning - the Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019, made under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, which applies specifically to the Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep groups and superseded the earlier island protection zone notification. It classifies coastal stretches into zones of decreasing sensitivity and restricts what may be done in each.
  • Habitat protection - marine national parks and wildlife sanctuaries under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, which operates independently of the shipping regime.
  • Jurisdictional reach - the Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976, which is what makes a measure in the exclusive economic zone legally available at all.
  • Response - the Indian Coast Guard under the Coast Guard Act, 1978 as central coordinating authority for oil spill response, with the national oil spill disaster contingency plan - and the practical difficulty that these are remote archipelagos a long way from the mainland response bases.
  • Port reception facilities and port State control of ships calling, which is what closes the loop between a discharge requirement on paper and what a ship actually does.

4. What a coherent programme would look like, in sequence

  • Establish the science and the traffic picture. Habitat and reef-condition surveys, and a study of the vessel types, tonnages, cargoes and traffic density actually passing. Without the traffic evidence no international proposal can succeed, for the reason in step 7.
  • Apply the domestic controls already available - the island coastal zone notification, the wildlife legislation, and the pollution provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 and its rules.
  • Build the response and reception capability first - reception facilities at island ports, pre-positioned response equipment and exercised plans - because Special Area status depends on it and a discharge prohibition without a place to discharge legally is unenforceable.
  • Seek the international measures - a PSSA supported by an area to be avoided and mandatory reporting, or Special Area status, with compulsory pilotage in the confined approaches.
  • Chart, promulgate and enforce. A measure that is not on the chart does not change what a master does.

5. What a Particularly Sensitive Sea Area is - and what it is not

  • An area that needs special protection through action by IMO because of its recognised ecological, socio-economic or scientific significance, and because it may be vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities.
  • The governing instrument is resolution A.982(24), the Revised PSSA Guidelines, as amended by MEPC.267(68).
  • It is a designation by IMO, not a declaration by the coastal State - which is what distinguishes it from a national marine protected area, and is the reason India could not simply declare one.
  • Decisively: a PSSA has no legal effect of its own. What regulates shipping is the Associated Protective Measure. A PSSA without an APM changes nothing that a ship must do.

That last point is the single highest-value proposition in limb (b), and most candidates miss it entirely.

6. The criteria for identification - three groups, and one is enough

The Guidelines set the criteria in three groups, and an area must meet at least one - not all three.

  • Ecological - uniqueness or rarity; critical habitat; dependency; representativeness; diversity; productivity; spawning or breeding grounds; naturalness; integrity; fragility; and bio-geographic importance.
  • Social, cultural and economic - social or economic dependency; human dependency, including of indigenous and local populations; and cultural heritage.
  • Scientific and educational - research value; use for baseline and monitoring studies; and educational value.

The Indian islands would satisfy several without difficulty. Meeting a criterion is not the hard part - which is why the next step matters more.

7. The vulnerability test - where proposals actually fail

Meeting a criterion is necessary but not sufficient. The proposal must also demonstrate vulnerability to damage by international shipping activities, and this is a separate test.

  • Vessel traffic characteristics - operational factors, the types of vessel, traffic density and routeing, and the harmful substances carried.
  • Natural factors - hydrographical conditions such as depth, seabed and confined channels; meteorological conditions such as wind, current, visibility and cyclone exposure; and oceanographic conditions determining where a spill would actually go.

A precious reef with no shipping near it fails the test, because IMO's jurisdiction is over ships and not over ecosystems. For the Indian islands the argument is strong: proximity to major east-west trade routes, laden tanker traffic passing close by, remoteness from response resources, cyclone exposure and confined reef approaches.

8. The designation procedure, and the associated protective measure

  • Only a Member Government may propose. The application goes to the Marine Environment Protection Committee and must describe the area, show that at least one criterion is met, demonstrate vulnerability to international shipping, and propose at least one Associated Protective Measure.
  • Every APM must have an identified legal basis - a routeing measure under SOLAS V/10, a reporting system under V/11, a discharge restriction or Special Area under MARPOL, compulsory pilotage, a measure adopted by IMO in its own right, or a measure under UNCLOS article 211(6).
  • The Committee may grant designation in principle once satisfied on the criteria and on vulnerability.
  • The APM is then referred to the appropriate IMO body - routeing and reporting measures to the navigation sub-committee and the Maritime Safety Committee; discharge measures to the environment committee itself.
  • Once the APM is adopted, final designation is granted.
  • The area and its measures are then charted and promulgated, so that mariners can see them and comply.

The idea underneath the question

Two layers of law protect a sea area and they come from opposite directions. Domestic law protects the place: India can zone its own coasts, declare its own sanctuaries and police its own waters, and it has done all three. International law protects it from ships, and only IMO can do that, because a ship of another flag is not bound by an Indian notification when she is passing. That is why the answer to 'how could these islands be protected' is a programme with two halves, and why the international half has to be applied for rather than declared.

Understand this first

The odd thing about the great safety convention is what it does not contain. It has a great deal to say about stability, subdivision, fire protection and lifeboats, and nothing whatever about how thick a plate has to be or how big a shaft must be. Hull strength was never public law: it was a private arrangement between the owner, the underwriter and a society that had grown up to give an independent technical opinion on ships people were being asked to insure. That left an awkward gap - a ship could satisfy every word of the Convention and still be structurally substandard, and losing that private approval was a commercial embarrassment rather than a legal failure. The regulation in question closes the gap by pointing at the private rule book and giving it legal force, and the crucial word is that the ship must be not only designed and built to it but kept to it. The second half of the question is about something quite different: two survey names that sound interchangeable and are not. Things aboard a ship deteriorate at very different rates - steel slowly, lifejackets and pyrotechnics quickly, and a distress radio invisibly - so the depth of inspection is graded to match, and a certificate valid for five years is only honest if there are checkpoints in between.

Model written answer

1. What a classification society is, and its two distinct roles

  • A body that publishes rules for the design, construction and maintenance of ships, and assigns and maintains class to ships built and kept to those rules. Its origin is the underwriters' need for an independent technical judgement of a ship they were being asked to insure.
  • Role one - classification. Private and contractual, between the society and the owner. It is not a public law function.
  • Role two - statutory work. Where the flag State delegates statutory survey and certification to it, the society acts as a recognized organization, recognized under SOLAS regulation XI-1/1 and governed by the RO Code, which has been mandatory since 1 January 2015.
  • The Administration retains responsibility. Delegation transfers the work, not the duty - which is why the RO Code imposes oversight obligations on the flag State as well as requirements on the society.

Keeping these two roles apart is the first discrimination on the question, because the SOLAS point in the second half of limb (a) is precisely about the first role being given legal effect.

2. How class rules are made

  • Service experience - defect and damage returns from the surveyed fleet, which is the society's largest single advantage: it sees what actually fails, in what service, at what age.
  • Casualty investigation feedback, both its own and the flag States'.
  • Research and direct calculation - finite element analysis, fatigue assessment, direct strength analysis and full-scale measurement.
  • Technical committees of owners, builders, makers and the society's own surveyors, which is how practical experience reaches the drafting.
  • Harmonisation through the association of classification societies - unified requirements which member societies must incorporate, unified interpretations of statutory instruments, and procedural requirements governing how the societies work.
  • The Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers - a single harmonised set for the two ship types where competitive rule differences had most cost the industry.

3. What the rules actually cover

DisciplineWhat the rules govern
StructuralScantlings, section modulus and hull girder strength; loading conditions and permissible still-water and wave bending; fatigue life; corrosion additions and coating requirements; close-up survey and thickness measurement regimes
MechanicalPropulsion machinery, shafting, gearing and propellers; boilers and pressure vessels; piping systems; steering gear; auxiliary machinery; type approval of components and materials

4. Implementation through the ship's life

  • Design - plan approval against the rules, and type approval of components and materials.
  • Construction - material certification, welder and welding-procedure qualification, non-destructive testing and tank testing witnessed, survey during construction, and attendance at trials.
  • In service - the class survey cycle: annual, intermediate and renewal surveys of hull and machinery, bottom and docking surveys, tailshaft surveys and boiler surveys, with continuous survey of machinery and an approved planned maintenance scheme available as alternatives.
  • Enforcement - condition of class imposing a defined action by a defined date; and suspension or withdrawal of class where it is not met or where a serious defect is not repaired.

5. Why compliance is now mentioned in SOLAS chapter II-1

The provision is regulation II-1/3-1, and the reason it exists repays being stated carefully.

  • SOLAS never contained scantling rules. It has a great deal to say about subdivision, stability, fire protection and life-saving, and nothing at all about how thick a plate must be. Hull strength was the classification societies' private business with the owner and the underwriter.
  • So class was commercially indispensable but legally optional: a ship could satisfy every word of SOLAS and still be structurally substandard, and losing class was an insurance and chartering problem rather than a breach of the Convention.
  • Regulation II-1/3-1 closed that gap by pointing SOLAS at the private rule book. Ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society recognized by the Administration under regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration providing an equivalent level of safety.
  • The word that does the work is 'maintained'. The obligation is not discharged at delivery; it runs for the ship's whole life. Loss of class therefore became a SOLAS non-compliance, enforceable by the flag State and inspectable by port State control.
  • The alternative route matters too. An Administration may use its own national standards, but they must provide an equivalent level of safety - so the regulation compels a recognised technical standard without compelling membership of any particular society.
  • And it connects forward. For bulk carriers and oil tankers of 150 m and above, regulation II-1/3-10 goes further and requires that the society's rules themselves have been verified as conforming to goals set by IMO. II-1/3-1 points at the rule book; II-1/3-10 audits it.

6. The harmonized system, and where these surveys sit

Limb (b) is worth the same eight marks as limb (a) on this paper, so the survey material is developed rather than sketched.

  • The harmonized system was introduced by the 1988 SOLAS and Load Lines Protocols and extended to the MARPOL Annexes by later environment committee amendments.
  • What it harmonised: a common five-year certificate cycle, common anniversary dates across the certificates, a survey window of three months either side of the due date, and the ability to combine surveys into a single attendance instead of a succession of separate visits.
  • The survey types are initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal and additional, together with the inspection of the ship's bottom.
  • The recommended practice for carrying them out is set out in the IMO Survey Guidelines under the Harmonized System, revised periodically by the Assembly. The edition in circulation at this sitting is the one adopted by the 32nd Assembly in 2021; the 33rd Assembly adopted a 2023 revision on 6 December 2023, within the month of this examination, and no claim is made here about which side of that date the paper was sat. Nothing in this answer depends on it, because the Guidelines are recommendatory - the resolution invites Governments to apply them - and the obligation to survey comes from the conventions and the Protocols, not from the resolution.

8. Annual versus periodical - the relevance

The examiner asked for the relevance to the harmonized system, not only the difference, and this is where the second half of limb (b)'s marks are.

  • A five-year certificate needs mid-cycle checkpoints to remain credible. Without them the Administration would be certifying for five years on the strength of one survey, which is not a safety judgement but an assumption.
  • Equipment is consumed in a way that structure is not. Lifejackets, liferafts, breathing apparatus, extinguishers, pyrotechnics and immersion suits degrade, expire and are used up - so they need a closer look partway through the cycle than a general annual examination provides.
  • Distress communication cannot be allowed to degrade unnoticed, which is why the radio survey is periodical at every anniversary rather than annual: the consequence of an undetected failure is that the ship cannot call for help at the moment she needs to.
  • The graded structure is the point. Annual, intermediate, periodical and renewal are a ladder of increasing depth matched to how quickly each thing can deteriorate - and matching survey effort to deterioration rate is what makes a five-year cycle safe rather than merely convenient.
  • Harmonisation makes the ladder practical. Common anniversary dates and the three-month window let all the surveys due at a given point be taken in one attendance, which is what stopped a ship being surveyed almost continuously by different attendances for different certificates - the problem the 1988 Protocols existed to solve.

The idea underneath the question

The odd thing about SOLAS is what it does not contain. It has a great deal to say about stability, fire and life-saving, and nothing at all about how thick a plate has to be - hull strength was always the societies' private business with the owner and the underwriter. Regulation II-1/3-1 is SOLAS pointing at that private rule book and saying: that one, and you must keep to it for the ship's whole life. That is how losing class stopped being a commercial embarrassment and became a breach of the Convention.

Limb (b) is a different kind of question. Two survey names that sound interchangeable are not, and the reason they differ is that things deteriorate at different rates - so the depth of inspection is graded to match.

Understand this first

The point of this method is that it argues about a rule before the rule exists, instead of after a casualty. Historically maritime regulation was written in the aftermath of disasters, which meant the price of every rule was paid before it was made. This is the attempt to do it the other way round: work out what could go wrong, how likely and how serious it would be, what could be done about it, what that would cost and who would pay - and only then decide what should be required. Two things about it are easy to miss. It is used to make rules for a whole class of ships, so it is applied to a representative model rather than to any real vessel, which is quite different from the risk assessment a chief engineer signs before a job. And it deliberately stops short of the last question. It will tell a committee how much a proposed measure costs for each life it would save; it will not tell them whether that price is worth paying. That judgement belongs to the member States, and the method's real contribution is to force them to say out loud what standard they are applying rather than leaving it buried inside a calculation.

Model written answer

1. What FSA is, and its objectives

Formal Safety Assessment is a structured and systematic methodology aimed at enhancing maritime safety - including protection of life, health, the marine environment and property - by using risk analysis and cost-benefit assessment. It is set out in the Revised Guidelines for its use in the IMO rule-making process, MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2.

Start by saying what kind of thing it is, because the whole answer follows from it: FSA is a tool for making RULES, not for running a ship. The Company's obligation under the ISM Code to assess risks to its ships and personnel and to establish safeguards is a different exercise, done by a different body, at a different scale. Confusing the two is the framing error on this question.

Its objectives are:

  • to provide a rational and systematic process for assessing the risks associated with shipping activity, and for evaluating the costs and benefits of options for reducing those risks;
  • to provide a means of evaluating new regulations or of comparing existing with proposed regulations, so that safety and environmental benefit can be balanced against cost;
  • to assist in demonstrating to decision makers that a proposed measure delivers benefits in proportion to what it costs;
  • to support equitable and consistent regulatory change and to aid the reaching of consensus among Member States, by making the reasoning visible rather than assertive.

One honest qualification belongs here: the Guidelines do not claim that FSA is applied in all circumstances. It is a tool for the questions that need it, not a mandatory precondition for every amendment.

2. The characteristics that define it

  • Proactive, not reactive. It argues about a rule before the rule exists, instead of after a casualty. This is the single most important characteristic and belongs first.
  • Structured and systematic, so that two studies of the same problem can be compared and the reasoning followed.
  • Transparent, documented and auditable - every assumption, data source and judgement is recorded, because the output is meant to persuade a committee of States.
  • Generic rather than ship-specific. It is applied to a generic model of a ship type, system or operation, not to one vessel. That is what makes the result usable as a rule.
  • Proportionate in depth. The work is screened so effort goes where the risk is, rather than being applied uniformly.
  • Iterative. The steps are not a one-way corridor - findings at a later step send you back to an earlier one, and step 5 draws on all of them.
  • Explicitly cost-aware. Alone among safety methods in common use, it requires the cost of a proposed measure to be estimated and compared with the risk it removes.

3. The process - and the step that is not one of the five

Before step 1, the decision makers define the problem: the ship type, system or operation to be studied, the scope and boundaries, the operating conditions, and the relevant regulations. This is preparatory and it is NOT one of the five steps. Calling it step 1 and pushing hazard identification to step 2 is the commonest structural error on this question.

Two definitions are needed before going further:

  • a hazard is a potential to threaten human life, health, property or the environment;
  • risk is the combination of the frequency of an occurrence and the severity of its consequence.

The five steps are then:

StepNameQuestion it answers
1Hazard IdentificationWhat could go wrong?
2Risk AnalysisHow likely is it, and how bad?
3Risk Control OptionsWhat could be done about it?
4Cost Benefit AssessmentWhat would that cost, against what it saves?
5Recommendations for decision-makingWhat should therefore be required?

4. Step 1 - Hazard Identification

Purpose: to produce a prioritised list of hazards and of the accident scenarios they lead to, specific to the problem defined.

  • The creative element - a structured group review by a balanced team of people with practical experience: seagoing officers, designers, operators, surveyors and risk specialists, using techniques such as structured what-if and hazard and operability review. Its purpose is to find hazards that the data do not contain because they have not happened yet.
  • The analytical element - casualty and incident data, port State control and class defect records, published studies and previous FSA work. Its purpose is to make sure known hazards are not overlooked.
  • Both are necessary. Data alone will miss the novel hazard; imagination alone will miss the common one.
  • A coarse analysis of causes and consequences is carried out for each hazard, sufficient only to rank it.
  • Ranking on a risk matrix of frequency against severity, so that the small number of scenarios carrying most of the risk are carried into step 2 and the rest are screened out - which is where the proportionality characteristic is actually applied.
  • Output: a ranked list of hazards and scenarios, with the initiating events identified.

5. Step 2 - Risk Analysis

Purpose: a detailed investigation of the causes and consequences of the important scenarios carried forward from step 1, and the quantification of the risk.

  • Causes and initiating events are modelled - typically by fault tree analysis, which works backwards from an undesired event to the combinations of failures that produce it.
  • Consequences are modelled - typically by event tree analysis, which works forwards from an initiating event through the success or failure of each barrier to the range of outcomes.
  • The risk contribution tree joins the two and shows which contributors dominate the total risk, which is what tells the study where control effort will actually pay.
  • Risk is expressed for each category the method protects - risk to people, to the environment and to property - because a measure that reduces one may not reduce another.
  • Expert judgement is used where data are absent, and is identified as such rather than disguised as measurement.
  • Uncertainty and sensitivity must be reported. A result that changes completely when one uncertain input is varied is a result that has to be presented with that caveat, and the Guidelines require it.
  • Output: the high-risk areas needing control, and the models against which any proposed measure can be tested.

6. Step 3 - Risk Control Options

  • A risk control measure acts on one element of risk. A risk control option is a combination of measures grouped so that it can be evaluated and, if adopted, implemented as one thing.
  • A measure can act on frequency - preventing the event - or on consequence - limiting the harm once it occurs. Recognising which a proposed measure does is essential, because they are not interchangeable.
  • Measures are engineering (design, redundancy, materials), operational (procedures, checklists, manning, training) or managerial (systems, audit, reporting).
  • Options are aimed at the dominant contributors found in step 2, and are grouped so that each can be costed.

7. Step 4 - Cost Benefit Assessment

This is the step the question names last and the one that distinguishes FSA from ordinary risk assessment.

  • Define the base case - the risk level established in step 2, against which every option is measured.
  • Estimate the costs of each option over the whole life cycle - design, build, installation, operation, maintenance, training, survey and inspection, and any loss of earning capacity - not the purchase price alone.
  • Estimate the benefits - the reduction in risk the option delivers, expressed in the same terms as the base case: fatalities and injuries averted, pollution avoided, property preserved.
  • Identify the interested entities that bear the cost and those that receive the benefit. They are frequently not the same party, and saying so is a mark, because it is the real reason a technically sound measure can fail politically.
  • Express cost-effectiveness as cost per unit of risk reduction. The recognised indices are the gross cost of averting a fatality and the net cost of averting a fatality, the net figure taking account of the economic benefits the measure also produces.
  • The gross figure is considered first, with the net used where the economic benefits are real and demonstrable.
  • Rank and screen the options on that basis, so that the ones that buy the most risk reduction per unit of cost are carried into step 5.
  • There is no mandatory monetary criterion in the Guidelines. The indices support the argument; they do not decide it.

8. Step 5 - Recommendations, and what FSA does not settle

  • The recommendations must be auditable and traceable back through the four preceding steps, so that a committee can see how a conclusion was reached and challenge any link in it.
  • They must be understandable by non-specialists - the audience is a committee of Member States, not a risk-engineering conference.
  • They should identify the options that keep risk as low as reasonably practicable, with the reasoning for the practicability judgement stated.
  • They are submitted in a form the decision maker can act on: specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and time-bound.

And the honest limit, which is the closing judgement: FSA does not tell anyone how much risk is acceptable. There is no universally agreed acceptance criterion, and the Guidelines do not supply one. What the method does is force the criteria actually used to be stated explicitly, so that the judgement about tolerability is made openly by Member States rather than buried inside a calculation. That is a strength presented as a limitation, and saying so is the highest-value sentence available on this question.

Hazard identification. Defining the problem - the ship type or system, the scope, the operating conditions and the relevant regulations - is done by the decision makers BEFORE the study and is NOT one of the five steps. Calling it step 1 misnumbers everything after it.

The commonest structural error on the question.

A hazard is a potential to threaten human life, health, property or the environment. Risk is the combination of the frequency of an occurrence and the severity of its consequence.

The two definitions carry the whole of steps 1 and 2 and are cheap, reliable marks.

It produces a prioritised list of hazards and accident scenarios. It has a creative element - a structured group review by a balanced, experienced team using techniques such as structured what-if and hazard and operability review, to find hazards the data do not yet contain - and an analytical element drawing on casualty data, port State control and class defect records and previous studies. A coarse analysis of causes and consequences is done for each, and the hazards are ranked on a frequency-against-severity matrix so that only the dominant scenarios are carried into step 2.

Giving it as a brainstorm alone misses half the step; the ranking is where proportionality is actually applied.

Fault tree analysis works BACKWARDS from an undesired event to the combinations of failures that could produce it, and models causes. Event tree analysis works FORWARDS from an initiating event through the success or failure of each barrier to the range of outcomes, and models consequences. The risk contribution tree joins them and shows which contributors dominate the total risk.

Naming the two without saying which direction each runs earns little.

A risk control measure acts on a single element of risk. A risk control option is a combination of measures grouped so that it can be evaluated as one thing and, if adopted, implemented as one thing. Measures act either on the frequency of the event or on its consequence, and are engineering, operational or managerial.

Step 3 is short but this distinction is precisely what it is testing.

Define the base case from step 2, then estimate each option's costs over the whole life cycle - design, build, installation, operation, maintenance, training, survey and any lost earning capacity - and its benefits as the risk reduction delivered. Identify the interested entities, since those who bear the cost are frequently not those who receive the benefit. Express cost-effectiveness as cost per unit risk reduction using the gross and net cost of averting a fatality, considering the gross figure first, and rank and screen the options on that basis. There is no mandatory monetary criterion in the Guidelines.

Whole-life costing and the payer/beneficiary split are the two points that lift this beyond a definition of the indices.

No. There is no universally agreed acceptance criterion and the Guidelines supply none. What the method requires is that whatever criteria are used be made explicit, so that the judgement about tolerability is made openly by Member States rather than buried inside a calculation.

The closing judgement, and the highest-value sentence available on the question.

FSA is a rule-making tool used by the Organization, applied to a generic model of a ship type, system or operation, and it includes cost-benefit assessment. Onboard risk assessment under the safety management system applies to your ship, your plant and today's job, is done by the ship's own staff, and is about establishing safeguards rather than about whether a rule should exist.

The framing distinction; getting it wrong colours the whole answer.

Quick revision
15-second recall
FSA = a structured and systematic methodology to enhance maritime safety - life, health, environment, property - using RISK ANALYSIS plus COST-BENEFIT ASSESSMENT, under the Revised Guidelines MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 of 2018. IT IS A RULE-MAKING TOOL, not onboard risk assessment. OBJECTIVES: a rational process for assessing risk and evaluating options; evaluating new or comparing existing and proposed regulations; demonstrating benefit against cost to decision makers; aiding equitable change and consensus. CHARACTERISTICS: proactive not reactive, structured, transparent and auditable, GENERIC not ship-specific, proportionate, ITERATIVE, explicitly cost-aware. PROBLEM DEFINITION COMES FIRST AND IS NOT ONE OF THE FIVE STEPS. Hazard = a potential to threaten; risk = frequency combined with severity. FIVE STEPS: 1 HAZARD IDENTIFICATION - creative (structured group review) plus analytical (data), coarse analysis, ranked on a matrix. 2 RISK ANALYSIS - FAULT TREES backwards for causes, EVENT TREES forwards for consequences, risk contribution tree, uncertainty and sensitivity reported. 3 RISK CONTROL OPTIONS - a measure acts on one element, an option is a combination; act on frequency or consequence; engineering, operational, managerial. 4 COST BENEFIT ASSESSMENT - base case, WHOLE-LIFE costs, interested entities (payer is often not beneficiary), GCAF and NCAF, gross considered first, NO mandatory monetary criterion. 5 RECOMMENDATIONS - auditable, traceable, understandable, ALARP, in actionable terms. FSA DOES NOT SAY HOW MUCH RISK IS ACCEPTABLE.
