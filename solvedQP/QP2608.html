Understand this first

Two different jobs are hiding in one question. The first is practical: a ship that has been sitting still for a long time is not a ship that is merely switched off. Oil drains away from surfaces, moisture works into windings, seals take a set, and the systems nobody ever ran while she was idle - the emergency generator, the emergency fire pump, the oily water separator - are exactly the ones that will not start when you need them. So the job is not to inspect her, it is to prove her, and you prove her in a deliberate order: read the paperwork first so you know what was done to her, undo the protection you or somebody else put on, check what can be checked while everything is stopped, then run her - first tied to the quay where a failure costs nothing, then at sea where the load is real. Only then do you invite the surveyor, and what you hand him is evidence, not confidence. The second job is a rules question with a counter-intuitive answer. Putting a ship into a declared idle state does not make her survey obligations go away, and it does not restart her clock. It parks them. When she wakes up, everything that fell due while she slept has to be done, and the big five-yearly examination is done to the standard that applied on the day it was originally owed - not the softer or harder standard that would apply on the day you finally get round to it. Idle time buys no time.

Model written answer

(a) Preparation and trials before offering the ship for survey

1. Establish the ship's status before touching a machine

Reactivation begins on paper. I would establish, and put in one folder: the lay-up record (what was preserved, when, and by what method); every outstanding Condition of Class, memorandum and statutory deficiency; the survey and certificate status - which surveys fell due during lay-up and which certificates have expired; the repair specification and the yard's completed-work list with material and welding certificates; and the state of the planned maintenance system, which will have accrued a backlog. This tells me what the surveyor will ask for and what I must prove.

2. Remove preservation and restore the systems that were shut down

Then, system by system: drain and renew preservation oils and dehumidification media; remove blanks, plugs and desiccant bags against the register that recorded them - the register is the control, not memory; re-fill and vent cooling, lubricating and fuel systems; renew or filter and test lubricating and hydraulic oils and system fuel after long standing; restore sea suctions, overboard valves and ship-side fittings and prove them tight; and re-establish the bilge, ballast and fire main.

3. Static checks on machinery, boilers, electrical and auxiliary systems

Before anything is started: crankcase and gear inspection, turning gear rotation and clearance checks; insulation-resistance tests on every machine and the main and emergency switchboards, with results recorded against the lay-up readings; battery and UPS capacity; boiler internal examination, gauge glasses, safety valves and water treatment; steering gear internal and hydraulic checks; and calibration of pressure gauges, thermometers and instrumentation.

4. Safety, emergency and pollution-prevention equipment

This is where a reactivation survey most often fails. Service and re-certify life-saving and fire-fighting appliances, breathing apparatus, fixed extinguishing systems and quick-closing valves; prove emergency generator, emergency fire pump, emergency lighting and the emergency steering; test fire and gas detection, watertight doors and remote stops; and re-commission the oily water separator with its 15 ppm alarm and stopping device, the sewage plant and the incinerator, with the record books brought up to date.

5. Harbour trials - prove alongside everything that can be proved alongside

Run every auxiliary on load; parallel and load-share the generators and carry out a blackout and dead-ship recovery test; run the main engine on the turning gear, then air-blast, then a short ahead and astern trial on the moorings with the shaft brake and locking arrangement removed and the propeller clear; prove bridge, engine-control-room and local control positions and the changeover between them; test the engineers' alarm, telegraph and both means of bridge-to-engine-room communication; and run the steering gear through the full hard-over to hard-over test on both power units.

6. Sea trials - what can only be proved under way

At sea, and with the Master, run a progressive load-up of the main engine with performance and indicator readings, exhaust temperature deviations and turbocharger condition logged; carry out crash-stop, ahead-astern and manoeuvring trials and the steering-gear trial under way; prove governor, overspeed trip and all shutdowns and slowdowns by actual test where the maker's procedure allows; take bearing temperatures, vibration and crankshaft deflections after the engine has come up to temperature; and hold the engine at service load long enough for a leak, an alarm or a temperature drift to declare itself before the surveyor is aboard.

7. What is put in front of the surveying authority

The surveyor is shown evidence, not assurances: completed repair reports and material certificates; trial records signed and dated; insulation, calibration and safety-valve test sheets; the planned maintenance system restored and current; the oil record book, garbage record book and other statutory records in order; and a clear list of anything still outstanding with its plan and date. Anything I cannot prove, I declare - a defect found by me is a finding, a defect found by the surveyor is a deficiency.

(b) The effect of a laid-up notation on the survey cycle

8. What the notation does while the ship is laid up, and what happens on reactivation

Under class rules, a ship laid up in accordance with the Society's own lay-up requirements is held in a recognised lay-up condition rather than in normal service, and the effect is one of postponement, not cancellation:

Three consequences follow. Lay-up condition surveys replace the normal cycle while the ship is idle - typically tightness of hull, stern gland and sea connections and confirmation that the lay-up conditions are maintained. A reactivation survey is required before return to service: where no due dates passed, a survey of annual scope; where they did, the postponed surveys themselves, plus clearance of any Condition of Class. And the lay-up buys no time overall - because credit runs from the original due date, the five-year cycle is not pushed out by the idle period. Finally, class lay-up does not extend a statutory certificate: SOLAS, Load Line and MARPOL certification is the Administration's business, and the flag State - in India, under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 - must be approached separately.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is worth 10 and limb (b) is worth 6, so roughly two-thirds of the writing belongs to the reactivation. The mark-winning move in (a) is to separate preparation from trials and then to separate harbour trials from sea trials, because that is the examiner's own word order and it gives four countable blocks instead of one long list. Limb (b) is short and specific: three named surveys, three answers. Do not turn it into a general essay on classification.

The idea that carries limb (b)

Lay-up postpones; it does not forgive. Everything in limb (b) falls out of that one sentence. The requirement survives the idle period, the survey is done on reactivation, and - the point most candidates miss - the scope and credit of a postponed special survey are fixed by the original due date, not by how old the ship is when it is finally surveyed. A ship laid up for two years does not get a two-year holiday from her five-year cycle.

Why the answer says 'under class rules' rather than naming a rule

The lay-up regime is a classification-society regime, not a convention one. No SOLAS regulation and no IMO resolution creates a laid-up notation. The shape above is common across societies - abeyance, condition surveys, an overdue notation, a reactivation survey, credit from the original due date - but the trigger periods, the notation wording and the additional time allowed before disclassing differ between them. In the exam, state the mechanism and attribute it to the Society's rules; do not quote a period as though it were universal.

Common mistakes

  • Answering (a) with a preservation list. That is the other question - how to lay a ship UP. This one is about proving her fit to come back.
  • Going to sea trials without harbour trials. The examiner has asked for 'trials' in a survey context, and a moored ahead-and-astern trial, a blackout recovery and a hard-over steering test are all scoring items that cost no sea time.
  • Forgetting that reactivation defects cluster in emergency and pollution-prevention equipment - emergency generator, emergency fire pump, OWS 15 ppm alarm - because those are the systems nobody ran during the lay-up.
  • Saying the laid-up notation cancels or resets surveys. It postpones them.
  • Saying a postponed special survey is done to the requirements for the ship's age on the day it is completed. It is done to the requirements at the original due date and credited from that date.
  • Assuming class lay-up carries the statutory certificates with it. It does not - flag State and RO are a separate approach.
  • Removing blanks and plugs from memory instead of from the register that recorded them.

Examiner traps

"Your ship was laid up for eighteen months and the special survey fell due in month six. What is her class status?" Not automatically suspended if she was properly laid up under the Society's rules - but past the special survey due date she is typically carrying an overdue-surveys notation, held by minimum lay-up condition surveys, and she cannot trade until the postponed special survey is completed.

"Which trial would you not skip, and why?" The steering gear hard-over test and the ahead-astern trial - both are required before departure in normal service anyway, both prove control rather than condition, and both are what the surveyor will ask to witness.

"Who tells you the ship may trade again - class or flag?" Both, separately. Class reinstates classification on completion of the reactivation surveys; the Administration or its RO deals with the statutory certificates.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is a lay-up condition survey, and what does it usually cover? Tightness of hull, stern gland and sea connections, and confirmation that the declared lay-up conditions are being maintained.
  • What determines the scope of a postponed special survey? The requirements at the original due date, not the ship's age at completion.
  • Name three trials you would run alongside. Blackout and dead-ship recovery, moored ahead-and-astern engine trial, steering gear hard-over to hard-over.
  • What insulation reading would concern you on reactivation? Any machine that has fallen materially below its recorded lay-up value - long standing with heaters off draws in moisture.
  • Does lay-up extend the certificate validity? No. Class postponement and statutory certificate validity are different things.

Uncertainty and jurisdiction notes

Two limits are declared in the answer rather than hidden. First, the lay-up survey detail is society-specific: the length of lay-up that triggers a condition survey, its interval, the exact notation and the extra period allowed before disclassing all differ, so the answer gives the mechanism and attributes the detail to the Society's rules. Second, the statutory position is flag-specific: the answer states only that class lay-up does not of itself extend a statutory certificate, and does not assert any Administration's practice on extension or re-issue. The reactivation programme in limb (a) is declared as engineering judgement and standard industry practice, because no instrument prescribes one.

Understand this first

A dry dock is the one operation where the ship stops being a floating object and becomes a supported one, and almost everything that matters follows from that changeover. While she is settling onto the blocks she is neither properly afloat nor properly landed: the blocks push up on her keel, and pushing up on the bottom of a ship has the same effect as taking weight out of the bottom of her - she gets less stable, not more. That is why she must go in upright, with a small deliberate stern trim so she lands progressively rather than all at once, and with no half-full tanks sloshing about making it worse. It is also why this is not something an engineer arranges alone: the person responsible for the ship's condition and safety is the Master, and the person who knows what the machinery, the tanks and the hull openings will be doing is you, so the two of you have to hand each other the halves of the same picture. Once she is landed, the whole ship is opened up - plugs out of the bottom, valves off, gratings off, transducers out - and the single fact that matters is that every one of those openings has to go back. That is a bookkeeping problem, not an engineering one, which is why it is answered with a register and a joint signed inspection rather than with skill. And undocking is not docking played backwards: it has its own inspection, and its own watch on every space that could take water, because the first minutes of flooding are when a missing plug tells you it is missing.

1. The exchange with the Master, and the information the dock requires

Docking is a joint operation and the Master carries the ship. What I give him: the docking plan and shell expansion, the approved docking condition (draught fore and aft and the trim I need), the tank plan with the intended state of every tank, the work list and its critical path, the machinery that must remain available and until when, and any defect, damage or Condition of Class that bears on entry. What I ask him for: the confirmed dock date and tide window, the dock's own entry conditions, the ballast and trim he needs for the passage in, the cargo and slop position, and the crew and watch arrangements ashore. Between us we settle one thing above all - the stability condition on entry, because the ship must be upright and at a controlled trim and must have adequate stability at the moment of taking the blocks.

2. Condition of the ship for docking - draught, trim, stability and tanks

The ship enters with no list and with the small stern trim the dock requires so that she takes the after keel blocks first. Free surface is minimised: tanks are pressed up or run dry, not left slack, because free surface bites hardest in the critical period between touching the blocks and taking the full weight, when the effective metacentric height is being reduced by the block reaction. Bunkers, fresh water, ballast and slops are set to the agreed figures and soundings taken and recorded immediately before entry. Any tank to be opened, gas-freed or repaired is emptied and cleaned in advance.

3. Preparing the machinery and services for shutdown

Arrange the changeover from ship's services to shore services - shore power, shore fresh water, shore fire main connection and the international shore connection - so nothing is lost in the gap. Prepare for the loss of sea water: the main engine, generators, air conditioning and refrigeration all lose their cooling once the dock is pumped down, so agree which will run on shore-supplied cooling and which will stop. Have bilge and drainage arrangements agreed with the dock, since gravity drainage overboard is available in dock and must be controlled. Sound and record all tanks, secure the boiler, and prepare the propeller shaft locking or turning arrangement so the shaft cannot rotate with the dock flow.

4. Delegation of responsibilities to the engineers

Every task gets a name, not a department:

5. Entering the dock and taking the blocks

During entry the engine room is manned and the main engine and steering are available until the dock master releases them. The ship is centred on the dock centreline against the docking plan block layout; divers or the dock check the fore-and-aft and athwartship position before the water is dropped. Once she is on the blocks the propeller shaft is locked, the rudder secured amidships, and the water pumped down in stages with the ship watched for movement or list.

6. In dock - services, safety and the plug and valve regime

Bottom plugs are drawn only against a written register, and the drawn plugs are landed in one controlled place - the single commonest cause of a flooded engine room on refloating is a plug that was never refitted. Sea suctions and overboard valves are opened for cleaning, examination and gauging with their blanks fitted and tagged. Anodes are renewed, the stern tube and rudder clearances (poker gauge and jumping-bar readings) taken and recorded with the class surveyor, and tailshaft, propeller and rudder examined. Fire and safety in dock is a joint regime: hot-work permits, gas-free certificates, fire watches, unobstructed gangways and escape routes, and the shore fire main connected and pressurised.

7. Inspections and co-operation with the dock authority before undocking

Nothing is flooded up until a joint ship-and-dock pre-undocking inspection has walked the bottom and signed for it. The inspection confirms: every plug refitted, torqued and witnessed against the register; all ship-side valves and blanks correctly restored and their positions logged; sea chest gratings refitted; anodes secured; propeller, rudder and stern gear complete with the clearances recorded and the class surveyor's attendance closed out; echo sounder and log transducers refitted and their fairings faired; all staging, scaffolding, tools and debris removed from the dock bottom and from sea chests; paint work cured; and the hull free of any opening not accounted for. In parallel we agree with the dock the undocking condition - draughts, trim, tank state and stability - and confirm class and statutory attendance is complete and any Condition of Class cleared.

8. Flooding up, undocking and restoration to service

During flooding the engine room is manned and all bilges, tank tops, sea chest spaces and the shaft tunnel are physically watched for leakage, with pumps ready; flooding is stopped at once if anything is found. When she lifts, soundings are retaken, the shaft unlocked and turned, machinery restarted in a controlled sequence and cooling water proved, steering gear tested hard-over to hard-over, and shore services released only when ship's services have taken the load. Before departure the Master and I confirm the ship's readiness for sea: engine trials ahead and astern, control from all three positions, communications, and the record of everything done handed over for the survey and maintenance record.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

This is one 16-mark question containing four separate demands, and the examiner has signalled them with four verbs - state the coordination with the Master, list the preparations, state the delegation, enlist the undocking inspections. Marks are almost certainly split across all four. The commonest failure is a long, well-informed preparation list that never mentions the Master, never names an engineer, and stops at the moment the dock is pumped down. Answer chronologically - before, entry, in dock, before undocking, undocking - and all four limbs get covered in their natural place.

The stability point that carries the docking condition

The dangerous moment in docking is not when the ship is sitting on the blocks; it is the interval between touching the after blocks and taking her full weight. As the water goes down, the blocks push up with a force P at the keel, and that upward force acts exactly like removing weight from the bottom of the ship - it reduces the effective GM. If she has a list, or slack tanks adding free surface, or too much trim so that P builds up over a long period on one point, she can become unstable and slip. That is why the answer insists on upright, minimum free surface and a small controlled stern trim. If you can say why, rather than just what, this paragraph is worth real marks.

The two things that actually sink ships on refloating

A plug that was never refitted, and a sea valve left open with its blank removed. Both are register problems, not skill problems, which is exactly why the answer makes the plug register and the joint pre-undocking inspection formal, signed steps rather than a walk-round. Write the register into your answer explicitly - it is the difference between a checklist and a control.

Operational practice is not statute - keep them apart

Almost nothing in this answer is prescribed by an international instrument. There is no SOLAS regulation on how to dock a ship. What the instruments do supply is the framework: the ISM Code requires the Company to establish procedures for key shipboard operations and for maintenance (elements 7 and 10) and fixes the Master's authority (element 5); SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 requires the condition to be maintained after survey and defects to be reported; and class rules require attendance and approval for repairs affecting class, and set the bottom-survey requirement that is usually the reason for the docking in the first place. Say which is which. Presenting dock practice as though it were regulation is a marked error.

Common mistakes

  • Writing only the preparation limb. There are four demands and three of them are not preparation.
  • Never naming an engineer. 'Delegation of responsibilities' means Second, Third, Fourth and ETO with a task each - a departmental sentence does not answer it.
  • Leaving tanks slack. Free surface is at its most damaging in the critical period, and a slack tank in a docking condition is a fault, not a neutral choice.
  • Entering with a list. She must be upright.
  • Forgetting the transducers. Echo sounder and speed log fairings are hull openings and belong on both the removal and the refitting list.
  • Forgetting to lock the shaft, or unlocking it before the ship is fully afloat.
  • Treating undocking as the reverse of docking with no separate inspection. The joint pre-undocking inspection is a scoring limb in its own right.
  • Releasing shore services before ship's services have taken the load.

Examiner traps

"Why does the ship need a stern trim entering the dock?" So that she lands on the after blocks first and settles progressively forward along the keel, rather than landing bodily and unpredictably. The trim must be small and controlled - excessive trim concentrates the block reaction at one point for longer and makes the critical period worse.

"Who is responsible for the plugs?" I am. The Second Engineer holds the register as a delegated task, but delegation transfers the task and never the accountability - which is also the ISM Code's own position on the Master's and the Company's responsibilities.

"You find a crack in a propeller blade in dock. What changes?" The Company and the classification society are informed at once, and no repair is started until the repair procedure is approved and attendance arranged - the same discipline as any repair affecting class.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What information does the dock master need from you? The docking plan and block layout, the shell expansion showing every ship-side opening, the docking draughts and trim, the tank condition and the ship's displacement.
  • Which readings are taken with the surveyor in dock? Tailshaft and stern tube clearances by poker gauge, rudder pintle and jumping-bar clearances, and propeller condition.
  • What is the international shore connection for? To allow the shore fire main to supply the ship's fire main when the ship's own pumps are unavailable.
  • What are you watching during flooding up? Every bilge, tank top, sea chest space and the shaft tunnel, for leakage, with pumps ready and flooding stopped at once if any is found.
  • Where does the ISM Code touch this operation? Element 7 procedures for key shipboard operations, element 10 maintenance, element 5 the Master's authority, element 8 emergency preparedness for the in-dock fire regime.

Memory framework

Run the clock: TALK - TRIM - SHUT DOWN - DELEGATE - LAND - WORK - INSPECT - FLOOD (steps 1 to 8). Within the work and inspect steps, one word covers the danger: PLUGS. Register out, register in, signed by two people.

  • SOLAS chapter I regulation 11 - maintenance of condition after survey and reporting of accidents or defects.

Uncertainty and jurisdiction notes

Two boundaries are declared rather than blurred. Dock-specific rules - entry conditions, hot-work permits, fire watches and the split of duties between ship and dock - are set by the individual dock and its national regulator; the answer names the categories and makes the dock's own rules controlling. Class involvement in a given repair - whether attendance, approval of a procedure, or neither is required - is determined case by case by the Society; the answer states the duty to notify and obtain approval and asserts no threshold. The docking and undocking sequence itself is declared as operational practice, not as regulation.

Understand this first

Start with the commercial problem, because the answer to the first half is just the solution to it. If the person who carries your goods is also the person who writes the contract, he will keep writing until he owes you nothing - and that is exactly what happened. The trouble is that this does not only hurt the cargo owner; it destroys the usefulness of the shipping document itself, because a bank asked to lend money against a piece of paper needs to know what that paper is worth, and a piece of paper promising nothing is worth nothing. So the fix was not to make the carrier liable for everything. It was to take the pen away from him for one specific part of the contract: to fix a floor of responsibility he cannot write himself out of, and to fix the same floor in every country so the document means one thing wherever it is presented. In exchange the carrier was given something real - a defined list of situations where he is not liable, a cap on what he can be made to pay, and a short window in which he can be sued at all. It is a settlement between two industries, not a protection statute, and the give and the take are both part of the objective. The second half is about a much more ordinary situation. Goods arrive and nobody comes for them - because the buyer has changed his mind, or the market has fallen, or the storage bill has quietly grown larger than the cargo is worth. The instinctive answer is that walking away ends the matter. It does not. Walking away from a thing does not walk you away from the promises you made about it, and somebody still has to pay the port, feed the reefer and eventually get rid of it.

Model written answer

(a) The primary objective of the Hague and Hague-Visby Rules

1. The problem the Rules were created to solve

Before the Rules, carriage was governed by freedom of contract, and the carrier drafted the bill of lading. Exclusion clauses grew until the carrier had contracted out of almost every liability, including his own negligence. The cargo owner, the endorsee and the bank financing the shipment held a document that promised very little and could not be relied on across jurisdictions, so goods and credit both became harder to move. National legislatures began to intervene separately, which threatened to fragment the law of the bill of lading.

2. The primary objective, stated

The primary objective is to impose a uniform, mandatory minimum standard of carrier liability on contracts of carriage covered by a bill of lading, so that the risk of loss or damage to cargo is allocated between carrier and cargo interest in a way that neither can contract out of, and is allocated the same way in every contracting State. Two words carry it: minimum, because the carrier may always assume more but never less; and uniform, because the value of the bill of lading depends on it meaning the same thing wherever it is presented. The commercial purpose behind both is certainty - a bargain both sides can price and insure.

3. How the objective is delivered - the two halves of the bargain

The Rules are a bargain, not a code of protection, and an answer that gives only the cargo half has missed the objective.

What the carrier must giveWhat the carrier gets in return

4. What the Protocols added, and the Indian position at this sitting

The Visby Protocol 1968 and the SDR Protocol 1979 did not change the objective; they repaired the machinery that was failing to deliver it. The package limitation had been eroded by inflation and by containerisation, so limitation was recast on a package or weight basis whichever is higher, expressed in Special Drawing Rights, with an enumeration rule for containers and pallets; the bill was made conclusive against a good-faith transferee; and the limitation was lost for conduct intended to cause damage or reckless with knowledge that damage would probably result.

In India the Rules do not apply of their own force - they operate through statute, and that statute changed shortly before this sitting. The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 came into force on 10 September 2025, repealing the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925, and re-enacts the Rules with modifications in its Schedule. Its section 5 is a statutory clause paramount: every bill of lading issued in India must state expressly that it takes effect subject to the applicable rules. Two Indian modifications are worth knowing - the one-year bar carries a proviso allowing a further period of not more than three months as the court allows, and the definition of goods expressly includes live animals and cargo carried on deck. The Bills of Lading Act, 2025 replaced the 1856 Act on the same day.

(b) When cargo is abandoned, and who carries the liability

5. What abandonment means - and the three senses that must not be mixed

No carriage convention and no Indian carriage statute defines abandoned cargo. It is a description of a factual state - the person entitled to the goods has shown, by conduct or by declaration, that he will not take them - to which customs law, port regulation and the contract of carriage then attach consequences. It must be kept apart from two different things that share the word: abandonment to the insurer, which is the notice of abandonment given on a constructive total loss under marine insurance law and transfers the proprietary interest to the underwriter; and abandonment of the ship as a wreck, which is a merchant shipping and wreck-removal question. This question is about the first.

6. The circumstances in which cargo is treated as abandoned

  • Non-collection. The consignee does not present himself, or presents no original bill of lading, within a reasonable time or within the period the port or customs allows.
  • Economic abandonment - the commonest cause. Accrued demurrage, detention, storage and duty exceed the value of the goods, so nobody has an economic reason to claim them.
  • Rejection under the sale contract - the buyer refuses the goods for quality, quantity, delay or a fallen market, and the seller is unwilling to take them back.
  • Regulatory refusal - import licence refused, goods prohibited, failed sanitary or phytosanitary inspection, or seizure by customs.
  • Condition of the goods - perished, contaminated, damaged beyond commercial value, or dangerous or misdeclared cargo which no one will own.
  • Insolvency or disappearance of the consignee, or a documentary failure that leaves nobody able to prove title.
  • Formal relinquishment in Indian law. Under section 23(2) of the Customs Act, 1962 the owner of imported goods may, at any time before an order for clearance or warehousing, relinquish his title, and is then not liable for the duty - except where an offence appears to have been committed, in which case relinquishment is not allowed. This is abandonment as a legal act rather than as a fact.
  • Statutory non-clearance. Under section 48 of the Customs Act, 1962, goods not cleared for home consumption, warehoused or transhipped within thirty days of unloading - or where title has been relinquished - may, after notice to the importer and with the permission of the proper officer, be sold by the person having custody. Animals, perishable and hazardous goods may be sold at any time with that permission.

7. Who carries the liability

The important point is that liability does not disappear when the goods are abandoned. Abandonment sheds the asset; it does not by itself shed the obligations.

PartyPosition
Consignee / cargo ownerPrimarily liable. Failing to take delivery is a breach of the carriage contract, and he remains liable for freight, demurrage, detention, storage, disposal and any damage the cargo causes.
ShipperRemains liable. Bills of lading almost always define 'Merchant' to include shipper, consignee, holder and receiver and make them jointly and severally liable, so the carrier can look back to the shipper when the consignee walks away. Under the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 the transfer of rights of suit to the consignee does not release the original shipper from freight.
Freight forwarder / NVOCCLiable in his own right where he contracted as principal on the bill rather than as agent - a distinction that decides who the line sues.
Carrier / shipping lineNot the owner of the goods, but carries the practical exposure: he pays the terminal, the customs charges and the disposal, and recovers from the merchant if he can. He also remains a bailee of the goods and owes reasonable care until they are lawfully disposed of.

8. What the carrier actually does

In practice the carrier: gives written notice to the merchant and sets a deadline; exercises the contractual lien on the goods for freight, general average and charges; discharges and warehouses at the merchant's risk and expense under the bill's liberty clauses; keeps the demurrage clock and the evidence, because the claim will be for accrued charges; obtains customs and port authority permission and then sells or destroys the goods, crediting the proceeds; and pursues the merchant, jointly and severally, for the shortfall - notifying the P&I club or cargo liability insurer early. Where the cargo is dangerous or misdeclared, the carrier's position is stronger still, because the shipper's undertaking as to the nature of the goods is broken.

The statute book moved, and it moved recently

This is the single most important currency point on the question. The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 and the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 came into force on 10 September 2025, repealing the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 and the Indian Bills of Lading Act, 1856 respectively. Name the 2025 Acts, mention the statutory clause paramount in section 5, and you have said something a candidate working from stale material cannot say.

Minimum, not maximum - the idea most answers miss

The Rules fix a floor. A carrier may agree to greater liability than the Rules impose, and that agreement is perfectly good; what he may not do is agree to less, because Article III(8) makes any such clause null and void. This one-way character is what makes the regime mandatory minimum rather than a standard-form contract, and it explains the anti-avoidance rule, the statutory clause paramount and the deeming of a benefit-of-insurance clause as an attempt to lessen liability.

Three different abandonments

The word does three jobs in maritime law and mixing them is the classic error in limb (b). Abandonment of cargo - the consignee will not take the goods; a commercial fact with customs and contractual consequences. Abandonment to the underwriter - notice of abandonment on a constructive total loss under marine insurance law, which transfers the insured's proprietary interest to the insurer on acceptance. Abandonment of the ship - the owner walks away from a wreck, which raises wreck removal and merchant shipping duties. If the question says cargo, answer on the first and mention the others only to show you know they are different.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (a) by reciting the articles. The objective is a proposition; the articles are evidence for it.
  • Giving only the cargo-protection half. The Rules are a bargain - defences, limitation and the one-year bar are part of the objective, not exceptions to it.
  • Writing the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925. It was repealed with effect from 10 September 2025, eleven months before this sitting.
  • Bringing in the Hamburg Rules as Indian law. India is not a party to them.
  • Saying that on abandonment the goods become the carrier's problem and the merchant is free. He is not - the merchant remains liable for freight, demurrage, storage and disposal, and the bill's Merchant clause usually makes shipper and consignee jointly and severally liable.
  • Confusing abandonment of cargo with notice of abandonment to an underwriter, or with abandonment of a wreck.
  • Asserting a rupee limitation figure. The unit of account is the Special Drawing Right and no conversion is given here.
  • Treating the thirty-day period in section 48 of the Customs Act as a definition of abandonment. It is a trigger for the custodian's power of sale, which is not the same thing.

Understand this first

Four labels are put in front of you under one heading, and the quiet test is whether you know that they do not all belong to the same thing. Two of them are rules the law imposes on the bargain, whatever the parties write. The first is about staying on the road you said you would take: if the ship wanders off the route the cover was priced for, the insurer stops being on risk from the moment she wanders, and it does not help that she found her way back before anything went wrong. There is a short list of good reasons for wandering, and it is narrower than most people expect - saving people counts, saving valuable property does not. The second is about promises you make about the ship that are treated as absolute. In ordinary contracts a promise like this is a minor term; here it is the harshest one on the page, because it has to be kept to the letter, it makes no difference whether keeping it would have mattered to the accident, and breaking it ends the cover from the moment it is broken rather than merely reducing what you can claim. The other two labels are not rules at all - they are pieces of the contract that the market writes. One of them exists to say what the ordinary policy will not pay for, so that dangerous political risks can be priced separately and switched off at short notice when the world changes. The last is about a simple fairness idea: when several parties all benefit from something that cost one of them, they share the bill in proportion to what they had at stake - and a charterer is one of those parties, because the fuel in the tanks and the money he stands to make are just as much at risk as the ship and the boxes.

Model written answer

1. The statute, and one caution this question requires

The governing statute in India is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. The United Kingdom Act of 1906 is its model but is not operative here, and the English Insurance Act 2015 reform does not apply in India. One further caution decides how two of these notes must be written: the Act governs the contract of marine insurance, while the clauses of a policy are contractual. Deviation and warranties are creatures of the Act. A war risk clause and a charterers' contribution clause are creatures of the market, and it is an error to attribute them to a section of the Act because they appear in an insurance question.

2. (a) Deviation

Definition. A ship deviates when she departs from the course of the voyage contemplated by the policy - from the course specifically designated by the policy where there is one, and otherwise from the usual and customary course. Intention to deviate is immaterial; there must be a deviation in fact.

Effect. Where a ship deviates without lawful excuse, the insurer is discharged from liability as from the time of deviation - and it is immaterial that she had regained her route before any loss occurred. The insurer remains liable for losses occurring before the deviation.

Lawful excuses. Deviation or delay is excused where it is authorised by a special term of the policy; caused by circumstances beyond the control of the master and his employer; reasonably necessary to comply with an express or implied warranty; reasonably necessary for the safety of the ship or the subject-matter insured; for the purpose of saving human life or aiding a ship in distress where human life may be in danger; reasonably necessary to obtain medical or surgical aid for a person on board; or caused by the barratrous conduct of master or crew where barratry is an insured peril. When the excuse ceases the ship must resume her course with reasonable despatch.

Practical point. The insurance rule is narrower than the carriage rule. Under the carriage regime a reasonable deviation, including one to save property at sea, is not a breach; under the Act, saving property alone is not among the excuses - it must be human life, or a ship in distress where life may be in danger. A master may therefore be safe under the bill of lading and have prejudiced the policy.

3. (b) Warranties

Nature. A warranty in marine insurance is a promissory warranty by which the assured undertakes that some thing shall or shall not be done, or that a condition shall be fulfilled, or affirms or negatives the existence of a particular state of facts. Its severity is the opposite of the ordinary contractual sense: it is the severe term, not the minor one.

Effect. It must be exactly complied with, whether or not it is material to the risk. On breach the insurer is discharged from liability as from the date of the breach, without prejudice to any liability incurred before it - so the assured cannot cure the breach and revive the cover. Breach is excused only where a change of circumstances makes the warranty no longer applicable, or where compliance is rendered unlawful by a subsequent law; and the insurer may waive a breach.

Express and implied. An express warranty is written into or upon the policy, or contained in a document incorporated by reference - in a hull and machinery policy, typically a classification warranty, a navigation or trading limits warranty, or a lay-up warranty. An implied warranty arises by operation of law without being written: seaworthiness at the commencement of the voyage in a voyage policy, and legality of the adventure in every policy. The twist candidates miss: a time policy carries no implied warranty of seaworthiness, but where the ship is sent to sea in an unseaworthy state with the privity of the assured, the insurer is not liable for loss attributable to that unseaworthiness.

Jurisdiction. English law changed under the Insurance Act 2015, where breach now suspends cover. In India the consequence remains discharge.

4. (c) War Risk Clause

Where it sits. The Act includes war-type perils within maritime perils - men-of-war, capture, seizure, restraint and detainment of princes and peoples. But the Act does not contain a war risk clause: it is a policy clause, and its practical effect is to define what the ordinary policy will not pay for.

What it does. The standard hull, machinery and cargo policy excludes war, civil war, revolution, insurrection, capture, seizure, arrest, restraint and detainment, derelict mines and torpedoes, and terrorism and malicious acts. Because the exclusion would otherwise leave the assured bare, the same perils are bought back separately under war risk cover - Institute war and strikes wordings on the hull and cargo side, and war risk protection and indemnity cover for the liability side.

How it operates. War risk cover is written on a different commercial basis from ordinary hull cover because the exposure moves: the market's war risk committee designates listed areas for which an additional premium is charged; the assured must notify entry into and departure from such an area, often with a limited number of days included; the cover is cancellable at short notice by either party as the political situation changes; and it terminates automatically on the outbreak of war between the major powers or on the hostile detonation of a nuclear weapon.

Why the Chief Engineer meets it. Because trading into a listed area triggers the notice, the additional premium, the crew's war bonus and the associated hardening measures, and because a detention or arrest is covered - if at all - by the war policy and not by the ordinary one.

5. (d) Charterers Contribution Clause

State the position first. The Marine Insurance Act contains no clause of that name. Two settled ideas lie behind the phrase, and a good short note gives both.

Contribution as the Act uses the word. Contribution in the Act is the doctrine of double insurance: where the assured is over-insured because the same risk is covered by more than one policy, the assured may claim under any of them but cannot recover more than the indemnity, and each insurer is bound, as between the insurers, to contribute rateably in proportion to the amount for which he is liable. An insurer who pays more than his rateable proportion has a right of contribution against the others.

The charterer's contribution in general average. General average is a contribution by all the interests in the adventure to an extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure intentionally and reasonably made for the common safety. Ship and cargo are the obvious contributors, but a charterer commonly has property or interest at risk too - bunkers he owns, containers and equipment he supplies, and freight or hire at risk - and so contributes in his own right. That exposure is insured under charterers' liability cover, and the allocation between owner and charterer is fixed by the general average clause of the charterparty, which typically incorporates the York-Antwerp Rules and may adjust allowances for wages, provisions and stores for periods when the ship is on hire.

Conclusion. A clause in a policy or charterparty dealing with the charterer's contribution is a contractual allocation of that general average exposure, read against the statutory doctrine of contribution - not a section of the Marine Insurance Act.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four notes at four marks each means four equal answers, and the commonest failure is to write two pages on warranties, which every candidate knows, and three lines on the two that decide the grade. Budget the time by the marks. Within each note the reliable shape is definition - legal effect - practical relevance, which is exactly what a short note is for; a definition alone is worth about one mark of four.

The move that separates a strong answer here

Two of these four are in the Act and two are not, and saying so is worth marks rather than costing them. Deviation and warranties are statutory doctrines with sections behind them. A war risk clause is a policy wording, and a charterers' contribution clause is a charterparty or policy provision. The examiner has written 'As per the Marine Insurance Act' across all four, and the honest, high-scoring response is to answer each on its own footing and to say which footing that is - not to invent a section number so that all four look statutory.

The deviation point worth remembering

The insurance excuses for deviation are narrower than the carriage ones. Carriage law protects a reasonable deviation, expressly including deviation to save property at sea. The Act's excuses stop at human life, or aiding a ship in distress where human life may be in danger. So a master who deviates to salvage an abandoned but valuable vessel may be perfectly safe against the cargo interest and may still have discharged the underwriter. Two regimes, two tests, one voyage - and the fact that the insurer's discharge runs from the time of deviation and is not cured by regaining the route is what makes it bite.

Why the warranty rule feels back to front

In ordinary contract law a warranty is the minor term: breach sounds in damages and the contract lives. In marine insurance the warranty is the severe term: it must be complied with exactly, materiality is irrelevant, and breach discharges the insurer from the date of breach. That inversion is the trap. Note also that the two jurisdictions have diverged - English textbooks now describe suspension under the Insurance Act 2015, while the Indian position is still discharge. In an Indian examination, answer on the 1963 Act.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the UK Marine Insurance Act 1906. The operative Indian statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.
  • Applying the English Insurance Act 2015 suspension rule to an Indian policy.
  • Saying deviation avoids the policy from the outset. It discharges the insurer from the time of deviation - losses before it are still covered.
  • Saying the ship is safe once she rejoins her route. Regaining the route does not cure the deviation.
  • Treating an intention to deviate as a deviation. There must be a deviation in fact.
  • Importing the carriage-law excuse of saving property into the insurance list. It is not there.
  • Saying a hull and machinery time policy carries an implied warranty of seaworthiness. It does not - but privity of the assured defeats a loss attributable to unseaworthiness.
  • Presenting the war risk clause as a section of the Act, or as something that grants cover in the ordinary policy. It is an exclusion, with the cover bought back separately.
  • Inventing a section number for a 'Charterers Contribution Clause'. There is none - and saying so, then explaining contribution and the charterer's general average exposure, is the answer.

Examiner traps

"Your ship diverts to tow a disabled but unmanned barge to safety. Any insurance consequence?" Potentially yes. Saving property is not among the Act's excuses; the excuses run to human life, or aiding a ship in distress where human life may be in danger. Under the carriage regime the same act may well be a reasonable deviation. Report it and let the underwriter be told at once.

"You breach the lay-up warranty by two days and nothing happens. Are you covered afterwards?" Under the Indian Act, no - the insurer is discharged from the date of the breach and the position is not cured by the breach ending, unless the insurer waives it. Under English law since 2015 the answer would be different.

"Who pays if two policies cover the same loss?" The assured may claim under either but cannot recover more than the indemnity; as between the insurers they contribute rateably, and one who overpays has a right of contribution against the others.

Understand this first

One sentence in the question does most of the work, and it is the one that looks like a throwaway: the lights are still on. If the ship had gone dark, everything would follow from that - no pumps, no cooling, no control - and the engine stopping would be a symptom rather than the problem. Because the power stayed up, all of those services were there when the engine stopped, so whatever went wrong went wrong inside the propulsion system itself. That single deduction turns an unmanageable list of possibilities into four manageable groups: it did not get fuel, it was not told to run or was told to stop, it lost the oil or the cooling it needs to survive, or something inside it broke or jammed. The other idea worth holding is about the third of those. A modern engine watches itself and will stop itself rather than be destroyed - so when it stops on its own, it has already made a diagnosis, and the record of which alarm came first is that diagnosis written down. Wiping it out by pressing reset, and then cranking to see what happens, is how a stoppage becomes a wreck. Finally, the weather changes the problem completely, and not in the way of making it merely more unpleasant. A ship keeps her nose into the sea by moving forward. Stop her and she slews round until the sea is hitting her on the side, which is the one attitude everything aboard is least designed for - and meanwhile the wind is quietly carrying her somewhere, and somewhere may have rocks in it. So in bad weather the question stops being 'what broke' and becomes 'which way is she pointing, how long have I got, and who is coming'.

Model written answer

(a) The reasons which could have led to this failure

1. What the stated condition tells you before any test is made

The question supplies one decisive fact: main electrical power remained intact. That rules out a blackout and everything downstream of it - no loss of the lubricating oil, cooling water, fuel booster or servo pumps through loss of supply, and no loss of the control and alarm system. The engine therefore stopped for a reason on its own side, and the causes divide into four families. It also means the ship is far better placed than she looks: steering gear, machinery, radio, lighting and the fire pumps are all still live.

2. Fuel-side causes

  • Loss of fuel supply pressure - booster pump failure, suction loss, or a service tank run down or drawn dry.
  • Filter blockage or a rapid differential-pressure rise, often after a fuel change or after heavy rolling has disturbed tank sediment.
  • Water, sludge or cat fines slug reaching the engine; a fuel of the wrong viscosity or a changeover error causing seizure of fuel pump plungers or injector needles.
  • Loss of heating or of viscosity control on a heavy fuel, stalling injection.
  • Air lock in the fuel system, or a leaking or vented high-pressure line.

3. Control, automation and protective-system causes

This family is the most likely single explanation for a sudden stop with power intact, and it splits three ways - the engine has lost its control signal, the protective system has stopped it deliberately, or the governing system has failed.

  • An automatic shutdown has operated on a genuine parameter - low lubricating oil pressure, high jacket or piston cooling outlet temperature, high thrust or main bearing temperature, oil mist detector alarm, or overspeed. The engine has protected itself and the trip is information, not a nuisance.
  • A spurious shutdown from a failed sensor, transmitter, wiring fault or a loss of the safety-system supply.
  • Loss of remote control from the bridge or engine control room - telegraph, remote control system, or the interface between them.
  • Governor or actuator failure, or loss of the governor's speed pick-up signal, taking fuel to zero.
  • Loss of control or starting air, or a control-air pressure low trip - even with generators running, an air compressor fault or a leak drops the control air that the whole system needs.

4. Lubrication, cooling, mechanical and transmission causes

  • Loss of lubricating oil pressure from a pump, filter, suction or sump problem - which will also have commanded the shutdown above.
  • Loss of cooling - sea water suction blocked or lost, central cooler, jacket circulating pump, or expansion tank level.
  • Mechanical seizure or damage - piston or bearing seizure, crankshaft or coupling damage, broken fuel cam or camshaft drive, turbocharger seizure or surge severe enough to stall the engine.
  • Transmission causes where fitted - controllable-pitch propeller hydraulic failure with the pitch running to zero, clutch or gearbox failure, or shaft, coupling or propeller damage such as a rope or net around the propeller.

(b)(i) Actions in calm weather

5. The first minutes - tell the bridge, then stabilise the plant

Inform the bridge immediately - before diagnosis, not after it. The Master must know at once that he has lost propulsion so that he can assess traffic and sea room, exhibit the lights or shapes for a vessel not under command, sound the appropriate signal, warn shipping and consider a navigational warning. Then, in the engine room: confirm steering gear is available and put both power units on; confirm the second generator is running or on standby so that a blackout cannot follow the propulsion failure; start standby lubricating oil, cooling and fuel pumps; start the air compressors and build control and starting air; and prepare the emergency generator for use. Log the time.

6. Diagnosis, and the restart decision

Read the alarm and shutdown record before touching a reset. The order in which alarms arrived usually names the cause, and resetting first destroys that evidence. Then work the four families in order of speed: control air and control position; the shutdown and slowdown channel that operated; lubricating oil pressure and sump level; cooling temperatures and flows; fuel supply pressure, filter differentials, temperature and viscosity. Turn the engine on the turning gear with indicator cocks open and look for water, oil or fuel; feel for local overheating; check crankcase and scavenge spaces once the engine is proved cool and safe to open, and check the shaft is free.

Do not attempt repeated blind restarts against an unexplained protective shutdown. A trip that fired for a real reason - low lubricating oil pressure, high bearing temperature, oil mist - will destroy the engine if it is reset and run. Restart only when the cause is identified and cleared, or when the trip has been positively proved spurious. Then start with the Master's permission, run at reduced load with the engine watched locally, take temperatures and pressures, and increase load progressively. Inform the Company and the DPA, record everything for the technical superintendent and class, and consider whether the defect must be reported.

(b)(ii) Actions in heavy weather

7. What changes - the ship is now in danger, not merely inconvenienced

Everything in (b)(i) still applies, and it applies faster. What is new is that a ship without propulsion in heavy weather loses steerage way and falls beam-on to the sea and swell. From that follow synchronous and heavy rolling with a real risk of cargo shift and of exceeding design accelerations; green water, deck damage and downflooding through vents and openings; slamming and structural loading; and, if there is a lee shore or an offshore installation downwind, a drift towards grounding with a calculable time to run. The engine room itself is now at risk: rolling causes loss of pump suction, sump and tank surging, boiler flame failure and filter disturbance, so a blackout can follow the propulsion failure even though power is presently intact.

8. The heavy-weather actions

  • Give the Master a realistic estimate, and update it. His decisions - to call for a tug, to broadcast, to anchor, to prepare for abandonment - all turn on how long you will be. An optimistic figure is worse than no figure.
  • Help him hold the head. Use the rudder while steerage way remains; use the bow thruster if fitted; consider a sea anchor or drogue if carried; and use ballast and trim to lower the centre of gravity, minimise free surface, improve the roll period and keep the propeller and rudder immersed.
  • Secure the engine room for rolling before working in it. Lash gear and stores; forbid work at height and under suspended loads; watch sump, tank and header levels and change over suctions before they are lost; keep standby pumps running rather than on standby; and keep the boiler and generators under close watch.
  • Protect the electrical supply. Put a second generator on load and keep the third available; prepare and test the emergency generator; prepare emergency steering and man the steering flat; and confirm emergency lighting and the fire pumps.
  • Get help early rather than late. Recommend to the Master that a tug be requested while there is still sea room - a tug ordered in the first hour arrives while it can still help. Prepare the emergency towing arrangement, rig the towing pennant and brief the crew forward, and consider anchoring only if depth, holding ground and the Master's judgement allow.
  • Communications are the Master's. An urgency (PAN PAN) broadcast warns shipping and calls assistance for a disabled ship; a distress (MAYDAY) is made when the ship is in grave and imminent danger - which a lee shore, a failing hull or a shifted cargo can create quickly. I supply the technical facts; the Master transmits.
  • Restraint on the engine. The temptation to force a restart is strongest here and the consequence is worst here. A restart that seizes the engine removes the last chance of self-help, so the same rule holds: establish the cause, or prove the trip spurious, before turning. If partial power is achievable, even slow ahead is decisive - it restores steerage, lets the ship head into the sea and stops the drift.
  • Prepare for the worst case in parallel - watertight integrity confirmed, pollution prevention and SOPEP material ready, the Company and DPA informed under the emergency procedure, and the crew briefed.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has done the candidate a favour by writing 5 / 5 / 6 on the paper, and most candidates ignore it. Heavy weather carries more marks than calm weather, which can only mean that the heavy-weather answer must contain something the calm-weather answer does not. Write the calm-weather response in full, then write the heavy-weather response as what changes and what is added - the loss of steerage, the beam-on roll, the drift, the risk to the engine room itself, the tug, the urgency broadcast. Repeating the calm list with the word 'heavy' in front of it forfeits the extra mark and the examiner's goodwill.

The clue in the question that most answers walk past

'With the main electrical power, however, remaining intact.' The word however is the examiner telling you this matters. It removes blackout from the cause list, and with it every consequential loss of pumps and control supply, so the cause lies on the engine's own side. It also tells you what you still have: steering gear, standby pumps, compressors, radio, lighting and fire pumps. An answer that starts by saying what the stated condition rules in and out is answering 'explain systematically'; an answer that lists thirty possible faults is not.

Trip, slowdown, and the discipline that separates a professional answer

A modern propulsion plant will slow itself down or stop itself to prevent damage. The parameters differ by installation - the ship's own trip schedule is the authority - but the principle does not: a shutdown is a diagnosis the engine has already made. Two consequences follow. First, read the alarm sequence before you reset anything, because the reset destroys the order of arrival, which is usually the whole diagnosis. Second, never crank against an unexplained shutdown. Low lubricating oil pressure, high bearing temperature and an oil mist alarm all mean that restarting turns a stoppage into a wrecked engine - and in heavy weather that removes the ship's last chance of saving herself. Saying this explicitly is worth marks; it is the single most common unsafe answer in this family.

Why beam-on is the whole of the heavy-weather problem

A ship keeps her head to the sea by going forward. Take the propulsion away and she loses steerage way, pays off, and settles beam-on to the sea and swell - the worst possible attitude. Rolling can become synchronous, accelerations rise, cargo and stores can shift, water comes on deck and can find vents and openings, and the ship begins to drift downwind at a rate that can be measured against the chart. This is why the heavy-weather answer is about heading, stability and time, and why any propulsion - even dead slow ahead - is transformative: it restores steerage, lets the ship head up, and stops the drift.

Common mistakes

  • Listing a blackout among the causes. The question says main power is intact.
  • Ignoring the 5 / 5 / 6 split and writing one undifferentiated response.
  • Resetting the trip first and reading the alarm log afterwards.
  • Recommending repeated restart attempts to 'see if it goes'. This is unsafe and is marked as such.
  • Forgetting to tell the bridge first. The Master cannot exhibit not-under-command signals or warn shipping if nobody tells him.
  • Treating not under command as something the engineer declares. The Master does, on your information.
  • Broadcasting distress from the engine room. Urgency and distress are transmitted on the Master's authority.
  • Leaving the ballast and stability limb out of the heavy-weather answer - it is the only lever the engine room shares with the bridge while the engine is dead.
  • Waiting to see whether the engine can be fixed before recommending a tug. Sea room is spent while you wait.
  • Forgetting that heavy rolling can blackout a ship whose power is currently intact, through lost suctions, surging sumps and boiler flame failure.

Examiner traps

"The shutdown was on low lubricating oil pressure but the pressure now reads normal. Do you start?" Not until you know why it fell. A momentary loss from a lost suction, a partly blocked filter or a surging sump in a seaway leaves normal pressure behind it and a damaged bearing ahead of it. Turn on the turning gear, check the sump, prove the pump and filter, and take bearing temperatures on restart.

"You are drifting towards a lee shore and can give slow ahead only. Do you use it?" Yes, and tell the Master at once. Steerage way is worth more than power - it lets him head up and stops the drift - and partial power ordered early beats full power promised later.

"Who declares not under command?" The Master. Your duty is to tell him immediately and honestly what you have and how long you need.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What does 'main electrical power intact' rule out? A blackout, and every loss of pumps, control supply and alarm system that follows from one.
  • What do you read before resetting? The alarm and shutdown record, in order of arrival.
  • Name three parameters that commonly command a shutdown. Low lubricating oil pressure, high jacket or piston cooling outlet temperature, and oil mist detection or overspeed - but the ship's own trip schedule governs.
  • What is the difference between a slowdown and a shutdown? A slowdown reduces load to protect the engine while leaving the ship some propulsion; a shutdown stops it. Both are diagnoses the safety system has already made.
  • What can a disabled ship do about her heading? Rudder while way remains, bow thruster if fitted, sea anchor or drogue if carried, and ballast and trim to manage rolling and keep rudder and propeller immersed.
  • When is it PAN PAN and when is it MAYDAY? Urgency for a disabled ship needing assistance without grave and imminent danger; distress when there is grave and imminent danger. Both on the Master's authority.
  • Why request a tug early? Because sea room, not engineering optimism, is what decides whether a tug can still help when she arrives.

Understand this first

The rule everyone remembers is about fuel: ships may only burn fuel below a certain sulphur content. But there is a second rule sitting behind it which says, in effect, we care about what comes out of the funnel, not about what goes into the tank - so if you can show that your exhaust is as clean as it would have been on the good fuel, you may burn the cheap fuel. A scrubber is the machine that makes that argument, by washing the sulphur out of the exhaust gas with seawater. Which immediately raises the question the whole second half turns on: where does that washing water go? In the simple version of the machine it goes straight back over the side, having done its job, and that is why the machine is cheap to run and why some ports do not want it anywhere near them. Now hold two facts together. The first is that a machine built to take water in and put it out again cannot be talked into keeping the water instead - that is a different, more expensive machine with tanks and chemicals, and you either bought it or you did not. The second is that the ban in the question is not the international rulebook talking; it is a particular harbour saying not here. Put those together and the situation resolves itself: this ship has exactly one lawful move, which is to stop making the argument and simply burn the expensive fuel while she is inside that boundary - and to have finished switching over before she crosses it, because a fuel changeover takes time and does not respect lines on a chart. Everything after that is about proving you did it, and about the harder commercial question of whether buying the machine was ever the better bet when the number of places that will accept it keeps falling.

Model written answer

(a) The primary regulatory driver

1. The limit, and the equivalence that makes a scrubber lawful

The primary driver is MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14, which caps the sulphur content of fuel oil used on board - 0.50 per cent m/m globally, and 0.10 per cent m/m inside an Emission Control Area. Regulation 14 controls the fuel, and on its face it would force every ship onto distillate or low-sulphur residual fuel.

What makes a scrubber possible is a second provision. Regulation 4 - Equivalents allows the Administration to accept a fitting or method as an alternative to a requirement of the Annex if it is at least as effective in terms of emission reductions. An exhaust gas cleaning system is approved under regulation 4 as an equivalent means of compliance: the ship may burn high-sulphur fuel provided the emissions meet the ratio corresponding to the applicable fuel limit, verified against the 2021 EGCS Guidelines, resolution MEPC.340(77), and evidenced by the approval documentation, the onboard monitoring manual and the continuous monitoring record.

So the correct one-line answer is: compliance with the regulation 14 sulphur limits, achieved by an equivalent method permitted under regulation 4, rather than by buying compliant fuel. The commercial driver behind the regulatory one is the price spread between high-sulphur and compliant fuel, which is what makes the capital investment worth making.

(b) HSFO and an open-loop scrubber entering a zero-discharge zone

2. The constraint the scenario imposes, before any action

Two things must be established first. An open-loop system cannot be turned into a closed-loop system. Only a hybrid unit can change mode, and only if it was built and approved with the alkali dosing plant, process tank, holding tank and cooling that closed-loop operation needs. If the ship truly has an open-loop scrubber, there is no operating mode that lets her keep burning HSFO in a zone where the discharge is prohibited.

Second, the zone is a matter of local law, not of MARPOL. At this date there is no global IMO prohibition on discharge water from exhaust gas cleaning systems; the international work is still in progress. What exists is a growing patchwork of national, port and regional restrictions, which differ from one another - some ban open-loop discharge only, some ban all EGCS discharge, some restrict the fuel as well. The port's own published requirement is the controlling document, obtained through the agent and the Company before arrival.

3. The operational switch - planning and timing

The switch is a changeover to compliant low-sulphur fuel with the scrubber discharge stopped, and it must be complete before the ship crosses into the zone. Working backwards from the entry position:

  • Establish the applicable limit for the zone - 0.50 per cent, or 0.10 per cent if it is also an ECA - and confirm which grades on board meet it.
  • Calculate the changeover time from the volume held in the service and settling tanks, the piping and the heaters, and the consumption rate. The system does not flush instantly; residual high-sulphur fuel persists long after the valve is changed.
  • Start early enough that the changeover is finished at the boundary, and follow the ship's written changeover procedure, which MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14.6 requires her to carry.
  • Brief the watchkeepers and the bridge, and agree the entry time and position with the Master.

4. The operational switch - engines, boilers, tanks and the unit itself

  • Tank segregation and compatibility. Use segregated tanks and avoid commingling HSFO and VLSFO, which can be unstable together and drop asphaltenic sludge; keep the retained bunker samples and the analysis results to hand.
  • Temperature and viscosity. Cool the fuel gradually to the required viscosity at the injector - a rapid temperature change is what seizes fuel pump plungers and injector needles and can stop the main engine. Respect the maker's rate-of-change limit.
  • Main and auxiliary engines. Change over together where they share the service system, watch fuel pump and injector behaviour, and adjust the cylinder oil feed rate and base number to the lower sulphur content.
  • Boilers. The question is explicit that the switch covers boilers too: change over the auxiliary and composite boiler burners, re-set burner atomising temperature and check the flame, since a low-viscosity fuel behaves differently and flame failure is a real risk during the change.
  • The scrubber itself. Stop the discharge. Bypass the tower or run it dry in accordance with the maker's instructions, close the overboard, secure the wash water pumps, and be careful of exhaust temperature and back pressure in the bypassed condition. If, and only if, the unit is a hybrid, closed-loop mode with the bleed-off retained in the holding tank is the alternative - subject to holding-tank capacity and to the zone permitting closed-loop discharge at all.
  • Residues. EGCS residue is never discharged to sea; it is retained and landed to a port reception facility, with the receipt kept.

5. Records and evidence

The switch is proved by paper. Record, as regulation 14.6 requires, the volume of low-sulphur fuel oil in each tank and the date, time and position of the ship when the changeover was completed prior to entry - and again when it is commenced prior to exit. Keep the EGCS monitoring record and the record book, the bunker delivery notes and the sealed samples, the port reception facility receipt for residues, and the entry in the engine room log. Port State control will read these before it looks at any machinery.

6. The compliance risks of failing to transition

  • Breach of the port or coastal State's own law - the immediate risk. Discharge in a prohibited zone attracts detention, fines on the company and often on the Master personally, and in some jurisdictions criminal liability.
  • Breach of MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14 itself if the ship instead keeps burning HSFO with the scrubber shut down. The equivalence under regulation 4 is only good while the equivalent is operating; a bypassed scrubber and high-sulphur fuel is simply non-compliant fuel.
  • Evidential failure. A gap in the continuous monitoring record, or a record that does not match the passage, is treated as seriously as the discharge - and a falsified record far more seriously.
  • Port State control detention and delay, with the deficiency recorded against the ship and the company on the regional inspection database.
  • Commercial consequences - off-hire, delay and demurrage, breach of the charterparty's compliance warranties, refusal of future port entry, and loss of standing with charterers who audit environmental performance.
  • Insurance consequences. A deliberate breach of a discharge restriction is not the same risk as an accident, and P&I cover responds differently to a knowing violation.
  • Safety consequence. A rushed or late changeover carries its own risk of loss of propulsion at exactly the moment the ship is entering confined water.

7. The economic trade-off - VLSFO against a scrubber

The scrubber case is a single arithmetic proposition: the annual saving equals the HSFO-to-VLSFO price spread multiplied by annual fuel consumption, and it must exceed the annualised capital cost plus the operating cost. The variables on each side are what the discussion is about.

Second, the regulatory envelope is narrowing rather than widening - the international work on EGCS discharge was still under development at this date, and in the North-East Atlantic a regional ban on open-loop discharges in internal waters and ports takes effect from 1 July 2027, with closed-loop discharges to follow from 1 January 2029. Both are in the future at present, but an investment appraisal made today has to price them. For a container ship on a fixed liner rotation the answer therefore depends less on the spread than on how much of her trading pattern lies inside restricted water - which is exactly why she is in this scenario carrying both a scrubber and compliant fuel.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is worth 4 and has a one-sentence answer; do not spend a page on the history of sulphur regulation. Limb (b) is worth 12 and contains three printed demands - the operational switch, the compliance risks, the economic trade-off - so budget roughly four marks to each and give each its own heading. The single highest-value sentence in the whole answer is the one that says an open-loop unit cannot become a closed-loop unit; most answers to this question quietly assume it can, and the whole of the rest of their operational limb is then fiction.

Regulation 14 sets the limit; regulation 4 lets you meet it another way

This pairing is the conceptual core of limb (a) and it is worth stating explicitly. Regulation 14 is written about the fuel - it caps the sulphur content of fuel oil used on board. Taken alone it would make a scrubber irrelevant, because a ship burning three per cent fuel breaches it however clean her funnel gas is. Regulation 4 is the door: it lets an Administration accept an alternative that is at least as effective in emission terms. The scrubber is that alternative, and everything else - the approval, the monitoring manual, the continuous record - exists to prove the equivalence is real and continuing. The corollary is the trap in limb (b): switch the equivalent off and keep burning the high-sulphur fuel, and you are straightforwardly in breach of regulation 14.

Where the zero-discharge rule actually comes from

It does not come from MARPOL. At this date there is no global IMO ban on EGCS discharge water - the international work was still in progress, with proposals invited to the pollution prevention sub-committee. What exists is local: national, port and regional restrictions, which differ in scope and change frequently. A candidate who says 'MARPOL prohibits open-loop discharge in ports' has made a real error. The correct statement is that MARPOL permits the equivalent, the 2021 EGCS Guidelines set the discharge water criteria and the monitoring, and the port decides whether it will accept the discharge at all. Know also that the direction of travel is one way: the North-East Atlantic regional ban bites on 1 July 2027 for open loop and 1 January 2029 for closed loop - both still ahead of this sitting, so state them as future.

Why the changeover is a propulsion-safety event, not just a paperwork event

Going from heavy fuel to a low-viscosity compliant fuel means cooling the fuel, and doing it too quickly is a classic cause of fuel pump plunger and injector needle seizure - which stops the main engine. The ship is by definition close to or entering port when this happens. So the changeover is planned backwards from the boundary with a calculated flushing time, executed at the maker's permitted rate of temperature change, and watched. The boiler limb matters for the same reason: burner atomising temperature and flame behaviour change with the fuel, and flame failure during a changeover is common. This is also where the question links to any propulsion-failure scenario - a botched changeover is one of the fuel-side causes of a sudden stop.

How to discuss economics without inventing numbers

The examiner asks for a discussion, not a spreadsheet, and quoting a fuel price or a payback period in an exam answer is a gift to a follow-up question you cannot win. State the proposition - saving equals spread times consumption, and must beat annualised capital plus operating cost - and then discuss the variables: the volatility of the spread, the off-hire cost of the retrofit, the parasitic load, the maintenance of a corrosive duty, residue landing, and above all the shrinking operating envelope as more water becomes restricted. That last point is the mature one, and it is the reason a ship in this scenario ends up paying for both solutions at once.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the crew simply switches the open-loop scrubber to closed loop. Only a hybrid unit can do that, and only if it was built and approved for it.
  • Saying MARPOL prohibits open-loop discharge in ports. It does not; the restriction is local or regional, and the international measure was still under development at this sitting.
  • Answering limb (a) with 'to reduce air pollution'. The driver is a specific limit - regulation 14 - met by a specific mechanism - the regulation 4 equivalent.
  • Bypassing the scrubber and continuing on HSFO. That is a breach of regulation 14, because the equivalence only holds while the equivalent is operating.
  • Starting the changeover at the boundary instead of finishing it there.
  • Cooling the fuel too fast and seizing fuel pumps on the approach.
  • Forgetting the boilers, which the question names expressly.
  • Forgetting the record: volume in each tank, and the date, time and position when the changeover was completed.
  • Discharging EGCS residue overboard. It is landed to a reception facility.
  • Quoting a fuel price, a spread or a payback period.
  • Treating the regional 2027 and 2029 bans as already in force. Both are future at this sitting.

Examiner traps

"Your scrubber fails at sea and you have only HSFO aboard. Are you compliant?" No. The regulation 4 equivalence holds only while the equivalent works. Inform the Administration and the port State, seek instructions, and consider a fuel oil non-availability report if compliant fuel genuinely could not be obtained - but a failed scrubber does not make high-sulphur fuel lawful.

"The port bans open-loop discharge but you have a hybrid unit. Problem solved?" Only if the port also accepts closed-loop discharge, and only within your holding-tank capacity for the time alongside. Some zones prohibit all EGCS discharge, in which case you are back to compliant fuel.

"Which is the greater risk to the investment - the fuel price or the regulator?" The regulator. A narrowed spread reduces the return; a restricted trading area removes it, and the direction of regulation has been one way.

Understand this first

The word to distrust in this question is dead. It sounds like a description of the switchboard, and it is really a claim about it - one that has to be earned, at the exact place a hand is going to go, moments before it goes there. A switch in the off position tells you what somebody intended, not what is true; a lamp that is not lit tells you nothing at all, because a broken lamp and a safe conductor look identical. So the first job is to hunt down every route by which electricity could arrive, and the surprising ones are the ones that hurt people - the emergency board that can push power backwards down the same cable it normally takes power from, a transformer fed from its far side, a small control supply inside the cabinet that comes from somewhere else entirely and never noticed the main breaker opening. Then you make your claim true, and you keep it true, because a measurement is a snapshot and a job lasts hours: that is what the locks and, on the big systems, the earths are for. The second thing worth holding is that the ship cannot go dark so that one cupboard can be cleaned. The panel beside the one he is working in is carrying everything the ship is doing, which is why so much of the briefing is about barriers, insulated tools counted in and out, and never blowing dust around with an air line. And the last idea is that finishing is a formal act, not a feeling. The paperwork closes in a fixed order, and the board is not working because the lights came back on - it is working because the insulation was measured, the protection was proved to still be set the way the ship's designers intended, and the emergency arrangements were shown to start when the main ones stop.

Model written answer

(a) The five-step safety briefing before entry and cleaning

I would open by correcting the word in the question: the board is not dead because it is switched off. It is dead only when it has been proved dead, at the point of work, and only in the section we have isolated - the rest of a main switchboard is still supplying the ship.

1. Isolate - and find every source of supply

Identify the exact section from the single-line diagram, not by eye. Open and rack out or withdraw the incoming breaker to the isolated position, and then hunt the supplies that do not come from where you expect: the other generators and the bus-tie, the shore supply, the emergency switchboard interconnector (which can back-feed), a shaft generator, transformer back-feed from the low-voltage side, and the separately fed control, heater, UPS and battery supplies inside the panel. Every one is listed on the isolation schedule and every one is opened.

2. Lock off, tag and hold the key

Each isolation is locked in the open or withdrawn position and carries a danger notice naming the permit, the work and the person. There is one key and it stays with the permit holder - not in a drawer and not with the duty engineer. Only the person who applied a lock removes it. Shutters over the busbar spouts are locked, and the breaker is padlocked in the test or isolated position.

3. Prove dead - and prove the instrument that proves it

Use an approved voltage detector rated for the system, and use it prove - test - prove: prove the detector on a known live source or an approved proving unit, test the conductors, then prove the detector again, so that a detector which failed during the test cannot mislead you. Test all phases to each other and every phase to earth, and test at the point of work. Never accept an indicator lamp, a panel voltmeter, a breaker position indicator or somebody's word as proof - lamps fail dark and that is precisely how people are killed.

4. Earth, and discharge the stored energy

On a high-voltage board, apply the earthing switch or portable earths after proving dead, so that any inadvertent re-energisation trips rather than kills; earths are applied and removed only by the permit holder and are recorded. On any board, deal with stored energy: discharge and short power-factor correction capacitors and let them stand, discharge the d.c. link of any variable-frequency drive, isolate UPS and battery-backed control supplies which do not die with the busbar, and discharge the closing and tripping springs of a spring-charged breaker mechanism before putting hands near it.

5. Control the space, the tools and the person

Screen or barrier every adjacent live section and mark the safe working limit, because the real hazard when cleaning a dead panel is the live one beside it. Wear arc-rated clothing, a face shield, insulating gloves and use an insulating mat; remove rings, watches and chains; use insulated tools and tally them in and out. Clean with a vacuum and lint-free cloths and an approved non-conductive cleaner - never water, and never compressed air, which drives conductive dust into the live section next door. Never work alone: a second competent person stands by, knows how to isolate, is trained in resuscitation, and the rescue hook and first-aid arrangements are to hand. Finally, I would ask him to tell me back what he is going to do - a briefing that is not repeated back has not been received.

(b) The Permit to Work under the Safety Management System

6. Issuing the permit

The permit is a controlled document of the Company's Safety Management System - required by the ISM Code, which obliges the Company to establish procedures for key shipboard operations concerning safety, and to ensure personnel are competent and properly familiarised. Issue runs as follows:

  • A risk assessment or job safety analysis is completed first, and a toolbox talk held with everyone who will work.
  • The Chief Engineer - or the person the SMS authorises - issues the permit to a named competent recipient. Nobody issues a permit to himself.
  • The permit states the exact equipment and the limits of the work, the isolations made and listed on an isolation schedule, the precautions taken, the PPE required, what nearby remains live, and the period of validity.
  • The recipient signs to accept, confirming he has understood the precautions and inspected the isolations himself.
  • Copies are displayed at the work site and on the permit board in the engine control room, and the bridge and duty engineer are informed so nothing is started or changed over in ignorance.

Where high voltage is involved the control tightens. Systems generally taken under class rules as exceeding 1,000 V require a separate high-voltage permit; work is authorised only for a person appointed in writing as competent for high voltage; earthing is mandatory and is itself recorded; and testing that requires the earths off is governed by a distinct sanction-to-test document with its own sign-off. The permit is time-limited, lapses at the stated expiry or at a change of watch, must be re-validated or re-issued rather than allowed to run on, and is suspended immediately if conditions change.

7. Closing the permit

Closing is the mirror image and is done in this order, not in any other:

  • All persons withdrawn and accounted for by name.
  • Tools, test leads, rags and materials removed and tallied against what went in.
  • Temporary earths removed by the permit holder and the removal recorded.
  • Covers, shrouds, barriers and screens replaced and the enclosure secured.
  • The recipient signs the work complete and declares the equipment safe to re-energise.
  • The issuer signs the permit cancelled - and only then are locks and tags removed, each by the person who fitted it.
  • The permit is filed as an SMS record, the work entered in the planned maintenance system, and the permit reviewed in the safety committee or internal audit under the Company's own verification and review obligations.

(c) Verification and power restoration

8. Verification before anything is energised

Mechanical and visual: everything reassembled to the maker's arrangement, no foreign object, tool tally reconciled, cable and busbar connections torqued to the specified value - a loose joint is the classic cause of a switchboard fire - covers and phase barriers refitted and labelling correct.

Electrical: take insulation resistance readings phase to phase and phase to earth at the correct test voltage, with electronics and surge devices disconnected, and compare against the previous recorded values rather than merely against a pass figure; confirm earth continuity and bonding of the enclosure; and check the earth fault monitoring is restored.

Protection: verify relay settings against the approved setting schedule and the discrimination study - a setting quietly changed during maintenance is a fault that only appears on the day of a real fault - and function-test the trips and interlocks: over-current and short-circuit, reverse power, under-voltage release, preferential trip or load shedding where fitted, breaker interlocks, and the automatic changeover to the emergency switchboard.

9. The power-restoration sequence

Restoration is done deliberately and with the ship informed:

  1. Confirm the permit is closed, all earths off, all locks and tags removed, and the space clear of people and tools.
  2. Inform the bridge and the duty engineer, agree the timing against the ship's operational state, and log it.
  3. Energise the busbar first with every outgoing breaker open. Check voltage, frequency, phase sequence and insulation monitoring, and confirm synchronising and load sharing between generators.
  4. Close the outgoing feeders progressively, essential services first and the largest loads last, watching each for abnormal current, arcing sound, smell, smoke or vibration.
  5. Take a thermographic or temperature check on the joints that were disturbed, once loaded.
  6. Restore the emergency switchboard interconnector and prove the emergency arrangements that were affected - emergency generator auto-start on loss of main power, emergency lighting, steering gear on both power units, and the fire pumps.
  7. Update the planned maintenance system with the test results and readings, inform the Company, and where the work touched a class item, arrange the surveyor's attendance before the equipment is credited.

Only when all of that is done, and the readings are recorded, do I declare the main switchboard fully operational.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three limbs at 5 / 6 / 5, and limb (a) comes with its own instruction: five steps. Give five, number them, and do not give four or seven - the examiner has told you the shape of the answer he wants and matching it is free marks. Limb (b) is the largest at 6 and has two halves - issuing and closing - plus a rider about high voltage; answer all three or lose a third of the limb. Limb (c) is a sequence question, so write it as a sequence.

The idea the whole question is built on

'Dead' is a conclusion you reach, not a condition you assume. A switch in the off position, a lamp that is not lit, a breaker showing 'open' - none of these is evidence. The only evidence is a test with an instrument that you have proved works, at the point where the hands will go, immediately before the hands go there. Everything else in limb (a) - the isolation hunt, the locks, the earths, the screens - exists either to make that conclusion true or to keep it true while the work goes on. If you write one sentence of judgement in this question, write that one.

The hazard that is actually going to hurt him

Not the panel he is cleaning. The one next to it. A ship cannot black herself out to clean one switchboard section, so the section is isolated while the board either side of it carries the ship's entire load. That single fact drives half of limb (a): screen the adjacent sections, mark the safe working limit, insulated tools, tally them, no jewellery, and no compressed air - because blowing dust out of a dead panel deposits a conductive cloud into a live one. It is also why he never works alone, and why the standby man must know how to isolate and be able to resuscitate.

Prove - test - prove, in one line

Prove the detector on a known live source or proving unit; test the conductors; prove the detector again. The last step is the one candidates omit and it is the only one that catches the failure mode that kills - an indicator that failed between the first proof and the test, and therefore reads dead on live conductors. Test all phases and every phase to earth, because a single-phase test can miss a back-feed.

Where the stored energy hides

Opening the breaker does not empty the box. Power-factor correction capacitors hold charge and must be discharged and shorted; a variable-frequency drive holds its d.c. link for some time after isolation; UPS and battery-backed control supplies inside the panel are fed from somewhere else entirely and do not die with the busbar; and a spring-charged breaker mechanism holds mechanical energy that will slam a linkage shut on a hand. On HV, the earths are what turn an inadvertent re-energisation from a fatality into a trip.

Common mistakes

  • Giving four or six steps when the question says five.
  • Treating 'dead switchboard' as a fact given by the question rather than a state to be established.
  • Relying on an indicator lamp, a panel voltmeter or a breaker position flag as proof of dead.
  • Proving the detector before the test and not after it.
  • Missing a back-feed - the emergency switchboard interconnector, a shore supply, a shaft generator, or the low-voltage side of a transformer.
  • Forgetting stored energy: capacitors, VFD d.c. links, UPS and battery supplies, and charged breaker springs.
  • Forgetting that the adjacent sections are live.
  • Cleaning with compressed air or water.
  • Letting someone other than the person who fitted a lock remove it.
  • Removing earths before the permit is closed, or letting anyone but the permit holder remove them.
  • Energising the busbar with outgoing breakers closed, or restoring the whole board at once.
  • Declaring the board operational without proving the emergency generator auto-start and the emergency switchboard changeover.
  • Calling the ISM Code's divisions 'regulations'. It has elements and paragraphs.

Examiner traps

"The breaker is racked out and the lamp is off. Is it dead?" No. Racking out removes one supply and the lamp proves nothing - lamps fail dark. It is dead when it has been proved dead at the point of work with a proved instrument, on all phases and to earth, after every source including back-feeds has been isolated.

"Why earth a high-voltage board you have already proved dead?" Because proving dead is a measurement taken at one instant, and the earth is a protection that lasts for the whole job: if the circuit is inadvertently re-energised, the earth turns a fatal contact into a fault the protection clears.

"Who may remove the earths?" The permit holder, and the removal is recorded. Not the duty engineer, not the person restoring power, and not before the permit is closed.

"You have restored the board. What proves the job is finished?" Not the lights coming on. The recorded insulation readings, the protection function tests against the approved setting schedule, the emergency generator auto-start and the emergency switchboard changeover.

  • What is the safe isolation sequence? Identify, isolate every source, secure by locking and tagging, prove dead with a proved instrument, and earth where required.
  • What is prove-test-prove? Prove the detector on a known live source, test the conductors, prove the detector again.
  • Name three back-feeds into a main switchboard section. The emergency switchboard interconnector, a shore supply, and the low-voltage side of a transformer - plus a shaft generator or the bus-tie.
  • What is high voltage on a ship? Generally systems exceeding 1,000 V under class rules, requiring a separate permit, a person appointed in writing as competent, and mandatory earthing.
  • What is a sanction to test? The separate authorisation covering testing that requires the earths to be removed, with its own sign-off.
  • In what order do you close a permit? Persons out, tools tallied, earths off by the permit holder, covers on, recipient signs complete, issuer cancels, then locks and tags off by whoever fitted them.

Three limits are declared. Understand this first

There is a single idea underneath the whole of this question, and it is that a ship which keeps failing is not unlucky. One failure is a broken thing. A pattern of failures is a ship where the information does not move - where somebody knew the emergency pump would not start, and either did not say so, or said so to someone who did nothing, or wrote it down somewhere nobody reads. That is why a new Chief Engineer's first move is not a tour of the machinery but a look at the paperwork and then a conversation, and why the conversation has to be held in a way that does not punish the first person to admit something. If the meeting opens with a search for who is at fault, everyone learns on day one that the safe thing is silence, and the pattern continues with a new name on the door. The multinational part is the same problem wearing different clothes. Information can fail to move because nobody sent it, but it can also fail because it was sent and not received - and a room full of people nodding is one of the most convincing ways in the world to look like communication while being none. Some of that is language; a lot of it is that in plenty of places it is simply not done to tell a senior he is wrong. So you stop asking questions that can be answered with agreement, and start asking ones that can only be answered with knowledge. The last piece, the instruction from the office, tests the same thing pointing the other way: information arriving from outside is equally useless until somebody turns it into a job with a name and a date on it. And the short second half is the human machinery that makes all of this run - which starts, unglamorously, with whether people have slept, been paid, and been told when they are going home.

Model written answer

(a) Joining a ship with a record of port State control failures

1. What I establish before I hold the meeting

A meeting held on impressions achieves nothing. Before I speak I read: the previous inspection reports and the deficiency codes, so I know what actually recurred rather than what people say recurred; the outstanding non-conformities, observations and internal audit findings; the planned maintenance system and its overdue list; the Conditions of Class and outstanding statutory items; the record books - oil, garbage, rest hours; and the handover notes of my predecessor. Repeated failure is almost never a series of unrelated defects. It is one system failing repeatedly, and the pattern is visible in the codes.

2. The initial meeting - what is said, and how

I hold it early, with all engine department officers and ratings together, and the Master informed and preferably present for the opening. The general procedure follows the shape the Safety Management System already prescribes for shipboard meetings:

  • State the position honestly and without blame. The record is a fact; the purpose of the meeting is what we do next. Opening by looking for the person responsible closes the reporting culture on the first day.
  • Familiarisation first. Under the ISM Code the Company must ensure new personnel are properly familiarised - so I confirm that mine has happened and that theirs has, and that everyone can find and use the SMS procedure for his own job.
  • Name the recurring findings from the reports and ask the department why each recurs. The people who work the machinery usually know, and have often said so before without being heard.
  • Fix the routes for information - daily work planning, the pre-job toolbox talk, the watch handover, the defect report, the near-miss report, the safety committee meeting, and the direct route to me and to the Master.
  • Set the standard - what will be reported, to whom, in what form and by when - and record the meeting as an SMS document with actions, owners and dates.

3. The flow of information after the meeting

The meeting establishes the channels; the channels must then work in both directions and be closed out. Downward: Company instructions, circulars and lessons learned, briefed and not merely posted. Upward: defect reports, near misses, non-conformities and suggestions, each acknowledged and answered - an unanswered report is the last report a man makes. Horizontally: watch handovers, deck-and-engine coordination and the permit system. And out to shore: the reporting line to the Company and the Designated Person's direct access to the highest level of management, which exists precisely so that a shipboard concern cannot be filtered out on the way up. Everything material is recorded, because the ISM Code obliges the Company to report, investigate and analyse non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences and to implement corrective action - and because on the next inspection, records are what will be examined.

4. Examples of the repeated failures such a ship carries

The deficiencies that recur where the safety management system is weak are recognisable, and they are systemic rather than technical:

  • Maintenance backlog - overdue planned maintenance jobs closed on paper, and machinery kept running past its interval.
  • Emergency equipment - emergency generator failing to start on load, emergency fire pump unable to deliver, quick-closing valves and remote stops seized, fire dampers and self-closing doors defective.
  • Pollution prevention - oily water separator with a defective 15 ppm alarm or stopping device, oil record book entries inconsistent with tank soundings, garbage management not followed.
  • Records and documentation - rest-hour records that are always exactly compliant, unsigned or retrospectively completed logs, missing test records.
  • Drills and familiarisation - drills recorded but not conducted, or conducted without briefing or debrief; new joiners not familiarised.
  • Permit systems - enclosed-space entry and hot-work permits issued as paperwork after the job rather than as control before it.
  • ISM implementation itself - which is what turns a list of individual deficiencies into a detainable ISM-related finding: the same defect recurring shows the system is not detecting, correcting or preventing.

5. Effective communication with a multinational crew

  • Establish the working language and require it for all safety-related communication - the ISM Code requires personnel to be able to communicate effectively in the execution of their duties.
  • Close the loop. Instructions are repeated back, not nodded at. A nod is a cultural response as often as it is comprehension, and in some cultures a junior will not contradict a senior even when he knows the senior is wrong. That is a safety problem before it is a politeness problem.
  • Show, do not only tell. Demonstrate the job, walk the line, point at the valve. Physical demonstration crosses a language barrier that words do not.
  • Use visual aids - marked-up diagrams, colour-coded systems, photographs in the procedure, pictorial checklists at the machine.
  • Check comprehension by asking open questions - 'show me what you will do first' - never 'do you understand?', which has one answer everywhere in the world.
  • Toolbox talk before every non-routine job, with everyone present who will touch the job, including the ratings.
  • Build the reporting culture deliberately. Thank the man who reports a defect, in front of others; never punish the messenger. Port State control performance is a downstream measure of whether defects get reported early enough to be fixed.
  • Watch for fragmentation by nationality at meals and in leisure, mix the work teams deliberately, and apply one standard of behaviour to everyone.

6. How a shore-office instruction is best utilised

A circular that is filed has changed nothing. The instruction is turned into shipboard action in six steps: read it and establish what it actually requires and by when; assess it against this ship, because a fleet instruction is written for a fleet and this ship's plant may differ; brief it at the safety meeting or toolbox talk in the working language, explaining why as well as what, because a reason is what survives; convert it into the ship's own routine - a planned maintenance job, a checklist item, a permit condition, an amended procedure - so that it outlives the people who heard the briefing; assign an owner and a date and verify that it was done, not that it was circulated; and report back to the office with the evidence and, where the instruction does not fit the ship, say so with reasons rather than silently not complying. Used that way the shore instruction becomes part of the plan-do-check-act loop that the safety management system is supposed to be; filed unread, it becomes the next non-conformity.

(b) Motivation, attitude and constructive competitiveness

7. Lack of motivation

Diagnose before prescribing - apathy is a symptom, and the cause is usually lower down than encouragement can reach. Start with the material conditions, which are also legal entitlements under the MLC 2006: rest hours as actually worked rather than as recorded - a record that is always exactly compliant is a warning, not a reassurance - watch patterns and port workload, food, accommodation, and whether wages, relief and repatriation are certain. Uncertainty about going home is one of the strongest demotivators at sea and is not fixed by exhortation. Then delegate real responsibility with the authority to match it - give an engineer ownership of the purifiers or the boiler and let him report on them - credit him publicly and in the appraisal, plan on-board training and prepare juniors for their next certificate. And lead by example: turn out at three in the morning, wear the protective equipment you demand, and apply the same standard to everyone.

8. Differences in attitude

The task is not to erase difference but to stop it becoming misunderstanding or division. Set one visible standard of work, safety and behaviour and apply it identically regardless of nationality, rank or age - perceived favouritism destroys morale faster than anything else. Fix communication first, using the working language and read-back. Use the structured briefing, so every attitude receives the same information and the same opportunity to object. Deal with conflict early, privately and factually, separating the behaviour from the person, and treat bullying, harassment and discrimination as a formal matter through the on-board complaint procedure rather than as a personality clash. Mix the teams deliberately, pair experience with inexperience, and adjust your own style - direct instruction where a job is safety-critical, consultation where the man doing the work knows more about it than you do.

9. Constructive competitiveness, and the framework this sits inside

Read competitiveness as constructive competition - measurable improvement against a target and against the department's own past - and not as rivalry between individuals, which damages the teamwork safety depends on. Make performance visible and measurable: fuel and lubricating-oil consumption against the shop trial, generator running hours in port, planned-maintenance completion and overdue count, spares stock accuracy, unplanned stoppages. Set targets that belong to the department, not to a person - a month with no overdue jobs, a clean unannounced inspection - and benchmark against the sister ship or the fleet, because competing with another ship unites a department while competing with each other divides it. Recognise promptly and by name. And guard the limit: never let competition encourage short cuts, under-reporting of defects or falsified records - a department that competes on how few defects it reports is more dangerous than one that reports many, and on a ship with this history that is exactly the failure to avoid.

None of this is merely good practice. The obligations are real: the ISM Code requires competent manning, familiarisation, identified training needs, effective communication and the reporting and analysis of non-conformities; STCW as amended by the Manila amendments makes leadership, teamworking and managerial skill an examinable management-level competence for a Chief Engineer; the MLC 2006, with its 2022 amendments in force, makes the material conditions entitlements rather than favours; and the flag State - in India, under the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 - enforces the result.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is one long sentence containing four separate demands, and it is worth 10 marks: the information-flow procedures at the initial meeting, examples of the repeated failures, communication with a multinational crew, and the use of a shore-office instruction. Each is almost certainly worth around two and a half marks, so each needs its own heading. The commonest failure is a good essay on port State control that never reaches the multinational limb or the shore-instruction limb at all. Limb (b) is worth 6 and has three named issues - motivation, attitude, competitiveness - so give three short blocks, not one.

The insight that turns this from a list into an answer

Repeated failure is one system failing repeatedly, not many unrelated defects. A ship that fails once has a defect; a ship that fails regularly has a safety management system that is not detecting, correcting or preventing - which is why inspectors escalate from individual deficiencies to an ISM-related finding, and why that finding is the one that detains. Say this early. It explains why the answer starts with information flow rather than with machinery, and it is the reason the examiner has joined 'failing PSC inspections' to 'poor safety management system implementation' in the same sentence.

Correction, corrective action, preventive action

Three different things, and the difference is what the ship on this record has never grasped. Correction fixes the defect in front of you. Corrective action removes the cause of a defect that has been detected, so that one does not come back. Preventive action removes the cause of a potential defect, before anyone has seen it. The whole distinction between the second and third is one word - detected against potential. A ship that only ever does correction will fail inspection after inspection while honestly believing it has fixed everything.

Why 'do you understand?' is the wrong question

It has one answer in every language and every culture. Replace it with an open, task-based question: show me what you will do first, which valve will you shut, what will you do if the pressure does not fall. Combine that with read-back of instructions and with physical demonstration, and you have crossed the language barrier without insulting anyone. This matters more than it sounds: in a number of cultures a junior will not contradict a senior even when he knows the senior is wrong, so the absence of an objection is not agreement - and on a ship that is a safety defect, not a courtesy.

A shore instruction is an input, not an outcome

The examiner's word is utilised. Circulating an instruction is not utilising it. The full-mark answer converts it into something the ship will still be doing in six months - a planned maintenance job, a checklist line, a permit condition, an amended procedure - with an owner, a date and a verification, and reports back with evidence. And it includes the honest half: where the instruction does not fit this ship's plant, say so with reasons. Silent non-compliance is how the next non-conformity is written.

Common mistakes

  • Answering only the PSC part and never reaching the multinational or shore-instruction limbs. Four demands, ten marks.
  • Inventing a real company's detention history or quoting inspection statistics. The scenario supplies the premise; do not manufacture evidence for it.
  • Opening the initial meeting by looking for who is to blame. It closes the reporting culture on the first day, which is the one thing the answer cannot afford.
  • Giving technical examples only. The repeated failures on such a ship are systemic - backlog, records, drills, familiarisation, permits.
  • Calling the ISM Code's divisions 'regulations'. It has elements and paragraphs; regulation is SOLAS's unit.
  • Treating communication as translation. It is working language plus read-back plus demonstration plus comprehension checking.
  • Asking 'do you understand?'
  • Filing the shore instruction rather than converting it into a routine with an owner and a date.
  • Reading 'competitiveness' as rivalry between individuals. On a ship that damages the teamwork safety depends on.
  • Forgetting that competition can suppress defect reporting - which on this ship would make the record look better and the ship more dangerous.

Examiner traps

"Why do inspectors treat the same deficiency recurring as worse than a new one?" Because a new deficiency is a defect, and a recurring one is evidence that the safety management system is not working - which is an ISM-related finding and a different order of seriousness.

"Your crew all nodded at the briefing and then did it wrong. What went wrong?" The briefing was never received. A nod is not comprehension; read-back and an open task-based question are. Deliver it again with a demonstration.

"The office sends an instruction that will not work on your plant. What do you do?" Reply with reasons and propose the equivalent measure that achieves the same object on this ship. Neither comply blindly nor stay silent - both end as non-conformities.

"Your department has reported far more defects since you joined. Is that a bad sign?" No, it is the first good one. Rising defect reports with falling repeat findings is exactly the pattern you want; the reverse is the ship you inherited.

Understand this first

The scheme this question sits in is simpler than its vocabulary. Every year a ship reports how much fuel she burned and how far she went, and from that a single number is worked out - roughly, the carbon she emitted per tonne of capacity per mile travelled. That number is compared against a line that was drawn from the whole world fleet's performance in one baseline year, and the line is dragged a little lower every year on purpose. Depending on which side of the line you fall, and by how much, you are given a letter from A to E. The letters are not decoration: fall far enough below often enough and you are required to write down what you are going to do about it, put that document inside the ship's energy management plan, and hand it to your flag State. Two things about this are worth understanding properly. The first is that the number is a ratio with distance on the bottom, which means the fastest way to look terrible is to sit still - a ship waiting at anchor burns fuel for her generators and boilers and covers no ground at all, so every day of waiting drags the figure down no matter how beautifully her engine is running. That is why so much of any real plan is about arriving when you are actually wanted rather than early, and why the biggest levers are held by the people who decide where the ship goes and how fast, not by the people who maintain her. The second is that the line moves. Getting back to the performance that was acceptable last year is not good enough, because this year's line is lower. A plan aimed at yesterday's standard fails on the day it succeeds.

Model written answer

1. The trigger, and what two consecutive D ratings actually means

Before drafting anything, the trigger must be stated correctly, because the question's premise is one year short of it. Under MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28.7, a plan of corrective actions is required where a ship is rated D for THREE consecutive years, or E in any one year. Two consecutive D ratings do not engage regulations 28.7 to 28.9. A ship in that position is one year away from a mandatory plan, and there is no sanction in regulation 28 itself - no detention, no fine, no certificate withdrawn.

That is not a reason to do nothing; it is the reason to do this now. A plan begun after the third D has one year of data behind it and almost no time to change the outcome, whereas a plan begun after the second D has a whole reporting year to work in. So the correct answer is: the plan below is drafted voluntarily now, in the form regulation 28.8 will require, so that if a third D occurs it is already written, already in the SEEMP and already running - and so that it prevents the third D instead.

2. What regulation 28.8 requires, and who verifies it

Regulation 28.8 is not the plan itself; it is the documentary and submission limb. It requires that the SEEMP be reviewed to include the plan of corrective actions accordingly, and that the revised SEEMP be submitted for verification no later than one month after reporting the attained annual operational CII. Regulation 28.7 fixes the plan's object - to achieve the required annual operational CII, that is, a rating of C or better. Regulation 28.9 adds the obligation that the plan is duly undertaken, so it is not discharged by submission. The plan therefore lives in SEEMP Part III, the mandatory CII implementation plan, and is verified by the Administration or its recognized organization - which since the amendments in force on 1 August 2025 also audits SEEMP Part III at company level.

Two consequences of that timetable are worth stating in the plan. First, the window is short: one month between reporting and submission, so the analysis has to be done before the reporting deadline, not after it. Second, the required CII tightens every year - the reduction factor Z against the 2019 reference line is 11 per cent for 2026, rising under resolution MEPC.400(83) to 13.625, 16.250, 18.875 and 21.500 per cent for 2027 to 2030 - so a plan that merely restores last year's performance will still fail.

4. Diagnose before prescribing

A plan that opens with a list of measures is a shopping list, not a corrective action plan. The first section establishes where the rating is coming from, using the ship's own data:

  • Decompose the attained CII by voyage, by leg and by condition - laden, ballast, at anchor, in port - because the metric for most ship types is a deadweight-based ratio of CO2 to distance, and it is punished by idle time and by ballast passage regardless of how efficiently the machinery is running.
  • Separate consumption by consumer - main engine, auxiliary engines, boilers - which the enhanced IMO DCS granularity now makes routine rather than exceptional.
  • Compare against the shop trial and the model test: speed-power performance, specific fuel consumption, and the deterioration since the last dry docking.
  • Identify hull and propeller condition from performance monitoring - added resistance shows as increased power for the same speed - and the date of the last cleaning and coating.
  • Identify the commercial pattern: waiting time at anchor, ballast ratio, cargo utilisation, and the speeds actually ordered.

The diagnosis decides which of the following measures belong in this ship's plan. Each measure entered carries an owner, a target, a cost and a date.

5. Technical measures

  • Hull and propeller. Hull cleaning and propeller polishing to a condition-based interval driven by performance monitoring rather than the calendar; a high-performance or foul-release coating at the next docking; and hull roughness measurement.
  • Propulsive efficiency devices. Propeller retrofit or re-pitching to the ship's actual operating profile rather than the design one; pre-swirl and post-swirl devices, propeller boss cap fins, rudder bulb and thrust fins.
  • Hull form. Bulbous bow modification where the ship consistently trades at a draught and speed far from those she was designed for - common after years of slow steaming.
  • Machinery. Main engine part-load optimisation and turbocharger cut-out; auxiliary engine load management and right-sizing; variable-frequency drives on sea water, cooling water and ventilation pumps and fans; LED lighting.
  • Energy recovery. Exhaust gas economiser and waste heat recovery; a shaft generator to remove auxiliary engines from the load at sea.
  • Larger interventions where the payback and the rating trajectory justify them - air lubrication, wind-assisted propulsion, battery hybridisation, shore power capability, and ultimately alternative fuel conversion.
  • Keep EEXI separate. The attained EEXI is a design requirement under regulation 23 and is already met; engine or shaft power limitation is not a corrective action for an operational rating, though its effect on the operating profile must be accounted for.

6. Operational measures

  • Speed management. Reducing service speed is the single most effective operational lever, because propulsive power varies roughly with the cube of speed - but it is the Company's and the charterer's decision, not the Chief Engineer's.
  • Voyage optimisation. Weather routeing, great-circle and current-assisted routeing, and minimising the distance actually steamed.
  • Just-in-time arrival. Steaming slowly to an agreed arrival slot rather than rushing to wait at anchor. This is the highest-value single change on most ships, because time at anchor produces emissions and no distance at all.
  • Trim and draught optimisation against the ship's own trim tables and, where available, a trim optimisation tool validated on measured data.
  • Ballast minimisation consistent with stability, propeller immersion and hull stress.
  • Autopilot and steering optimisation to reduce rudder movement and its added resistance.
  • Machinery load management in port - minimum generators on load, boiler and cargo-heating discipline, and shore power where fitted and available.
  • Performance monitoring and crew engagement - noon and continuous monitoring, a monthly review of the figures with the department, and training so that the men making the decisions can see their effect.

7. Commercial measures, and the limit of the Chief Engineer's control

An honest plan says what the ship cannot fix by herself. The rating is driven substantially by decisions the ship does not make - the speed ordered, the ballast ratio, the cargo utilisation and the time spent waiting. On a time-chartered ship those belong to the charterer, which is why the industry developed contractual mechanisms such as the BIMCO CII Operations Clause for Time Charter Parties to allocate responsibility for the rating. The plan must therefore be a Company document with a shore owner, not an engine room document: it records which measures the ship controls, which the Company controls, which require charterer agreement, and which need capital approval and a docking window. A plan that assigns every action to the Chief Engineer is one that will not be delivered.

8. Monitoring, verification, and the state of the measure itself

The plan closes with its own control loop, in the SEEMP's plan - implement - monitor - self-evaluate and improve form: a defined baseline, a target attained CII for the coming year set against the required CII with the year's Z factor applied, quarterly review against that trajectory, named owners, and a documented escalation if the trajectory is not met. It is submitted to the Administration or recognized organization for verification, and since the 2025 amendments the Company's implementation of SEEMP Part III is itself subject to company audit.

Two currency points belong in the covering note. The measure is under review: regulation 28.11 required a review of the CII requirements to be completed by 1 January 2026, and its second phase - which expressly covers reinforced corrective action, enforcement and revision of the Z factor and reference lines - had opened but not concluded at this date. And the IMO Net-Zero Framework, approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025, was not adopted: the extraordinary session convened to adopt it in October 2025 was adjourned to reconvene about twelve months later. Neither changes what regulation 28 requires today, and a plan written now should say so rather than anticipate either outcome.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The command verb is draft, and the two riders are timelines and examples of both technical and operational measures. So the answer must look like a document - sections, dates, owners - and it must give both kinds of measure, because giving only technical measures forfeits half of one of the three named demands. Sixteen marks with three demands means roughly a third each to the regulatory frame and timeline, the technical measures and the operational measures, with the diagnosis and the monitoring loop as the framing that turns a list into a plan.

The premise is one year short of the regulation, and saying so is the highest-value sentence on the paper

Regulation 28.7 triggers on D for THREE consecutive years, or E in one year. The question says two. Two consecutive D ratings therefore do not engage regulations 28.7 to 28.9 at all. This is not a quibble and it is not a reason to refuse the question: the right response is to state the trigger correctly, place the ship one year short of it, and then draft the plan anyway - because a plan written after the second D has a full year to change the outcome, while a plan written after the third has almost none. An answer that silently accepts the premise cannot demonstrate that it knows the rule; an answer that corrects the premise and still delivers the deliverable demonstrates both.

Regulation 28.8 is the paperwork limb, not the plan

Candidates routinely attribute the whole scheme to 28.8. Keep the sub-paragraphs apart. 28.7 creates the obligation and fixes its object - a plan of corrective actions to achieve the required annual operational CII. 28.8 says where the plan goes and when: the SEEMP is reviewed to include it, and the revised SEEMP is submitted for verification no later than one month after reporting the attained annual operational CII. 28.9 requires the plan to be duly undertaken. 28.10 encourages incentives for ships rated A or B. And note what is absent: regulation 28 prints no sanction at all - no detention, no fine, no certificate withdrawn. The consequence is a plan in the SEEMP, and the real pressure is commercial.

Why the metric punishes things the engine room does not control

For most ship types the operational carbon intensity is measured as a deadweight-based ratio of CO2 emitted to distance sailed. Two consequences follow and both belong in the plan. Time at anchor is the worst possible state: the ship burns fuel for the auxiliaries and boilers and travels no distance, so every hour waiting makes the ratio worse. And a ballast passage counts its distance and its fuel but earns no cargo credit under the deadweight-based metric. That is why just-in-time arrival and the ballast ratio matter more than almost any machinery measure, and why the answer must say plainly that the speed ordered, the waiting time and the cargo pattern are charterer and Company decisions. The industry's contractual response to exactly this problem is why CII clauses exist in time charters.

The moving target

The required CII tightens every year. The reduction factor Z against the 2019 reference line runs 5 per cent for 2023, 7 for 2024, 9 for 2025 and 11 for 2026, and resolution MEPC.400(83) of April 2025 extended it to 13.625, 16.250, 18.875 and 21.500 per cent for 2027 to 2030. So a corrective action plan that restores last year's performance still fails, because last year's performance is measured against a looser line. The plan's target must be set against next year's required CII, not against this year's attained one - and that single point separates a plan that works from one that reads well.

What is new at this sitting

Two changes make an answer written in 2026 different from one written in 2024. The amendments in force on 1 August 2025 require enhanced granularity in the IMO DCS from 1 January 2026 - fuel consumption by consumer type, consumption when not under way, onshore power taken in kilowatt-hours, laden distance, and transport work in tonne-miles, TEU-miles or passenger-miles - and they make SEEMP Part III subject to company audits by the Administration or a recognized organization. For a corrective action plan both matter directly: the diagnosis that used to require special measurement is now a by-product of mandatory reporting, and the plan is now audited at company level rather than merely filed.

Common mistakes

  • Accepting the two-year premise without noticing that the trigger is three consecutive D ratings or one E.
  • Attributing the trigger, the plan and the submission all to regulation 28.8. It is 28.7 that triggers, 28.8 that submits, 28.9 that requires the plan to be undertaken.
  • Saying a D rating leads to detention, a fine or a withdrawn certificate. Regulation 28 prints no sanction.
  • Writing a list of measures with no diagnosis, no owners, no costs and no dates - which is not a plan.
  • Giving only technical measures, or only operational ones. The question names both.
  • Setting the target against this year's attained CII rather than next year's required CII, which is tighter.
  • Confusing the SEEMP parts: Part I is the energy efficiency management plan, Part II the data collection plan, and Part III is where the CII implementation plan and this corrective action plan live.
  • Offering EEXI engine power limitation as a corrective action. EEXI is a design requirement under regulation 23 and is a different obligation.
  • Quoting a percentage saving for a coating, a propeller device, air lubrication or trim optimisation. They are hull-form, draught and trade specific.
  • Assigning every action to the Chief Engineer when the largest levers - speed, waiting time, ballast ratio, cargo utilisation - are commercial.
  • Writing the IMO Net-Zero Framework in as though it were adopted. At this sitting it was approved but not adopted.

Examiner traps

"You have had two D ratings. What does MARPOL require you to do?" Strictly, nothing under regulation 28.7 - the trigger is three consecutive D ratings or one E. What it requires is that you continue to collect and report data under regulation 27 and maintain SEEMP Part III. What prudence requires is the plan, now.

"Your ship is rated D and your machinery is in excellent condition. How?" Very easily. The metric divides emissions by distance on a deadweight basis, so a ship that spends weeks at anchor, sails in ballast, or is ordered to arrive early and wait will rate badly however well her engine is running. That is the criticism the regulation 28.11 review is examining.

"Who signs off the plan?" It goes into the SEEMP and the revised SEEMP is submitted for verification to the Administration or its recognized organization within one month of reporting the attained CII - and since the 2025 amendments the Company's implementation of SEEMP Part III is itself company-audited.

"What happens if you do nothing?" No penalty in regulation 28. But the rating is published, charterers screen on it, financiers and cargo owners ask for it, and the required line tightens every year - so the commercial consequence arrives long before any regulatory one.

  • Trigger: D for THREE consecutive years, or E in any one year
  • Attained annual operational CII reported within THREE months of the end of the calendar year
  • Revised SEEMP submitted for verification within ONE month of reporting the attained CII
  • Reduction factor Z against the 2019 reference line: 5 per cent 2023, 7 per cent 2024, 9 per cent 2025, 11 per cent 2026
  • MEPC.400(83), 11 April 2025: 13.625, 16.250, 18.875 and 21.500 per cent for 2027 to 2030
  • MEPC.385(81) in force 1 August 2025 - enhanced IMO DCS granularity from 1 January 2026 and company audits of SEEMP Part III
Critical regulation
MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28.7 requires a ship rated D for three consecutive years, or rated E, to develop a plan of corrective actions to achieve the required annual operational CII; regulation 28.8 requires the SEEMP to be reviewed to include that plan and the revised SEEMP to be submitted for verification not later than one month after reporting the attained annual operational CII; and regulation 28.9 requires the plan to be duly undertaken. Regulation 28 prints no penalty for a poor rating.
Major trap
Accepting the question's premise that two consecutive D ratings require a plan under regulation 28.8. The trigger in regulation 28.7 is D for THREE consecutive years or E in any one year, so the ship in this scenario is one year short of the mandatory obligation - which is precisely why the plan is worth drafting voluntarily now. The related traps are attributing the trigger to 28.8 rather than 28.7, saying a poor rating leads to detention or a fine when regulation 28 prints no sanction, and setting the plan's target against this year's attained CII when the required CII tightens every year.
