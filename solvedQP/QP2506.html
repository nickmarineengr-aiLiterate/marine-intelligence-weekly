Understand this first

A propeller does not convert all the power it is given into useful thrust. Some of what it leaves behind is axial - water thrown astern faster than it needs to be - and some is rotational: the race leaves the disc spinning, and that spin is wasted energy. Behind the propeller of a single-screw ship sits a large flat object in exactly that spinning race: the rudder. Every device in this question does one of two things with it - it either recovers some of the rotational energy and turns it into forward thrust, or it stops wasting more, by removing a vortex or a separation the rudder itself creates. Read the four limbs that way and they stop being four unrelated gadgets.

Model written answer

1. Where the energy to be recovered is

A screw propeller working behind a hull typically converts only about 65 to 72 per cent of delivered power into useful thrust. The losses are of three kinds: axial, in the sternward momentum of the race; rotational, in the swirl the blades impart to it; and frictional and vortex losses, principally the hub vortex shed from the boss. The rotational component alone is commonly of the order of 8 to 12 per cent of delivered power on a single-screw merchant ship.

The rudder is the only large body standing in that race. It is therefore both an opportunity - the swirl arrives at it already rotating, so a correctly oriented surface there sees an angle of attack for nothing - and a liability, because a plain rudder behind a bare boss adds drag and lets the hub vortex run on unchecked. The four devices below exploit the first and reduce the second.

2. (a) Rudder thrust fins

A rudder thrust fin is a horizontal foil, usually fitted as a pair port and starboard, mounted on the rudder at or near the level of the propeller shaft, inside the slipstream. Because the race is rotating, the flow arrives at the fin with a tangential component: upward on one side of the shaft and downward on the other. Each fin is set at the angle that turns that oncoming flow into lift, and because the flow is inclined, the lift vector is tilted forward. Its horizontal component is thrust, generated with no additional power.

  • The two fins are set at different angles, or in mirror image, because the swirl direction differs above and below the shaft.
  • Gains quoted in industry literature are typically of the order of 2 to 5 per cent in delivered power, and are strongly dependent on span, chord and angle of attack.
  • The angle is fixed at build. The device is optimised for one draught and speed and gives less, or in extreme off-design conditions nothing, elsewhere.
  • It is a source of added appendage drag when not loaded, and a candidate for cavitation erosion if it sits too close to the blade tips.

3. (b) Asymmetric and twisted rudders

Here the recovery is built into the rudder itself rather than added to it. On a single-screw ship the propeller turns one way, so the race strikes the upper half of the rudder from one side and the lower half from the other. A conventional symmetrical rudder, aligned with the centreline, therefore meets the flow at an angle of attack of the wrong sign over half its span, producing side force, drag and early cavitation.

An asymmetric rudder cures this by making the two halves different. In the developed form - the twisted leading edge rudder - the leading edge is twisted in opposite directions above and below the shaft line, so that each half is aligned with the swirl it actually meets.

  • The rudder becomes a swirl-recovery surface: the side force on the two halves is turned into a net forward component.
  • It reduces drag and cavitation, and with it rudder erosion and propeller-excited vibration.
  • It is handed - built for one direction of rotation - so it is a design decision, not a universal retrofit.
  • Because it changes the rudder body rather than adding to it, it carries no added appendage and its benefit degrades more gracefully off design than a fixed fin.

4. (c) Rudder bulbs

The propeller boss sheds a strong hub vortex, and behind a bare boss the flow separates, leaving a low-pressure wake that pulls the ship astern. A rudder bulb is a streamlined body of revolution fitted to the rudder leading edge, coaxial with the shaft and sized and positioned so that it forms an almost continuous body with the propeller hub cap, closing the gap between them.

  • It suppresses or displaces the hub vortex, removing a pure loss.
  • It restores pressure behind the boss by giving the flow a body to close around instead of separating, which is a genuine reduction in resistance rather than a recovery of energy.
  • The diameter and the axial gap govern the result; too large or too far aft and the bulb adds more drag than it saves.
  • It is most often fitted together with fins, the bulb conditioning the flow the fins work in, so the two are designed as one installation and their savings are not simply additive.

5. (d) Grim vane wheels

The vane wheel, patented by Otto Grim in 1966, is the most ambitious of the four and the only one that moves. It is a freely rotating bladed wheel mounted on the shaft line immediately aft of the propeller, of larger diameter than the propeller, with no mechanical connection to it or to the shaft. It runs at its own speed, typically around a third to a half of propeller revolutions, driven entirely by the water.

Its cleverness is that one blade does two jobs at different radii:

  1. The inner portion, standing inside the propeller race, works as a turbine. It extracts energy from the swirl and axial velocity of the slipstream, and that is what drives the wheel round.
  2. The outer portion, standing outside the race in undisturbed water, works as a propeller. Driven by the torque the inner part has extracted, it produces additional thrust over a larger disc area and therefore at higher efficiency.

The wheel thus takes wasted energy from a small, fast, disturbed stream and returns it as thrust in a large, slow, clean one. Gains of the order of 5 to 10 per cent were claimed and some were demonstrated. In service the device proved difficult: the long, thin, unsupported blades of the outer portion are fatigue-loaded and blade loss and shedding occurred; performance falls away in a seaway and off design, when the wheel can windmill unhelpfully; and it adds a rotating component aft of the propeller with its own bearing and clearance problems. It is now of more importance as a principle - and as an examination question - than as a device in general service.

6. Whether a claimed gain is real on this ship

All four claims are hull-specific. A device recovers only what the propeller behind that hull actually wastes, so the gain depends on the wake field, block coefficient, propeller loading, draught and service speed. Three consequences follow, and they are where the marks are at this level. First, a quoted percentage means nothing without the condition it was measured in, and model-scale results require a scale-effect correction before they can be believed at full scale. Second, devices interact: a bulb changes the flow the fins see, so savings must be assessed for the installation as a whole. Third, the real case is the operating profile - a ship that spends its life slow-steaming at part draught will not realise a gain optimised for design condition, and the payback calculation must be built on the actual profile, on class approval of the attachment and its structure, and on the effect on manoeuvring, which a rudder-mounted device necessarily touches.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has done the structuring for you. Four devices are named and each carries 4 marks, so the answer is four equal blocks and nothing else can be substituted for them. The single most common way to lose marks here is to write a good general essay on energy-saving devices - pre-swirl stators, ducts, propeller boss cap fins, air lubrication - none of which is asked for. Answer the four printed limbs.

The one idea that unlocks all four

Rotational loss. The propeller leaves the race spinning, and that spin is energy already paid for and thrown away. Two of these devices recover it - the fin and the twisted rudder turn swirl into forward force, and the vane wheel takes it mechanically. Two stop further waste - the bulb removes the hub vortex and the separation behind the boss. If you can say which of the two categories a device is in, you can reconstruct its explanation from first principles.

Keep recovery and drag reduction apart

  • Recovery - thrust fins, asymmetric and twisted rudders, vane wheels. They convert energy already in the water into thrust.
  • Drag and vortex reduction - rudder bulbs. They do not recover anything; they stop a loss from happening.
  • The bulb is often described loosely as recovering hub-vortex energy. The defensible statement is that it suppresses the vortex and restores pressure behind the boss. Say that and the point is safe either way.

Common mistakes

  • Describing an asymmetric rudder as a rudder offset to one side of the centreline. The asymmetry is in the section and the twist about the shaft line, matching the opposite swirl above and below.
  • Saying the Grim vane wheel is geared to or driven by the shaft. It is entirely free-running; there is no mechanical connection, and that is the whole point.
  • Making the vane wheel the same diameter as the propeller. It is larger, and it must be, because the outer portion has to stand outside the race to act as a propeller.
  • Quoting a saving as a hard number. Every figure is conditional on the hull and the operating condition.
  • Adding the percentages of several devices together.
  • Ignoring manoeuvring. Anything bolted to a rudder changes the way she steers, and class will want to see the structure.

Examiner traps

  • "Currently in use" in the stem. The Grim vane wheel is the odd one out - historically important, largely not in service. Say so rather than pretending it is common; an answer that quietly treats all four as current practice is weaker, not stronger.
  • Limb (d) is worth the same 4 marks as the others even though it is the least used. Do not short it.
  • The stem says the devices contribute to propulsion effectiveness, not to steering. Manoeuvring belongs in the closing judgement, not in place of the hydrodynamics.

Model written answer

1. Adoption is not entry into force

A convention is adopted either by the competent IMO organ - the Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text and does no more: it binds no State, and an adopted convention may never enter into force at all. The text is then opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General of IMO as depositary.

2. How a State expresses consent to be bound

Between adoption and entry into force sits the step that actually matters: each State must express its consent to be bound, by one of three routes -

  • signature alone, where the treaty so provides or the negotiating States so agreed;
  • signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, which is the normal course for multilateral conventions;
  • accession, where the State did not sign within the signature period.

In the second and third cases the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument with the depositary, who records it, counts it towards the entry into force condition, and notifies all other States.

3. The two terms the question names

(ii) Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval. The State signs, but the signature does not bind it; it indicates an intention to proceed. The State becomes a party only on later depositing an instrument of ratification. Its modern purpose is to give the State time to enact the necessary domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements before committing internationally. Acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names, used where a constitution does not demand the fuller procedure: VCLT Article 14(2) provides that consent is expressed by acceptance or approval under conditions similar to those which apply to ratification. In legal effect the three are identical.

(i) Accession. The means by which a State becomes party to a treaty it did not sign - because the signature period has closed, or because it was not a negotiating State. It is a single step: no prior signature, simply deposit of an instrument of accession, permitted by VCLT Article 15 where the treaty so provides. Once deposited it has exactly the same legal effect as ratification. The difference is the route, not the destination.

4. The entry into force provision, and why it matters

Every convention contains a final clause fixing entry into force, normally a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. Its importance is fourfold:

  • it prevents an instrument binding a handful of States while the rest operate to another standard, which would distort competition;
  • it fixes a certain date from which obligations, surveys and certificates run, so Administrations, recognised organisations, owners and yards all work to one deadline;
  • it provides the interval in which flag States legislate, class develops rules, and industry builds or retrofits;
  • it gives legitimacy - a convention in force across most world tonnage can be enforced through port State control against ships of non-parties, which is what makes no more favourable treatment work.

5. Conventions of an important technical nature

For a convention of important technical nature - one dictating construction, equipment or operation - States observe a stiffer double condition: a number of States and a percentage of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping. A count of States alone could be satisfied by many small registries while the major fleets stayed outside, leaving a convention in force on paper and irrelevant at sea.

  • SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, requiring not less than 25 States whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; in force 25 May 1980.
  • The 1978 MARPOL Protocol - in force twelve months after not less than fifteen States constituting not less than fifty per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage had become Parties.

Older conventions typically required two-thirds of contracting States, which occasionally meant more acceptances were needed to bring an instrument into force than to amend it.

6. Keeping the convention current once in force

A technical convention is worthless if it cannot keep pace, and explicit acceptance failed on exactly that point: amendments to the 1960 SOLAS Convention adopted between 1966 and 1973 never received enough acceptances and none entered into force.

IMO's answer was the tacit acceptance procedure, used in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL and the collision regulations. It reverses the default: an amendment to the technical annexes is deemed accepted on a specified date unless objections are received beforehand from an agreed number of Parties. For SOLAS 1974 an amendment is deemed accepted at the end of two years from communication to Contracting Governments, unless objected to by more than one-third of Contracting Governments, or by Governments whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross merchant tonnage. Amendments now enter into force typically within 18 to 24 months.

The sequence can be seen running at this very sitting. Under MARPOL article 16(2)(a) the Secretary-General circulated the draft revised Annex VI on 11 April 2025 for consideration with a view to adoption at an extraordinary session under article 16(2)(b), (c) and (d). Circulated, therefore, but not yet adopted - and still not adopted on the day this paper was sat. Finally, once in force internationally each flag State must still give the instrument domestic effect, in India through the Merchant Shipping Act and the rules made under it, and it is that domestic instrument which is enforced against the ship.

Model written answer

1. (a) Port of refuge - what it is and why a ship puts in

A port of refuge is a port or place, not the intended destination, into which a ship deviates because she cannot safely continue the voyage. The usual causes are heavy weather damage, grounding or collision damage, fire, a shift of cargo, main engine or steering failure, or a medical emergency. Putting in is a deviation, and in insurance terms that is the first thing to say about it: a deviation not justified by the policy discharges the insurer from liability from the moment of deviation, but a deviation reasonably made for the safety of the ship or of life is excused, as is one made to save human life or to secure necessary assistance.

The regulatory backdrop should be stated correctly, because it is the point on which most answers are out of date. Guidance is given by the IMO Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, resolution A.1184(33) adopted on 6 December 2023, which by its operative paragraph 4 revokes the earlier resolution A.949(23) of 2003. The Guidelines are recommendatory, and their central premise is blunt: a coastal State is under no obligation in international law to grant a place of refuge. It must weigh the ship's need against the protection of its own coastline, which is why refusal or delay is a real commercial risk and not a theoretical one.

2. (a) Port of refuge - who pays

This is where the insurance content lies. If the ship put in for the common safety of ship and cargo, the entry is a general average act within Rule A, and the York-Antwerp Rules then allow the resulting expenditure as general average - to be shared rateably by ship, cargo and freight according to contributory values:

  • Rule X allows the port charges of entering the port or place of refuge and of leaving it, and the cost of handling, discharging, storing and reloading cargo, fuel and stores, where that handling is necessary for the common safety or to allow damage caused by sacrifice or accident to be repaired.
  • Rule XI allows the wages and maintenance of master, officers and crew, and the fuel and stores consumed, during the prolongation of the voyage occasioned by putting in or being detained there.

Two qualifications carry the marks. First, the allowance depends on the purpose: expenditure incurred for the common safety is general average, whereas repairs and costs relating only to the ship's own accidental damage are particular average on the ship and fall on her owner and his hull underwriter alone. A single call at a port of refuge routinely generates both, and the average adjuster's task is to separate them. Second, general average is not affected by fault as to entitlement: the party at fault may still claim contribution, leaving the other parties to their separate remedies for the breach - which is what keeps the adjustment moving. The practical consequence for the ship is that average security - an average bond from cargo receivers and an average guarantee from their underwriters - must be collected before cargo is released.

3. (b) Particular average

Particular average is a partial loss of the subject-matter insured, caused by a peril insured against, which is not a general average loss. Its three defining features are that it is accidental and not deliberate; that it affects a particular interest only - hull, or one parcel of cargo; and that it lies where it falls. There is no sharing: the owner of the damaged property bears it, and recovers from his own underwriter if the loss was proximately caused by a peril insured against, subject to the deductible or franchise in his policy. Heavy weather damage to a hatch cover, or seawater damage to one hold of cargo, is particular average.

4. (b) General average, and the contrast

General average arises from a general average act, and Rule A defines it exactly: "there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure." The loss is then shared rateably by all the interests saved, in proportion to their contributory values, through an adjustment prepared by an average adjuster. Jettison of cargo to refloat, flooding a hold to fight a fire, and the cost of engaging salvors are the standard examples.

Set out as a contrast, the distinction is clean:

  • Cause - particular average is accidental; general average is intentional.
  • Purpose - general average is for the common safety of the whole adventure; particular average has no purpose at all.
  • Incidence - particular average lies where it falls; general average is contributed to by everyone saved.
  • Machinery - particular average is a claim on the assured's own policy; general average requires an adjustment, average security, and contribution from parties who may have suffered no damage at all.
  • The extraordinary requirement - ordinary use of the ship, however hard, is not a sacrifice. This is where most general average arguments are actually fought.

5. (c) Actual total loss

A total loss is either actual or constructive. There is an actual total loss where the subject-matter insured is destroyed; or is so damaged that it ceases to be a thing of the kind insured - cement set hard in the hold, a ship burnt to a shell; or where the assured is irretrievably deprived of it, as by capture or by a sinking beyond recovery. To these the law adds the missing ship: where a ship is missing and no news of her is received after a reasonable time, an actual total loss may be presumed. On an actual total loss the assured claims the full sum insured and no notice of abandonment is required, because there is nothing left to abandon.

6. (c) Constructive total loss and notice of abandonment

The examiner's printed words are "Total Loss and Constructive Loss"; the term of art is constructive total loss, and using it correctly is itself worth something. There is a constructive total loss where the subject-matter is reasonably abandoned because its actual total loss appears unavoidable, or because it could not be preserved from actual total loss without an expenditure which would exceed its value when that expenditure had been incurred. In practice it arises in three ways: the ship is so damaged that the cost of repair would exceed her repaired value; the assured is deprived of possession and is unlikely to recover her, or the cost of recovery would exceed her value; or cargo is so damaged that the cost of repairing and forwarding it would exceed its value on arrival.

The machinery matters as much as the test. To claim for a constructive total loss the assured must give notice of abandonment to the underwriter, with reasonable diligence after receiving reliable information of the loss; if he does not, the loss can be treated only as a partial loss. Notice may be waived by the underwriter, and is unnecessary where there would be no possibility of benefit to him. If the abandonment is accepted it is irrevocable, the acceptance conclusively admits liability and the sufficiency of the notice, and the underwriter takes over the assured's interest in whatever remains, together with proprietary rights incidental to it - which is how underwriters come to own wrecks. The assured is not bound to abandon: he may elect to treat the loss as partial instead. To these two legal categories the market adds a third, purely commercial one, the compromised or arranged total loss, where liability is disputed or the tests are marginal and the claim is settled at an agreed figure without either side conceding the point.

Particular average is an accidental partial loss of one interest by an insured peril; it lies where it falls and is recovered on the assured's own policy. General average is an extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure intentionally and reasonably made for the common safety to preserve the property in a common maritime adventure; it is shared rateably by all the interests saved, on contributory values, through an average adjustment.

Accident against intention, and borne alone against shared. The marks are in the contrast, not in two separate definitions.

Where the subject-matter insured is destroyed; or is so damaged that it ceases to be a thing of the kind insured; or where the assured is irretrievably deprived of it. The "ceases to be a thing of the kind insured" limb is the one candidates forget, and it is the one that decides set cement and burnt-out hulls.

Where the subject-matter is reasonably abandoned because its actual total loss appears unavoidable, or because it could not be preserved from actual total loss without an expenditure exceeding its value when the expenditure had been incurred. In practice: repair cost exceeding repaired value, deprivation of possession with recovery unlikely or too costly, or cargo not worth repairing and forwarding.

The test is economic, not physical. "Badly damaged" is not a constructive total loss.

The loss can be treated only as a partial loss. Notice must be given with reasonable diligence after receiving reliable information of the loss. It may be waived by the underwriter and is unnecessary where there could be no benefit to him. If accepted, the abandonment is irrevocable, the acceptance conclusively admits liability and the sufficiency of the notice, and the underwriter takes over the assured's interest in the residue.

Those incurred for the common safety: the port charges in and out, and the handling, discharge, storage and reloading of cargo where necessary for the common safety or to allow the damage to be repaired, under Rule X; and crew wages and maintenance and stores consumed during the prolongation, under Rule XI. Understand this first

The two limbs look unrelated and are joined by one idea: who pays, and who may punish, when a casualty happens outside anybody's territory. Limb (a) is about paying a salvor when the ordinary no-cure-no-pay bargain would leave him unpaid for work society wants done. Limb (b) is about the opposite reflex - the instinct that after a collision somebody must prosecute - and the answer is that on the high seas the number of States entitled to do so is deliberately very small. Keep penal and disciplinary jurisdiction (limbs i and ii, which UNCLOS restricts) rigidly apart from civil liability (limb iii, which it does not touch at all).

Model written answer

1. The problem SCOPIC was written to cure

Salvage is no cure - no pay. Under the Salvage Convention 1989, in force 14 July 1996, the salvor earns a reward under Article 13 only if he saves property, and the reward cannot exceed the salved value. That is a poor bargain for a laden tanker aground on a falling tide with no realisable property value but an enormous pollution risk. Article 14 was the Convention's answer: where the vessel threatened damage to the environment and the Article 13 reward was less, the salvor recovers special compensation equal to his expenses, increasable by up to 30 per cent and, if the tribunal thinks it fair and just, up to 100 per cent of expenses.

In practice Article 14 failed the people it was written for, for four reasons. It required proof of a "threat of damage to the environment", which was itself litigated. "Expenses" was judicially confined to out-of-pocket cost and a fair rate for equipment and personnel, with no profit element. Everything was assessed after the event by arbitration, so the salvor financed a long operation on an uncertain outcome. And there was no security mechanism to make sure he was eventually paid.

2. What SCOPIC is

The Special Compensation P&I Clause is a contractual clause agreed between the International Salvage Union, the International Group of P&I Clubs and property underwriters, first effective 1 August 1999 and revised since; the version current at this examination is SCOPIC 2020, in effect from 1 January 2020. It is supplementary to a Lloyd's Form salvage agreement and, once incorporated and invoked, it substitutes its own method of assessment for Article 14(1) to (4). It is not part of the Convention and not law: it works because the parties agree to it.

3. How it answers each of the salvor's difficulties

  • The threshold argument disappears. By sub-clause 2 the salvor may invoke SCOPIC by written notice at any time of his choosing, regardless of the circumstances and in particular regardless of whether there is a threat of damage to the environment. Assessment runs from the notice, so there is a real incentive to invoke early.
  • Remuneration becomes calculable, not litigable. Sub-clause 5 replaces "expenses" with a pre-agreed tariff for personnel, tugs, craft and portable equipment on a time-and-materials basis, plus out-of-pocket expenses, plus a standard bonus of 25 per cent of those rates. The bonus is the profit element the courts held Article 14 did not provide.
  • He is secured. By sub-clause 3(i) owners must provide a bank guarantee or P&I Club letter for US$3 million within 2 working days of the notice; the sum may be increased or reduced as the job develops. If it is not provided, sub-clause 4(i) lets the salvor withdraw from SCOPIC and revert to Article 14.
  • Facts are recorded as they happen. Sub-clause 12 provides for a Special Casualty Representative, appointed by the owners and attending the casualty, who reports on the salvage as it proceeds. An agreed contemporaneous record removes most of what arbitration used to be spent arguing about.
  • He is paid before final assessment. Sub-clause 8 requires 75 per cent of the excess of assessed SCOPIC remuneration over the Article 13 security to be paid within 1 month, with interest at US prime plus 1 per cent.

Two balancing provisions stop the clause being abused, and naming them is what separates a good answer from a recitation. By sub-clause 6(i) the Article 13 assessment continues, and SCOPIC remuneration is payable by the shipowner alone and only to the extent it exceeds the Article 13 award - so the salvor gets the better of the two, not both. And by sub-clause 7, if the Article 13 award turns out to be the greater, that award is discounted by 25 per cent of the difference. The salvor therefore pays for the safety net if it turns out he did not need it, which is what keeps SCOPIC for the cases it was meant for.

4. (i) Disciplinary action against the master or crew

The right belongs to the flag State of the ship, or to the State of nationality of the person concerned - and to no one else. The authority is UNCLOS Article 97(1): in the event of a collision or any other incident of navigation concerning a ship on the high seas, involving the penal or disciplinary responsibility of the master or of any other person in the service of the ship, no penal or disciplinary proceedings may be instituted against such person except before the judicial or administrative authorities either of the flag State or of the State of which such person is a national.

The justification is the legal character of the high seas. No State has territorial sovereignty there, so the only connecting factors are the flag - the ship being subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State under Article 92, whose duties of effective jurisdiction and control are set out in Article 94 - and the nationality of the individual. The rule is also a deliberate historical correction: it reverses the result of the Lotus case, in which a State was held entitled to try the officer of a foreign ship for a high-seas collision, and it was first put into treaty form in the Brussels Convention 1952 on Penal Jurisdiction in Matters of Collision. Article 97(3) completes the protection: no arrest or detention of the ship, even as a measure of investigation, may be ordered by any authorities other than those of the flag State.

5. (ii) Cancellation of certificates of competency

Only the State which issued the certificate may cancel it. UNCLOS Article 97(2) is explicit: in disciplinary matters, only the State which issued a master's certificate or a certificate of competence or licence is competent, after due legal process, to pronounce the withdrawal of such certificates, even if the holder is not a national of that State.

This is a narrower rule than limb (i) and must not be merged with it. For proceedings generally there are two candidate States, flag or nationality; for withdrawal of a certificate there is exactly one, the issuing administration - and the closing words settle the case the examiner is really testing, where an officer holds the certificate of a State he is not a national of. Note also what is not restricted: the words are "withdrawal of such certificates". A flag State that has issued an endorsement recognising a foreign certificate may withdraw its own endorsement, and a port State may act against the ship under its own port State control powers. Neither cancels the certificate.

6. (iii) Payment of compensation

This limb is different in kind and the mark lies in saying so. Compensation is a matter of civil liability between the parties, not of a State's right to act, and UNCLOS Article 97 does not touch it. The applicable convention is the Collision Convention 1910 - the Brussels Convention for the unification of certain rules of law with respect to collisions between vessels - whose salient points are these:

  • If the collision is accidental, or caused by force majeure, or its cause is left in doubt, the damages are borne by those who have suffered them.
  • If caused by the fault of one vessel, that vessel bears the loss.
  • If both are at fault, liability is in proportion to the degree of fault of each; where the degrees cannot be established, or the fault appears equal, liability is divided equally. This replaced the older English rule of equal division in every case of mutual fault.
  • There is no legal presumption of fault from the collision itself; fault must be proved.
  • For loss of life and personal injury the vessels at fault are jointly and severally liable to the victims, with a right of recourse between them; for property damage liability is only proportionate, so a cargo owner recovers from each ship only its share.

The forum is then a separate question, governed by the Brussels Convention 1952 on Civil Jurisdiction in Matters of Collision - broadly, the court of the defendant's residence or place of business, or where the vessel has been arrested or security given, or where the collision occurred if that was in a port or inland waters. In practice the claimant chooses by arresting the ship. Two riders complete the answer: the total payable may then be capped by the LLMC regime, and both Conventions apply only between States party to them, so the position turns on the ratifications of the two flag States - which this question does not identify. Where India is the forum, the collision provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act 1958, in force at this examination, give domestic effect.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints no marks between (a) and (b), so the safe reading is roughly half each, with limb (b)'s eight marks split three ways. That matters, because limb (b) has three printed sub-limbs and each needs a named State, a justification and a convention. An answer that discusses collision liability generally and never answers "which State" has not answered the question asked.

The one provision that answers two-thirds of limb (b)

UNCLOS Article 97. Sub-article (1) gives limb (i) - flag State or State of nationality. Sub-article (2) gives limb (ii) - only the issuing State, even for a non-national. Sub-article (3) gives the bonus point that no other State may even arrest the ship as a measure of investigation. If you learn one thing for this question, learn Article 97.

Keep the three limbs in different legal categories

  • (i) is penal and disciplinary jurisdiction - a State punishing a person. UNCLOS restricts it to two States.
  • (ii) is a narrower disciplinary power - withdrawing a certificate. UNCLOS restricts it to one State, the issuer.
  • (iii) is civil liability - one shipowner paying another. UNCLOS says nothing about it; the 1910 Convention apportions it and the 1952 Convention decides where the case is heard.
  • Candidates lose marks by giving the same answer three times. The examiner has deliberately built a question where the answer narrows from (i) to (ii) and then changes category at (iii).

Common mistakes

  • Saying the coastal State, or the State of the other ship, may prosecute. On the high seas neither may.
  • Saying the flag State may cancel any certificate. It may cancel the ones it issued, and its own endorsement of a foreign certificate - not the foreign certificate itself.
  • Treating compensation as a jurisdictional question with a single answer. It is a civil claim; the real questions are apportionment and forum.
  • Saying liability is always divided equally when both are at fault. That is the pre-1910 rule; the Convention apportions by degree of fault, and divides equally only where the degrees cannot be established.
  • In limb (a), describing SCOPIC as part of the Salvage Convention. It is a contractual clause supplementary to a Lloyd's Form.
  • Saying SCOPIC pays in addition to the Article 13 award. It is payable only to the extent it exceeds it.

Examiner traps

  • "On the high seas" is doing all the work in limb (b). Inside a territorial sea the coastal State's criminal jurisdiction is engaged and the answer changes completely. Say why the location matters.
  • "With justification" is an instruction, not decoration. Each sub-limb needs the reason - exclusive flag State jurisdiction, the issuing administration's competence, the civil character of a compensation claim.
  • The 25 per cent in SCOPIC appears twice with opposite effects: the bonus on the tariff and the discount on the Article 13 award. Mixing them up is a common and visible error.
  • The stem asks how SCOPIC solves "the difficulties of salvors" - so the Article 14 problems must be stated before the cure, or the cure has nothing to bite on.

Model written answer

1. What the Convention is, and the bargain it strikes

The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 - the LLMC - was adopted on 19 November 1976 and entered into force on 1 December 1986. Its 1996 Protocol, in force 13 May 2004, substantially raised the limits and introduced a tacit acceptance procedure for raising them again; amendments adopted under it in 2012 took effect on 8 June 2015. India gives effect to the regime through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act 1958, which is the statute in force at this examination.

The Convention's purpose is to allow a shipowner to limit his total liability arising out of a single occurrence to a fund calculated by reference to the tonnage of the ship, irrespective of the actual amount of the loss. The policy behind it is commercial rather than moral: maritime adventure exposes an owner to potential liabilities out of all proportion to the value of the vessel and the freight, and without a cap the risk would be uninsurable at an acceptable premium. The Convention therefore buys certainty and insurability for the owner, and in exchange gives the claimant a fund that is almost unbreakable but almost automatic: by Article 2 the listed claims are subject to limitation "whatever the basis of liability may be", so the owner does not have to prove absence of fault to rely on it.

2. The two heads of claim under Article 6

Article 6 sets the general limits, and it does so under two separate heads, each with its own fund:

  1. claims for loss of life or personal injury; and
  2. any other claims - in practice the property head, covering damage to ships, harbour works, basins, waterways and aids to navigation, loss of or damage to property on board, and consequential loss.

The two funds are distinct and are not interchangeable: a property claimant cannot reach into the personal-injury fund. Their significance is the deliberate priority given to life - the life and injury limit is set materially higher, and where that fund is insufficient the balance of those claims ranks rateably with property claims against the other fund. Both limits rise in steps with tonnage.

One refinement should be stated to show precision. Claims by passengers of a ship for loss of life or personal injury are dealt with separately, under Article 7, by a limit calculated on the number of passengers the ship is certified to carry rather than on tonnage. So the two heads asked for are the Article 6 heads; the passenger limit sits alongside them and is not one of them.

3. Who may limit - and what cannot be limited at all

Article 1 answers the question directly. The persons entitled to limit are shipowners and salvors, and the Convention defines both widely:

  • "Shipowner" means the owner, charterer, manager and operator of a seagoing ship. A bareboat or time charterer may therefore limit in his own right.
  • A "salvor" is any person rendering services in direct connexion with salvage operations. This was added deliberately in 1976 to close the gap exposed where a salvage master working in the water, and not from a vessel, was held unable to limit under the earlier regime.
  • Any person for whose act, neglect or default the shipowner or salvor is responsible - master, crew, servants and agents - may invoke the same limits.
  • An insurer of liability for claims subject to limitation is entitled to the benefits of the Convention to the same extent as the assured, which is what makes direct action against the club workable.

The counterpart is Article 3: certain claims are excepted from limitation altogether. They include claims for salvage and for contribution in general average, oil pollution claims falling within the CLC regime, nuclear damage claims, and claims by the servants of the shipowner or salvor whose contracts of service are governed by a law that does not permit limitation. Note the position of salvage on both sides of the line: a salvor may limit his own liability, but a claim for salvage cannot be limited against.

4. Plaintiff and Defendant

Plaintiff. The party who commences a civil action, asserting a right and asking the court for a remedy, and who carries the burden of proving his case on the balance of probabilities. In modern English procedure the term has largely been replaced by "claimant"; in Indian civil procedure the plaintiff is the party who files the plaint. In a limitation context he is typically the cargo owner, the injured seafarer or the port authority claiming against the fund.

Defendant. The party against whom the action is brought, who must answer the claim and who may raise defences, set-offs and counterclaims. In this context he is typically the shipowner, and it is the defendant who invokes limitation. The distinction matters procedurally: limitation is not something the court applies of its own motion, it is relied on by the party sued, and in most jurisdictions it is pursued by constituting a limitation fund and seeking a decree limiting liability.

5. Privity - and why it is the old test

Privity means knowledge of and concurrence in the act or condition complained of. A person is privy to something if he knew of it and assented to it, expressly or by turning a blind eye - it is more than negligence and less than intention, and it is personal to him, not something imputed from the acts of his servants.

Its importance is historical and it should be presented that way. Under the regime that preceded the LLMC, an owner lost his right to limit if the loss occurred with his "actual fault or privity". That test was comparatively easy for a claimant to satisfy: proof that the owner knew the ship was undermanned or that a defect had been reported to the office could break the limit, and limitation actions were routinely fought on it.

Article 4 of the LLMC deliberately replaced it with a far harder test. A person loses the right to limit only if the loss resulted from his personal act or omission, committed with the intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result. The burden is on the claimant, the conduct must be personal - the alter ego of the company, not the master - and recklessness must be coupled with actual knowledge of the probable consequence. That is why the modern limit is described as almost unbreakable, and it is the single most important thing to say about privity: the word belongs to the old law, and quoting it as the current test is the error the examiner is looking for.

6. Unlimited liability

Unlimited liability is liability that is not capped by any limitation regime, so that the claimant may recover the full amount of his proven loss however large. Under this Convention it arises by three distinct routes, and a good answer separates them: first, where the Article 4 conduct test is satisfied, so the right to limit is lost; second, where the claim is excepted by Article 3, so the right never applied to it - salvage and general average contribution are the everyday examples; and third, where the Convention does not govern at all, because the forum State is not party to it or the liability arises under a separate regime with its own rules. The practical consequence is the one the Chief Engineer meets: it is the owner's own knowledge and decisions - what the office was told, what was recorded, what was authorised - that put the limit at risk, and that is why defect reporting and the ship-to-shore record matter far beyond the engineering department.

  • Passengers are Article 7 and are separate. Naming them as one of the two heads is wrong; naming them as a distinct additional limit is a mark.

Common mistakes

  • Treating limitation as a denial of liability. It is an admission of liability with a cap.

  • Forgetting that salvage and general average contribution cannot be limited against, while a salvor himself may limit.

Understand this first

General average is not insurance and not liability - it is a rule of contribution, and it is one of the oldest ideas in maritime law. The problem it solves is a problem of incentives at the worst moment: if the master must sacrifice one interest to save the whole adventure, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, the owner of that interest would be ruined for everyone else's benefit and would fight the decision. Making the loss shared removes the conflict. Everything in limb (a) follows from that purpose - the sacrifice must be deliberate (or there was no decision to protect), for the common safety (or there is nothing to share), and reasonable (or the decision does not deserve protection). And limb (b) is simply that same test applied to an engine: damage suffered because the master chose to risk the machinery to save the ship is a sacrifice; damage suffered in the ordinary course is not.

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

The statement in the question is correct, and the features are not a matter of opinion: they are drawn from the definition itself. Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules provides that there is a general average act 'when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure'.

Note first how the Rules apply. They are not law: they take effect by incorporation into the contract of carriage - the bill of lading or charterparty - and the Rule of Interpretation then makes them apply 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. In India the concept is also recognised in the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

2. The essential features

Every phrase of Rule A is doing work, and each yields one essential feature:

  1. Extraordinary. The sacrifice or expenditure must be outside the ordinary performance of the contract of carriage. Fuel burned on a normal passage, or wear on machinery in ordinary service, is not extraordinary however costly.
  2. Intentional and voluntary. The loss must be deliberately incurred. Cargo washed overboard by a sea is accidental and lies where it falls; cargo jettisoned by decision is a sacrifice. This is the single feature that most sharply separates general from particular average.
  3. Reasonable. Reinforced by the Rule Paramount, which overrides everything else: 'In no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.'
  4. Peril. There must be real danger - not necessarily immediate, but genuine and substantial, and more than the ordinary risk of the sea.
  5. Common safety and common adventure. The act must be for the benefit of the whole adventure, not one interest alone, and there must be more than one interest at risk. A ship in ballast with no cargo on board has no common adventure and can have no general average.
  6. Preservation of property. The purpose must be to preserve the property from that peril. Success matters: there must be something saved to contribute.

3. The lettered rules that qualify the definition

  • Rule C - only losses which are the direct consequence of the general average act are allowed. It expressly excludes demurrage, loss of market, loss caused by delay and any indirect loss whatsoever, and excludes environmental damage and the consequences of escaping pollutants.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected even though the event was due to the fault of one of the parties, without prejudice to any remedies or defences in respect of that fault. So the adjustment proceeds first, and blame is fought separately.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming, who must give notice in writing to the average adjuster and supply particulars, generally within twelve months.

4. How the loss is shared, and what general average is not

Under Rule A.2 the sacrifice is borne by the different contributing interests - ship, cargo and freight at risk - rateably in proportion to their values at the termination of the adventure. The adjustment is prepared by an average adjuster, and security is taken before cargo is released, normally an average bond from the receiver and an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter.

Two distinctions complete the picture. General average is not particular average: particular average is accidental damage, falls on the interest that suffers it and is not shared. And general average is not insurance: it is a rule of contribution between the parties to the adventure, which predates marine insurance entirely, though the contribution is in practice recoverable under the policy.

5. b) The refloating scenario - the rule that governs it

This situation is dealt with expressly, and by a rule written for exactly these facts. Rule VII, 'Damage to Machinery and Boilers', provides:

'Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.'

6. Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not

Yes, the burnt bearings can be allowed in general average - provided four things are shown.

  1. The ship was ashore. She was aground, so the first condition is met on the facts given.
  2. She was in a position of peril. This must be established, not assumed. A vessel lying safely aground on soft mud in fine weather, in no danger and awaiting the tide, is not in peril; one working on a hard bottom, or exposed to weather, tide or the risk of breaking her back, is. This is the condition on which the claim will actually turn.
  3. The machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety. Running the engine ahead and astern to shake her off is precisely that - a deliberate act directed at the safety of the whole adventure, not a routine manoeuvre.
  4. The damage arose at the risk of such damage. The master must have accepted the risk of damaging the engine as the price of refloating. Working an engine hard, astern and ahead, against a grounded hull is known to overload the bearings, and it is that knowing acceptance that makes the loss a sacrifice rather than an accident.

The damage then satisfies Rule A as well: it is extraordinary (not ordinary service), intentional (the engine was deliberately worked in a way likely to damage it), reasonable if refloating was a sensible response, and directed at the common safety. It is also the direct consequence of the act, as Rule C requires. Under Rule D it makes no difference to the right of contribution that the grounding may have been somebody's fault.

The converse must be stated, because it is the trap. Rule VII closes with an absolute prohibition: where the ship is afloat, damage from working the propelling machinery is 'in no circumstances' allowed. So if she had already come off and the bearings burnt while manoeuvring clear, or if she were merely touching and not in peril, the same physical damage would be particular average - the owner's own loss, recoverable if at all under the hull policy and not shared with cargo. The engine damage is identical; the legal answer is opposite; and what decides it is the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is a 'discuss the statement' question, and the highest-scoring way to discuss it is to show that the essential features are not a list somebody invented but the clauses of the Rule A definition read one at a time. That converts a memory answer into an analytical one. Limb (b) is worth the same 8 marks and is decided by a single rule, so the answer must quote Rule VII, apply its four conditions to the facts, and then state the converse. An answer that says 'yes, it is a sacrifice' without naming the rule or testing peril has taken about half the marks available.

Why general average exists at all

It is far older than marine insurance and older than most maritime law - the principle is found in the Rhodian sea law and passed through Roman law into every modern maritime system. The problem it solves is one of incentives at the worst possible moment. If a master must jettison cargo to save the ship, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, then whoever owned that particular cargo would bear everything while the rest of the adventure was saved at his expense. Nobody would consent, and the master's decision would be contested at the moment when hesitation is most dangerous. Making the loss a shared loss removes the conflict: every interest knows that a sacrifice made for the common safety will be contributed to by all. Seen that way, general average is a rule about decision-making under peril, not really a rule about money.

Sacrifice versus accident - the line that decides everything

Almost every general average problem in an examination is a disguised question about whether the loss was chosen. Cargo swept overboard by a sea is an accident; cargo jettisoned is a sacrifice. An engine that fails on passage is an accident; an engine deliberately worked to destruction to refloat the ship is a sacrifice. Water damage from a leak is an accident; water damage from firefighting is a sacrifice. Rule VII is simply that same line drawn for one specific case - and the drafters drew it so firmly that they added an absolute prohibition for the afloat case, because otherwise every hard manoeuvre would generate a claim.

The four conditions of Rule VII, and which one is really in issue

Ashore; in a position of peril; an actual intention to float her for the common safety; at the risk of such damage. In practice the first and third are usually obvious on the facts and the fourth follows from them. The condition that is actually litigated is peril. A ship aground is not automatically in peril - the question is whether she was in real danger, having regard to the bottom, the weather, the tide, her condition and the exposure of the position. That is why a careful answer says the claim 'turns on establishing peril' rather than simply asserting it, and why the master's log, weather records and any surveyor's evidence matter so much afterwards.

Which version of the Rules applies

The York-Antwerp Rules are contractual, not statutory. They apply because the bill of lading or charterparty says they do, which is why the Rule of Interpretation has to declare that they operate 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. The consequence is that the version in force between the parties may be the 1994, 2004 or 2016 text, depending on what the contract incorporates. On this question it does not matter: Rule VII is materially identical in all three. On other questions it can matter a great deal, so the habit of asking which version is incorporated is worth forming.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the features without tying them to Rule A, which is where they come from.
  • Omitting the requirement of a common adventure - with no cargo on board there is nothing to contribute and no general average.
  • Forgetting the Rule Paramount. Even a textbook sacrifice is disallowed if unreasonably made.
  • Answering limb (b) 'yes' without naming Rule VII or testing whether the ship was in peril.
  • Missing the afloat prohibition, which is the other half of the rule and the source of the marks for a complete answer.
  • Saying the owner's fault in grounding the ship defeats the claim. Rule D says it does not, without prejudice to separate remedies.
  • Confusing general average with particular average, or treating general average as a form of insurance rather than of contribution.

Examiner traps

  • 'General average is based on certain essential features' - the word essential invites a definitional answer. Give the features as conditions, each of which must be satisfied.
  • 'Discuss the statement' - agreement plus explanation. Do not merely define general average.
  • 'Give justification to your answer' in limb (b) - the justification is the marks. A bare yes or no scores almost nothing.
  • The facts say 'ahead and astern directions ... to shake the vessel loose'. That wording is doing deliberate work: it establishes both the intention to refloat and the acceptance of risk, so quote it back.

Model written answer

1. (a) The mandatory basis in SOLAS and MARPOL

  • SOLAS regulation XI-1/6 is the operative provision. Adopted by resolution MSC.257(84), it makes Parts I and II of the Casualty Investigation Code mandatory and took effect on 1 January 2010 - converting investigation from an undertaking into an obligation.
  • SOLAS regulation I/21 is the older and broader provision: each Administration undertakes to conduct an investigation of any casualty occurring to its ships subject to the Convention when it judges that the investigation may assist in identifying what changes to the Convention might be desirable. Its paragraph (b) completes the regulation and is the limb candidates omit: each Contracting Government undertakes to supply the Organization with pertinent information concerning the findings of such investigations, and no reports or recommendations of the Organization based upon that information may disclose the identity or nationality of the ships concerned, or in any manner fix or imply responsibility upon any ship or person. Note precisely where that duty sits - the non-disclosure rule binds IMO's own output, not the investigating Administration's report.
  • MARPOL Article 12 - Casualties to ships: each Administration undertakes to investigate any casualty occurring to its ships subject to the Convention where the casualty has produced a major deleterious effect upon the marine environment, and to supply the Organization with information concerning the findings.
  • Behind both sits UNCLOS Article 94(7), requiring an inquiry into marine casualties on the high seas involving loss of life, serious injury to another State's nationals, or serious damage to ships, installations or the environment. The Code cites SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7) together.

2. (b) The purpose and structure of the Code

The Code - resolution MSC.255(84) - provides a common approach to marine safety investigations. Its defining principle, and the thing to state first, is that a marine safety investigation is conducted to prevent future casualties, not to apportion blame or determine liability. It is separate from, and must not be prejudiced by, any criminal, disciplinary or civil proceedings. It is arranged in three parts:

  • Part I - General provisions (chapters 1 to 3): purpose, definitions and application.
  • Part II - Mandatory standards (from chapter 4): what a State Party must do.
  • Part III - Recommended practices (from chapter 15): what it should do.

3. Salient mandatory standards - Part II

  • An impartial Marine Safety Investigation Authority (chapter 4), properly resourced.
  • Notification (chapter 5) - ship name and flag, IMO number, nature, location, time and date, numbers seriously injured or killed, consequences to persons, property and environment, and any other ship involved.
  • A safety investigation into every very serious marine casualty (chapter 6.1), with the flag State responsible for ensuring it is conducted and completed (6.2).
  • Agreement between substantially interested States (chapter 7) - the flag State may agree that another State conducts the investigation, and participating in it discharges its obligations under the Code, SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7).
  • Powers of investigation, parallel investigations and co-operation (chapters 8 to 10).
  • Independence (chapter 11) - the investigation shall not be subject to external direction.
  • Obtaining evidence from seafarers (chapter 12) - they are informed of the nature and basis of the investigation and may be legally represented.
  • Draft reports (chapter 13) circulated to substantially interested States for comment, normally within 30 days.
  • Reports (chapter 14) - the final report of every very serious marine casualty investigation is submitted to the Organization, and so is any other report containing information that may prevent future casualties.

4. Salient recommended practices - Part III

  • Administrative responsibilities and principles of investigation (chapters 15 and 16), and guidance on investigating casualties and incidents.
  • Confidentiality of marine safety records - statements, communications, medical and private information, analyses and voyage data recorder information - so that people speak freely to investigators.
  • Protection for witnesses and involved parties, notification to parties involved, co-ordination, evidence collection, and the re-opening of an investigation where significant new evidence emerges.

5. (c) Very serious marine casualty

The Code defines it at paragraph 2.22: "A very serious marine casualty means a marine casualty involving the total loss of the ship or a death or severe damage to the environment." Three limbs, any one of which is sufficient. Reading it needs the supporting definitions: a marine casualty (2.9) is an event, or sequence of events, resulting directly in connection with the operation of a ship in death or serious injury, loss of a person overboard, loss, presumed loss or abandonment of a ship, or material damage; a serious injury (2.18) is one causing incapacitation for more than 72 hours commencing within seven days of the injury; and severe damage to the environment (2.19) is damage which, as evaluated by the affected State or the flag State, produces a major deleterious effect upon the environment.

The classification matters because it triggers obligations: a safety investigation is mandatory for every very serious marine casualty, and the final report must go to IMO. Note also that the Code defines only this term - the related expression "serious marine casualty" appears nowhere in it and comes from IMO's separate reporting guidance.

Common mistakes

  • Giving only the SOLAS provision when the question asks for SOLAS and MARPOL. MARPOL Article 12 is the one - an Article, not a regulation in an Annex.
  • Confusing "very serious marine casualty" with "serious marine casualty". Only the first is defined in the Code; the second comes from IMO's reporting guidance and is not in MSC.255(84) at all.
  • Writing that the purpose of an investigation is to find who was at fault. It is the opposite, and it is the single most important point in the question.
  • Getting the serious-injury definition loosely. It is incapacitation for more than 72 hours, commencing within seven days of the injury.
  • Treating the whole Code as mandatory. Parts I and II are mandatory through SOLAS XI-1/6; Part III is recommended practice, and the question asks about both.

Examiner traps

"A crew member died - is that a very serious marine casualty?" Yes. The three limbs are total loss of the ship, or a death, or severe damage to the environment - any one is sufficient, and a death alone qualifies.

"Who investigates if the ship is Indian-flagged but the casualty is in another State's waters?" The flag State is responsible, but chapter 7 allows agreement with another substantially interested State to conduct it, and participation in that investigation discharges the flag State's obligations under the Code, SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7).

"Can the investigation report be used in court?" The Code makes safety records confidential and keeps the safety investigation separate from criminal, disciplinary and civil proceedings, precisely so that evidence is given freely. That separation is a design feature, not an oversight.

Model written answer

1. A) What the human element means in STCW

STCW refuses to treat competence as something that time served automatically confers. It replaces experience with demonstrated competence, and then addresses the conditions - fitness, rest, teamwork and company responsibility - under which competence can actually be exercised. The instrument is the STCW Convention and Code, 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force from 1 January 2012.

2. Competence-based standards

  • The Part A tables of competence specify, for every function and level, the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods for demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard, not an examiner's impression.
  • Part A is mandatory; Part B is guidance - a distinction worth stating explicitly.
  • The Manila amendments added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking at operational level and leadership and managerial skill at management level - where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention. It is the explicit recognition that technically competent individuals still fail as a team.
  • Regulation I/6 governs training and assessment, and regulation I/8 requires a quality standards system, so the training system itself is audited rather than assumed.
  • Regulation I/11 requires periodic revalidation, and chapter VI mandatory safety familiarisation and basic training before a seafarer is assigned duties.

3. Fitness for duty - the physical human being

  • Regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require that watch systems are arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The Manila-amended A-VIII/1 minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Authorised exceptions are tightly conditioned: weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours in any 7-day period, exceptions from the 77-hour norm may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • The Manila amendments also introduced alcohol limits for masters, officers and other seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards, and regulation VIII/2 with section A-VIII/2 governs watchkeeping arrangements and principles.

4. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring that seafarers hold valid certificates, are familiarised with their duties and with the ship's arrangements, and that documentation is maintained - so the human element is not left as an individual's problem. Administrations operate quality standards systems and are subject to independent evaluation and reporting to IMO.

5. B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The guidance is MSC.1/Circ.1598, 'Guidelines on fatigue', of 24 January 2019, approved at the 100th session of the Maritime Safety Committee and finalized by the HTW Sub-Committee. It supersedes MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, not mandatory - the binding rest-hour requirements remain in STCW chapter VIII and in MLC 2006. The Guidelines are arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party:

  • Module 1 - Fatigue: what it is and its causes, grouped as sleep and rest, the biological clock and circadian rhythm, health and stress, environmental factors such as noise, vibration and motion, sleep disorders and operational factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods.
  • Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company: assessing, mitigating and managing fatigue risk in the safety management system, in manning decisions and in scheduling.
  • Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer: practical guidance for the master, officers and ratings on sleep quality, diet and the limits of countermeasures - notably that caffeine is not a substitute for sleep.
  • Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training.
  • Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design: mitigation designed in - accommodation location, noise, vibration, lighting and habitability.
  • Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities: how regulators and inspectors should approach it.

6. How the guidance and the rules fit together

The Guidelines are explicit that hours of rest are set by a prescriptive formula in STCW chapter VIII, and that complying with the formula is not the same as being rested: managers must allow for circadian rhythm and sleep debt, and - in the Guidelines' own words - rest means rest, not the substitution of a different form of work. The same theme runs through MLC 2006 Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7. A management-level engineer should therefore treat recorded compliance as the floor, and a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant as a warning rather than a reassurance.

Model written answer

1. What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship

The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 gives no definition of unseaworthiness in a definitions section. The description sits inside section 334 itself, which treats a ship as unseaworthy when the following are not such as to render her in every respect fit for the proposed voyage or service:

  • the materials of which she is made and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, boilers and machinery.

Two features carry the marks. The test is relative - fitness for the proposed voyage or service - so a ship adequate for a sheltered coastal run may be unseaworthy for an ocean passage. And it is far wider than material condition: an undermanned ship, one whose officers hold the wrong certificates, or a mechanically perfect ship with badly stowed cargo is unseaworthy under the Act.

2. The offence, and the defences the Act allows

  • s.334(1) - every person who sends or attempts to send an Indian ship to sea from any port in India in such an unseaworthy state that the life of any person is likely to be thereby endangered is guilty of an offence, unless he proves he used all reasonable means to insure her being sent to sea in a seaworthy state, or that sending her to sea in that state was, in the circumstances, reasonable and justifiable.
  • s.334(2) - a master who knowingly takes such a ship to sea commits the offence, with only the second defence available to him.
  • s.334(3) permits a person charged to give evidence in his own defence; s.334(4) provides that no prosecution shall be instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government.

Liability is therefore not automatic. The threshold is danger to life, the burden of the defence lies on the accused, and the standard demanded is reasonable means rather than a guarantee of result.

3. What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her

Section 336 is directed at a different problem - stopping the ship rather than punishing anybody. An unsafe ship is one which, by reason of the defective condition of her hull, equipment or machinery, or by reason of overloading or improper loading, is unfit to proceed to sea without serious danger to human life, having regard to the nature of the service for which she is intended. The procedure is staged:

  1. the Central Government, having reason to believe on complaint or otherwise that she is unsafe, may order her provisionally detained for the purpose of survey;
  2. a written statement of the grounds must be served forthwith on the master;
  3. the matter is referred to the court of survey for the port, or a competent person is appointed to survey the ship and report;
  4. on that report she is either released or finally detained.

4. The real difference between the two

  • Unseaworthy is a state; unsafe is the trigger for a power. Section 334 describes a condition and attaches criminal liability to sending a ship to sea in it. Section 336 describes a condition that entitles the Government to stop her.
  • One looks backwards, the other forwards. The offence punishes a decision already taken; detention prevents the voyage from happening at all.
  • Unseaworthiness is the wider concept - it expressly takes in crew numbers and qualifications and the stowage of cargo and ballast. The unsafe-ship definition lists a narrower set of causes.
  • The thresholds differ - life likely to be endangered under s.334; unfitness to proceed without serious danger to human life under s.336.
  • In practice, a ship unsafe within s.336 will invariably be unseaworthy within s.334, but a ship may be unseaworthy - one certificated engineer short - without being unsafe.

5. The owner's obligation to the crew

Section 335 converts seaworthiness from a matter between owner and cargo into a duty owed to the people on board. In every contract of service, express or implied, between the owner of an Indian ship and the master or any seaman, and in every contract of apprenticeship, there is implied - notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary - an obligation that the owner, the master and every agent charged with the loading of the ship, or preparing her for sea, or sending her to sea shall use all reasonable means to ensure the seaworthiness of the ship for the voyage at the time when the voyage commences, and shall keep her in a seaworthy state during the voyage.

Three consequences follow. The duty is non-excludable - a term in the crew agreement waiving it is void. It is a due diligence standard, not a warranty of result. And it is continuing throughout the voyage rather than being discharged at departure. It also binds people other than the registered owner: the loading agent is named in the section.

6. How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force at this sitting

Section 335(2) allows the Central Government, on the owner's request or otherwise, to arrange a survey of the hull, equipment or machinery. In practice the duty is policed through the wider system - class and flag State statutory survey and certification, ISM auditing, and port State control under SOLAS chapter I regulation 19 - with the section 336 detention power as the last resort.

One point of currency should be stated rather than assumed. The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 was the operative law on the examination date, and there was at this sitting no other candidate for that role. The Merchant Shipping Bill, 2024 - the Bill that became the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 - had been introduced in the Lok Sabha on 10 December 2024 and was still before Parliament: it was passed by the Lok Sabha only on 6 August 2025 and by the Rajya Sabha on 11 August 2025, received assent on 18 August 2025 as Act No. 24 of 2025, and was brought into force only on 15 March 2026, when section 324(1) repealed the 1958 Act. A Bill is not an Act, and assent is not commencement. Every one of those steps came after this paper was sat, so the answer is correctly and necessarily given on the 1958 Act.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem asks three questions and prints no limbs, so the examiner has no printed structure to mark against and will mark against the three questions actually asked. Answering them in the order asked, with the middle one made explicitly comparative, is what makes the script easy to mark. The comparison in step 4 is the highest-value paragraph in the answer and the one most often missing: candidates define both terms and then never say how they differ, which is literally what was asked.

Why the Act splits the idea in two

A criminal offence needs fault, a trial and proof beyond reasonable doubt, and it operates after the event - which is no use at all to the crew of a ship that is about to sail. An administrative detention power needs only a reasonable belief and can act within hours. The 1958 Act therefore does both: section 334 attaches consequences to a decision, and section 336 supplies the fast preventive lever, with the survey and the right to be told the grounds as the safeguards against its misuse. Seeing that the two sections exist to solve different problems is the whole of the distinction.

Seaworthiness has three different legal lives - keep them apart

  • Statutory and criminal - MSA 1958 s.334, which is this question. A state of the ship, punishable, with a due-diligence defence.
  • Contractual, owed to the crew - MSA 1958 s.335. Implied, non-excludable, and continuing through the voyage.
  • Insurance and carriage - a warranty or a duty of due diligence depending on whether the policy is a voyage or a time policy and on the contract of carriage. Under Indian law that is the Marine Insurance Act 1963, not the English Act of 1906, and a breach affects recovery rather than attracting a penalty.

The examiner asks about the first two. Answering about the third is the commonest way to lose this question.

A Bill is not an Act - and this sitting proves it

A candidate sitting this paper in June 2025 could easily have read about the new Merchant Shipping Bill and assumed the 1958 Act was on its way out. It was, but it had not gone. The Merchant Shipping Bill, 2024 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 10 December 2024 and, at this examination, had been passed by neither House. It cleared the Lok Sabha on 6 August 2025, the Rajya Sabha on 11 August 2025, received the assent of the President on 18 August 2025, and even then did not become law: an Indian Act ordinarily commences on a date the Central Government appoints by notification in the Official Gazette, and here that notification, S.O. 1244(E), appointed 15 March 2026. So the ladder has four rungs - introduced, passed, assented, commenced - and in June 2025 the Bill was still on the first. That is the same staged problem Q2 of this paper examines for an international convention, where adoption, ratification and entry into force are three different things, and the two are worth revising together.

Common mistakes

  • Describing seaworthiness in general engineering terms without quoting the section 334 formula.
  • Leaving crew numbers, certification and stowage out of the definition - they are expressly inside it.
  • Treating unseaworthy and unsafe as interchangeable, or saying the difference is only one of degree.
  • Saying the owner guarantees seaworthiness. The duty is to use all reasonable means.
  • Forgetting that the section 335 duty continues during the voyage.
  • Answering under the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 or the doctrine of stages.
  • Answering under the Merchant Shipping Act 2025. At this sitting it was not merely uncommenced - it had not been passed by either House of Parliament.

Examiner traps

  • 'Within the meaning of the Merchant Shipping Act (MSA), 1958, as amended' - the examiner is asking for the statutory meaning, and a general definition cannot reach the top band.
  • 'What according to you is the difference' invites an opinion, but the marks are for the legal difference, not a preference.
  • 'Obligations of owners to crew' - section 335 also binds the master and the loading agent, so an answer confined to the registered owner is incomplete.
  • The printed words 'with respect seaworthiness' are incomplete on the paper. Read them as 'with respect to seaworthiness' and answer the obvious demand; do not waste time on the typography.

Salvage Convention 1989 in force 14 July 1996; Article 14 gives expenses plus up to 30%, raisable to 100% of expenses if fair and just		 Giving the same State for all three sub-limbs of (b). The answer narrows from two States, to one State, and then changes category entirely at compensation, which is civil and not a question of a State's right at all. Q6 General Average - Essential Features and a Refloating Claim
16 marks		 a) What a general average act is → The essential features → The lettered rules that qualify the definition → How the loss is shared, and what general average is not → b) The refloating scenario - the rule that governs it → Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not York-Antwerp Rules Rule A with the Rule Paramount for limb (a), and Rule VII, Damage to Machinery and Boilers, for limb (b).
York-Antwerp Rules, Rule A - the definition of a general average act		 Answering limb (b) 'yes, it is a general average sacrifice' without naming Rule VII, without testing whether the ship was in a position of peril, and without stating the converse. Rule VII allows the claim only where the ship is ashore and in peril and the machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety at the risk of such damage - and it then prohibits absolutely, 'in any circumstances', any allowance for machinery damage caused by working the propelling machinery while the ship is afloat. The same burnt bearings are therefore general average or particular average according to the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked. A second trap in limb (a) is forgetting that there must be a common adventure: a ship in ballast with no cargo has no general average, because there is no other interest to contribute.

Q8 Human Element in STCW and IMO Fatigue Guidance
16 marks		 A) What the human element means in STCW → Competence-based standards → Fitness for duty - the physical human being → Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations → B) The IMO guidance on fatigue → How the guidance and the rules fit together STCW regulation VIII/1 with section A-VIII/1 for fitness for duty and hours of rest, and regulation I/14 for company responsibility; with MSC.1/Circ.1598 as the IMO guidance on fatigue.
Manila amendments in force 1 January 2012		 Quoting 70 hours as the normal weekly rest requirement. The norm is 77; 70 is the floor that applies only where an exception has been authorised. This error has a specific and dangerous source: a freely available IMO circular copy of section A-VIII/1, STCW.6/Circ.1, reproduces the pre-Manila 1995 text - five paragraphs, 70 hours weekly and no 14-hour interval provision at all. It looks official because it is official; it is simply the wrong edition. The second trap is treating MSC.1/Circ.1598 as mandatory - it is recommendatory, and the binding requirements are in STCW chapter VIII and MLC 2006. The third is answering limb B with general remarks about tiredness instead of naming the circular and its six modules.
Q9 Unseaworthy and Unsafe Ships under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958
16 marks		 What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship → The offence, and the defences the Act allows → What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her → The real difference between the two → The owner's obligation to the crew → How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force at this sitting Merchant Shipping Act 1958 sections 334, 335 and 336, which were the operative Indian law throughout June 2025 and remained so until 15 March 2026.
MSA 1958 s.334 unseaworthy ship; s.335 owner's obligation to crew; s.336 unsafe ship and detention		 Answering with the English common-law or marine-insurance idea of seaworthiness as a warranty in a contract of carriage. This question is on Indian statute, where unseaworthiness founds criminal liability and a non-excludable duty owed to the crew. The second trap is treating unseaworthy and unsafe as synonyms - they are a state and a power respectively, used by the Act for different purposes. The third is a currency trap in reverse: the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 is easy to reach for, but at this sitting it did not exist as an Act at all - the Bill was introduced in December 2024, was passed only in August 2025 and did not commence until 15 March 2026, so an answer given on it is answering under a statute that had not been enacted.
