Understand this first

An underwriter cannot inspect what he is insuring. He is quoting on a ship he has never seen, in a trade he is told about, with a history that only the owner holds. So the law does something it does not do for ordinary contracts: it makes silence a breach. The assured must volunteer everything a careful underwriter would want to know, before the contract is made, and if he does not, the insurer can unwind the policy from the beginning - even if the thing hidden had nothing whatever to do with the eventual claim. Everything in this question follows from that one asymmetry of knowledge. The four limbs are simply: why the rule exists, what it demands, where it stops, and what happens when it is broken.

Model written answer

1. The governing statute

This is an Indian examination, so the operative statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. It is closely modelled on the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 and much of the wording is common, but the 1963 Act is the one to cite. England reformed its own law by the Insurance Act 2015; that reform is English, it did not amend the Indian Act, and it does not govern here.

2. The principle of Uberrimae Fidei

Section 19: a contract of marine insurance is a contract based upon the utmost good faith, and if utmost good faith be not observed by either party, the contract may be avoided by the other party.

Three features give the principle its shape. The duty is mutual - section 19 says either party, so it binds the underwriter as well as the assured. It operates at the pre-contractual stage, because the underwriter must price a risk whose facts lie almost entirely with the proposer: the ship, her history, her trade and her management are known to the owner and not to the insurer. And it displaces the ordinary commercial rule of caveat emptor - in a marine policy silence about a material fact is itself a breach, whereas in an ordinary contract it usually is not.

3. Disclosures the assured must make

Section 20(1): before the contract is concluded the assured must disclose to the insurer every material circumstance which is known to the assured, and the assured is deemed to know every circumstance which in the ordinary course of business ought to be known to him. He cannot escape by not asking his own managers.

Section 20(2) supplies the test. A circumstance is material if it would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium, or in determining whether he will take the risk. Section 20(4) adds that materiality is in each case a question of fact, and section 20(5) that "circumstance" includes any communication made to, or information received by, the assured.

The duty is not confined to the assured personally. Section 21 imposes the same duty on an agent effecting the insurance, covering what the agent knows and what he ought to know; section 22 requires every material representation made during negotiation to be true.

In underwriting practice the circumstances disclosed typically include: the vessel's age, type, flag, class and standing with her society; ownership and technical management; the intended voyage, trading limits and any war-risk or sanctioned area; the nature, packing and stowage of the cargo; the loss and claims record; and known defects or outstanding conditions of class. (Practice, not statute - the Act states the test, not a list.)

4. Circumstances which need NOT be disclosed

Section 20(3) gives a closed list of four, and all four are subject to the opening words "in the absence of enquiry":

  1. any circumstance which diminishes the risk;
  2. any circumstance which is known or presumed to be known to the insurer - the insurer is presumed to know matters of common notoriety and matters which an insurer in the ordinary course of his business ought to know;
  3. any circumstance as to which information is waived by the insurer;
  4. any circumstance which it is superfluous to disclose by reason of any express or implied warranty.

The qualifier is doing real work. Once the insurer asks, the exception falls away and the question must be answered truthfully; and section 22 then makes an untrue answer a misrepresentation in its own right.

5. Effect of non-disclosure at the conclusion of the contract

Section 20(1) states the consequence directly: if the assured fails to make such disclosure, the insurer may avoid the contract.

Avoidance operates ab initio. The contract is treated as never having existed, the premium is normally returnable, and no claim is payable - including a claim wholly unconnected with the matter concealed. Four qualifications complete the answer. It is a right to avoid, not automatic nullity: the policy is voidable, not void, and the insurer may elect to affirm it. That right may be lost by waiver or by delay once the insurer knows the facts. The non-disclosure need not be fraudulent - innocent or negligent concealment of a material circumstance is enough. And because section 19 is mutual, the assured may equally avoid for want of good faith by the insurer.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question prints as one block of 16 marks but contains four distinct tasks, and the examiner will be marking four things: the principle, what must be disclosed, what need not be, and the consequence. Give each its own heading and the marks are visibly separated on the page. A candidate who writes one long essay on good faith is answering a quarter of the question at length.

The third limb is the cheapest scoring in the question and the one most often skipped. It is a closed statutory list of four items. Written out with the words "in the absence of enquiry" in front of them, it takes ninety seconds and banks the marks. Do it early, before the clock becomes a problem.

The jurisdiction trap - this is the point of the question

Almost every readily available account of uberrimae fidei is written about English law. This examination is Indian. The operative statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963: section 19 (utmost good faith), section 20 (disclosure by the assured), section 21 (disclosure by an agent), section 22 (representations). Citing the UK Marine Insurance Act 1906 as the governing statute in an Indian paper is a real and entirely avoidable error, and it is visible to the examiner in one line.

Naming the 1906 Act as the model for the 1963 Act is correct and shows reading. Naming it as the governing law is wrong. The distinction is worth getting right in one clean sentence at the top of the answer.

The English reform, and why it does not belong in the answer

English law on this subject was materially reformed by the Insurance Act 2015, which replaced the blunt all-or-nothing remedy of avoidance with a duty of fair presentation and a scheme of proportionate remedies - so that an innocent non-disclosure which would merely have raised the premium now leads to a proportionate reduction in the claim rather than the loss of cover altogether.

That reform is English and statutory. It did not amend the Indian Act, under which avoidance remains the remedy. Knowing the contrast is worth a sentence and is excellent oral material; writing the English position as though it were the Indian one would be marked wrong.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the Marine Insurance Act 1906 as the governing statute in an Indian examination.
  • Importing the UK Insurance Act 2015 proportionate remedies into Indian law.
  • Saying the duty binds only the assured. Section 19 is expressly mutual - either party.
  • Saying the contract is void. It is voidable at the option of the innocent party.
  • Omitting the words "in the absence of enquiry" from the section 20(3) exceptions. Once asked, every one of them must be answered.
  • Claiming the non-disclosure must be fraudulent. It need not be.
  • Spending the whole answer on the meaning of good faith and never reaching the fourth limb.

Examiner traps

  • "The owner did not mention a grounding two years ago, but it was widely reported in the trade press. Must he disclose it?" Arguably not - section 20(3)(b) excepts circumstances known or presumed known to the insurer, including matters of common notoriety. But if the insurer asks, it must be answered truthfully.
  • "The undisclosed fact had nothing to do with the loss. Can the insurer still avoid?" Under the Indian Act, yes. Avoidance is not limited to matters causative of the loss. That harshness is precisely what the English 2015 Act was passed to soften - and it has not been softened in India.
  • "Is the policy void or voidable?" Voidable. The distinction carries a mark on its own, and it is the reason the insurer can affirm.
  • "The assured disclosed something that made the risk look better. Was he obliged to?" No - section 20(3)(a) excepts circumstances which diminish the risk. Volunteering them is permitted, not required.
  • "The broker knew but the owner did not." Section 21. The agent effecting the insurance owes the duty for what he knows and what he ought to know.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which Act governs marine insurance in India, and from what year? The Marine Insurance Act, 1963.
  • State the test of materiality. Whether the circumstance would influence the judgment of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium or determining whether to take the risk.
  • List the four circumstances which need not be disclosed.
  • Is the contract void or voidable? Voidable, at the option of the other party.
  • Does the duty bind the insurer too? Yes - section 19 says either party.
  • Can the insurer lose the right to avoid? Yes, by affirming the contract, or by waiver or delay once he knows the facts.

Model written answer

(a) Key provisions and objectives

1. What the Convention is, and the problem it was written to solve

The Convention on Facilitation of International Maritime Traffic, 1965 was adopted on 9 April 1965 and entered into force on 5 March 1967. It is the IMO's facilitation instrument, and it sits apart from SOLAS and MARPOL in what it regulates: not the ship, but the paperwork and formalities imposed on the ship by the shore.

The problem it addressed was over-regulation by accumulation. Every State had developed its own customs, immigration, health and port declarations, so a ship on a multi-port voyage faced a different set of forms in every country, in different formats, demanding the same facts in different ways. The cost was not compliance but delay.

2. The stated objective

The Convention's objective, in its own terms, is to prevent unnecessary delays in maritime traffic, to aid co-operation between Governments, and to secure the highest practicable degree of uniformity in formalities and other procedures. Three things are being pursued at once - speed, cooperation and uniformity - and limbs (b) and (c) of this question are simply those objectives examined in turn.

3. Structure, and the Standards / Recommended Practices mechanism

The instrument is in two parts: the Articles, which bind the Contracting Governments as a treaty, and the Annex, which carries the operative facilitation measures. The Annex divides its measures into two classes, and the distinction is the mechanism that makes the Convention work:

  • a Standard is a measure that is necessary and practicable for facilitating international maritime traffic. A Government which finds it impracticable to comply, or which applies a different measure, must notify the Secretary-General of the difference. That obligation is what stops non-compliance being silent.
  • a Recommended Practice is a measure it is desirable to apply. Here notification runs the other way - a Government is asked to notify when it brings its practice into full accord.

So the Convention neither imposes uniformity by force nor leaves it to goodwill. It creates a published record of every divergence, which is a far more workable instrument for a subject where port procedure is bound up with national customs and immigration sovereignty.

4. The Annex sections and the FAL Forms

The Annex is arranged in seven sections: definitions and general provisions; arrival, stay and departure of ships; arrival and departure of persons; stowaways; arrival, stay and departure of cargo and other articles; public health and quarantine, including sanitary measures for animals and plants; and miscellaneous provisions. Three appendices carry the maximum information that may be required, the exceptional paper declarations and the stowaway material.

The most visible provision is the standardised set of IMO FAL Forms - the General Declaration, Cargo Declaration, Ship's Stores Declaration, Crew's Effects Declaration, Crew List, Passenger List and the Dangerous Goods Manifest, supplemented since 2016 by further clearance documents. The governing principle is maximum information: a public authority may ask for no more than the Convention permits.

(b) Impact on efficiency and security

5. Efficiency, and why security is served rather than sacrificed

Efficiency. A single standard data set, submitted once, removes duplicated declarations across a voyage; pre-arrival submission moves clearance off the critical path so that formalities are settled before the ship is on the berth; and a maximum-information rule caps what any authority may demand. Since the 2016 amendments, in force 1 January 2018, electronic exchange of that information has been mandatory from 9 April 2019, which converted the saving from paper handling to data handling.

Security. The instinctive answer - that facilitation and security pull against each other - is the wrong one, and saying why earns the mark. Standardised, structured, electronic data is more amenable to screening than a stack of differently formatted paper forms: crew and passenger lists arriving pre-arrival in a known format can be checked against watch lists before the ship berths; the cargo declaration and the dangerous goods manifest support risk-based targeting; and the Convention's provisions on stowaways address a security and humanitarian problem directly. The Convention also carries the ISPS-related security information within the same pre-arrival machinery. Facilitation done properly gives the authorities better information earlier, which is what security actually needs.

(c) Global cooperation and standardization

6. Why a facilitation treaty produces cooperation and standardization

Standardization here is not an aspiration in the preamble; it is built into the machinery. The maximum-information rule caps demands; the FAL Forms fix the format; the notification of differences makes every divergence visible and therefore negotiable; and the IMO Compendium on Facilitation and Electronic Business supplies an agreed data model so that the same fact carries the same name in every system. At FAL 46 five further data sets were added to the Compendium, covering ship reporting systems, certificates, registry details, inspections and port State control history.

The commercial significance follows. A common data set is what allows a port community system in one country to speak to a customs system in another; it lowers the entry cost for developing States, which need to build one interface rather than many; and it aligns the maritime leg with the wider single-window movement in trade facilitation, so that the ship is not the odd link in an otherwise digital chain.

7. The position at this sitting - the single window is already mandatory

This is the currency of the question. The 2022 amendments, resolution FAL.14(46), adopted 13 May 2022, entered into force on 1 January 2024 - days before this examination. They make it mandatory for public authorities to establish, maintain and use single window systems for the electronic exchange of information required on the arrival, stay and departure of ships, and to combine or coordinate transmission so that information is submitted only once and re-used to the maximum extent possible. They also require Contracting Governments to allow ships and ports to remain fully operational during a public health emergency of international concern, with seafarers and port workers designated key workers irrespective of nationality or flag - the codified lesson of the crew-change crisis - and they introduce a systemic approach to corruption at the ship-shore interface, requiring authorities to assess the risk and develop preventive measures.

Note the amendment position precisely, because it is where marks are lost. The next amendments, resolution FAL.15(47), were adopted on 17 March 2023 but do not enter into force until 1 January 2025. At this sitting they are adopted but not in force. Amendments to the Annex are made by tacit acceptance under Article VII(2)(b): they take effect on the stated date unless at least one third of Contracting Governments object beforehand, which is what allows a technical annex to keep pace with practice.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed marks are 6 + 4 + 6 and the three limbs are not three separate topics - they are the Convention's three objectives examined in turn. Limb (a) is the instrument; limb (b) is speed; limb (c) is cooperation and uniformity. Recognising that turns a vague-looking discussion question into a structured one, and it stops the same material being written three times.

Limb (a) carries six marks and hides four tasks - what the Convention is, its objective, its structure, and its concrete provisions. Splitting it across four short route steps is what stops the six marks disappearing into one paragraph about reducing paperwork. Limb (b) is only four marks: two on efficiency, two on security, and the security half is the half candidates get wrong.

The single window is ALREADY mandatory at this sitting

This is the highest-value fact in the question and it is date-critical. Resolution FAL.14(46) entered into force on 1 January 2024. A candidate sitting this paper is sitting in the first month of the mandatory maritime single window.

Writing "the single window will become mandatory", or "is being introduced", is wrong at this date. So, in the other direction, is presenting the 2023 amendments as operative: FAL.15(47) was adopted 17 March 2023 but did not enter into force until 1 January 2025. Adopted is not in force. A candidate who states both dates correctly demonstrates exactly the distinction the examiners test across this whole syllabus.

Facilitation versus security - the argument to make

Limb (b) invites the answer that facilitation and security are in tension and must be balanced. That answer scores something, but the better one is that well-designed facilitation improves security. The reason is concrete: security screening is a data problem, and the Convention delivers a standard data set, submitted electronically, before arrival. Watch-list checks on crew and passenger lists, targeting from the cargo declaration and dangerous goods manifest, and stowaway procedures all depend on getting structured information early. Twenty different paper formats arriving at the gangway is worse for security, not better.

The honest qualification, which is worth adding, is that a single window concentrates data and therefore concentrates risk - which is exactly why FAL 46 also approved guidelines on the authentication, integrity and confidentiality of single window exchanges. Naming that shows the tension has been thought about rather than waved away.

Standards and Recommended Practices - get the notification direction right

This is a precision point that separates candidates. For a Standard, a Government notifies the Secretary-General when it cannot comply or applies something different. For a Recommended Practice, it notifies when it does bring its practice into full accord. The two run in opposite directions, and the reason is structural: the Convention wants divergence from Standards to be visible, and convergence towards Recommended Practices to be visible. Getting the direction backwards is a common and easily avoided error.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the single window as a future or forthcoming requirement. It became mandatory on 1 January 2024.
  • Presenting the 2023 amendments as in force. They were adopted 17 March 2023 and entered into force 1 January 2025 - after this sitting.
  • Reversing the notification duty for Standards and Recommended Practices.
  • Treating facilitation and security as simple opposites, and never explaining how standardised electronic data actually helps screening.
  • Confusing the FAL Convention with the ISPS Code or with SOLAS chapter XI-2. FAL governs formalities; ISPS governs security measures.
  • Quoting a number of Standards and Recommended Practices. The 2022 amendments followed a comprehensive review of the whole Annex and circulating counts are unreliable.
  • Forgetting that the Annex caps what may be demanded - the maximum-information rule is a provision, not a courtesy.

The ship's own fuel and distance figures, already reported to the flag State since 2019, are turned into a number; that number is compared with a target derived from the 2019 industry average and tightened by a percentage that grows each year; and the comparison produces a letter from A to E. Model written answer

1. Where CII sits in law, and to whom it applies

The Carbon Intensity Indicator is regulation 28 of MARPOL Annex VI, introduced by the 2021 revised Annex VI (resolution MEPC.328(76)), which entered into force on 1 November 2022. It is the operational half of the short-term GHG measure; EEXI, regulation 23, is the technical half. EEXI is assessed once against the ship's design; CII is assessed every year against how the ship is actually run.

It applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above falling into the cargo and passenger categories listed in regulation 28.1. Under regulation 28.1 the ship calculates its attained annual operational CII after the end of calendar year 2023 and after the end of each following calendar year, over the period 1 January to 31 December, using the fuel oil consumption data already collected under regulation 27 (the IMO DCS, running since calendar year 2019). Under regulation 28.2 it reports that value to its Administration within three months of the year end.

So at the date of this examination the position is precise: 2023 was the first calculation year, the first attained CII falls due for reporting by 31 March 2024, and no ship has yet been assigned a CII rating.

2. The mechanism - attained, required, Z and the reference line

The rating is a comparison, not an absolute measurement. Three quantities are needed.

  • Attained annual operational CII - what the ship actually achieved. In its simplest form it is the ratio of the total mass of CO2 emitted, M, to the total transport work undertaken, W, in the calendar year. M is derived from the DCS fuel figures using the fuel-to-CO2 conversion factors.
  • Required annual operational CII - the target. Regulation 28.4: Required CII = (1 − Z/100) × CIIR.
  • CIIR is the reference value for the ship type, set on the 2019 reference line under the G2 guidelines, resolution MEPC.353(78).

Z is the annual reduction factor, and it is what makes the standard tighten each year. Under the G3 guidelines, resolution MEPC.338(76) table 1, relative to the 2019 line:

YearReduction factor Z
20235 %

202611 %
2027 to 2030not yet set - to be further strengthened and developed

The consequence for a ship is blunt: the same operating pattern earns a worse rating every year, because the target moves and the ship does not.

3. The rating, and what attaches to it

Under regulation 28.6 the attained CII is verified against the required CII to give a rating of A, B, C, D or E - major superior, minor superior, moderate, minor inferior, inferior - and the middle point of rating level C is the required annual operational CII itself. The G4 guidelines, resolution MEPC.354(78), place four boundaries around that midpoint - superior, lower, upper and inferior - for each year from 2023 to 2030.

The teeth are in regulations 28.7 to 28.9. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or rated E in any single year, must develop a plan of corrective actions; the SEEMP is reviewed to incorporate it and submitted to the Administration for verification no later than one month after reporting the attained CII; and the ship must then duly undertake those actions. Regulation 28.10 works the other way, encouraging Administrations, port authorities and other stakeholders to provide incentives to ships rated A or B.

Note what the regulation does not do. There is no detention, no fine and no certificate withdrawn for a poor rating. The pressure is commercial - charterers, financiers and cargo owners read the rating - and administrative, through the SEEMP. Regulation 28.11 requires a review to be completed by 1 January 2026 covering, among other things, the need for reinforced corrective actions, enforcement, and revision of the Z and CIIR values. That review has not reported at this sitting.

4. AER - what it measures and why the regulation uses it

The G1 guidelines, resolution MEPC.352(78), draw the distinction the question is really about. A demand-based CII uses the actual mass or volume of cargo carried. A supply-based CII uses the ship's capacity as a proxy for cargo. AER - the Annual Efficiency Ratio - is the supply-based CII which uses DWT as the capacity; the variant using GT is cgDIST.

So AER is, in grams of CO2 per deadweight-tonne-mile:

  • numerator - total CO2 from all fuel consumed in the calendar year, from DCS data;
  • denominator - ship's capacity × total distance travelled, from DCS data;
  • capacity is DWT for bulk carriers, tankers, container ships, gas carriers, LNG carriers, general cargo ships, refrigerated cargo carriers and combination carriers, and GT for cruise passenger ships, ro-ro cargo ships including vehicle carriers, and ro-ro passenger ships.

Its virtue is entirely practical: every input is already reported, auditable and comparable. Nothing new has to be measured, and two sister ships can be compared on identical terms.

5. EEOI, and the critical comparison

EEOI - the Energy Efficiency Operational Indicator - is defined in MEPC.1/Circ.684 and measures CO2 per tonne of cargo actually carried per mile. It is the demand-based metric, and it is a truer measure of transport work: a ship that sails full has done more work than one that sails in ballast, and EEOI says so where AER does not.

Its status at this sitting must be stated exactly. EEOI is not the regulatory metric. G1 section 5 lists it - with EEPI, cbDIST and clDIST - among metrics encouraged to be used for trial purposes. It is voluntary. Regulation 28 is satisfied by the supply-based indicator.

The critical case against AER is real and should be made:

  • it rewards distance, not cargo. Long ballast legs, and idle days at anchor burning fuel while travelling no distance, both worsen AER; steaming light on a long passage can improve it;
  • it is blind to utilisation. Two identical ships, one full and one half empty, score the same if they burn the same fuel over the same distance - so the metric cannot reward the commercially efficient operator;
  • it penalises short-sea and multi-port trades, where port time and manoeuvring are a large share of consumption and sea distance is small;
  • it can be gamed at the margin by slow steaming with more ships, which lowers each ship's AER while the cargo moves no more efficiently overall.

The case for choosing it anyway is equally real. EEOI needs cargo mass data that the DCS does not collect, that is commercially confidential, that is not verifiable by an Administration in the way DCS data is, and that is not comparable across trades - a car carrier, a container ship and a crude tanker cannot be ranked on tonne-miles. IMO therefore chose the metric that could be audited and enforced over the one that is conceptually correct. That trade-off - enforceability bought at the cost of validity - is the answer to "critically discuss", and regulation 28.11's inclusion of the CIIR and Z values in its review is the acknowledgement that it is not settled.

6. Operational measures to improve the rating

Because AER is fuel over capacity-distance, only two things can be done: burn less, or travel further for it. Operational measures dominate because they are available now and cost little.

  • Speed and voyage management. Consumption rises far faster than speed, so optimising speed to the required arrival time is the single largest lever. Just-in-time arrival converts waiting at anchor - which burns fuel and adds no distance - into a slower, cheaper passage.
  • Hull and propeller condition. Planned in-water cleaning and propeller polishing against measured performance, rather than to a calendar.
  • Trim and draught optimisation against the ship's own trim tables or CFD-derived guidance, and optimum ballast - carry no more than is needed.
  • Weather routeing to avoid added resistance, and autopilot tuning to cut rudder-induced drag.
  • Machinery and auxiliary load. Shut down surplus generators, use the shaft generator where fitted, run pumps and fans on variable frequency drives, manage cargo heating and inert gas use, and control accommodation HVAC and lighting load.
  • Reduce port and idle consumption - shore power where available, minimise boiler use, avoid unnecessary main engine running.

7. Technical, fuel and calculation measures

Technical retrofit. Low-friction and foul-release hull coatings; propeller retrofit or upgrade and energy saving devices such as ducts, fins and rudder bulbs; engine derating with engine or shaft power limitation; waste heat recovery; air lubrication; and wind assistance. Each must be justified for the specific ship and trade - none is universally worthwhile.

Fuel. Because the numerator is CO2 and not fuel mass, switching to a fuel with a lower carbon factor - LNG, methanol, or a biofuel blend - reduces attained CII directly for the same energy delivered.

Data and calculation. Two legitimate levers that are not fuel savings at all. First, DCS data quality: the attained CII is only as good as the reported fuel and distance figures, so flowmeter calibration, consistent bunker figures and correct noon-report distances matter directly to the rating. Second, the G5 interim guidelines, resolution MEPC.355(78), allow defined voyage adjustments - which deduct fuel and the associated distance for qualifying periods - and correction factors, which adjust the numerator or denominator, for cases such as ice conditions, ship-to-ship operations and refrigerated containers. G5 states the boundary itself: their use should in no way undermine the goal of reducing the carbon intensity of international shipping.

All of this is administered through the SEEMP. Under regulation 26.3 an affected ship's SEEMP had to include, on or before 1 January 2023, the calculation methodology, the required CII for the next three years, an implementation plan for achieving it, and a procedure for self-evaluation and improvement - and it is subject to verification and company audit. The SEEMP is where an examiner expects the measures above to live, not in a loose list.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question is one unlimbed block of 16 marks setting three tasks - explain CII rating, critically discuss AER and EEOI, and list improvement measures - and the middle one is where the marks actually are. "Critically discuss" is not "describe": a candidate who defines AER and EEOI and stops has answered half of the highest-value limb.

Steps 1 to 3 build the mechanism, and they must come first because the AER discussion is meaningless without knowing that the rating is a comparison against a moving target. Steps 4 and 5 are the critical core. Steps 6 and 7 are the improvement measures, split operational from technical so that the answer reads as a plan rather than a shopping list - and the operational half comes first because it is what a ship's engineer actually controls.

At this sitting, no ship has a rating yet

The critical argument, stated cleanly

The examiner's word is critically. The argument to make is that IMO chose an enforceable metric over a valid one.

EEOI is the better measure of what shipping is for - moving cargo. AER measures how far a hull travelled per tonne of CO2, regardless of whether it carried anything. But EEOI requires cargo mass, which is commercially confidential, is not collected by the DCS, cannot be verified by an Administration, and cannot be compared across ship types. AER's inputs are already reported, already audited and already comparable. So the regulation uses AER and the guidelines relegate EEOI to trial purposes under G1 section 5.

The honest closing point is that regulation 28.11 puts the CIIR and Z values inside the scope of its own review, which is IMO conceding that the metric is provisional. A candidate who says that has genuinely criticised the scheme rather than listed its flaws.

AER and EEOI - the distinction in one line each

AER - supply-based; capacity is a proxy for cargo; capacity is DWT; every input comes from the DCS; this is the regulatory metric under regulation 28. cgDIST is the same idea with GT instead of DWT, used for cruise passenger, ro-ro cargo and ro-ro passenger ships. EEOI - demand-based; uses actual cargo carried; defined in MEPC.1/Circ.684; voluntary, for trial purposes.

The word to hold on to is proxy. AER's every strength and every weakness follows from substituting capacity for cargo.

Common mistakes

  • Saying ships already hold CII ratings at this sitting. 2023 is the first data year and the first reports are due 31 March 2024.
  • Giving the wrong reduction factor. For 2024 it is 7 %, relative to the 2019 reference line.
  • Asserting Z values for 2027 onwards. They were not set at this sitting.
  • Treating EEOI as a regulatory requirement. It is voluntary and, in G1, for trial purposes.
  • Confusing CII with EEXI. EEXI is technical and assessed once on design; CII is operational and assessed every year.
  • Saying a D or E rating leads to detention or a fine. The consequence is a corrective action plan in the SEEMP; the real pressure is commercial.
  • Saying a single D rating triggers corrective action. It is D for three consecutive years, or E.
  • Listing improvement measures with invented percentage savings. Quote mechanism, not vendor figures.
  • Forgetting that improving data quality and applying the G5 voyage adjustments are legitimate levers alongside burning less fuel.

Examiner traps

  • "Where does rating C sit?" Regulation 28.6 - the middle point of rating level C is the required annual operational CII. C is not a pass mark, it is the target itself.
  • "What happens to a ship rated D?" Nothing, for one year, and for two. It is D for three consecutive years, or a single E, that triggers regulation 28.7.
  • "A ship waits three weeks at anchor. What does that do to AER?" It worsens it - fuel is burned and no distance is travelled, so the numerator grows and the denominator does not. It is the clearest illustration that AER measures distance, not work.
  • "How would EEOI treat the same three weeks?" The same way in the numerator, but EEOI at least credits the cargo actually carried on the voyage either side.
  • "Can a shipowner improve CII without touching the ship?" Yes - speed and voyage management, better DCS data quality, and the G5 voyage adjustments and correction factors where the ship genuinely qualifies.
  • "Which guideline gives what?" G1 the indicators and calculation, G2 the reference lines, G3 the reduction factors, G4 the rating boundaries, G5 correction factors and voyage adjustments.
  • "Is CII in the SEEMP?" Yes - regulation 26.3, the SEEMP Part III content, required on or before 1 January 2023.

Model written answer

(a) Essential elements of preventive maintenance

1. What preventive maintenance is, and where it sits

Preventive maintenance is work done before a failure, on a plan, to keep equipment fit for service. It sits between two alternatives which are still used deliberately: corrective (breakdown) maintenance, acceptable only for non-critical items where failure is cheap and safe, and condition-based maintenance, where the intervention is triggered by measured condition rather than by elapsed time. A mature ship uses all three - the skill is allocating each item to the right one.

2. The essential elements

The elements below are a system, not a list of good intentions. Remove any one and the rest degrade.

  • An equipment register with criticality ranking. Every item identified, and each one classified by the consequence of its sudden failure. This is what decides which strategy applies, and it is the same judgement the ISM Code requires at element 10.3.
  • A defined maintenance task for each item, on a defined trigger. The task comes from the maker's recommendations and class requirements as the baseline, refined by the ship's own experience. The trigger is running hours, calendar time, or measured condition.
  • Condition monitoring for the items that warrant it - lubricating oil analysis, vibration analysis, thermography, performance and pressure-temperature trending, wear-down measurement. Its purpose is to convert time-based overhaul into evidence-based overhaul.
  • Spares, stores and tools. Critical spares identified and held on board, minimum stock levels, and control of the special tools and lifting gear each job needs. A planned job with no spare is not planned.
  • Competent people and safe procedures - trained and familiarised staff, work planned around manning and rest hours, and the permit-to-work, isolation, enclosed-space and hot-work controls that the job actually requires.
  • Records and feedback. What was done, when, by whom, what was found, and what was renewed. Records are the evidence at survey and audit, and the raw material for revising intervals.
  • Review and improvement. Defect and failure analysis feeding back into the intervals and the tasks, so that the plan changes when the evidence says it should.

Behind all seven sits the Planned Maintenance System - normally a software system - which holds the register, raises the jobs, and keeps the records. The PMS is the tool; the elements above are the system it serves.

(b) Statutory survey, class survey, routine maintenance and the SMS

3. Statutory survey - what it is and who requires it

A statutory survey is required by an international convention or national law and is carried out by the flag Administration, or by a Recognized Organization to which the Administration has delegated the work under the RO Code. It leads to a statutory certificate - Safety Equipment, Safety Construction, Safety Radio, Load Line, IOPP, IAPP and the rest. Delegation is of function, not of responsibility: the Administration remains responsible for the certification of its ships.

The harmonized system of survey and certification aligns the survey dates and certificate validities so that one visit can serve several certificates. 4. Classification survey - and how the two differ

A classification survey is required by the society's own rules under a contract between owner and society. It verifies that hull, machinery and essential systems remain in accordance with class rules, and it maintains class - special (renewal) survey on a five-year cycle, annual and intermediate surveys within it, plus tailshaft, boiler and other item surveys on their own cycles. The outcome is not a legal certificate but the class notation, with any deficiency recorded as a condition of class or recommendation carrying a due date.

The distinction that carries the mark is one of authority, not of activity. Statutory survey is public law, owed to the flag State, and enforced by port State control and detention. Class survey is private contract, owed to the society, and enforced commercially - by insurers, charterers and financiers, for whom loss of class is fatal. The same surveyor from the same society often does both in the same attendance, which is exactly why candidates conflate them - but he wears two hats, one as the society and one as the RO.

5. The mechanism that links routine maintenance to survey

The link is not merely that good maintenance makes surveys go well. It is structural.

Survey verifies condition; maintenance produces it. A surveyor attending an annual survey does not rebuild the ship - he samples, examines, witnesses tests, and reads the records. Where the maintenance system is sound the records are the evidence, and where it is not the survey is the point at which that becomes visible.

The formal instrument is the class-approved planned maintenance scheme. Instead of opening machinery for a surveyor on a fixed cycle, the owner submits the PMS for class approval; once approved, maintenance carried out by the ship's own chief engineer to the approved scheme, and properly recorded, is credited towards the machinery survey requirement, with the society confirming at an annual audit that the scheme is being followed. A condition monitoring scheme extends the same principle to evidence from vibration and oil analysis in place of opening up. The commercial consequence is real - surveys stop dictating the docking plan - and so is the obligation: the credit depends entirely on records that stand up.

The link also runs the other way. A defect found in routine maintenance may require notification to class, may generate a condition of class or an additional survey under A.1186(33) 2.7 after important repairs or renewals, and - where it affects a statutory item - may make the ship's certificate invalid until it is rectified.

6. How the ISM Code merges it into the SMS

Element 10, Maintenance of the Ship and Equipment, is what converts all of the above from good practice into an auditable requirement. Its four paragraphs each do a distinct job:

  1. 10.1 - the Company shall establish procedures to ensure the ship is maintained in conformity with the relevant rules and regulations and with any additional requirements established by the Company. "Rules and regulations" is what pulls both class rules and statutory requirements inside the SMS; the final clause is what lets the Company set a standard above them.
  2. 10.2 - inspections at appropriate intervals; any non-conformity reported with its possible cause, if known; appropriate corrective action taken; and records maintained. That is the audit trail.
  3. 10.3 - the Company shall identify equipment and technical systems the sudden operational failure of which may result in hazardous situations, and the SMS shall provide specific measures to promote the reliability of such equipment, including the regular testing of stand-by arrangements and equipment not in continuous use. This is critical equipment, and it is the direct statutory root of the criticality ranking in limb (a).
  4. 10.4 - the inspections at 10.2 and the measures at 10.3 shall be integrated in the ship's operational maintenance routine. The Code expressly forbids running a separate safety inspection regime alongside the PMS.

The surrounding elements complete the loop. Element 9 requires non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences to be reported, investigated and analysed, with corrective action to prevent recurrence - which is where a repeated failure becomes a revised maintenance interval rather than a repeated repair. Element 11 controls the documents, so that the manual on board is the current one. Element 12 requires internal safety audits at intervals not exceeding twelve months, exceptionally extendable by not more than three, by auditors independent of the area audited.

7. How the chain closes

Put end to end, the ship carries one maintenance process and three different bodies read it for three different purposes:

  • the PMS plans and records the work, and criticality decides how much attention each item gets;
  • class reads those records at survey, and may credit them against the machinery survey where the scheme is approved;
  • the flag State or its RO reads the same evidence at statutory survey and issues or endorses the certificate;
  • the ISM audit reads the process itself rather than the machinery, and asks whether 10.1 to 10.4 are actually being done - leading to the SMC for the ship and the DOC for the Company;
  • port State control then tests the whole thing from the outside, and a pattern of maintenance-related deficiencies is treated as an ISM failure, not merely a machinery one.

That is the merger the question is asking about. Maintenance, class survey, statutory survey and audit are not four regimes bolted together; they are one body of evidence examined by four readers, and the ISM Code is the instrument that requires the evidence to exist.

Two hats, one surveyor

Examiner traps

  • "What is critical equipment, and where does the requirement come from?" ISM 10.3 - equipment and technical systems the sudden operational failure of which may result in hazardous situations. The measures must include regular testing of stand-by arrangements not in continuous use.
  • "Your PMS is approved by class. Does that remove the surveyor?" No. It credits the chief engineer's recorded work against the machinery survey requirement, subject to the society confirming annually that the approved scheme is being followed. It is a change of method, not an exemption.
  • "A pump is overhauled and the job is not recorded. What is the consequence?" Under ISM 10.2.4 the record is part of the requirement, so the non-conformity is in the system, not only in the pump. Under a class-approved scheme it may also cost the survey credit.

  • "What is an additional survey?" A.1186(33) 2.7 - general or partial, after a repair resulting from investigation or whenever important repairs or renewals are made, or after change, replacement or significant repair of structure, equipment, systems, fittings, arrangements and material.
  • "How does the annual survey relate to maintenance?" Directly. A.1186(33) 2.5 defines it as a general inspection to ensure the items have been maintained and remain satisfactory.

Model written answer

(a) IACS structure, UR / UI / PR, and their significance

1. What IACS is and how it is organised

The International Association of Classification Societies is a non-governmental association of classification societies which holds consultative status at IMO. It makes no law. Its output is technical, and it reaches ships only through its Members' own rules or through Administrations choosing to rely on it.

  • Council - the governing body, with one Council Representative per Member. It sets strategy, approves the budget, admits and suspends Members, and adopts Resolutions.
  • General Policy Group (GPG) - the executive deputy to Council, made up of Members' Permanent Representatives, which runs the work programme between Council sessions and approves technical Resolutions for implementation.
  • Panels and Expert Groups - the technical bodies which draft and revise the Resolutions, covering hull, machinery, survey, environmental and safety subjects, with further panels added as the Association's work has extended into decarbonisation and digitalisation.
  • Permanent Secretariat, in London, which administers the Association.
  • Quality Committee, which oversees Members' quality systems under the IACS quality system certification scheme - membership is conditional on meeting it.

No membership figure or panel count is given here deliberately: both have changed over time, and neither is needed to answer the question.

2. UR, UI and PR - what each acts upon

All three are IACS Resolutions. They are distinguished not by subject but by what they act upon.

ResolutionWhat it isWhat it acts upon, and its effect
UR - Unified RequirementA minimum technical requirement on a matter directly connected with class rules and practices.Acts on the society's own Rules. Members must incorporate it into their Rules, normally within one year of GPG approval. It reaches a ship through class rules, never directly.
UI - Unified InterpretationA common interpretation of a provision of an IMO instrument which is vaguely worded, or left to the satisfaction of the Administration.Acts on an IMO convention. It secures uniform application across Members. It interprets; it does not create a class requirement.
PR - Procedural RequirementA requirement about the Members' own procedures - how surveys are conducted, how class is transferred, how matters are reported.Acts on the society itself. It governs how the society works, not the ship's technical standard.

The cleanest way to hold them apart is ship / convention / society: a UR ends up in the ship through the rulebook, a UI tells you what a convention means, and a PR disciplines the society.

3. Significance to owners and operators

The answer is yes, and the question is really asking for reasons rather than a yes.

  • A UR is a floor. No Member's Rules may fall below it, so an owner gets a predictable technical standard whichever Member classes the ship - and cannot shop between societies for a materially softer rule. That is a benefit and a constraint at once: it also means a new UR can impose a real cost on an existing fleet.
  • A UI reduces regulatory risk. An arrangement accepted by one society is far less likely to be challenged by another Administration or at port State control if the interpretation is unified. That matters commercially, because the cost of a disputed interpretation is delay.
  • A PR has direct commercial bite. The procedural requirements on transfer of class prevent an owner escaping outstanding recommendations or conditions of class by moving society - the receiving society must take up the outstanding items.

There is a limit worth stating: IACS Resolutions bind Members, not owners. An owner classed with a non-IACS society is outside the system - which is itself a reason charterers and financiers care which society the ship is with.

(b) Recognized Organizations and the RO Code

4. What a Recognized Organization is

A Recognized Organization is an organization to which a flag Administration has delegated authority to carry out statutory survey and certification work on its behalf - most commonly, but not necessarily, a classification society.

Two points carry the marks. First, the delegation is of function, not of responsibility: the Administration remains responsible for the statutory certification of its ships and cannot answer a casualty by pointing at its RO. Second, class society and RO are not synonyms. A society classes ships under its own rules as a commercial matter; it becomes an RO only where an Administration has delegated statutory work to it, only to the extent of that delegation, and only for that flag. The same society may be an RO for one flag and not another, and may hold different scopes from different flags. A surveyor attending one ship may therefore act in two distinct capacities in the same visit.

5. The RO Code and its salient features

The Code for Recognized Organizations was adopted by resolution MSC.349(92) on 21 June 2013 and by resolution MEPC.237(65). Its purpose is to provide a harmonised, transparent and independent standard for assessing, authorising and overseeing ROs, so that a flag State's delegation is measured against a single international benchmark rather than each flag's own judgement.

  • Part 1 - general provisions: purpose, definitions and application.
  • Part 2 - mandatory requirements for the flag State and the RO: competence and qualification of surveyors and auditors, a quality management system, independence, the written authorization agreement, and reporting duties.
  • Part 3 - guidelines for the oversight of ROs by flag States.
  • Parts 1 and 2 are mandatory; Part 3 is guidance. That distinction is examinable and is often got wrong.
  • It was made mandatory from 1 January 2015 by resolutions MSC.350(92), MSC.356(92) and MEPC.238(65), amending SOLAS, MARPOL Annexes I and II and the 1988 Load Line Protocol.

Note the pattern - a Code drafted as a standard, made binding by amending the conventions that refer to it. It is the same technique used for the III Code, and recognising it is worth a mark in an oral.

6. How Administrations monitor ROs

Monitoring runs on three levels.

Before delegation. The Administration determines that the organization has adequate technical, managerial and research capability; assesses it against the Code; and puts the delegation into a formal written agreement which defines the scope and limits of the authority conferred, the applicable national law and interpretations, the instructions to be followed where a ship is found unfit to proceed to sea, and the records to be kept.

During delegation. Periodic audit of the RO's quality management system; verification that surveyors and auditors are qualified to the Code's standard; vertical contract audits and witnessed surveys, in which the Administration follows a certificate back through the evidence or attends alongside the RO's surveyor; monitoring of performance indicators, principally port State control detentions and casualties attributable to certificates issued under the delegation; and a requirement that the RO refer any matter outside its delegated scope back to the Administration.

Sanction and audit of the Administration itself. The Administration may restrict, suspend or withdraw the authorization. And the Administration's own performance in doing all of this is not unexamined: under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme, the flag State is audited against the III Code, whose Part 2 requires an oversight programme for delegated authority, and against the obligations listed in resolution A.1187(33), adopted 6 December 2023. So the chain is complete - the RO answers to the Administration, and the Administration answers to IMO.

Model written answer

1. What tribology is, and where it bites on board

Tribology is the science of friction, wear and lubrication between surfaces in relative motion. On a ship it is not an abstraction - it decides the condition of the cylinder liner and piston rings, the main, crankpin and crosshead bearings, the camshaft and gearing, the stern tube, and every deck machinery bearing on board.

The engineering aim in each of those contacts is to hold a full hydrodynamic film where possible, and to survive boundary and mixed lubrication where it is not - at top and bottom dead centre, at start-up, and at low load. Almost every development below is an attempt to widen the conditions under which the film survives, or to make the surfaces tolerant of the moments when it does not.

2. The fuel change since 2020, and what it did to cylinder oil

The most consequential recent development in marine tribology was not a lubricant at all - it was a fuel regulation. Since 1 January 2020, MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14 has imposed a global sulphur limit of 0.50 % m/m, and 0.10 % m/m in emission control areas.

The consequences reach straight into the cylinder:

  • Base number selection is now a decision, not a default. Cylinder oil alkalinity exists to neutralise sulphuric acid formed from fuel sulphur. Burn a compliant low-sulphur fuel on a high-BN oil and the surplus alkaline additive is not consumed - it deposits, giving hard calcium-rich deposits, bore polish and scuffing. Burn a high-sulphur fuel behind a scrubber on a low-BN oil and the acid is not neutralised - giving cold corrosion and rapid liner wear. Both failures are now live on the same fleet.
  • Two-oil and blending-on-board arrangements became mainstream, letting the BN delivered to the cylinder be varied with the fuel actually in use rather than fixed at a bunkering decision.
  • VLSFOs are blends, and they vary. Stability and compatibility between bunkers, wax appearance and sludging all became operational concerns, and a fuel that drops asphaltenes fouls the purifier and eventually the cylinder.
  • Catalytic fines - aluminium and silicon from refinery catalyst - remain the classic abrasive. They embed in the running surfaces and cut, and the defence is entirely mechanical: settling, purification at the right throughput and temperature, and drain checks.

3. Cylinder lubrication control

The second development is the move from mechanical, camshaft-driven lubricators to electronically controlled timed injection. The oil is delivered as a metered quantity, timed to a specific crank angle so that it lands on the ring pack rather than the liner wall generally, and distributed through the quills at the moment the rings pass.

That control makes feed-rate optimisation possible. Dosing is set proportional to engine load and to fuel sulphur, and then trimmed against evidence - which is where limb two of this question joins limb one. The evidence is scrape-down (drip) oil analysis: oil collected from the piston underside and tested for iron content, which indicates wear, and residual base number, which indicates whether alkalinity is being consumed or wasted. The feed rate is reduced until iron begins to rise, and then set back. Lubrication has become a measured, closed-loop process rather than a fixed setting.

4. Surfaces, coatings and bearing materials

If the film cannot always be maintained, the surfaces must tolerate its loss. Hence:

  • Liner surface engineering - plateau honing to give a bearing surface carrying oil-retaining grooves; hardened and thermally sprayed or laser-clad running surfaces; and anti-polishing rings at the top of the liner, which scrape the carbon crown from the piston and prevent the bore polishing that destroys the honing pattern.
  • Coated piston rings - chromium-ceramic and thermally sprayed coatings, and low-friction hard coatings including diamond-like carbon on smaller, higher-speed components, to raise scuffing resistance and cut boundary friction.
  • Bearing materials - tri-metal and polymer-overlaid bearings which tolerate thinner films, misalignment and momentary contact better than traditional white metal alone.
  • Base oils and additives - higher-quality Group II and III base stocks with better oxidation stability, and improved detergent, dispersant and anti-wear chemistry. Nanoparticle additives are an active research direction in the tribology literature, not established shipboard practice, and should be described as such.

5. Environmentally acceptable lubricants and the stern tube

The third driver is environmental rather than mechanical. Where a lubricated interface can discharge to the sea - stern tube, controllable-pitch propeller hub, stabilisers, thrusters, deck cranes - the industry moved to Environmentally Acceptable Lubricants, biodegradable and minimally toxic, driven principally by United States Vessel General Permit requirements for oil-to-sea interfaces.

That is a genuine tribological change, not a paper one. EALs are typically ester-based, which brings different seal compatibility, greater sensitivity to water - an ester that takes up water can hydrolyse - and different behaviour on mixing with mineral oil. The parallel development is the water-lubricated stern tube bearing, with polymer or composite staves, which removes the oil-to-sea interface entirely at the cost of a bearing operating in a far less forgiving lubricant.

6. Condition monitoring as applied tribology

The final development is that tribology has become measured continuously rather than assessed at overhaul: on-board test kits for BN, water and viscosity; shore laboratory spectrographic analysis; particle counting and ferrography to size and shape the debris rather than merely count metal; vibration analysis for bearing condition; and liner wear measurement and scavenge-port inspection as the physical cross-check. The point of all of it is to move from time-based overhaul to evidence-based intervention.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question prints as one 16-mark block and sets two tasks of unequal difficulty. "Latest developments in tribology" is open-ended and easy to waffle at; "inferences from a lub oil analysis report with the proposed corrective action in each case" is closed, specific, and where the reliable marks are.

So split the time roughly evenly but structure the two halves differently. The developments half is five named developments, each with a reason - not a general essay on friction. The analysis half is two tables, because the examiner has asked for a corrective action in each case and a table makes each pairing countable. A candidate who writes the analysis half as prose loses marks the table would have banked.

"Latest developments" - answer for the sitting you are in

This limb is sitting-relative by construction, and the honest way to handle it is to organise the developments around why they happened rather than around dates. The single largest driver at this sitting is the 2020 sulphur cap, and it is a genuinely tribological event: it changed what is burned, which changed what the cylinder oil has to neutralise, which reopened base number selection as a live engineering decision on every ship.

The second organising idea is that developments come in three families: the lubricant (base stocks, additives, EALs), the delivery (timed electronic dosing, feed-rate optimisation, blending on board), and the surfaces (honing, coatings, anti-polishing rings, bearing materials) - with measurement running underneath all three. Naming those families first turns an open question into a structured one.

One discipline: nanoparticle additives are research, not fleet practice. Mentioning them shows reading; presenting them as current shipboard technology does not.

The BN trap runs in both directions

This is the most examinable single idea in the developments half, and most candidates know only half of it.

Too little alkalinity for the sulphur burned gives cold corrosion: sulphuric acid condenses on the liner and corrodes it, and wear rates climb. That half is well known. Too much alkalinity for the sulphur burned is equally damaging: the calcium-bearing detergent is not consumed, so it deposits, giving hard deposits on the piston top land, bore polish and eventually scuffing. Since 2020 both failure modes exist in the same fleet at the same time, because a ship on compliant fuel and a scrubber-fitted ship on high-sulphur fuel need opposite oils. That is why blending on board and two-oil systems matter.

Read the report as a trend, and always find the source

Two rules carry the second half. A single sample is a sampling question; three samples moving together are a machinery question. And renewing the oil is not a corrective action - it is what you do after you have found the leak, the fouled cooler or the worn ring. An answer that proposes "change the oil" for every finding has not answered the question, which asks for the corrective action in each case.

The strongest single pairing to know is aluminium with silicon. Read alone, aluminium suggests piston or bearing material. Read with silicon, rising together in a broadly fixed ratio, it is catalytic fines - a fuel treatment problem, not a wear problem, and the corrective action is the purifier and the settling tanks, not the engine. Getting that right in an oral is worth more than reciting the whole table.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a general essay on friction and wear instead of naming developments and saying what drove them.
  • Missing the 2020 sulphur cap entirely. It is the largest tribological driver of the period and it is a regulation the candidate already knows.
  • Knowing only the cold-corrosion half of the base number problem and not the over-alkalinity half.
  • Presenting nanoparticle additives, or any laboratory development, as established shipboard practice.
  • Answering the second half in prose so that the inference and the corrective action are not visibly paired.
  • Proposing "renew the oil" as the corrective action for every finding, without finding the source.
  • Reading aluminium without silicon and diagnosing piston wear when the answer is catalytic fines.
  • Failing to distinguish fresh water from sea water when water is found - the chloride or sodium test decides the severity.
  • Quoting numeric limits, feed rates or BN grades from memory. They are maker-specific, and a wrong number is worse than none.

Examiner traps

  • "Your system oil BN is rising. Is that good?" No. In the system oil it usually means over-carry of alkaline cylinder oil into the crankcase, or an excessive feed rate - review the feed rate and the stuffing box.
  • "Viscosity and flash point have both fallen. What is it?" Fuel dilution. Find the leaking injector, pump or high-pressure pipe. Note that it is also a fire risk, not only a lubrication one.
  • "Copper is rising. Which is it - bearings or cooler?" Look at the water content. Copper with water suggests the oil cooler; copper alone points at bearing overlay or bushes.
  • "How do you know the feed rate is right?" By scrape-down analysis - iron content and residual BN in the piston underside drain oil - not by the setting on the panel.
  • "What does an anti-polishing ring do?" It scrapes the carbon deposit from the piston top land at the top of the liner, preventing that deposit from polishing the bore and destroying the honing pattern that retains oil.
  • "Why can an EAL be more troublesome than mineral oil?" Ester base stocks bring different seal compatibility, greater sensitivity to water and possible hydrolysis, and different mixing behaviour with mineral oil.
  • "Spectrographic analysis shows low iron but the magnetic plug is loaded. Which do you believe?" Both - spectrographic analysis under-reports large particles, which is why a PQ index or ferrous debris monitor is read alongside it.

(a) The concept, its significance, and its effect on safety culture

1. What vetting is, and what it is not

Vetting is a commercial risk assessment carried out by a charterer, oil major or terminal operator to decide whether it is prepared to employ a particular tanker for a particular cargo on a particular voyage. It is entirely voluntary in law and is enforced by the market rather than by an Administration.

The distinction from everything else that inspects a tanker is worth stating precisely, because it is the concept the question is asking about:

  • Statutory survey asks: does the ship comply with the conventions? The sanction is legal - an invalid certificate.
  • Classification survey asks: does the ship comply with class rules? The sanction is loss of class.
  • Port State control asks: is this ship substandard, here and now? The sanction is detention.
  • Vetting asks a different question altogether: am I willing to put my cargo, my terminal and my reputation behind this ship and this crew? The sanction is that the ship does not get the fixture.

It arose because after the major tanker pollution casualties the oil majors accepted that a charterer's liability and reputation do not stop at the ship's rail. OCIMF was formed in the wake of that realisation, and its Ship Inspection Report Programme (SIRE) was launched in 1993 to replace a proliferation of duplicated company inspections with one shared report.

2. How the SIRE programme actually works

The mechanism matters, and one feature of it is routinely got wrong. SIRE is an information programme, not an approval scheme.

An accredited inspector, nominated by a submitting member company, attends the ship and completes an inspection report against the standard questionnaire. The report - together with the operator's comments, which the operator has a right to submit - is uploaded to the SIRE database. Recipient members then download it and make their own decision. OCIMF neither approves nor rejects any ship. It supplies the information; the vetting decision belongs to the individual charterer or terminal, against that company's own acceptance criteria.

The commercial value of that design is that one inspection can serve many potential charterers, which is what stops a tanker being inspected by every oil company that might fix her.

3. The instruments

  • VIQ - the Vessel Inspection Questionnaire. The standard question set against which the shipboard inspection is conducted. VIQ7 is the operative edition at this sitting.
  • HVPQ - the Harmonised Vessel Particulars Questionnaire. The standing record of the ship's particulars and equipment, maintained by the operator, which saves the inspection re-establishing what the ship is.
  • TMSA - Tanker Management and Self Assessment, currently in its third edition. This is the crucial complement: SIRE inspects the ship, TMSA assesses the shore-side management system, element by element, against staged levels of achievement with supporting key performance indicators. Vetting has therefore never been only about the ship.
  • CDI operates the parallel scheme for chemical and gas carriers; the question is confined to the oil tanker sector.

4. Significance in the maritime oil trade

For a tanker operator, vetting is not one commercial factor among many - it is close to a licence to trade.

  • Employment. Major charterers will not fix a tanker without an acceptable vetting position, so an adverse report can remove a ship from a whole trade without any regulatory finding against her.
  • Access. Terminals apply their own acceptance criteria before a ship is allowed to berth, so vetting governs where the ship can physically go.
  • Rate and reputation. A strong vetting and TMSA record is a marketing asset; a weak one is priced in.
  • Wider consequence. The same record is read by insurers, financiers and cargo interests, so its effect outlives the individual fixture.

The practical consequence is that the vetting standard, not the statutory minimum, sets the real operating standard in the tanker sector. A ship can be fully certificated, in class and never detained, and still be commercially unemployable.

5. Continuous improvement and safety culture - and the honest criticism

The contribution is real, and it works through four mechanisms:

  • Frequency and independence. A tanker is vetted far more often than she is statutorily surveyed, by inspectors with no relationship to the flag State, the society or the owner. That is a level of external scrutiny no other sector experiences.
  • Closed-loop correction. Observations require a response, and a serious operator routes them into the SMS as non-conformities under ISM element 9, with root-cause analysis and fleet-wide corrective action rather than a one-ship repair.
  • Management maturity, not just hardware. TMSA's staged self-assessment obliges a company to describe how it manages, measure itself against defined levels and show improvement over time - which addresses the causes of incidents rather than their symptoms.
  • Industry learning. Aggregated observations across thousands of inspections reveal trends that no single operator could see, and those trends feed back into OCIMF guidance and into the question sets themselves.

The honest criticism must also be made, because "discuss" invites it. Frequent inspection can produce compliance behaviour rather than safety behaviour: ships prepared for the inspection, paperwork optimised for the questionnaire, and crews drilled on the expected questions. A predictable question set is learnable, and what is learnable can be presented without being lived. There is also genuine inspection burden - vetting, PSC, class, flag, terminal and internal audits arriving on the same crew. That criticism is precisely what the developments in limb (b) are designed to answer, which is why the two limbs of this question belong together.

(b) Emerging trends and advancements

6. SIRE 2.0 - the position at this sitting

The dominant development is SIRE 2.0, OCIMF's rebuilt inspection regime. Its status must be stated accurately.

SIRE 2.0 was being delivered in a four-phase roll-out rather than by a single switch-over: internal testing; a closed beta of the full end-to-end process; an unlimited beta open to all programme users as a transition period, so that submitting companies, operators and recipients could work with the system before full launch; and finally the phase in which VIQ7 is withdrawn. The unlimited beta phase was anticipated to commence towards the end of Q4 2023.

So at this examination, VIQ7 remains the operative SIRE inspection tool and SIRE 2.0 is in transition - being used, tested and prepared for, but not yet the regime. It is an emerging advancement, which is exactly the word limb (b) uses.

8. Data analytics, remote inspection, and the limits

Data analytics is what a structured digital report makes possible, and it works at three levels. At ship level, an operator can trend its own observations by category and by vessel instead of reading reports one at a time. At fleet and company level, the same data supports benchmarking and integrates with TMSA key performance indicators. At industry level, aggregated observations identify emerging risks - and the campaign question mechanism is the direct feedback loop, allowing the industry to interrogate a newly identified risk across every inspection within weeks rather than waiting for the next revision of a questionnaire.

Three further trends belong in the answer:

  • Remote and hybrid inspection. Accelerated by the pandemic and now an accepted technique. The statutory world moved the same way - the 2023 HSSC Survey Guidelines, resolution A.1186(33), expressly took account of the application of remote surveys from experience gained during COVID-19 and from advanced technology.
  • Harmonisation and burden reduction - aligning questionnaires, sharing data between schemes and reusing standing particulars so that one crew is not asked the same questions by five organisations.
  • Broadening scope - cyber security, crew welfare and human element considerations entering assessment criteria alongside hardware.

The limits should be stated, because a discussion question rewards judgement. Analytics inherits the quality of the input, and an inconsistent observation is not improved by aggregation. The data is commercially sensitive, which constrains how widely it can be shared. Risk-based targeting can create blind spots in whatever the model treats as low risk. And a tablet does not open a crankcase: the inspector's judgement remains the instrument, and the technology's real achievement is to make that judgement consistent, evidenced and comparable rather than to replace it.

Understand this first

A fault tree is a way of being systematic about the question every engineer asks after a breakdown: how could this possibly have happened? Instead of listing everything that might go wrong, you start with the one failure you cannot tolerate and work backwards, one honest level at a time, asking what would have to be true immediately beneath. The gates are the whole analysis: OR means any one of these alone does it, AND means they all have to happen together - which is what redundancy looks like when you draw it. Once the tree is finished you look for the places where a single circle, on its own, reaches the top. Those are your critical items, and on a ship that list is not academic: it is exactly what the ISM Code obliges the Company to identify and to keep testing. The second half of this question also hides a small trap - the examiner's own words describe two different failures, and noticing that is itself the first step of the method.

Model written answer

(a) Fault Tree Analysis

1. What FTA is, and how it differs from the alternatives

Fault Tree Analysis is a deductive, top-down technique. It begins with one precisely defined undesired event - the top event - and works backwards through the immediate, necessary and sufficient causes of that event, level by level, until it reaches events which need no further development. The logic connecting the levels is Boolean, expressed through gates.

The direction of travel is what distinguishes it, and naming the alternatives earns the mark:

  • FTA - deductive, from one failure backwards to its many causes. Answers: how can this happen?
  • FMEA - inductive, from each component forwards to its effects. Answers: what happens if this fails? It is exhaustive on components but poor at combinations.
  • Event Tree Analysis - inductive, from an initiating event forwards through the success or failure of each barrier, to the range of outcomes.
  • HAZOP - guideword-driven, applied to a process or design rather than to a defined failure.

FTA is the right tool when there is one failure you care about very much and you need to know every way it can arise - a blackout, loss of steering, failure of the emergency fire pump to start on demand. It is the wrong tool for a general survey of everything that could go wrong; FMEA does that better.

2. Symbols and the two gates

SymbolMeaning
RectangleThe top event, and every intermediate event - a fault developed further by a gate below it.
CircleA basic event - a primary failure requiring no further development. The resolution limit of the tree.
DiamondAn undeveloped event - one which could be developed but is not, for want of information or because it is outside the scope.
HouseA house or external event - something normally expected to occur, or a boundary condition switched on or off to test the tree.
TriangleTransfer in and out - a branch continued on another sheet, which is how a large tree stays readable.
AND gateThe output occurs only if ALL inputs occur. An AND gate is the signature of redundancy.
OR gateThe output occurs if ANY input occurs. An OR gate is the signature of a series dependency - and of a single-point failure.

An INHIBIT gate, drawn as a hexagon with a conditioning event alongside, passes its input only when a stated condition holds - useful where a failure only matters in a particular operating mode.

3. How a tree is built

  1. Define the top event precisely, with its boundary conditions - which system, which operating mode, what counts as failure. "Engine trouble" is not a top event; "main engine fails to start on demand from the bridge, at sea, in remote control" is.
  2. Identify the immediate, necessary and sufficient causes of that event - the level directly beneath it, and no lower. Skipping a level is the commonest drafting error and it hides gates.
  3. Choose the gate - OR if any one cause alone produces the event, AND if all are required together.
  4. Develop each intermediate event the same way, one level at a time.
  5. Stop at the resolution limit - a component failure, a human error or an external event that you will not or cannot analyse further. Draw it as a basic event, or as a diamond if it is merely undeveloped.
  6. Evaluate - qualitatively by minimal cut sets, and quantitatively only if credible failure data exists.

4. What the finished tree is used for

A cut set is any combination of basic events which together cause the top event. A minimal cut set is one from which no event can be removed without the combination ceasing to cause the top event. The order of a cut set is the number of basic events in it, and the order is the whole point:

  • an order-1 minimal cut set is a single-point failure - one basic event, on its own, defeats the system. Finding these is the primary value of the technique;
  • a low-order cut set concentrates risk; a high-order one implies real redundancy;
  • a basic event appearing in many cut sets is a common-cause candidate, and common cause is what makes nominal redundancy illusory - two pumps on one switchboard, two systems in one flooded space.

On board, the outputs are practical. The single-point failures identify critical equipment - which is the judgement ISM Code element 10.3 requires, and it drives the specific reliability measures and the regular testing of stand-by arrangements not in continuous use that the same paragraph demands. The tree also supports incident investigation under element 9, redundancy decisions at design and retrofit, and the risk analysis step of a Formal Safety Assessment. Quantification is optional and depends on failure data the ship does not usually hold; the qualitative tree is valuable without it.

(b) A fault tree for the printed top event

5. Resolving the printed top event

The printed question defines two different failures and this must be dealt with, not ignored. It names the top event "Engine Starting Failure" and then glosses it as "the engine speed goes down and finally stops" - which is a running engine losing speed and stopping, not a failure to start.

The safe response, and the one taken here, is to state the ambiguity in one line and develop both under a single top event: MAIN ENGINE UNAVAILABLE TO PROPEL - fails to start on demand, or having started, loses speed and stops. The two share their fuel, control and support branches, so the tree is not doubled by covering both. Boundary conditions: main propulsion engine, at sea, bridge or engine-room control, no external damage assumed.

The top gate is an OR - any one branch alone produces the top event.

6. Branch A - starting air and admission

ENGINE FAILS TO TURN ON AIR — via OR:

  • Insufficient starting air pressure — OR: air compressors fail to deliver (compressor mechanical failure; motor or starter failure; power loss); receiver isolated or drained; system leakage; non-return valve failure; repeated failed start attempts exhausting the receivers.
  • Main starting air valve does not open — OR: control air pressure low; solenoid or pilot valve failure; valve seized; blocked control air line.
  • Starting air distributor fault — OR: distributor timing incorrect; pilot air passage blocked; distributor seized.
  • Cylinder starting air valve fails to open — OR: valve seized; spring failure; air supply to that valve blocked.
  • Interlock not satisfied — OR: turning gear engaged; main starting valve in shut or blocked position; control air low; auxiliary blower not running where required; control lever not in the correct position.
  • Engine turns but does not fire — develops into Branch B (transfer).

Note that most of these sit under OR gates - each is a single-point failure. Where two compressors and two receivers are fitted, that sub-branch becomes an AND gate and the failure order rises. That contrast is exactly what the tree is drawn to reveal.

7. Branch B - fuel and combustion

NO OR INSUFFICIENT COMBUSTION — via OR:

  • Fuel not reaching the injection pumps — OR: service tank empty or valve shut; supply or booster pump failure (mechanical, or motor and power loss); filters blocked; air locked in the system; water in the fuel; fuel too viscous through heater or trace-heating failure; wax at low temperature on a distillate.
  • Fuel not injected — OR: injection pump defect or plunger seizure; injector nozzle blocked or lifting pressure incorrect; high-pressure pipe fracture; on an electronically controlled engine, servo or control oil pressure low, or a control system fault.
  • Fuel rack at zero — OR: governor failure or hunting; actuator or linkage failure; a shutdown or trip signal commanding zero fuel; emergency stop operated locally or from the bridge; remote control system fault; fuel limiter acting.
  • Combustion conditions inadequate — OR: insufficient scavenge air; low compression from leaking piston rings, exhaust or starting air valves; charge air cooler flooded or fouled.

8. Branch C - trips, load and support systems

This is the branch the printed gloss - speed going down and the engine stopping - actually describes.

ENGINE SHUTS DOWN OR IS SLOWED WHILE RUNNING — via OR:

  • Automatic safety shutdown or slowdown operates — OR: lubricating oil low pressure; jacket cooling water high temperature or low pressure; piston cooling failure; thrust bearing high temperature; oil mist detector high; overspeed; camshaft or servo oil low pressure; and, on each of these, the possibility of a spurious trip from a faulty sensor or transmitter rather than a real condition.
  • Support system lost — OR: blackout, taking lubricating oil, cooling water and fuel supply pumps with it; control air failure; sea water cooling failure through a blocked suction, a failed pump or a fouled cooler.
  • Excessive load — OR: heavy running propeller from a fouled hull or a damaged blade; severe weather; shallow water; ice.
  • Turbocharger failure or surging, reducing scavenge air and torque - which then reappears in Branch B under inadequate combustion conditions.
  • Mechanical distress — OR: bearing wipe or seizure; piston or liner seizure; broken piston rod or coupling.
  • Fuel supply interrupted while running - transfer to Branch B.

Human and organisational events sit across all three branches and belong in the tree rather than in a footnote: indicator cocks left open, wrong control station selected, turning gear left engaged, incorrect starting procedure, tanks not changed over or topped up, a trip not reset, maintenance not carried out.

9. Reading the finished tree

The value is in what the structure shows once it is drawn.

  • The tree is dominated by OR gates, which means the top event has many order-1 minimal cut sets - many single-point failures. That is the correct and sobering finding for a single-screw ship.
  • Where the design provides genuine redundancy the gate becomes AND - two air compressors, two receivers, duplicated lubricating oil pumps, two generators on the board - and the cut set order rises accordingly. Comparing the OR-heavy branches with the AND branches is precisely how a fault tree justifies a redundancy decision.
  • Common cause is the trap. Two lubricating oil pumps fed from one switchboard, or two air receivers charged by compressors on one starter panel, are drawn as an AND gate but behave as an OR. A basic event appearing in many cut sets should be examined for exactly this.
  • Control and instrumentation appear more often than machinery. A spurious trip stops the engine as effectively as a real fault, which is why trip testing, sensor calibration and override philosophy carry so much weight.
  • The output is a work list. Every order-1 cut set is a candidate for critical equipment under ISM 10.3, and the stand-by arrangements it identifies are exactly the ones that paragraph requires to be regularly tested.

In the examination, draw this with the standard symbols - rectangles for the top and intermediate events, circles for basic events, and the AND and OR gates clearly marked - and use transfer triangles for the fuel branch rather than drawing it twice.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed split is 8 + 8, and the two limbs need completely different treatment. Limb (a) is theory and is best answered with a symbol table and a numbered method - it is bookwork and should be written quickly and precisely. Limb (b) is a drawing, and eight marks for a drawing means the examiner is counting branches, gates and levels.

The single most valuable habit in limb (b) is to develop breadth before depth. Get the three or four main branches down under the top gate first, then fill each one. A tree with three well-developed branches scores far better than one branch analysed to exhaustion while the others are missing - and if time runs out, a broad shallow tree still shows you understand the method.

The printed top event contradicts itself - say so

This is the discriminating feature of the question and most candidates will not notice it. The examiner names the top event "Engine Starting Failure" and then defines it as "the engine speed goes down and finally stops". Those are two different failures: one is an engine that will not start, the other is a running engine that stops.

Do not choose one and hope. Do not silently correct the examiner. State the ambiguity in a single line and develop both under one top event - main engine unavailable to propel: fails to start, or having started, loses speed and stops. The two failures share their fuel, control and support-system branches, so covering both costs far less than it looks, and it demonstrates exactly the precision about top-event definition that limb (a) says is the first step of the method. That is a mark for reading the question.

AND and OR are the whole of the analysis

Everything a fault tree tells you comes out of which gate you drew. OR means any one input alone causes the output - a series dependency, and a single-point failure. AND means all inputs are required - which is what redundancy looks like on paper.

So an engine tree that is almost entirely OR gates is not a badly drawn tree; it is a true statement about a single-screw ship. The interesting places are the few AND gates - two compressors, two receivers, duplicated pumps - and the places where an AND gate is a lie because of common cause. Two lubricating oil pumps on one switchboard are drawn as AND and behave as OR. Say that in an oral and the examiner will know you have used the technique rather than read about it.

Cut sets, and why order matters

A cut set is any set of basic events that together cause the top event; a minimal cut set is one you cannot remove anything from. The order is how many events it contains.

Order 1 is a single-point failure. Finding those is the main reason to draw the tree at all, and on board it feeds straight into the critical equipment list required by ISM element 10.3 - which is also why that paragraph insists on regular testing of stand-by arrangements not in continuous use. A basic event that turns up in many cut sets is a common-cause suspect. Connect the tree to ISM 10.3 explicitly: it converts an academic answer into a management-level one.

Common mistakes

  • Not noticing that the printed top event describes two different failures.
  • Defining the top event loosely. "Engine problem" cannot be developed; a precise event with boundary conditions can.
  • Skipping levels - jumping from the top event straight to basic events. It hides the gates, which are the analysis.
  • Drawing the tree with no gates at all, as a mind map of causes. Without AND and OR it is not a fault tree.
  • Using AND where OR is meant. If either cause alone stops the engine, it is OR.
  • Analysing one branch to exhaustion and never drawing the others. Breadth first.
  • Forgetting human and organisational events - turning gear engaged, indicator cocks open, wrong control station, trip not reset.
  • Forgetting spurious trips. A faulty sensor stops the engine exactly as effectively as a real low oil pressure.
  • Confusing FTA with FMEA. FTA works backwards from one failure; FMEA works forwards from every component.
  • Quoting failure probabilities. Quantitative FTA needs plant-specific data no candidate holds.

Examiner traps

  • "Is FTA inductive or deductive?" Deductive - top-down, from one defined failure backwards to its causes. FMEA and event tree analysis are inductive.
  • "What does a diamond mean, and how does it differ from a circle?" A circle is a basic event, developed no further because it needs no further development. A diamond is an undeveloped event - it could be developed but is not, for want of data or because it lies outside the scope.
  • "What is a minimal cut set of order one?" A single-point failure. One basic event alone causes the top event.
  • "You have two lubricating oil pumps and drew an AND gate. Is the engine safe?" Only if the redundancy is real. If both pumps take power from the same switchboard, the common cause defeats the AND gate.
  • "Your engine stopped and every parameter was normal." Suspect a spurious trip - a faulty sensor or transmitter - or a control system fault. That branch belongs in the tree.
  • "What would you do with the completed tree on board?" Extract the single-point failures, use them to build or challenge the critical equipment list under ISM 10.3, and set the testing regime for stand-by arrangements accordingly.
  • "When would you use FMEA instead?" When surveying a whole system for the effects of every component failure, rather than investigating one specific failure you already care about.

Model written answer

1. The Code, and what made a recommendatory text mandatory

The IMO Instruments Implementation Code (III Code) is the annex to resolution A.1070(28), adopted by the Assembly on 4 December 2013. Its operative paragraph 3 revoked resolution A.1054(27), the Code for the Implementation of Mandatory IMO Instruments, 2011, which had underpinned the earlier voluntary audit scheme.

The Code reads throughout in the language of recommendation - it says what a State "should" do - and candidates are often unable to explain why such a text binds anyone. The answer is that the Code was never redrafted into obligation language; the conventions were amended to require its use. The chain is recited in the preamble to A.1187(33):

  • A.1083(28), A.1084(28) and A.1085(28) amended Load Lines 1966, Tonnage 1969 and COLREG 1972;
  • MSC.366(93), MSC.373(93), MSC.374(93) and MSC.375(93) amended SOLAS 1974, STCW 1978, the STCW Code and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol;
  • MEPC.246(66) and MEPC.247(66) amended MARPOL and its 1997 Protocol.

That chain is what makes the Code the standard against which a Member State is audited under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme. The Code has four parts - Part 1 Common Areas, Part 2 Flag States, Part 3 Coastal States, Part 4 Port States - and each of Parts 2, 3 and 4 ends with an Evaluation and review section. Limb (b) is simply the flag State's evaluation section opened up.

2. The objective of the Code

Paragraph 1 states it in a single sentence: to enhance global maritime safety and protection of the marine environment, and to assist States in the implementation of instruments of the Organization. The second half is dropped by most candidates and it is half the sentence - the Code is an assistance instrument, written because the Organization accepted that some States face real difficulty in complying fully.

Paragraph 2 qualifies it immediately. States will view the Code according to their own circumstances and should be bound only for the implementation of those instruments to which they are Contracting Governments or Parties; by virtue of geography and circumstance some States act principally as flag States, others as coastal or port States. The Code is therefore not a uniform checklist applied identically everywhere.

Two general duties frame everything that follows. Paragraph 4 makes States responsible for promulgating laws and regulations and taking all other steps necessary to give those instruments full and complete effect. Paragraph 5 requires that, in acting against pollution, a State must not transfer damage or hazards from one area to another, nor transform one type of pollution into another.

3. The strategy recommended to the State

Paragraph 3 answers the second half of limb (a). To meet the objective a State is recommended to:

  1. develop an overall strategy to ensure that its international obligations and responsibilities as a flag, port and coastal State are met (3.1);
  2. establish a methodology to monitor and assess that the strategy ensures effective implementation and enforcement of the relevant international mandatory instruments (3.2); and
  3. continuously review the strategy to achieve, maintain and improve overall organizational performance and capability as a flag, port and coastal State (3.3).

Read together those three are a develop - monitor - review cycle, not a document written once and filed. Paragraph 9 completes it: the State should communicate its strategy, including information on its national legislation, to all concerned.

Paragraph 7 then says what the legislation and administrative procedures must actually address - nine areas: jurisdiction; organization and authority; legislation, rules and regulations; promulgation of the applicable mandatory instruments; enforcement arrangements; control, survey, inspection, audit, verification, approval and certification functions; the selection, recognition, authorization, empowerment and monitoring of recognized organizations and nominated surveyors; investigations required to be reported to the Organization; and reporting to the Organization and other Administrations. Paragraph 8 adds the three initial actions a Government must be capable of when a new or amended instrument enters into force: the ability to promulgate laws giving effective jurisdiction and control, a legal basis for enforcement including investigative and penal processes, and sufficient personnel with maritime expertise.

4. Which IMO instruments the Code covers

This limb has two correct answers and they are not the same, which is where the marks are usually lost.

First, the Code's own scope. Paragraph 6 says the Code addresses those aspects necessary to give full and complete effect to the applicable international instruments pertaining to six subject areas: safety of life at sea; prevention of pollution from ships; standards of training, certification and watchkeeping for seafarers; load lines; tonnage measurement of ships; and regulations for preventing collisions at sea.

Note what is absent. Maritime security is not one of the six. The resolution's preamble speaks of safety, security and protection of the marine environment, but the operative scope at paragraph 6 does not extend to the ISPS Code or SOLAS chapter XI-2.

Second, the treaty instruments under which use of the Code is mandatory, which therefore define the audit scope. These are listed in resolution A.1187(33), the 2023 Non-exhaustive list of obligations under instruments relevant to the III Code, adopted on 6 December 2023 - one month before this examination. They are SOLAS 1974 with its 1978 and 1988 Protocols; MARPOL and its 1997 Protocol; STCW 1978 and the STCW Code; Load Lines 1966 and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol; Tonnage 1969; and COLREG 1972.

Two features of A.1187(33) are worth carrying into the answer. Its operative paragraph 4 revoked A.1157(32), the 2021 list, so material citing the 2021 list is superseded at this sitting. And its annex is arranged in seven annexes - obligations of Contracting Governments and Parties; specific flag, coastal and port State obligations; instruments made mandatory under IMO conventions; a summary of amendments; and, as Annex 7, amendments expected to be accepted and to enter into force between 1 January 2024 and 1 July 2024. At this sitting that last annex is a forward-looking list - a clean illustration that adopted is not the same as in force.

5. Key performance indicators for flag State evaluation

Paragraph 42 requires a flag State to evaluate its performance on a periodic basis with respect to the administrative processes, procedures and resources needed to meet its obligations. Paragraph 43 then lists the measures, inter alia:

  • port State control detention rates;
  • flag State inspection results;
  • casualty statistics;
  • communication and information processes;
  • annual loss statistics, excluding constructive total losses (CTLs); and
  • such other performance indicators as may be appropriate.

Quoting the stated purpose earns the mark, because it shows the paragraph has been read rather than remembered: the indicators exist in order to determine whether staffing, resources and administrative procedures are adequate to meet its flag State obligations. They are for the State's own self-evaluation, not a return submitted to IMO.

Paragraph 44 then recommends ten areas for regular review: fleet loss and accident ratios to identify trends over selected periods; the number of verified cases of detained ships in relation to the size of the fleet; verified cases of incompetence or wrongdoing by holders of certificates or endorsements issued under its authority; responses to port State deficiency reports or interventions; investigations into very serious and serious casualties and the lessons learned; technical and other resources committed; results of inspections, surveys and controls of ships in the fleet; investigation of occupational accidents; the number of incidents and violations under the applicable pollution prevention regulations; and the number of suspensions or withdrawals of certificates, endorsements or approvals.

6. Flag State responsibilities

Part 2 is the longest part of the Code, paragraphs 15 to 44, and its shape is implementation - delegation - enforcement - surveyors - investigations - review.

  • Implementation (15 to 17). Issue national legislation and guidance implementing the safety and pollution conventions, and assign responsibility for keeping it updated. Paragraph 16 requires a safety and environmental protection programme with five minimum elements, of which the load-bearing one is 16.2 - compliance verified by an audit and inspection programme independent of any body issuing the certificates. Paragraph 17 requires ships to be sufficiently and efficiently manned, on the Principles of Safe Manning.
  • Delegation (18 to 21). A flag State authorising a recognized organization must determine that it has adequate technical, managerial and research capability; base the authorisation on a formal written agreement; issue specific instructions for a ship found unfit to proceed to sea; supply its national law and interpretations; and require records to be kept. Paragraph 20 requires an oversight programme with supplementary surveys and competent supervising staff.
  • Enforcement (22 to 27). Prohibit a ship from sailing until it complies; inspect periodically to verify that the actual condition of ship and crew matches the certificates; verify seafarer familiarity and the crew's ability to coordinate in an emergency; provide penalties of adequate severity; and institute proceedings after investigation irrespective of where the violation occurred. Paragraphs 26 and 27 permit certification only after determining that all applicable requirements are met.
  • Surveyors and investigations (28 to 41). Surveyors qualified against a defined standard, worked under a documented qualification system and issued an identification document. Marine safety investigations by impartial and objective investigators, taking account of the Casualty Investigation Code, with the report forwarded to the Organization together with the flag State's observations.

7. Coastal State and port State responsibilities

Parts 3 and 4 open on the same principle, at paragraphs 45 and 52: coastal and port States have rights as well as obligations, and when they exercise those rights they incur additional obligations. Both must implement policy through national legislation and guidance, and ensure their procedures deliver consistent implementation and verification.

Coastal State (Part 3). Paragraph 48 lists the rights, obligations and responsibilities, inter alia: radiocommunication services; meteorological services and warnings; search and rescue services; hydrographic services; ships' routeing; ship reporting systems; vessel traffic services; and aids to navigation - eight service areas. Note their character: they are services to shipping, not controls over ships. Paragraph 50 requires a control and monitoring programme allocating statistical data for trend analysis, mechanisms for timely response to pollution incidents in its waters, and cooperation with flag and port States in casualty investigations.

Port State (Part 4). Paragraph 53 records that the port State role derives from a combination of treaties, conventions, national laws and in some cases bilateral and multilateral agreements. Paragraph 56 gives three obligations: provision of appropriate reception facilities or the capability to accept all waste streams regulated under IMO instruments; port State control; and keeping a register of fuel oil suppliers. Paragraph 59 carries the no more favourable treatment principle - the conditions of the instruments are imposed on ships of non-parties as well as parties. Paragraphs 60 and 61 require a PSC programme consistent with the relevant Assembly resolution - at this sitting that is resolution A.1185(33), the Procedures for Port State Control, 2023, adopted 6 December 2023 and revoking A.1155(32) - carried out only by authorized and qualified port State control officers. Paragraph 62 requires those officers to be free from commercial, financial and other pressures, to hold no commercial interest in the port, the ships inspected, repair facilities or support services, and not to be employed by or work on behalf of recognized organizations or classification societies.

Both parts close as Part 2 does: paragraphs 51 and 63 require the coastal State and the port State to periodically evaluate their own performance. That symmetry - implement, enforce, evaluate - is the single most useful thing to carry into the examination hall, because it lets you structure all three States from one remembered shape.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed marks are 6 + 4 + 6, and limb (a) hides four tasks inside its six: what this Code is, its objective, its recommended strategy, and which instruments it covers. Splitting limb (a) across route steps 2, 3 and 4, with the framing in step 1, is what stops the six marks being spent on a single paragraph about the objective.

Limb (b) is only four marks but has a precise source - paragraphs 43 and 44 - so a candidate who names the indicators in the Code's own words banks those marks quickly and buys time for limb (c). Limb (c) covers three States in six marks, roughly two each, and the Code makes that tractable because Parts 2, 3 and 4 share one skeleton: implementation, enforcement, evaluation and review. Answer all three on that skeleton and the examiner sees structure rather than a list.

The mandatory-application chain, and why it matters

Candidates routinely assert that "the III Code is mandatory" without being able to say why a text written entirely in "should" binds anybody. The answer is that the Code was never redrafted into obligation language; the conventions were amended to require its use. That is why it still reads as recommendation throughout.

Being able to give the chain in one sentence - A.1083, A.1084 and A.1085(28) for Load Lines, Tonnage and COLREG; MSC.366, MSC.373, MSC.374 and MSC.375(93) for SOLAS, STCW, the STCW Code and the 1988 Load Lines Protocol; MEPC.246 and MEPC.247(66) for MARPOL and its 1997 Protocol - distinguishes a candidate who has opened the instrument from one who has read about it. The chain is recited in the preamble to A.1187(33), so it is easy to check and easy to learn.

The obligations list is ONE MONTH old at this sitting

This is the currency point of the question and it is unusually sharp here. Resolution A.1187(33) was adopted on 6 December 2023 - one month before this examination - and its operative paragraph 4 revoked A.1157(32), the 2021 list. Any material citing the 2021 list is superseded at this sitting.

There is a second, better point available. A.1187(33)'s annex states that it takes account of amendments which have, or are expected to have, entered into force by or on 1 July 2024, and its Annex 7 is headed amendments expected to be accepted and to enter into force between 1 January 2024 and 1 July 2024. At a January 2024 sitting that annex is looking forward - it lists things that have not happened yet. If you want one sentence that proves you understand the difference between adopted and in force, that is it.

The six-versus-instruments trap, in detail

The printed words are "Which all IMO instruments covered in the code". Two different things are being asked at once and the safe answer gives both.

The Code's scope at paragraph 6 is expressed as six subject areas and does not name a single convention: safety of life at sea; prevention of pollution from ships; training, certification and watchkeeping; load lines; tonnage measurement; and preventing collisions at sea. The instruments under which the Code is mandatory are a different list entirely - the SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, Load Lines, Tonnage and COLREG family with their Protocols, set out in A.1187(33). Give both, and say why they differ.

A count is risky. The audit scope is commonly summarised as nine mandatory instruments - SOLAS 1974, SOLAS PROT 1988, MARPOL, MARPOL PROT 1997, STCW 1978, LL 1966, LL PROT 1988, TONNAGE 1969 and COLREG 1972 - but A.1187(33) also carries a SOLAS PROT 1978 column, which is why ten also circulates, and neither resolution states a number anywhere. List them; do not lead with a count. If pressed, say "the SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, Load Lines, Tonnage and COLREG instruments together with their Protocols, commonly summarised as nine".

The absence of maritime security from paragraph 6 is a genuine examinable point, not a drafting slip: the preamble uses the phrase "safety, security and protection of the marine environment" while the operative scope does not extend to security instruments.

Common mistakes

  • Writing a general essay on flag State duties under UNCLOS article 94. That is a different question. The examiner has named a specific Code and wants its paragraphs.
  • Answering "which instruments" with the six subject areas only, or with the convention list only. Give both and explain why they differ.
  • Confusing the III Code with the ISM Code. The III Code binds States; the ISM Code binds companies and ships.
  • Treating the performance indicators as a return made to IMO. They are for the State's own periodic self-evaluation under paragraph 42.
  • Giving the coastal State a control-and-inspection role. Paragraph 48 is a list of services - SAR, hydrography, VTS, aids to navigation.
  • Citing A.1054(27) as current. It was revoked by A.1070(28).
  • Citing the 2021 obligations list, A.1157(32). It was revoked by A.1187(33) one month before this sitting.
  • Leading with a count of mandatory instruments. Neither resolution states one.
  • Spending half the answer on limb (c) because it is familiar. It is worth six marks, the same as limb (a).

Examiner traps

  • Six subject areas versus the instrument list. The single most likely place to lose marks on this question.
  • "Should" throughout the Code. Asked whether the Code is mandatory, the answer is that its use is mandatory under the amended conventions while the text remains recommendatory in form.
  • Excluding constructive total losses. Paragraph 43 says annual loss statistics excluding CTLs. Reproducing that qualifier shows the source has been read.
  • Two independence requirements, and do not merge them. The flag State's audit and inspection programme must be independent of the body issuing the certificates (16.2); port State control officers must be free of commercial interest and must not work for recognized organizations or classification societies (62). Different Parts, different purposes.
  • "Which PSC procedures apply?" At this sitting, A.1185(33), the Procedures for Port State Control 2023, adopted 6 December 2023 and revoking A.1155(32).
  • "Name the three port State obligations." Reception facilities, port State control, and a register of fuel oil suppliers. The register is the one candidates forget.
  • "What does Annex 7 of A.1187(33) contain, and why is that interesting today?" Amendments expected to enter into force between 1 January and 1 July 2024 - which at this sitting have not yet done so.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Is the III Code mandatory, and how? Use the amendment chain.
  • What is IMSAS and what does it audit against? The Code supplies the auditable areas; A.1187(33) supplies the obligations list.
  • Your flag State has a rising detention rate. What does the Code expect it to do? Paragraphs 43 and 44 - treat it as a performance indicator, review detentions against fleet size and responses to PSC deficiency reports, and test whether staffing and resources are adequate.
