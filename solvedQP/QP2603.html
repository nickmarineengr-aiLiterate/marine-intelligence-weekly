Understand this first

The single idea behind this question is that agreeing the words and being bound by them are two separate events, often years apart. A conference settles the text - that is adoption, and at that moment the convention binds nobody at all. Each State then decides separately whether to take it on, and the treaty itself specifies how many must do so, and how much of the world's shipping they must represent, before it becomes law for anyone. That second condition is why a technical convention carries a tonnage threshold: a safety rule in force only among States that own no ships would achieve nothing. The two terms named in the question are simply two routes to the same destination - one where a State signs first and confirms later, once its parliament has passed the legislation it needs, and one where it never signed at all and joins afterwards in a single step. And because the gap between adoption and force can run to a decade, conventions carry a faster machinery for amending themselves.

Model written answer

1. Adoption is not entry into force

A convention is adopted either by the competent IMO organ - Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text and does no more: it binds no State, and an adopted convention may never enter into force at all. The text is then opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General of IMO as depositary.

2. How a State expresses consent to be bound

Between adoption and entry into force sits the step that matters: each State must express its consent to be bound, by one of three routes -

  • signature alone, where the treaty so provides or the negotiating States so agreed;
  • signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, the normal course for multilateral conventions;
  • accession, where the State did not sign within the signature period.

In the second and third cases the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument with the depositary, who records it, counts it towards the entry into force condition and notifies all other States.

3. The two terms the question names

(ii) Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval. The State signs, but signature does not bind it; it indicates an intention to proceed. The State becomes a party only on later depositing an instrument of ratification. Its modern purpose is to give the State time to enact domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements before committing internationally. Acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names, used where a constitution does not demand the fuller procedure: VCLT Article 14(2) provides that consent is expressed by acceptance or approval under conditions similar to those which apply to ratification. In legal effect the three are identical.

(i) Accession. The means by which a State becomes party to a treaty it did not sign - because the signature period closed, or it was not a negotiating State. It is a single step: no prior signature, simply deposit of an instrument of accession, permitted by VCLT Article 15 where the treaty so provides. Once deposited it has exactly the same legal effect as ratification. The difference is the route, not the destination.

4. The entry into force provision, and why it matters

Every convention contains a final clause fixing entry into force, normally a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. Its importance is fourfold:

  • it prevents an instrument binding a handful of States while the rest operate to another standard, which would distort competition;
  • it fixes a certain date from which obligations, surveys and certificates run, so Administrations, ROs, owners and yards work to one deadline;
  • it provides the interval for flag States to legislate, class to develop rules and industry to build or retrofit;
  • it gives legitimacy - a convention in force across most world tonnage is enforceable through port State control against the remainder, which is what makes no more favourable treatment work.

5. Conventions of an important technical nature

For a convention of important technical nature - one dictating construction, equipment or operation - States observe a stiffer double condition: a number of States and a percentage of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping. A count of States alone could be met by many small flags while the major fleets stayed outside, leaving the convention in force on paper and irrelevant at sea.

  • SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, requiring not less than 25 States whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; in force 25 May 1980.
  • The 1978 MARPOL Protocol - in force twelve months after not less than fifteen States constituting not less than fifty per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage had become Parties.

Older conventions typically required two-thirds of contracting States, which occasionally meant more acceptances were needed to bring an instrument into force than to amend it.

6. Keeping the convention current once in force

A technical convention is worthless if it cannot keep pace, and explicit acceptance failed on that point: amendments to the 1960 SOLAS Convention adopted between 1966 and 1973 never received enough acceptances and none entered into force.

IMO's answer was the tacit acceptance procedure, used in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL and the collision regulations. It reverses the default: an amendment to the technical annexes is deemed accepted on a specified date unless objections are received beforehand from an agreed number of Parties. For SOLAS 1974 an amendment is deemed accepted at the end of two years from communication to Contracting Governments, unless objected to by more than one-third of Contracting Governments, or by Governments whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross merchant tonnage. Amendments now enter into force typically within 18 to 24 months. Once in force internationally each flag State must still give the instrument effect domestically - in India through the Merchant Shipping Act and rules made under it - and it is that domestic instrument which is enforced against the ship.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no limbs but makes four separate demands, and the commonest failure is to answer the first and drift. Following one instrument through its life from adoption to amendment answers all four in a single narrative, and putting the two named terms side by side in step 3 lets the contrast be drawn explicitly - which is what an explain the terms instruction is asking for.

Why the machinery looks so cumbersome

A treaty binds sovereign States, and no State can be bound without its own consent - that single constitutional fact generates the whole apparatus. Adoption cannot bind, because the conference does not speak for any government. Signature cannot ordinarily bind, because most constitutions require a legislature to act before the executive can commit. Hence the two-step route, the deposited instrument, the depositary who counts, and the threshold that has to be reached before anyone is obliged to do anything. Tacit acceptance is the one place where the system bends: consent is still required, but silence is treated as consent, and that inversion is what made a technical convention maintainable.

Why this matters to a Chief Engineer

It explains a situation seen constantly in practice: an amendment has been adopted, the industry press is full of it, class has issued guidance - and it does not yet apply to the ship. It also explains why a ship may be subject to a requirement her flag State has not ratified, through port State control and the no more favourable treatment principle, and why the certificate on the bulkhead is issued under national law rather than under the convention directly. The IMO Net-Zero Framework - the draft new MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 5, which was approved but not adopted when the extraordinary MEPC session adjourned in October 2025 - is a live illustration of an instrument stalled between approval and adoption. It is examined directly in QP2602 Q8.

Four words that are not synonyms

  • Adoption - the text is settled. Nobody is bound.
  • Signature - intent indicated. Ordinarily still nobody is bound.
  • Ratification / acceptance / approval - a signatory State becomes bound on deposit. The three differ in domestic formality only.
  • Accession - a non-signatory State becomes bound on deposit, in one step.

And separately: entry into force of the convention is not entry into force for a particular State, which usually follows some months after that State deposits its own instrument.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a convention is in force once adopted, or once signed.
  • Using ratification and accession as synonyms, or inventing a difference between ratification, acceptance and approval.
  • Giving only a number of States for a technical convention and omitting the tonnage percentage - the double condition is the point of that part of the question.
  • Describing tacit acceptance as the way a convention enters into force. It is the way an amendment does.
  • Forgetting that the flag State must still legislate domestically.
  • Confusing a reservation with a declaration, or asserting that reservations are freely available - many technical conventions restrict them.

Examiner traps

  • 'State the provision and its importance' - two things. Most candidates state the provision and never say why it exists.
  • 'For a convention of important technical nature' is a specific category, and the expected answer is the double condition with a real example. A general answer scores poorly here.
  • The two terms are printed as (i) and (ii) with no marks attached, which tempts candidates to treat them as an afterthought. They are a named, explicit demand and should be answered properly.

Model written answer

1. Why roll governs container securing

The forces a lashing system must hold - racking of the container frame, tipping, tension in lashing rods, shear and tension in twistlocks - are driven overwhelmingly by transverse acceleration, which rises with both roll angle and roll acceleration. A stack safe at 15 degrees can exceed its permissible racking load well before 30. Roll is therefore the governing design case, and keeping the ship out of excessive roll is itself a securing measure.

2. The dynamic failure modes the software assesses

  • Parametric rolling - the container-ship problem. Large bow flare and a wide flat stern make the waterplane, and therefore GM, vary substantially between wave crest and trough. In head or following seas, when the roll period is roughly twice the wave encounter period, that periodic stiffness variation pumps energy into roll and large angles build in a few cycles, with no beam sea to warn of it.
  • Synchronous rolling - roll period close to the encounter period in beam or quartering seas.
  • Excessive acceleration - a stiff ship with high GM and heavy containers stowed high has a short roll period and severe lateral accelerations even at modest angles.
  • Pure loss of stability, and surf-riding and broaching in following seas.
  • These five are the failure modes of the Second Generation Intact Stability Criteria (MSC.1/Circ.1627), which at this date are interim guidelines, not a mandatory SOLAS requirement.

3. What the software does with them

The criteria apply in three levels: levels 1 and 2 screen a design by empirical formulae and simplified models, and level 3, direct stability assessment, simulates the ship in irregular waves in the time domain across the range of loading conditions, headings, speeds and sea states. The output is not a pass mark but two practical products: operational limitations - a restricted GM range, a limiting sea state, stowage constraints written into the loading manual - and operational guidance, typically a polar diagram of speed against relative heading marking the combinations to avoid.

4. How it is used on board

  • The loading and lashing computer calculates the accelerations for the actual planned condition and checks the resulting forces against the permissible values in the Cargo Securing Manual before the stow is fixed. Stack weight, stack height and GM are adjusted until the lashing forces come within limits.
  • Decision-support software presents the operational guidance in service, so the master can alter speed or heading to move out of a dangerous combination - the classic remedy in MSC.1/Circ.1228.
  • The calculation is only as good as its inputs. Under SOLAS chapter VI regulation 2 the shipper must declare the verified gross mass of a packed container; a misdeclared weight corrupts both the GM and the stack-load check.

5. Status, purpose and approval of the CSM

Under SOLAS chapter VI regulation 5 and chapter VII regulation 5, cargo units and cargo transport units must be loaded, stowed and secured throughout the voyage in accordance with a Cargo Securing Manual approved by the Administration - in practice by a recognized organization on its behalf. It is ship-specific and a mandatory carriage document, not guidance, and is laid out to the standard structure of MSC.1/Circ.1353/Rev.2.

6. Content of the manual

  • General - purpose, definitions and application.
  • Securing devices and arrangements - an inventory and specification of the fixed fittings (lashing eyes, deck sockets, container foundations) with their locations and maximum securing load, and of the portable devices (twistlocks, lashing rods, turnbuckles, bridge fittings), with their strength, inspection, maintenance and replacement criteria.

The idea that makes limb (i) coherent

Securing and stability are usually taught as separate subjects, and the question is really asking you to join them. The lashing gear has a fixed strength; the sea does not negotiate. The only variables the ship controls are how much roll it experiences and how the weight is distributed. So the software is not an academic seakeeping exercise - it is the thing that tells you which GM range, which stack weights and which speed and heading combinations keep the accelerations inside what the lashings can already hold. Parametric roll is the sharpest case because it happens in head and following seas, where a watchkeeper is least expecting a roll problem.

Why parametric roll is a container-ship problem specifically

It is a hull-form consequence. Container ships have pronounced bow flare and a wide, flat transom to maximise deck stowage. When a wave crest is amidships the waterplane at the ends is narrower than in still water and stability falls; when the crest is at the ends the waterplane widens and stability rises. Encounter that variation at about twice the roll period and you are pumping the roll at its own resonance - the same physics as a child pumping a swing by shifting weight twice per cycle. Bulk carriers and tankers, with fuller and more prismatic ends, vary far less and are much less prone to it.

Common mistakes

  • Describing the second generation criteria as a mandatory SOLAS requirement. At this sitting they are interim guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1627. Say so - the qualification is worth more than the claim.
  • Treating the CSM as guidance or as a company document. It is approved by the Administration and required by SOLAS; carrying an unapproved or non-ship-specific manual is a detainable deficiency.
  • Listing lashing gear but omitting the maximum securing load and the permissible stack weight and height - those are the numbers the manual exists to provide.
  • Forgetting the people. Annex 14 and the Cargo Safe Access Plan are about the lashing gang's safe access, not about the cargo, and a full answer says so.

Examiner traps

Model written answer

1. Why war risk is a separate cover

War perils are not uninsurable. Under section 2(e) of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, "maritime perils" expressly include war perils, captures, seizures, restraints and detainments of princes and peoples. What removes them from the ordinary policy is the policy wording, not the statute: the standard hull and cargo forms carry a war exclusion - historically the free of capture and seizure clause. The cover is then bought back separately, on different terms, by a different market, at a separately negotiated price. That deletion-and-buy-back is the whole architecture of war risk cover.

2. Where the cover is written

  • Hull war risks - written on the Institute War and Strikes Clauses (Hulls-Time) by specialist war underwriters or by a war risks mutual association, covering physical loss of or damage to the ship.
  • P&I war risks - the Clubs exclude war risks under their Rules. In their place the International Group provides a separate excess war risks P&I cover, sitting above the ship's hull war policy or proper value, up to a fixed limit and cancellable at short notice. It answers third-party liabilities: crew death and injury, pollution, wreck removal, collision liability.
  • Cargo war risks - the Institute War Clauses (Cargo), attaching while the goods are waterborne.
  • Extensions bought alongside: war loss of hire, crew war bonus and kidnap and ransom.

3. What the cover includes

  • War, civil war, revolution, rebellion, insurrection and civil strife arising from them, and any hostile act by or against a belligerent power.
  • Capture, seizure, arrest, restraint or detainment, and the consequences of any attempt at them.
  • Derelict weapons of war - mines, torpedoes and bombs.
  • Strikes, riots and civil commotions, and labour disturbances, in the combined War and Strikes forms.
  • Terrorism, and persons acting maliciously or from a political motive, including sabotage.
  • Confiscation or expropriation by a government or authority.
  • Blocking and trapping: where the ship is detained or prevented from free movement for a stated continuous period, the assured may treat her as a constructive total loss without proving physical damage. This is the aspect most specific to a Gulf scenario and the one candidates most often miss.

4. What war cover itself excludes

  • Hostile detonation of a nuclear weapon.
  • Outbreak of war between the major powers - the "five powers" provision, on which cover terminates automatically.
  • Requisition or pre-emption by the ship's own flag State.
  • Ordinary judicial process, arrest for debt, and financial default.
  • Cyber loss under the market cyber exclusion, and any loss whose payment would breach a sanctions regime.

5. Containing the exposure - area, notice and price

  • Listed Areas. The London market's Joint War Committee publishes areas of perceived enhanced risk for hull war, strikes, terrorism and related perils, assessed against a risk benchmark by an independent consultant. The list is the trigger for everything that follows.
  • Notification and additional premium. Entering a Listed Area requires prior notice to underwriters; the annual cover is in effect suspended for that transit and reinstated only against a separately negotiated additional war risk premium, priced per transit and per vessel and moving with the threat.
  • Trading warranties. The policy warrants that the ship will not enter specified areas without consent. Entering without notice is a breach and prejudices the cover.
  • Short cancellation. War cover is not an annual promise. It is cancellable on seven days' notice under London market wordings - and on forty-eight hours under some United States wordings - and terminates automatically on the great-power and nuclear triggers. This is the single most powerful protection the underwriter holds.

6. Limits, conditions and the Club position

  • Limits and deductibles - per vessel, per event and in the aggregate, so a single incident cannot be open-ended.
  • Conditions of cover - routeing and transit windows, restricted port stays, convoy or naval escort participation, hardening and watchkeeping measures, and armed guards where lawfully permitted.
  • Reinsurance. Underwriters retrocede war exposure to a specialist market; the International Group's own reinsurance programme treats war separately, which is precisely why the Clubs write it as a distinct excess cover rather than inside the pooled entry.
  • Sanctions and due diligence - screening of trades, counterparties and ownership, with a sanctions clause allowing cover to be withheld.
  • Underwriting discipline - continuous monitoring, shorter policy periods, declining or re-rating vessels in particular trades, and Club circulars warning members before a situation deteriorates.

Why this structure scores

The architecture in one idea

Almost every marine cover question is about what a policy includes. War risk is the opposite: the ordinary policy deletes the peril and a second contract writes it back. That is why there are two underwriters, two premiums, two sets of conditions and two cancellation regimes on one ship. Once that is clear, the P&I structure follows without memorising: the Club excludes war under its Rules, so the war P&I cover has to sit outside the pool as a separate excess layer - and being outside the pool is exactly why it carries a fixed limit and a short cancellation notice, which ordinary Club cover does not.

Why the underwriter's protections look the way they do

War risk is uninsurable in the actuarial sense: the losses are correlated. An ordinary hull book relies on the fact that one ship's grounding tells you nothing about the next ship's. In a war zone every insured vessel is exposed to the same event on the same day, so the law of large numbers stops working. Every protection in limb (b) is a response to that single problem - the Listed Area identifies the correlated zone, the additional premium prices each entry into it, the notice period lets the underwriter exit before exposure accumulates, and the limits cap what any one event can cost. Presenting them as a list of tricks scores less than presenting them as one coherent answer to correlation.

Common mistakes

  • Saying war perils are uninsurable or excluded by law. They are maritime perils under section 2(e) of the Marine Insurance Act 1963; the exclusion is contractual.
  • Treating war cover as part of the P&I entry. The Clubs exclude it and provide a separate excess cover instead.
  • Omitting blocking and trapping. Detention for a stated continuous period founds a constructive total loss claim with no physical damage at all - the aspect a Gulf question is most likely aimed at.
  • Quoting a specific premium rate or reciting today's Listed Areas. Both move continuously; describe the mechanism instead.
  • Confusing piracy with war risk. Depending on the form, piracy may sit in the hull policy or in the war policy - say that it depends on the wording rather than asserting one answer.
  • Forgetting that war cover can be cancelled at seven days. Candidates assume an annual policy is an annual promise.

Model written answer

1. The governing instrument

Ship health and vector control are governed by the International Health Regulations (2005), a binding instrument of the World Health Organization, as amended in 2014, 2022 and 2024. The 2024 amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025. The IHR work through designated points of entry, competent authorities at those points, and a small set of ship documents.

2. The role of WHO

  • It makes the rules. WHO adopts the IHR and prescribes the model certificates in Annex 3 and the Ship Declaration of Health in Annex 8, so the documents are the same worldwide.
  • It identifies where vector control is needed. Under Annex 5, WHO publishes on a regular basis a list of areas where disinsection or other vector control measures are recommended for conveyances arriving from them, through temporary or standing recommendations.
  • It advises methods and materials. Where WHO has advised methods for disinsection, deratting, disinfection or decontamination, those should be used unless the competent authority is satisfied that others are as safe and reliable (Article 27), and States should accept measures applied elsewhere by WHO-advised methods.
  • It certifies and publishes. On a State Party's request WHO may, after appropriate investigation, certify that a port meets the IHR requirements (Article 20(4)), publishes certification guidelines and the list of certified ports (20(5)), and publishes the lists of authorized ports notified by States Parties (20(3)).
  • It provides technical guidance - notably the WHO Handbook for inspection of ships and issuance of ship sanitation certificates - and declares Public Health Emergencies of International Concern with temporary recommendations.

3. National agencies in India

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare - the health authority for IHR purposes, under which the National IHR Focal Point sits.
  • Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), and under it the Port Health Organisation, headed by the Port Health Officer - the competent authority at the point of entry. It inspects ships, baggage, cargo and containers, supervises control measures and issues the ship sanitation certificates, working under the Indian Port Health Rules, 1955 with the IHR 2005.
  • Directorate General of Shipping, through its Maritime Health Branch, on the seafarer side - approving medical examiners and governing seafarers' medical fitness examination and certification.
  • Port authorities and the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways support these functions at the ports themselves.

4. Certificates issued to a vessel

  • Ship Sanitation Control Exemption Certificate (SSCEC) - issued where the competent authority is satisfied that the ship is free of infection and contamination, including vectors and reservoirs, so that no control measures are required (Article 39(6)).
  • Ship Sanitation Control Certificate (SSCC) - issued where control measures were required and have been satisfactorily completed, and it records the evidence found and the measures taken (Article 39(5)). Under Annex 5 the presence of vectors on board and the measures used to eradicate them are recorded on this certificate. If a satisfactory result could not be obtained, the authority notes that on the certificate (Article 39(7)).

Both follow the model in Annex 3, and they replaced the former Deratting and Deratting Exemption Certificates. Alongside them the master submits a Ship Declaration of Health under Article 37, to the model in Annex 8 - a declaration, not a certificate. Separately, and concerning the crew rather than the ship, seafarers hold medical fitness certificates under MLC 2006 and STCW.

5. Issue in India, validity and extension

  • In India they are issued by the Port Health Officer at a port authorized for the purpose and notified to WHO. Under Article 20(3) a State Party notifies which ports may issue the SSCC, which may issue the SSCEC only, and which may grant the one-month extension.
  • Validity: a maximum of six months, for both the SSCEC and the SSCC (Article 39(1)).
  • Extension: by one month, where the inspection or the required control measures cannot be accomplished at that port - the extension carries the ship to a port where the certificate can be obtained.
  • Where no valid certificate is produced, or evidence of a public health risk is found on board, the State Party may proceed under Article 27(1) - treating the ship as affected and requiring disinfection, decontamination, disinsection or deratting.
  • Control measures are to be carried out whenever possible when the ship and holds are empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading (Article 39(4)).
  • For Indian coastal trade calling where no Port Health Officer is available, DG Shipping has provided for a Statement of Compliance issued by an authorized medical examiner, to be endorsed at the earliest available opportunity.

Ship health and crew health are two different systems

The question asks about "the ship and the crew", and the two run on separate tracks with separate authorities. Ship health is WHO and the IHR, administered in India by the Port Health Organisation under DGHS and the Ministry of Health, and evidenced by the SSCC or SSCEC. Common mistakes

  • Calling the SSCC/SSCEC a Deratting Certificate. Those were the predecessors under the earlier Regulations and were replaced under the IHR 2005 - the scope widened from rodents to all infection and contamination including vectors and reservoirs.
  • Saying the certificates are valid for six months extendable by six months. The extension is one month, and only where the inspection or control measures cannot be accomplished at that port.
  • Confusing the SSCC with the SSCEC. Exemption means no measures were needed; Control means measures were required and were completed.
  • Treating the Ship Declaration of Health as a certificate. It is a declaration by the master, to the Annex 8 model, and a State may dispense with it entirely under Article 37(4).
  • Naming WHO as an enforcement body. WHO recommends, certifies ports on request and publishes; the competent authority at the point of entry enforces.

Examiner traps

"Your certificate expired yesterday and the port cannot inspect - what happens?" The one-month extension in Article 39(1) exists precisely for this, and under Article 20(3)(c) States notify which of their ports may grant it, so that the ship can reach a port where the certificate is obtainable.

"When should the inspection be done?" Whenever possible with the ship and holds empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading - Article 39(4). Answering "any time" misses a printed requirement.

"What does WHO actually do about mosquitoes?" It publishes the list of areas where vector control is recommended for conveyances arriving from them, and advises the methods. Model written answer

1. The framework these processes sit inside

A ship using LNG as fuel is designed and operated under SOLAS chapter II-1 part G and the IGF Code; the bunkering operation itself is specified by ISO 20519 - equipment, procedures, competence and documentation. Safety rests on a two-stage emergency shutdown system, with the delivery facility and the receiving ship joined by an ESD link so both act together. Before transfer, a safety zone is set, compatibility and mooring agreed, and the checklist and bunkering plan completed.

2. Gassing up

Carried out when a tank is being brought back into service after refit, repair or inerting - before any liquid is introduced. The tank is full of inert gas or nitrogen, and that atmosphere must be replaced with methane vapour.

  • Why. Any carbon dioxide in inert gas would freeze at LNG temperature and block valves, filters and lines. Nitrogen does not freeze but is non-condensable: it accumulates in the vapour space, raises tank pressure, degrades the fuel's calorific value and upsets reliquefaction and gas supply.
  • How. LNG is taken from the supply through a vaporiser to produce warm methane vapour, which is admitted at the top of the tank. Being lighter than nitrogen, it forms a layer that pushes the heavier inert gas down and out through the bottom liquid line.
  • Where it goes. The displaced gas is led to the vent mast with attention to dispersion, or returned to the supply.
  • Completion. Sampling at the outlet continues until the hydrocarbon content reaches the specified value and carbon dioxide falls below its limit. The figures are set by the ship's manual and the containment system, not by a universal rule.

3. Cooling down

Once the tank holds methane vapour it is still warm. Cooling down brings the tank, pipework and manifold gradually towards LNG temperature, about minus 162 degrees Celsius, before bulk filling begins.

  • Why. To avoid thermal shock and excessive thermal stress in the tank structure and insulation; and to avoid the violent flash-off that occurs when LNG meets warm surfaces, which would over-pressurise the tank and swamp the vapour handling capacity.
  • How. LNG is admitted in small controlled quantities through spray nozzles at the top of the tank, evaporating as it falls and cooling the structure evenly.
  • Rate control. The permitted rate of temperature fall is limited by the tank designer and class, and temperatures are watched at fixed sensors through the tank to keep the structure cooling uniformly rather than locally.
  • Completion. When the sensors reach the target and are stable, bulk transfer starts at a low initial rate and is ramped up only once pressures and temperatures are seen to be steady.

4. Vapour management

LNG boils continuously from heat ingress, and filling a tank displaces the vapour already in it. Vapour management is the control of tank pressure within the design limit throughout, and it is the process that actually governs how fast bunkering can proceed.

  • Vapour return line - the primary method. A connection back to the bunker source returns the displaced vapour to its tanks, balancing pressure on both sides and removing the need to vent to atmosphere.
  • Consumption - burning the gas in the ship's engines, boilers or generators where the plant is running.
  • Reliquefaction where fitted, returning boil-off to the tank as liquid.
  • Gas combustion unit - burning off surplus gas safely where it cannot be used.
  • Controlled venting to the vent mast - a last resort only, because released methane is a potent greenhouse gas and venting is operationally and environmentally constrained.
  • Supporting controls. Spray filling to condense vapour; monitoring against the relief valve setting; level alarms and ESD trips; and attention to stratification and rollover where LNG of a different density is loaded onto an existing heel.

5. The emergency release coupling procedure

The emergency release system exists so that the transfer connection can be parted quickly and without spilling if the two vessels move beyond safe limits, if moorings fail, or in fire. Its heart is the emergency release coupling - a dry-break coupling with interlocked isolating valves which close before the halves separate, so the liquid held in the line is contained and the release of LNG and gas is minimised.

  • ESD-1 - stop the pumps and close the manifold valves on both sides. Transfer ceases. Triggered manually from either end or automatically by tank level, pressure, gas detection or fire.
  • Drain and reduce inventory in the transfer line so far as the time available allows.
  • ESD-2 - the release stage. The coupling's isolating valves close first, then the coupling parts at its designed point. Initiation is manual, or automatic from mooring load or vessel position monitoring exceeding its limits.
  • After release - both sides are purged with nitrogen, the parted hose end is made safe, and personnel stay clear of the release zone while residual liquid boils off.
  • Before transfer the ESD link must be connected and function-tested, the coupling confirmed armed and certified, communications and agreed emergency signals established, and the release covered in the bunkering plan and drilled.

The idea that ties the four together

Three of the four are about vapour and temperature, not liquid. That is the whole point. In LNG work the danger is rarely the liquid sitting in a tank - it is the gas you create by doing something too fast. Gassing up removes an atmosphere that would freeze or accumulate; cooling down prevents the flash-off that a warm tank would produce; vapour management disposes of the gas that filling inevitably generates. The emergency release is the fourth because it accepts that despite all of that, the connection may still have to be broken - and even then the design goal is the same, to part the line without releasing what is inside it. The order is also the order of the operation: you cannot cool a tank you have not gassed up, and you cannot fill one you have not cooled.

Why gassing up comes before cooling down

Because the freezing problem has to be solved while everything is still warm. Inert gas from a generator carries carbon dioxide, which becomes a solid well above LNG temperature. Introduce liquid into a tank that still holds carbon dioxide and you produce dry ice inside valves, strainers and spray lines - exactly where you least want a blockage. Gassing up removes it first. Nitrogen is a gentler problem but a persistent one: it will not freeze, but because it does not condense it stays in the vapour space, builds pressure, and thins the gas going to the engines. So gassing up is not a formality before the real work; it is what makes the cooling stage survivable.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the order - cooling down before gassing up. Liquid must not enter a tank still holding inert gas.
  • Saying gassing up removes air. Air was removed earlier by inerting; gassing up removes the inert gas or nitrogen and replaces it with methane vapour.
  • Describing cooling down as "filling slowly". It is spray cooling through nozzles at a controlled rate of temperature fall, not a slow bulk fill.
  • Treating venting as normal vapour management. It is a last resort; the vapour return line is the primary route and methane venting carries a real environmental penalty.
  • Describing the emergency release coupling as a hose that simply breaks. Its defining feature is the interlocked isolating valves that close before separation.
  • Conflating ESD-1 and ESD-2. Stopping the transfer and parting the connection are two different stages, and ESD-2 follows ESD-1.

Examiner traps

"Why not use inert gas from the generator and go straight to loading?" Carbon dioxide in the inert gas freezes at LNG temperature and blocks the system. That single sentence is what the gassing-up limb is really testing.

"What limits how fast you can bunker?" Not the pump - the vapour handling capacity and the tank pressure limit. Bunkering is paced by where the gas can go.

"The bunker vessel starts to drift. What happens first?" ESD-1 stops the transfer and closes the manifold valves; only then does ESD-2 operate the release coupling. Going straight to "the hose disconnects" misses the sequence.

"You loaded a different LNG on top of your heel. What is the risk?" Stratification and rollover - a sudden mixing of layers of different density releasing a large volume of vapour and spiking tank pressure.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the purpose of gassing up? To displace inert gas or nitrogen with methane vapour before liquid is admitted, chiefly to remove carbon dioxide that would freeze.
  • Why is warm vapour admitted at the top of the tank? It is lighter than nitrogen, so it layers above and pushes the heavier gas out through the bottom line.
  • What is the approximate temperature of LNG? About minus 162 degrees Celsius at atmospheric pressure.
  • What is the primary route for displaced vapour during bunkering? The vapour return line back to the bunkering facility.
  • What does an emergency release coupling do that an ordinary quick-release does not? Its interlocked isolating valves close before the coupling parts, containing the line inventory.
  • What links the two ESD systems? A ship-to-ship or ship-to-shore ESD link, so that shutdown on either side acts on both.

Model written answer

1. What the Enhanced Survey Programme is

The ESP is a mandatory regime of intensified hull inspection for the two ship types whose structural failures have historically been catastrophic. It sits in the 2011 ESP Code, adopted as resolution A.1049(27) on 30 November 2011 and made compulsory by SOLAS regulation XI-1/2. Annex A covers bulk carriers, Annex B oil tankers, each split between single- and double-skin or double- and non-double-hull construction, and the Code is closely aligned with IACS Unified Requirement Z10.

It works by requiring, in addition to the ordinary periodical survey, a planned programme of close-up survey of critical structure within arm's reach, thickness measurement by an approved firm, and tank testing - all governed by a Survey Programme agreed with the Administration or its recognized organization before the renewal survey begins.

2. What a Condition Assessment Programme survey is

CAP is a voluntary, additional classification-society service, carried out at the owner's request. Nothing in SOLAS or MARPOL requires it. Its purpose is commercial: it produces a numerical rating of the ship's actual condition, judged not against the minimum acceptable standard but against a new-build standard, and its scope is selectable - hull, machinery, cargo systems, or a combination. Owners buy it because charterers, oil-major vetting inspectors, buyers and financiers want an independent grading of an older ship, and it involves far more extensive gauging and testing than an ESP survey does.

  • Reports of port State control inspections recording structural deficiencies, and relevant safety management system records and non-conformities.
  • Any other information helping identify suspect areas and critical structural areas.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three printed limbs, and the marks are unevenly available. Limb (a) is where the real discrimination happens, because the difference is conceptual, not a matter of listing two definitions. Limbs (b) and (c) are pure recall and reward organised lists - so give them structure rather than a stream of items. Splitting (b) into documents needed to plan and records of the ship's history and condition makes it far easier to write completely under pressure, and it is how the survey programme itself is organised.

The idea behind limb (a)

ESP asks a pass/fail question; CAP asks a scoring question. The ESP exists to establish that the structure is still at or above the minimum standard the ship must meet to hold its certificates - a floor. CAP exists to say how far above that floor the ship actually sits, measured against a new-build standard - a grade. That single distinction generates every other difference: because ESP answers a legal question it is mandatory, flag-driven and hull-focused, and failure stops the ship trading. Because CAP answers a commercial question it is voluntary, owner-requested, scope-selectable, and failure costs charters rather than certificates.

The abbreviation trap that decides this question

CAP is not CAS. The Condition Assessment Programme is the voluntary classification-society grading described above. The Condition Assessment Scheme is a mandatory regime under MARPOL Annex I applying to single-hull oil tankers, adopted by IMO resolution. A candidate who answers limb (a) by describing CAS has answered the wrong question and has, worse, said that the thing is mandatory when the question is precisely about a voluntary scheme. If you are asked to distinguish ESP from CAP and you find yourself writing "MARPOL", stop - you have taken the wrong turning.

Why the paperwork in limb (b) exists

None of it is bureaucracy for its own sake, and saying why earns more than the list alone. A thickness reading is meaningless without the scantling drawing that says what the plate started at and whether it is high-tensile steel, because HTS carries a different diminution allowance. The cargo and ballast history tells the surveyor where to look, since a tank that has carried corrosive cargo or has been used for ballast corrodes differently. The coating condition and the Coating Technical File predict where wastage will appear next. And the Survey Report File makes the ship's structural condition a trend rather than a snapshot - which is the whole point of an enhanced programme.

Common mistakes

  • Describing CAP as a statutory requirement, or confusing it with the Condition Assessment Scheme under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Saying CAP replaces or extends class or statutory surveys. It does neither, and a good rating does not maintain class.
  • Saying the ESP applies to all ships. It applies to bulk carriers and oil tankers.
  • Omitting that the Survey Programme must be agreed before the survey starts, and that a planning meeting is held.
  • Treating the safe access plan as a generic risk assessment. It is specific - which means of access, for which space, reaching which structure.
  • Forgetting that thickness measurement must be carried out by a firm approved by the society, not by the crew.

Model written answer

1. Secure the evidence and establish the facts

  • Do not dress, grind or blend the crack. Material removed before assessment destroys the evidence class, the underwriter and the root-cause investigation all need.
  • Record it. Photographs and video with a scale in frame, and a dimensioned sketch.
  • Locate it precisely - blade number from the stamping on the boss, pressure or suction face, radial position as a fraction of the blade radius, distance from the leading or trailing edge and from tip and root, and the crack's length and orientation.
  • Examine the whole propeller, not just the one blade - the remaining blades, the leading and trailing edges, the tips, the boss, the keyway and the fairing cone. A crack is rarely isolated.
  • Look for related damage - rudder, stern frame, rope guard, stern tube seal, hull marks - which would point to contact or grounding.
  • Assemble the service history - vibration or unusual noise, shaft power and revolution anomalies, any grounding, ice contact or rope entanglement, stern tube oil analysis and the planned maintenance record.

2. Establish the nature and extent

  • Clean the area only as far as the surveyor permits, then carry out liquid dye penetrant testing. Propellers are cast in non-ferromagnetic alloys - nickel-aluminium bronze or manganese bronze - so magnetic particle inspection cannot be used. Where depth must be established, eddy current or ultrasonic techniques are applied.
  • Have the testing done by a qualified operator from an approved firm and obtain a written report.
  • Establish what the defect actually is: a genuine fatigue crack, a casting defect, or cavitation erosion pitting that resembles cracking. The three have different consequences and different causes.

3. Report to the Company

  • Inform the Master first, then report to the Company - the technical superintendent and, under the ISM Code, the Designated Person Ashore - and raise it in the safety management system as a hazardous occurrence or non-conformity so that it enters the analysis and corrective-action process.
  • The report must be factual and complete: description and precise location, dimensions, NDT method and result, photographs and sketch, propeller particulars (maker, material, drawing and serial number, diameter and pitch), relevant service history, and the effect on the dry-dock programme and undocking date.
  • State the options and a recommendation, with the implications of each for cost and time - but recognise the division of responsibility: the Chief Engineer establishes the facts and recommends; the Company decides and instructs.
  • Request instructions, and confirm who will liaise with class, the repairer and the underwriter.

4. Involve classification and settle the repair

  • Notify the classification society and request the attending surveyor to examine. The owner is under a duty to report any damage or defect which affects or may affect classification, and concealing it puts class - and with it the statutory certificates and the insurance - at risk.
  • The surveyor assesses the crack against the society's rules. Acceptance depends heavily on where on the blade it lies: the highly stressed region is the most restricted and repairs there are limited or prohibited, while damage near the tip is treated far more permissively. ISO 484 governs the tolerances against which repaired geometry is judged.
  • The possible outcomes are: accept as found; grind out and blend to a smooth radius within permitted limits; weld repair by an approved procedure; crop and re-form; or replace the blade or the propeller.
  • Where a repair is done it must be by a class-approved repairer to an approved welding procedure with the pre-heat and post-weld heat treatment the alloy requires, followed by re-testing, a check of pitch and geometry, and static balancing.
  • Obtain the surveyor's endorsement. If the repair is deferred, expect a condition of class with a time limit rather than a clean survey.
  • Consider separately whether the occurrence is reportable to the flag Administration. That turns on its cause and on whether seaworthiness was affected - damage arising from a grounding or contact is reportable; wastage found at a routine docking generally is not.

5. Insurance and preservation of the claim

  • Through the Company, notify the hull and machinery underwriters, and the P&I club if any third-party element arises, and arrange attendance of the underwriter's surveyor before repairs begin.
  • Whether a claim lies depends on the cause. Damage from an insured peril such as contact or grounding is recoverable; ordinary wear and tear and cavitation erosion are not, and a latent defect is recoverable only so far as the policy provides for it.
  • Do not commit to a repair method or cost without the underwriter's agreement where a claim is contemplated, and retain removed material and samples for metallurgical examination.

6. Root cause, prevention and close-out

  • Establish the cause: cavitation erosion; fatigue initiating at cavitation pitting or a casting defect acting as a stress raiser; impact with a submerged object or ice; dezincification of manganese bronze; galvanic attack where anodes or the impressed current system are defective; or sustained overload from heavy running or excessive astern working.
  • Act on it - review hull and propeller roughness, fouling and polishing intervals, confirm cathodic protection is functioning, monitor shaft vibration, and review astern working and heavy running.
  • Close the loop: file the NDT and survey reports, repair procedure, welder qualifications and balancing record; update the machinery history, planned maintenance system and underwater inspection schedule; feed the lessons through the safety management system; and hand over fully.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The command is state the steps, so the answer must be a sequence, not an essay about propellers. Six headed steps in a defensible order will outscore a longer piece of prose every time. Notice what the question actually asks: it is a management question with a technical trigger. The examiner wants to see that a Chief Engineer knows who must be told, in what order, and what must not be touched in the meantime. Metallurgy earns marks only where it changes a decision.

The instinct you have to suppress

The engineer's reflex on finding a surface crack in a dock, with the undocking date approaching, is to dress it out and carry on. That reflex is the most expensive mistake available here. Grinding the crack away before anyone has seen it destroys three things at once: the class surveyor's ability to assess what was actually there, the underwriter's ability to accept a claim, and the root-cause investigation that would stop it happening on the sister ship. The propeller can be repaired later; none of that evidence can be recovered. So the first step is not a repair step at all - it is to stop, record, and report.

Why location on the blade decides almost everything

A propeller blade is not uniformly stressed. Bending stress from thrust and torque is greatest towards the root, and the blade is thinnest towards the tip and the edges, so a crack of the same length means very different things depending on where it is. Classification rules divide the blade into zones accordingly: in the highly stressed region, welding is restricted or not permitted at all and the realistic outcomes are limited grinding or blade replacement; near the tip the same defect may be blended out without difficulty. This is why the answer must record blade number, face, radius and distance from the edge before anything else - those four facts, not the crack's length, are what determine the outcome.

Common mistakes

  • Proposing magnetic particle inspection. Propellers are nickel-aluminium or manganese bronze and are not ferromagnetic, so MPI simply does not work. Use dye penetrant.
  • Grinding or welding before class attends and before the extent is established.
  • Reporting to the Company but not to class, or assuming the superintendent will do it.
  • Treating every crack as a claim. Cavitation erosion and ordinary wear are not insured perils.
  • Deciding the repair yourself. The Chief Engineer recommends; the Company instructs and class approves.
  • Omitting balancing and geometry check after repair - a repaired blade that is out of pitch or out of balance introduces vibration and a new fatigue problem.
  • Stopping at the repair and never establishing the cause, which guarantees a repeat.

Examiner traps

"Why not MPI?" Because the material is not ferromagnetic. If you cannot answer this you have shown you have never actually stood at a propeller in a dock.

"The dock is due to flood tomorrow. What do you do?" Report and get class to attend. Commercial pressure does not change the sequence, and undocking with an unreported defect affecting class is a far worse outcome than a delay.

"Is this a general average or a hull claim?" Neither is automatic - it depends entirely on cause. Establish that first.

"You blended out the crack and it looked fine. Any problem?" Yes - the blade geometry and pitch may now be outside tolerance, the blade is out of balance with the others, and you have removed the evidence. Blending is permitted only within limits and with class agreement.

"Must you report it to the flag State?" It depends on cause and on whether seaworthiness was affected - not automatically.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which NDT method for a bronze propeller, and why? Dye penetrant - the alloy is not ferromagnetic, so magnetic particle inspection is unusable.
  • What four facts locate the defect? Blade number, pressure or suction face, radial position, and distance from the leading or trailing edge.
  • Who approves a propeller weld repair? The classification society, and it must be carried out by an approved repairer to an approved procedure.
  • What is done after a blade repair? Re-testing, a pitch and geometry check against tolerance, and static balancing.
  • What happens if the repair is deferred? A condition of class with a time limit may be imposed.
  • Name three causes of blade cracking. Cavitation erosion leading to fatigue, impact with a submerged object, and a casting defect acting as a stress raiser.
  • Who do you tell first on board? The Master, then the Company and the Designated Person Ashore.

1. a) What FSA is, and who it is for

Formal Safety Assessment is set out in MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment (FSA) for use in the IMO rule-making process, of 9 April 2018. The title states its purpose: a structured, auditable methodology by which the Organization decides whether, and how, to regulate a hazard, balancing safety benefit against cost. It is not a shipboard risk assessment or a permit-to-work system. The question is therefore how a proportionate rule for this cargo was, and is being, arrived at.

2. Step 1 - identifying the hazards of this cargo

  • Thermal runaway - an exothermic internal reaction that becomes self-sustaining and generates its own oxidant, so it cannot be smothered and continues without external air;
  • propagation cell to cell and module to module, and thence vehicle to vehicle in a close stow;
  • initiators - internal cell defects, mechanical damage in handling or lashing, over-charge, high state of charge on shipment, charging on board;
  • vent gas - flammable and toxic, able to accumulate in an enclosed vehicle space and produce a gas explosion rather than a simple fire;
  • re-ignition hours or days after apparent extinction, defeating a normal fire-out judgement;
  • mis-declaration and concealment - cells as cargo may be undeclared or wrongly classified, and a battery electric vehicle is not a labelled package at all, so the energy is invisible to the stowage plan.

3. Step 2 - assessing the risk on a ro-ro deck

Risk is frequency times consequence, estimated from casualty returns, incident and near-miss data, cell testing and fire modelling - with the Rev.2 requirement that the reliability, uncertainty and validity of that data be assessed and reported rather than assumed.

  • Frequency is low per vehicle but rising with fleet electrification, and exposure is concentrated - one deck may hold thousands of units;
  • consequence escalates because a ro-ro space is a large undivided volume, vehicles stowed centimetres apart, with no cargo-hold fire divisions and ramps and ventilation trunks carrying heat and smoke between decks;
  • on a ro-pax the dominant risk is to life; on a pure car and truck carrier it is total loss of the ship, and a series of well-known casualties supplied the evidence;
  • manual firefighting on a loaded deck is near impossible, so the model must assume fixed systems and boundaries are what will actually be available.

4. Steps 3 and 4 - risk control options and whether they pay

Risk control options are generated and grouped, then tested by cost-benefit assessment using indices such as the cost of averting a fatality, so a measure is recommended only where the safety gain justifies the cost across the world fleet:

  • detection - earlier, better-located, individually identifiable so the seat of fire is known, with combined smoke and heat sensing;
  • suppression - drencher and water-based systems of adequate coverage and capacity, aligned with detection sections so the right section operates;
  • containment - insulated boundaries, ventilation shut-down and control of openings;
  • operational and design - state-of-charge limits on shipment, segregation and dedicated stowage, restrictions on charging at sea, video monitoring, crew procedures;
  • options are ranked, and those effective but disproportionately costly are rejected transparently - which is why the outcome is a specific set of measures rather than everything technically possible.

5. Step 5 - what the recommendations actually became

The risk-based work on ro-ro fire safety - notably the EMSA FIRESAFE I and FIRESAFE II studies of fire dynamics, system performance and operational factors - entered the regulatory system in two stages:

  • MSC.1/Circ.1615, Interim Guidelines for Minimizing the Incidence and Consequences of Fires in Ro-Ro Spaces and Special Category Spaces, carried key outcomes into guidance while mandatory work continued;
  • resolution MSC.550(108) then amended SOLAS chapter II-2 regulations 7 and 20 and MSC.555(108) amended FSS Code chapters 7 and 9, both adopted at MSC 108 in May 2024 and in force on 1 January 2026 - earlier in the year in which this paper was sat;
  • requirements include individually identifiable fixed detection with combined smoke and heat detectors, detection aligned with deluge sections, detection on weather decks used for vehicles, an effective video monitoring system with at least seven days of replay at a continuously manned control station, and A-60 boundaries on passenger ships carrying more than 36 passengers;
  • they apply to ships constructed on or after 1 January 2026, existing ships in scope complying not later than the first survey on or after 1 January 2028;
  • further amendments aimed at new energy vehicles were still in development, so the regulatory answer is partly delivered and continuing.

6. b) Measures by stakeholder

  • IMO - complete the new-energy-vehicle amendments, keep IMDG provisions current, maintain interim guidance meanwhile;
  • flag State and class - approve and survey the amended detection, suppression and boundary arrangements, and issue guidance ahead of the compliance dates;
  • shipper, manufacturer and packer - correct IMDG classification, packing and marking, state-of-charge control, testing and quality control, and no offering of damaged, defective or recalled batteries;
  • port and terminal - screen and refuse visibly damaged vehicles, check declared state of charge, separate electric vehicles in marshalling areas, inform the ship promptly;
  • ship operator - a written stowage and segregation policy, limits on charging on board, training in lithium fire behaviour, vehicle-deck drills, thermal imaging, and a plan that assumes re-ignition;
  • ship's staff - verify cargo against the declaration, maintain detection and drencher readiness, patrol during and after loading, and fight fire by early activation of fixed systems and boundary cooling rather than entering a loaded deck;
  • insurers and industry bodies - loss-prevention guidance, incident and near-miss data sharing, and premium signals rewarding compliant stowage - the same data that feeds the next FSA.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The 10-mark limb is a method question, and method questions are marked on whether the method is visible. Naming the five steps and then walking the cargo through them is what separates a top answer from a general essay about battery fires. Step 5 is where most candidates stop short: an FSA that produces no recommendation has not been applied, so ending with the actual resolutions is what closes the loop.

Why FSA exists at all

Before FSA, IMO regulation was largely reactive - a casualty happened, and a rule followed. FSA was introduced after the Herald of Free Enterprise and Piper Alpha era to allow the Organization to act proactively and proportionately, on evidence rather than on the emotional weight of the most recent disaster, and to be able to say publicly why a proposed rule was or was not worth its cost. That is why the Guidelines insist on auditability, traceability and the explicit reporting of uncertainty: an FSA is as much an accountability document as a technical one.

The lithium hazard in engineering terms

Thermal runaway begins when a cell's internal temperature passes the point at which exothermic decomposition of the electrolyte and cathode outpaces heat loss. The decomposition liberates oxygen from the cathode material, which is why the reaction sustains itself without air and why smothering agents - CO2, foam blanket, an inert atmosphere - do not stop it. Cooling is the only lever the ship holds, and it works by removing heat from cells that have not yet reached onset temperature, so its real objective is to stop propagation to neighbouring vehicles rather than to extinguish the cell already in runaway. That is also why re-ignition is normal rather than surprising: the cells that were merely heated may still cross the threshold hours later.

Vehicles, containers and packages are three different regulatory problems

  • Cells and batteries as cargo - dangerous goods under the IMDG Code, class 9, with UN numbers such as 3480 and 3481 for lithium-ion and 3090 and 3091 for lithium-metal, subject to packing, marking, documentation and segregation.
  • Batteries in or with equipment - the same UN family but different provisions, and often carried under exceptions.
  • A battery electric vehicle - carried as a vehicle, not as a declared dangerous goods package. This is the regulatory gap: the ship may know exactly how many tonnes of declared lithium cells she has in containers, and not know how much energy is sitting on the vehicle deck.

Common mistakes

  • Describing FSA as a shipboard risk assessment. Read the circular's title.
  • Listing the five steps and then never using them - a hazard essay wearing an FSA hat.
  • Saying water must never be used on a lithium fire. Cooling and boundary protection with copious water is the accepted tactic.
  • Saying the fire can be smothered with CO2. The reaction supplies its own oxidant.
  • Declaring the fire out when flames stop. Re-ignition must be assumed and monitored for.
  • Treating a battery electric vehicle as though it were declared dangerous goods, or vice versa.

Understand this first

Ship recycling is where two hard truths meet. An old ship contains materials nobody would allow aboard a new one, and the countries that do the work most cheaply are the least equipped to absorb the harm of doing it badly. The Convention's answer is to make the ship carry her own disclosure: a list of what hazardous material is actually built into her, kept current throughout her life, so that the yard which finally cuts her up knows what it is cutting into before it starts. The other half of the scheme binds the yard - authorised facilities, a specific plan for each ship, and a controlled place to do the work. For India this is not a foreign imposition; it is the direction the better yards were already travelling, given legal force. That is what converts the cost into an advantage, because a yard that has already upgraded now has something its unregulated competitors cannot offer. The impact falls hardest on the smaller plots that cannot fund the upgrade.

Model written answer

1. The Convention and when it bit

The Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships, 2009 was adopted at a diplomatic conference in Hong Kong, China in May 2009 and entered into force on 26 June 2025 - more than fifteen years later. The delay is explained by Article 17, which imposes a triple condition: twenty-four months after not less than 15 States have consented to be bound, whose combined fleets are not less than 40 per cent of world gross tonnage, and whose combined maximum annual ship recycling volume over the preceding ten years is not less than 3 per cent of their combined tonnage - the third condition making the Convention impossible to bring into force without the major recycling States. At entry into force there were 24 Parties representing about 57 per cent of world tonnage, including Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Turkiye.

2. What it requires of ships

  • An Inventory of Hazardous Materials in three parts - materials in the structure and equipment, operationally generated wastes, and stores - with Parts II and III completed before recycling.
  • Survey and certification: an International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials, and, after a final survey, an International Ready for Recycling Certificate.
  • Application to ships of 500 GT and above, except those operating throughout their life only in waters under the sovereignty or jurisdiction of the flag State (Article 3.3); existing ships must comply by 26 June 2030 or before going for recycling, whichever is earlier.
  • Control or prohibition of listed hazardous materials - asbestos, PCBs, ozone-depleting substances, and anti-fouling systems containing organotin compounds or cybutryne.
  • Recycling only at a facility authorised under the Convention in a Party State, working to a ship-specific Ship Recycling Plan, with notification of intent to recycle and a statement of completion.

3. What it requires of recycling facilities

An IMO instrument normally regulates ships; this one reaches ashore and regulates the yard.

  • Authorisation by the competent authority of the State, evidenced by a Document of Authorisation to conduct Ship Recycling.
  • A Ship Recycling Facility Plan covering worker safety and training, emergency preparedness and response, and the management of hazardous materials.
  • Safe-for-entry and safe-for-hot-work conditions to be established and maintained.
  • Controlled downstream management of the wastes removed - which is where the real burden falls.

4. India's position and domestic implementation

  • India is one of the world's principal ship-recycling States, centred on the Alang-Sosiya yards in Gujarat, and it acceded on 28 November 2019, the Director General of Shipping depositing the instrument at the thirty-first IMO Assembly. That accession was decisive: it took the count to the required fifteen States and contributed heavily to the recycling-volume condition.
  • Domestically the Convention is given effect by the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 and the Recycling of Ships Rules, 2019, which anticipated entry into force by six years and apply Convention standards to ships recycled in India.
  • The maritime administration is the competent authority, authorising yards and approving ship recycling plans.
  • The Recycling of Ships Act 2019 stands unaffected by the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. That Act's repeal provision, s.324(1), repeals the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 and the Coasting Vessels Act 1838 only. The 2025 Act touches recycling only at s.17, providing for temporary registration of a vessel sought to be recycled.

5. How India stands to benefit

  • Legitimacy and access to better tonnage. Owners who avoided South Asian yards on reputational grounds can now send ships to an authorised Indian facility with a defensible answer, which should raise both volume and quality of tonnage.
  • A level playing field. Competing recycling States must now meet the same standard, so the money India's yards spent upgrading ahead of entry into force is rewarded rather than punished on price.
  • Worker safety and environmental gain. Enforceable safe-for-hot-work and safe-for-entry requirements, training, protective equipment and controlled waste handling address the fatality and contamination record that has defined the industry's reputation.
  • Downstream industry and investment. Formalisation supports the steel re-rolling and second-hand equipment trades, and certification makes yards financeable and contractable on longer terms.

6. How it stands to be impacted, and the balance

  • Cost of compliance. Impermeable floors, containment, lifting equipment, and hazardous waste storage and treatment are capital-intensive. Small operators may not afford them, so the industry consolidates and some yards close.
  • Downstream waste capacity is the binding constraint. The Convention's obligations do not end at the yard gate, and treatment and disposal capacity for asbestos and other hazardous waste must exist for compliance to be real rather than documentary.
  • Price and leakage. Higher costs mean a lower price per light displacement tonne, creating an incentive to recycle elsewhere - including through a change of flag before the last voyage to a State outside the Convention.
  • Convention compliance is not European acceptance. The Convention does not expressly prohibit beaching, whereas the EU Ship Recycling Regulation maintains its own list of approved facilities on stricter terms. An Indian yard may be fully Convention-compliant and still not be EU-listed.
  • Enforcement is the credibility test. Authorisation has to be inspected and enforced; the industry remains under close external scrutiny, and a documentary-only regime would forfeit the reputational gain that is the main prize.
  • On balance, entry into force is net positive for India: it converts an early and expensive investment into a competitive advantage and legitimises the industry. The gain is conditional on downstream waste infrastructure and real enforcement keeping pace with certification - without them, the cost is incurred and the reputational benefit is not.

Why this structure scores

The feature that makes this Convention different

Almost every IMO instrument regulates the ship - its construction, equipment, certificates and crew. The Hong Kong Convention is one of the very few that reaches ashore and regulates a facility on land, requiring the yard itself to be authorised, planned and inspected. That is why it took so long to bring into force: it needed the consent of States whose domestic industry, not merely whose fleet, would be regulated. And it is why Article 17's third condition exists - a recycling-volume threshold that made it impossible to bring the Convention into force over the objections of the recycling States. Read that way, India's accession in 2019 is not a footnote; it is one of the two or three events that made the Convention real.

The distinction that separates a good answer from a very good one

The Convention does not ban beaching. It requires safe and environmentally sound recycling, authorisation, planning and control - but it does not prescribe the method by which a ship is brought ashore. The EU Ship Recycling Regulation takes a stricter line and maintains its own list of approved facilities, which is why the acceptability of the Alang yards to European owners has been contested for years and is not settled by entry into force. So the accurate proposition is: Convention compliance is necessary for legitimacy but is not sufficient for European acceptance. A candidate who writes that the Convention has ended beaching, or that compliance automatically opens the EU market, has overstated it in exactly the way the examiner is watching for.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Convention bans beaching, or that it makes Indian yards automatically acceptable to EU owners.
  • Giving the adoption year as the operative date. Adopted 2009, in force 26 June 2025 - the gap is the interesting part.
  • Reciting only the ship-side obligations and omitting the facility obligations, which are half the Convention.
  • Confusing the ICIHM (held through service life) with the Ready for Recycling Certificate (issued after the final survey, immediately before recycling).
  • Answering only the benefits when the question says benefit or be impacted, and giving no concluding judgement.
  • Attributing India's recycling law to the Merchant Shipping Act. It is the separate Recycling of Ships Act, 2019.

  • Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 s.324(1) - repeals the 1958 Act and the Coasting Vessels Act 1838 only, leaving the Recycling of Ships Act 2019 in force; s.17 - temporary registration of a vessel sought to be recycled (see QP2607 Q7)
  • EU Ship Recycling Regulation - a separate and stricter standard with its own list of approved facilities

Uncertainty, currency and jurisdiction

The Convention dates and the accession are primary-verified on the IMO's own briefings: adoption in May 2009, entry into force on 26 June 2025 with 24 Parties at about 57 per cent of world tonnage, and India's deposit on 28 November 2019. The statutory point about the 2025 Act is also strong, because s.324(1) was read in the Gazette for QP2607 Q7 and its repeal list is closed - this is the one place where a March 2026 paper could have been caught by the commencement of the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 on 15 March 2026, and it is not, because the Recycling of Ships Act 2019 is separate legislation that the repeal does not touch. What is weaker is stated as such. MIW holds no licensed copy of the Convention text, so article and appendix detail rests on IMO briefing material and classification-society guidance. No percentage share of world recycling is asserted, because published figures for India's share differ widely and none could be verified; Alang-Sosiya is named instead. The number of authorised Indian yards and of EU-listed facilities changes continuously and is deliberately not reproduced. And the commercial argument in step 6 - that compliance cost depresses the price per light displacement tonne and creates leakage to non-Party States - is presented as a mechanism, not as a measured outcome, because no price data was obtained.

The third condition is what made the recycling States indispensable.

No to both. The Convention requires safe and environmentally sound recycling, authorisation and planning, but does not prescribe how a ship is brought ashore. The EU Ship Recycling Regulation applies a stricter standard through its own list of approved facilities, so a fully compliant Indian yard may still not be EU-listed.

The single most overstated proposition in this topic.

No. Section 324(1) repeals the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 and the Coasting Vessels Act 1838 only. The Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 is separate legislation and stands. The 2025 Act touches recycling only at s.17, on temporary registration of a vessel sought to be recycled.

A paper sat in the month the 2025 Act commenced invites exactly this error.

The International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials is held through the ship's service life, following initial and renewal surveys. The International Ready for Recycling Certificate is issued after the final survey, immediately before recycling, once Parts II and III of the inventory are completed and the ship recycling plan is in place.

One runs for the ship's life; the other is issued once, at the end.

A Document of Authorisation to conduct Ship Recycling issued by the competent authority, and a Ship Recycling Facility Plan covering worker safety and training, emergency preparedness and response, and management of hazardous materials, with safe-for-entry and safe-for-hot-work conditions maintained.

The facility obligations are half the Convention and are routinely omitted.

Downstream waste treatment and disposal capacity. The Convention's obligations do not end at the yard gate, and without treatment capacity for asbestos and other hazardous waste, compliance becomes documentary rather than real - which would forfeit the reputational gain that is the main prize.

The sharpest point available in the evaluation limb.

The Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 and the Recycling of Ships Rules, 2019, enacted six years before the Convention entered into force. The maritime administration is the competent authority, authorising yards and approving ship recycling plans.

India legislated ahead of entry into force, which is part of the significance.

15-second recall

IMPACT: capital cost and consolidation, downstream waste capacity as the binding constraint, price depression and leakage to non-Party flags, and the fact that the Convention DOES NOT BAN BEACHING so compliance is not EU acceptance.
