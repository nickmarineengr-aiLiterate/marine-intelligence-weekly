Understand this first

This question is two questions wearing one number, and they reward different things. Limb (a) is a map: who sits where, who decides what, and - the part most candidates drop - what the sub-committees are actually for. The answer to that last part is one idea: a sub-committee prepares and never adopts. It does the technical drafting a week-long committee has no time for, hands the text back up, and the committee or the Assembly does the adopting. That separation is deliberate: it keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands. Limb (b) is a hierarchy of legal force, and it has one organising idea too: force is borrowed, not intrinsic. A Convention binds because States accept it. A Protocol is a second treaty they accept separately. A Code is only a technical document until a convention says it is mandatory - which is why the 2008 IS Code can be mandatory in Part A and recommendatory in Part B. A Resolution is a container, and whether it is binding depends entirely on what is inside it. Hold those two ideas - prepare versus adopt, and borrowed force - and both limbs write themselves.

Model written answer

1. What IMO is, and the organs that do the work

The International Maritime Organization is the specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and for the prevention of pollution from ships. It is a regulator that owns no ships, inspects nothing and enforces nothing directly: it produces instruments which flag States give force to in their own law and which port States then check. Its architecture follows that purpose exactly - a two-tier governing structure of an Assembly and a Council, five main committees that do the technical work, seven sub-committees that prepare it, and a permanent Secretariat headed by the Secretary-General.

The division worth stating at the outset, because it organises the rest of the answer: the governing organs decide policy, budget and membership; the committees produce the regulation that reaches a ship. A candidate who describes only the Assembly and Council has described the constitution and none of the output.

2. The Assembly - the governing body

  • The highest governing body. All Member States sit in it, each with one vote.
  • Meets in regular session once every two years. The 33rd Assembly is the operative cycle at this sitting; its resolutions were adopted on 6 December 2023.
  • Approves the work programme, votes the budget and determines the financial arrangements of the Organization.
  • Elects the Council. The Council serving at this sitting was elected at the 33rd Assembly for the 2024-2025 biennium and is in the second year of that term.
  • Receives the reports of the Council and of the committees, and adopts Assembly resolutions - recommendations, guidelines and codes, carrying the A. prefix.
  • Approves the appointment of the Secretary-General made by the Council.

3. The Council - the executive organ

  • 40 Members, elected by the Assembly for a two-year term, in three categories: (a) ten States with the largest interest in providing international shipping services; (b) ten States with the largest interest in international seaborne trade; (c) twenty States elected to secure representation of all major geographic areas.
  • Between Assembly sessions it performs all the functions of the Assembly - with one reserved exception: the function of making recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention, which the Assembly keeps to itself.
  • Supervises the work of the Organization and co-ordinates the activities of its organs.
  • Considers the draft work programme and budget and submits them to the Assembly with its comments and recommendations.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General, subject to the approval of the Assembly. The present Secretary-General took office on 1 January 2024 and has been in post for thirteen months at this sitting.
  • ⚠ An amendment would enlarge the Council to 52 Members - resolution A.1152(32), adopted in December 2021. It amends the IMO Convention itself and therefore requires acceptance by two thirds of Members. It is NOT in force at this sitting: the correct figure to write in February 2025 is 40.

The two that matter most to an engineer are MSC and MEPC, because they are the organs that adopt amendments to the mandatory instruments - and they do so by tacit acceptance, which is why a MARPOL or SOLAS amendment can enter into force without any State ratifying anything.

5. The seven sub-committees, and the role they perform

  • HTW - Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping
  • III - Implementation of IMO Instruments
  • NCSR - Navigation, Communications and Search and Rescue
  • PPR - Pollution Prevention and Response
  • SDC - Ship Design and Construction
  • SSE - Ship Systems and Equipment
  • CCC - Carriage of Cargoes and Containers

Their role is preparatory and technical, and the reason for it is practical. A committee sits for about a week and cannot itself draft detailed regulation, so it remits the work to the sub-committee holding the relevant expertise. The sub-committee develops the draft text, guidelines or unified interpretation, works through the technical argument with the flag States, classification societies and industry observers who attend, and reports back to its parent committee - MSC, MEPC, or both where the subject straddles them.

A sub-committee adopts nothing. It prepares; the parent committee approves; the committee or the Assembly adopts. That separation is what keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands, and it is the specific point the last sentence of limb (a) is asking for.

6. What a convention is

A convention is a formal, legally binding treaty between States. It is negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference or by the Assembly; it is then opened for signature and for ratification, acceptance, approval or accession; and it enters into force only when the conditions written into the convention itself are satisfied - characteristically a stated number of Contracting States representing a stated share of world gross tonnage.

Three consequences follow, and they are what separate a convention from everything else in limb (b):

  1. It binds only the States that have become party to it. A convention in force is not law everywhere; it is law in the States that have accepted it and, through them, on their ships.
  2. It has no effect on a ship until the flag State enacts it domestically and issues the certificates. IMO does not legislate for ships; States do.
  3. Adoption, entry into force and application are three different dates, and confusing them is the single most common error in this subject.

Example: the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974 (SOLAS).

The organising idea, and the mark limb (b) is really testing: a convention and a protocol are treaty instruments that States accept one by one; a code and a resolution are outputs of the Organization whose legal force is borrowed from whatever convention points at them. Ask of any instrument what makes this binding? - and if the answer is not "a convention says so", it is guidance.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs at 8 marks each, so two visible regions and roughly equal length. Limb (a) has four named objects - Assembly, Council, main committees, sub-committees - and a marker will look for four. Giving each its own heading, and answering the sub-committee sentence as a separate question about role rather than as a list of acronyms, is worth more than any amount of extra prose about what IMO does. Limb (b) prints four instrument types and demands an example of each: a table is the fastest way to score it, and the four examples are half the marks.

The one idea that unlocks limb (b)

Legal force in this system is borrowed, not intrinsic. A Code is a technical document with no more legal effect than a textbook until a Convention says it is mandatory. A Resolution is a container - it can carry a mandatory amendment or a recommendation, and its number tells you nothing about which. Once a candidate can say that in one sentence, the distinctions stop needing memorisation, because they follow.

The tacit acceptance point, and why it is worth a line

Amendments to SOLAS and MARPOL enter into force by tacit acceptance: MSC or MEPC adopts an amendment, a date is set by which a stated proportion of Parties may object, and if the objections do not come the amendment is deemed accepted and enters into force on a later stated date. This is why the committees, not the Assembly, are where regulation is actually made - and it explains the three-date sequence adopted / deemed accepted / in force that examiners test repeatedly.

Common mistakes

  • Describing IMO's purposes at length and never naming the organs. The stem says structure.
  • Omitting the sub-committee question entirely. It is the last sentence of limb (a) and it is asked separately for a reason.
  • Saying a sub-committee adopts or approves regulation. It prepares and reports; the parent committee approves; the committee or Assembly adopts.
  • Writing that the Council has 52 members. The enlargement under A.1152(32) is an amendment to the IMO Convention that was not in force at this sitting.
  • Giving four instrument types and no examples, when the stem says "give at least one example of each".
  • Saying a Code is always mandatory, or that a Resolution is always recommendatory. Both are false and both are examiner favourites.
  • Confusing a Protocol with an amendment. A protocol is a separate treaty a State accepts separately; an amendment adopted by tacit acceptance is not.

Examiner traps

  • Asking how many members the Council has and how they are chosen, expecting the three categories and their 10 / 10 / 20 split.
  • Asking who appoints the Secretary-General. The Council appoints, subject to Assembly approval - not the Assembly alone.
  • Asking what the Council cannot do between Assembly sessions. It cannot make recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention; that is reserved to the Assembly.
  • Asking how MARPOL Annex VI came into existence, expecting the 1997 Protocol rather than an amendment.
  • Asking for an instrument that is partly mandatory, expecting the 2008 IS Code - Part A mandatory, Part B recommendatory.
  • Asking how often the Assembly meets. Once every two years in regular session.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which committee would adopt an amendment to the IGF Code, and which sub-committee would have prepared it?
  • A new SOLAS amendment is adopted today. Walk me through to the day it applies to your ship.
  • Your flag State has not ratified a convention. Does it apply to your ship in a port State that has?
  • Name an IMO instrument that is mandatory in part and recommendatory in part.
  • What is the difference between a resolution number beginning A. and one beginning MSC.?

Uncertainty and currency

The IMO Convention itself is not held by the MIW corpus and was not read. The organs, their functions and the Council's three categories are stated from the Convention's architecture as authoritative secondary, and no Article number is asserted - which is deliberate, because an invented Article reference is worse than none.

Resolution A.1152(32) is the one live boundary and it is flagged. It was adopted in December 2021 and requires acceptance by two thirds of Members. It was not in force at February 2024, when the exact-stem MIW answer for that sitting was written, and it was re-checked and found still not in force for February 2025. It is flagged for a currency check because it is precisely the kind of fact that changes without any announcement reaching a ship.

The Secretary-General's date of taking office - 1 January 2024 - rests on the earlier MIW record and is not corpus-verified. It is stated as a tenure rather than as a recent event, which is the correction the twelve-month gap between the two sittings requires and the reason a sitting-relative sentence is never copied forward.

Understand this first

Two limbs, and the trap is that they look like the same question twice. Limb A is about an instrument and wants mechanisms with numbers on them. STCW addresses the human element by defining competences and requiring them to be demonstrated - Part A's tables have a column headed criteria for evaluating competence, which is the whole idea in three words - and by regulating the conditions under which a person can use that competence: medical fitness, revalidation, rest, sobriety and watchkeeping. The provision most candidates forget is regulation I/14, which puts the duty on the company. Limb B is about guidance, and its first mark is a status mark: MSC.1/Circ.1598 is recommendatory - it is not law. What obliges is STCW A-VIII/1, MLC Standard A2.3 and the ISM Code. Hold one further idea and limb B writes itself: a schedule can comply with every rest-hour rule and still be fatiguing, because the rules govern quantity while fatigue is driven by timing, continuity and quality of sleep. Model written answer

1. What the human element is, and why STCW is where it is addressed

The human element is the whole of the human contribution to the safe, secure and clean operation of a ship - competence, health, alertness, communication, decision-making, leadership, culture and the organisational conditions a company creates around all of them. IMO's own position, in resolution A.947(23), is that it is a complex, multi-dimensional issue affecting safety, security and environmental protection across the entire spectrum of shipboard and shore-side activity.

STCW is the instrument that regulates it directly, and it does so in a particular way that is worth naming at the outset: SOLAS regulates the ship, MARPOL regulates the discharge, and STCW regulates the person. Every mechanism below is a variation on one method - define a competence, require it to be demonstrated, and require the conditions under which a person can exercise it.

2. The competence-based architecture - the mechanism STCW actually uses

The single most important structural fact about STCW is that it is competence-based, not time-based. Part A of the Code is mandatory and sets out, for every function and level, a table with four columns:

  1. the competence itself;
  2. the knowledge, understanding and proficiency (KUP) underpinning it;
  3. the methods for demonstrating competence - approved training, service, simulator, workshop skills;
  4. the criteria for evaluating it.

That fourth column is the human-element mechanism in miniature: it forces an assessor to judge observable performance rather than accept a certificate of attendance. Part B is guidance and is recommendatory.

3. Chapter I - the controls on the person and on the company

  • Regulation I/6 - training and assessment. Those who train, assess and supervise must themselves be qualified for the task.
  • Regulation I/8 - quality standards. Every Party must run a quality standards system covering training, assessment and certification, independently evaluated at intervals. This is what stops the competence tables becoming a paper exercise.
  • Regulation I/9 - medical standards. Fitness for duty begins with medical fitness, including eyesight and hearing.
  • Regulation I/11 - revalidation. Certificates are revalidated at intervals not exceeding five years on evidence of continued professional competence - the recognition that competence decays.
  • Regulation I/14 - responsibilities of companies. The one every engineer should be able to quote: the company must ensure seafarers are certificated and assigned in accordance with the Convention, that documentation is held, that seafarers receive ship-specific familiarization before being assigned duties, and that the ship's complement can co-ordinate its activities in an emergency. It places the human element on the employer, not only on the seafarer.
  • Chapter VI - safety familiarization, basic training, personal survival, fire fighting, first aid and personal safety and social responsibilities: the behavioural and communication floor for everyone on board.

4. Chapter VIII - fitness for duty, rest and the alcohol limit

Regulation VIII/1 and Code section A-VIII/1 address the human element at its most physiological. Administrations must require, and take account of, the danger of fatigue, and must enforce:

  • a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period;
  • rest divided into no more than two periods, one of which is at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods;
  • musters and drills conducted so as to minimise disturbance of rest periods;
  • posted watch schedules and records of daily hours of rest, so that compliance is monitorable rather than asserted;
  • a blood alcohol limit of 0.05 %, or 0.25 mg/l in breath, for masters, officers and other seafarers on designated safety, security and marine-environmental duties, together with drug and alcohol abuse prevention.

These limits are deliberately aligned with the ILO's Maritime Labour Convention and are enforced in practice through both. Section 9 below returns to that.

5. Chapters VIII and III - watchkeeping principles and resource management

Regulation VIII/2 and Code section A-VIII/2 set the principles to be observed in keeping a watch. For the engine department these are explicitly human-element provisions: the chief engineer officer, in consultation with the master, determines the watch composition having regard to the ship's machinery, the mode of operation, and - expressly - the fitness for duty of the personnel available; watchkeepers must be familiar with the machinery and its controls, and a periodically unattended machinery space is treated as a mode requiring its own arrangements rather than as an absence of watchkeeping.

The 2010 Manila amendments added the competences that make the human element examinable rather than merely aspirational. Tables A-III/1 and A-III/2 now require engine-room resource management at operational level and use of leadership and managerial skills at management level - allocation and prioritisation of resources, effective communication, assertiveness, obtaining and maintaining situational awareness, consideration of team experience, workload and task management, and decision-making techniques. That is crew resource management under another name, made a certificated competence.

MSC.486(103) and MSC.487(103), in force 1 January 2023, are a current example of the same mechanism keeping pace with technology - a defined high-voltage threshold and formal recognition of the electro-technical officer in the operational-level definition.

6. What is adopted and NOT yet in force - the mark most candidates miss

⚠ Resolution MSC.560(108) introduces mandatory minimum training on the prevention of harassment, bullying and sexual assault into STCW Code table A-VI/1-4. It was adopted in 2024 and it enters into force on 1 January 2026. At this sitting it is adopted but NOT in force. Writing that STCW requires this training is wrong; writing that it will require it from 1 January 2026 is right, and shows the examiner exactly the distinction being tested.

The comprehensive review of STCW being taken forward through the HTW Sub-Committee is likewise in progress and not adopted. Nothing from it is law and nothing from it may be cited as such.

7. The IMO fatigue guidance - what it is, and what it is not

The current IMO instrument is the Guidelines on fatigue, MSC.1/Circ.1598, approved by the Maritime Safety Committee at its 100th session and issued in 2019. It superseded MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001, and the revision was substantial rather than cosmetic: the science of sleep, circadian disruption and sleep debt had moved on by nearly two decades.

Its status must be stated before its content. It is a circular - recommendatory guidance, not law. It creates no obligation of its own. What makes fatigue management mandatory is the combination of STCW A-VIII/1, MLC Standard A2.3 and the ISM Code; the circular tells the industry how to discharge those duties intelligently. An answer that presents the Guidelines as a requirement has misdescribed the whole regime.

The Guidelines are addressed not to one actor but to several - the seafarer, the master and senior officers, the company and its shore-side management, the Administration, and those who design ships and organise work on board - on the explicit reasoning that fatigue cannot be managed by the person suffering it.

8. Causes and effects - what the guidance asks the industry to recognise

CauseHow it operates at sea
Insufficient and fragmented sleepThe dominant cause. A six-on / six-off watch system can satisfy the rest-hour rules and still make a consolidated sleep period impossible, so sleep debt accumulates over a voyage
Circadian disruptionNight watches, rotating schedules and repeated time-zone change put the work period against the body clock's lowest alertness, characteristically in the early hours
Workload and port turnaroundIntense port periods, surveys, inspections, bunkering and repairs concentrated into short stays, with the administrative burden falling on the same senior officers
Environmental stressorsNoise, vibration, ship motion, heat and humidity in machinery spaces - each degrades sleep quality even where sleep time is nominally available
Manning and organisationReduced complements, short-sea trading, unfamiliar routines, poor handovers, and non-work stressors such as fatigue from travel to join
Health and lifestyleDiet, hydration, exercise, medical conditions including sleep apnoea, and the effect of alcohol and some medication on sleep quality

The effects are cognitive before they are physical, which is why fatigue is dangerous and hard to self-detect: reduced vigilance and situational awareness, slowed reaction, impaired judgement and risk assessment, memory lapses, degraded communication and teamwork, mood change - and, at the extreme, uncontrollable micro-sleeps. The fatigued person's own assessment of their capability is itself impaired.

9. Mitigation and management - the three levels, and the statutory backstop

Company and shore-side - where the leverage actually is.

  • Treat fatigue as an identified risk within the safety management system under the ISM Code, with controls, monitoring and review - element 6 (resources and personnel) and element 7 (shipboard operations) are the natural hooks.
  • Set realistic manning. The Minimum Safe Manning Document issued under SOLAS regulation V/14, following the principles in resolution A.1047(27), is a floor and not a target - and the assessment behind it is supposed to account for the actual workload.
  • Plan voyages, port calls, surveys and inspections so that they do not systematically collide with rest, and reduce the administrative burden placed on senior officers.
  • Maintain a non-punitive reporting culture so that fatigue and near-misses are reported rather than concealed - a punitive culture destroys the data the system depends on.

On board - what the Chief Engineer controls.

  • Design watch and work patterns to protect at least one consolidated sleep period, rather than merely to satisfy the arithmetic of the rest-hour table.
  • Monitor the rest-hour records as data, not as a filing task. Persistent use of the exception provisions is a signal that the manning or the plan is wrong.
  • Schedule planned maintenance and drills to minimise rest disturbance, and manage the unattended-machinery-space call-out pattern deliberately - repeated night call-outs are a fatigue mechanism in themselves.
  • Use proper handovers, task rotation, controlled napping where the company policy permits it, and adequate rest before high-workload periods.
  • Set the example. A department in which the senior engineer visibly works through rest periods will not report fatigue.

Design and habitability. Accommodation location and insulation, noise - the Code on Noise Levels On Board Ships, resolution MSC.337(91), mandatory through SOLAS II-1/3-12 - vibration, lighting, ventilation, bunk quality and the layout of alarms and controls all determine whether the rest hours a seafarer is given are rest hours they can actually use.

The statutory backstop, stated precisely. Under MLC Standard A2.3 a Member must fix either a maximum of 14 hours of work in any 24-hour period and 72 hours in any 7-day period, or a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24 and 77 in any 7 days. Rest may be in no more than two periods, one at least 6 hours, with the interval between consecutive periods not exceeding 14 hours. Drills must be conducted so as to minimise the disturbance of rest periods and not induce fatigue. Where a seafarer is on call - the Standard names the unattended machinery space expressly - they must receive adequate compensatory rest if the normal rest period is disturbed by call-outs. A table of shipboard working arrangements is posted in the working language and in English, and records of daily hours are kept, copied to the seafarer and endorsed by the master. Exceptions are permitted only through authorised or registered collective agreements, and nothing in the Standard removes the master's authority to require work necessary for the immediate safety of the ship, persons on board or cargo, or to assist others in distress at sea.

The 2022 amendments to the MLC Code entered into force on 23 December 2024 - two months before this sitting - and are the latest set in force. The amendments approved at the 113th Session in June 2025 are later than this sitting and are not in force.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs at 8 marks each. Limb A fails most often through vagueness - a page about how important people are, with no regulation number in it. The fix is to organise it by chapter: I, VI, VIII and III, in that order, each with what it actually requires. Limb B fails through the opposite problem - a list of causes and a list of cures with no instrument named. The fix is to open limb B by identifying MSC.1/Circ.1598 and stating that it is guidance, then to give causes, effects and mitigation at three levels, and to close on the mandatory instruments that make any of it enforceable.

The single distinction that carries limb B

The IMO fatigue Guidelines are not law and the rest-hour limits are. A circular recommends; STCW A-VIII/1, MLC Standard A2.3 and the ISM Code oblige. Say that in one sentence early and the rest of the limb reads as an informed answer rather than a description of a pamphlet. It is also the honest position: MSC.1/Circ.1598 creates no duty on anyone.

Why the rest-hour rules are necessary but not sufficient

The most valuable single idea in this whole subject is that a schedule can be fully compliant and still be fatiguing. Six-on / six-off satisfies 10 hours in 24 and 77 in 7 - two six-hour blocks is exactly the maximum permitted division - and yet it can deny a consolidated sleep period for weeks. The rules set a floor on quantity; fatigue is driven by quality, timing and continuity. A candidate who states that, and then says the Guidelines exist precisely because compliance is not the same as alertness, has understood the topic rather than memorised it.

The company, not the seafarer, is the duty-bearer that matters

STCW regulation I/14 puts responsibility on the company; the ISM Code puts the system on the company; the MLC puts the hours obligation on the Member and the shipowner; and the IMO Guidelines are addressed to shore management as much as to the seafarer. The common thread is that fatigue is an organisational output, not a personal failing - and the fatigued individual is the one least able to assess it. Saying so is worth more than another paragraph of symptoms.

Common mistakes

  • Writing limb A with no chapter or regulation numbers. Chapter I, VI, VIII and the chapter III competence tables are the skeleton.
  • Omitting regulation I/14. The company's responsibilities are the human-element provision most directly examinable at management level.
  • Presenting MSC.1/Circ.1598 as mandatory. It is recommendatory guidance.
  • Stating that STCW requires training on prevention of harassment and bullying. MSC.560(108) enters into force on 1 January 2026 and is not in force at this sitting.
  • Quoting only the 10 and 77 figures and omitting the two-period division, the six-hour block and the fourteen-hour interval - which is where most real non-compliance sits.
  • Forgetting the on-call compensatory rest provision, which is the one that bites hardest on an unattended machinery space and is therefore the most relevant of all to an engineer.
  • Treating the Minimum Safe Manning Document as a target rather than a floor.
  • Confusing the instruments: STCW and the fatigue Guidelines are IMO; the MLC is ILO.

Examiner traps

  • Asking for the maximum interval between consecutive rest periods, expecting 14 hours - a figure that is easy to confuse with the 14-hour maximum work period in the MLC's alternative limb.
  • Asking whether a six-on / six-off system complies. It can; that is precisely why the question is asked.
  • Asking who the IMO fatigue Guidelines are addressed to, expecting more than "the seafarer".
  • Asking what STCW requires of the company, expecting regulation I/14 rather than a certificate list.
  • Asking for the blood alcohol limit - 0.05 %, or 0.25 mg/l in breath.
  • Asking what happens to rest when the ship is on UMS and the duty engineer is called out repeatedly. Adequate compensatory rest is required.
  • Asking whether the master can override the rest hours - yes, for the immediate safety of ship, persons or cargo, or to assist others in distress.

Understand this first

The first thing to notice about this question is that the term in it is not an IMO term. There is no instrument called an Integrated Safety Management System and no IMO definition of one. What exists is the ISM Code, adopted by resolution A.741(18) and made mandatory by SOLAS chapter IX, which requires a Safety Management System. Saying that plainly is worth marks, because the alternative is to invent requirements for an instrument that does not exist. Underneath, the Code's own idea is simpler than its twelve elements suggest: it prescribes almost no technical content at all. It says have a policy, write down how you work, define who decides, report what goes wrong, analyse it, audit yourself and review the result - a self-correcting loop. Integration is nothing more than the recognition that a company can honestly run one such loop, not four. And integration never softens the Code: compliance is still proved by the DOC to the company and the SMC to the ship, and an SMC cannot exist without a valid DOC.

Model written answer

1. What the term means - and what it is not

⚠ Begin by being accurate about the term, because the whole answer depends on it. There is no IMO instrument called an Integrated Safety Management System, and no IMO definition of one. What the ISM Code creates and defines is a Safety Management System (SMS) - a structured and documented system enabling Company personnel to implement effectively the Company safety and environmental protection policy - and SOLAS chapter IX makes it mandatory.

"Integrated" is industry and management usage, and it carries two related meanings, both of which the question is reaching for:

  1. Horizontal integration - the SMS is not run as a separate system alongside the company's quality, environmental, occupational health and safety, energy, security and labour-compliance systems, but merged with them into one management system with a single documentation structure, one audit programme, one corrective-action process and one management review.
  2. Vertical and operational integration - the SMS is embedded in how the ship is actually run rather than kept as a manual in the office: joined to the planned maintenance system, the permit-to-work system, the risk assessments, the bridge and engine-room procedures, the purchasing and crewing processes, and the shore organisation that supports them.

Stating this is worth marks rather than costing them. An answer that treats ISMS as a defined IMO instrument will invent requirements the Code does not contain; an answer that quietly rewrites the question as "describe the SMS" has not answered what was asked. The correct handling is to answer the printed question and be precise about the vocabulary once.

2. The ISM Code itself - instrument, hook, objectives and functional requirements

Instrument and hook. The International Safety Management Code was adopted by resolution A.741(18) and made mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX, introduced by the 1994 SOLAS Conference and phased in from 1 July 1998. It has been amended five times, most recently by resolution MSC.353(92), in force 1 January 2015 - which is the text in force at this sitting.

Objectives (section 1.2). Safety at sea; prevention of human injury or loss of life; and avoidance of damage to the environment, in particular the marine environment, and to property. The safety-management objectives are to provide for safe practices and a safe working environment; to assess all identified risks to ships, personnel and the environment and establish appropriate safeguards; and to continuously improve safety-management skills ashore and aboard, including preparing for emergencies.

That middle objective is the risk-assessment mandate and it is the reason the Code is compatible with an integrated system at all: it obliges a company to identify, assess and control risk, which is precisely the common core of every other management-system standard.

Functional requirements for an SMS (section 1.4) - six things every SMS must contain:

  1. a safety and environmental protection policy;
  2. instructions and procedures for safe operation and environmental protection, in compliance with international and flag State legislation;
  3. defined levels of authority and lines of communication between and among shore and shipboard personnel;
  4. procedures for reporting accidents and non-conformities;
  5. procedures to prepare for and respond to emergencies;
  6. procedures for internal audits and management reviews.

The practical form integration takes is unglamorous and is exactly what an examiner wants named: one manual structure, one document-control procedure, one risk register, one non-conformity and corrective-action process, one internal audit programme covering all the standards in a single visit, and one management review at which all of them are considered together.

4. Why it matters - the significance in maritime operations

  • It removes contradiction. Parallel systems generate conflicting instructions for the same task, and the crew resolve the conflict by ignoring one of them. A single system means one procedure for one job.
  • It cuts the administrative burden, which is a safety measure and not merely an economy. Duplicate manuals, duplicate audits and duplicate reporting consume the time of the same senior officers who are also expected to supervise the work - and they are a documented contributor to fatigue.
  • It makes risk visible in one place. A single risk register lets a company see that the same defect is producing an environmental incident, an injury and an off-hire, instead of recording it three times in three systems and correcting none of them properly.
  • It makes audit meaningful. One combined audit examines the operation; three separate audits examine three sets of paperwork.
  • It aligns the shore organisation. The Code's requirement for defined authority and lines of communication is only real if the technical, safety, environmental and crewing departments answer to one system - otherwise the ship receives four sets of priorities.
  • It is the condition for continual improvement. Plan-do-check-act only closes if reports, analysis, audit and management review feed one loop.

5. How the arrangement ensures ISM compliance - certification and verification

Integration does not soften the Code. Compliance is tested, and the test is the certification chain in sections 13 to 16, which is the answer to the second half of limb (a).

  • Document of Compliance (DOC) - issued to the Company after verification that its shore and shipboard systems comply, for the ship types it covers. Valid five years, subject to annual verification within three months of the anniversary date. A copy is carried on board.
  • Safety Management Certificate (SMC) - issued to the ship after verification that the Company and its shipboard management operate in accordance with the approved SMS. Valid five years, subject to at least one intermediate verification, between the second and third anniversary. An SMC cannot exist without a valid DOC.
  • Interim certification - an Interim DOC for a new company or a new ship type, and an Interim SMC for a new ship, on delivery, on flag transfer or on change of company.
  • Internal audit - the Company audits its own safety-management activities at intervals not exceeding twelve months, by personnel independent of the areas being audited unless the size of the company makes that impracticable. This is the mechanism that keeps the system honest between external verifications.
  • Management review - the Company periodically evaluates the effectiveness of the SMS, and this is where an integrated system pays: one review covers every standard at once.
  • Reports and analysis - element 9 requires non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences to be reported, investigated and analysed with the objective of improving safety and pollution prevention. Reporting alone is not compliance; the analysis and the corrective action are.
  • The Designated Person Ashore - element 4 - has direct access to the highest level of management and provides the link between the Company and those on board. In an integrated system the DPA is the single route by which a shipboard concern reaches the top, regardless of which standard it arises under.
  • The Master's overriding authority - element 5 - remains untouched by any amount of integration: the master retains the authority and responsibility to take whatever action is necessary in the interests of safety and pollution prevention, and to request Company assistance. The Company must state that in the SMS.
  • Administration oversight - the flag Administration verifies and certifies, following the Revised Guidelines on implementation of the ISM Code by Administrations, resolution A.1184(33), and port State control checks the certificates and can pursue clear grounds into the system itself.

6. Key elements aligned with the Code - enhancing safety

  • Element 1.2.2.2 - risk assessment. The requirement to assess all identified risks and establish safeguards is the shared spine of every integrated standard, and it is where an OH&S regime and the SMS become the same document.
  • Element 6 - resources and personnel. Qualified, medically fit, properly familiarised crew, with training needs identified and met, and SMS documents in a working language understood by the crew.
  • Element 7 - shipboard operations. Documented procedures for key shipboard operations, including those where an error is not recoverable - enclosed-space entry, hot work, bunkering, high-voltage isolation.
  • Element 8 - emergency preparedness. Identified potential emergencies, drills and exercises, and a response organisation that includes the shore side.
  • Element 9 - reports and analysis. Non-conformity, accident and near-miss reporting, and the analysis that turns it into corrective action.
  • Element 10 - maintenance. A planned maintenance system, with critical equipment and systems identified and specific measures - including regular testing of standby arrangements - to promote their reliability.
  • Element 12 - internal audit and review. The self-correcting loop.
  • Resolution MSC.428(98) - cyber risk treated as an ISM risk within the SMS rather than as an IT matter kept somewhere else.

7. The same elements aligned to environmental protection

  • Element 2 - the policy is expressly a safety AND environmental protection policy. Environmental performance is not bolted on; it is in the Code's own objective.
  • Element 7 carries the operational procedures that prevent pollution: oily-water separator and 15 ppm equipment operation, sludge and bilge management, garbage segregation, ballast water exchange or treatment, cargo and bunker transfer, and the Oil Record Book discipline.
  • Element 10 keeps pollution-prevention equipment working, which is where most detentions in this area actually originate - and, integrated with the SEEMP, links maintenance condition to fuel consumption and emissions.
  • Elements 8 and 9 carry the SOPEP or SMPEP response and the analysis of spills and near-spills.
  • Element 12 audits environmental compliance in the same visit as safety compliance, so a systemic MARPOL weakness is found by the company rather than by a port State.

8. The same elements aligned to operational efficiency

  • One documentation set (element 11) - fewer manuals, controlled revisions, and a crew that can find the procedure it needs.
  • Planned maintenance and critical-equipment identification (element 10) - fewer unplanned failures, less off-hire, better spares provisioning, and a rational basis for condition monitoring.
  • Analysis of non-conformities (element 9) - recurring defects are engineered out rather than repeatedly repaired.
  • Defined authority and communication (element 3 and functional requirement 3) - decisions taken at the right level, without the delay of an ambiguous chain.
  • Combined audits and one management review - materially less time consumed on board, and one coherent set of findings.
  • Better vetting, port State control and charter outcomes - a clean, integrated system is what an inspector or a charterer is actually looking for, and it converts directly into commercial acceptance.
  • Reduced administrative burden on senior officers - which returns supervision time to the deck and the engine room, and closes the loop back to safety.

The closing idea, and the one that unifies both limbs: the ISM Code does not prescribe a particular set of procedures - it prescribes a system that finds and fixes its own weaknesses. Integration is simply the recognition that a company can only run one such loop honestly, and that splitting it into four produces four sets of records and no improvement.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The mark split is 10 + 6, and limb (a) is doing three jobs - explain the concept, state its significance, and say how it ensures ISM compliance - while limb (b) is doing one. So limb (a) gets five sections and limb (b) three, and the third job in limb (a) is answered as a separate section on certification and verification rather than left implicit. The highest-value decision in the whole answer is the first paragraph: naming the term honestly. It costs three sentences, it prevents every downstream invention, and it demonstrates exactly the precision about instruments that a Class I examiner is looking for.

Answer the question that was printed

There is a temptation to treat "ISMS" as a typographical error and write about the SMS. Do not. The examiner has used a real industry term, and the marks are in integration - one manual, one audit programme, one risk register, one management review. An answer that silently rewrites the question loses the very thing being tested, and an answer that invents an IMO instrument called the ISMS is worse. Name it, place it, then answer it.

The idea underneath the Code

The ISM Code is unusual among IMO instruments because it prescribes almost no technical content. It does not say how thick a plate must be or how a separator must perform. It says: have a policy, write down how you do things, define who decides what, report what goes wrong, analyse it, audit yourself, and review the result. It is a goal-based, self-correcting loop - plan, do, check, act - and its whole theory is that a company that runs that loop honestly will find its own failures before a casualty does. Once that is understood, the case for integration is obvious: a company can only run one such loop with any honesty.

Certification is where integration is tested

Integration is a management choice; the Code's requirements are not negotiable, and the DOC and SMC are what prove it. Two facts are worth holding precisely because examiners return to them: an SMC cannot exist without a valid DOC - the ship's certificate depends on the company's - and the internal audit interval does not exceed twelve months, by auditors independent of the area audited where the size of the company allows. Those two are the load-bearing numbers in limb (a).

Common mistakes

  • Treating ISMS as an IMO instrument and inventing requirements for it.
  • Rewriting the question as "describe the SMS" and never mentioning integration at all.
  • Listing the twelve elements as a memorised catalogue with no link to safety, environment or efficiency - which is what limb (b) explicitly asks for.
  • Saying the DOC is issued to the ship. It is issued to the Company, for stated ship types; the SMC is the ship's.
  • Forgetting that element 9 requires analysis, not merely reporting. A company that files reports and does nothing with them is non-compliant.
  • Omitting the Designated Person Ashore and their direct access to the highest level of management.
  • Suggesting that integration or a busy shore organisation can qualify the master's overriding authority. It cannot.
  • Treating cyber risk as an IT matter outside the SMS. Resolution MSC.428(98) puts it inside.

Examiner traps

Understand this first

The whole question turns on one distinction, and a candidate who states it in the first two lines has already separated themselves from most of the room: a vetting inspection is commercial, not statutory. No State is involved, no convention compels it, no certificate is issued, and there is no pass and no fail - only a report, published to charterers, with the operator's response beside it. The reason the system exists is the charterer's own exposure - an oil major that fixes a substandard tanker owns the liability and the reputational damage when it spills - so it inspects the ship through SIRE and the office through TMSA, having learnt that the shore organisation predicts performance better than one well-presented visit does. For the Chief Engineer the practical consequence is uncomfortable and simple. A modern inspection draws its questions from a pool, records them photographically, and asks the crew to demonstrate rather than describe - so it cannot be rehearsed. The only preparation that works is continuous readiness, which is what the ISM Code required anyway. And underneath all of it sits one judgement call: a defect that is recorded, risk-assessed and being managed is an ordinary finding; the same defect concealed is a finding about integrity, and that is the one a ship does not recover from.

Model written answer

1. What a vetting inspection is - and the distinction the whole answer turns on

A vetting inspection is an inspection of a tanker carried out on behalf of the oil and chemical industry so that a charterer, cargo owner or terminal can decide whether to employ that ship. It is carried out by an accredited inspector against an industry question set, and the result is a report, not a certificate.

⚠ Vetting is commercial, not statutory - and this is the single most important thing to state.

Statutory inspection (flag or port State)Vetting inspection
AuthorityA State, under an international convention given force in national lawA commercial party, under a contract and an industry programme
BasisSOLAS, MARPOL, MLC, STCW and the flag State's own lawIndustry standards and guidance - which frequently sit above the statutory minimum
OutcomeCertificates, deficiencies, and the power to detainA report. No pass, no fail, no certificate, and no power to detain

The paradox worth stating explicitly is that the commercially weaker instrument is often the more consequential one. A port State control deficiency is rectified and the ship trades on. A pattern of poor vetting reports removes the ship from the trade altogether, because no oil major will nominate her.

2. Purpose - why the industry built this in the first place

  • To manage the charterer's own risk. An oil major that charters a substandard tanker exposes itself to liability, cargo loss, terminal damage and severe reputational harm if that ship has a pollution incident. Vetting is the due diligence it performs before accepting that exposure - and it exists because the great tanker casualties demonstrated that a valid certificate is not the same as a well-run ship.
  • To decide ship acceptance for a specific employment. The screening is not abstract: it is against this cargo, this berth, this terminal, this trade and this season, taking account of the ship's age, condition, structure, previous inspection history and the operator's record.
  • To assess the operator, not only the ship. A well-presented ship with a weak shore organisation is a temporary condition, and the industry knows it.
  • To supply terminals with an acceptance basis, so that a terminal can decline a ship before she arrives rather than after she is alongside.
  • To raise standards above the statutory floor. This is the deliberate design of the whole system: the conventions set a minimum that every ship must meet, and vetting creates a market in which meeting only the minimum is not commercially sufficient.
  • For the owner, it is an asset. A consistently clean vetting record is what secures long-term charters, better rates, easier finance and favourable insurance terms - so preparing for vetting is an investment rather than an imposition.

3. The instruments - ship level, company level, and the reference standards

  • The OCIMF Ship Inspection Report Programme (SIRE) - the ship-level inspection and the database its reports are published in. The current generation is SIRE 2.0, and its distinguishing features are worth naming: the inspection is risk-based and tailored to the individual ship rather than delivered from a single fixed questionnaire; the questions are drawn from a pool, combining a core set with rotational and campaign questions so that no two inspections are identical and the ship cannot be rehearsed to a script; each question is examined for its hardware, its process and its human element; the inspection is captured digitally on a tablet with a photographic record; and the inspector is expected to have the crew demonstrate rather than describe.
  • Tanker Management and Self Assessment (TMSA) - the company-level companion. The operator assesses its own shore management against a set of elements at staged levels of maturity, and charterers review that self-assessment alongside the ship's inspection reports. SIRE looks at the ship; TMSA looks at the office, and a candidate who names both has shown they understand the system rather than the visit.
  • The Chemical Distribution Institute (CDI) scheme performs the equivalent function for chemical and gas carriers.
  • ISGOTT, the International Safety Guide for Oil Tankers and Terminals, is the joint industry reference against which much tanker and ship/shore practice is assessed - including the ship/shore safety checklist.
  • The Pre-Inspection Questionnaire, completed by the operator before the inspection, supplies the ship's particulars, equipment and history and is one of the inputs from which the risk-based question set is assembled. Its accuracy is itself examined: a questionnaire that does not match the ship is a finding in its own right.

4. The process, in sequence

  1. Screening. A charterer considering the ship screens her against the programme database, her inspection history, her age and structure, the operator's TMSA position and the requirements of the intended cargo and terminal.
  2. Request and scheduling. The operator requests or accepts an inspection and it is scheduled for a port where the ship will be in a meaningful operational condition - characteristically during cargo operations, because an inspector needs to see the ship working rather than idle.
  3. Pre-inspection submission. The operator submits the Pre-Inspection Questionnaire and the ship's particulars. An accredited inspector, independent of the operator, is assigned.
  4. Opening meeting. With the master and senior officers - scope, duration, safety arrangements, and the areas to be visited.
  5. Document review. Statutory and class certificates and survey status; class conditions and recommendations; the safety management system and its manuals; the planned maintenance system; the Oil Record Book and other record books; drill, training and familiarisation records; hours of rest; previous inspection reports and the corrective actions taken.
  6. Physical inspection. Bridge and navigation; cargo control room and cargo systems; the inert gas system; deck, mooring arrangements and lifesaving and firefighting equipment; the pump room; the engine room and steering gear compartment; and the accommodation.
  7. Crew interaction. Officers and ratings are asked to demonstrate equipment and procedures relevant to their duties - the human-element component, and the part of a modern inspection least amenable to preparation.
  8. Closing meeting. Observations are reviewed with the master so that the ship knows exactly what has been recorded before the inspector leaves.
  9. Report submission and publication. The report is submitted through the programme and published to subscribing charterers within a defined period.
  10. Operator response. The operator comments on each observation, stating the root cause and the corrective action, and those comments are published alongside the report. The report then remains available for screening for a defined period.
  11. Screening again. Charterers read the report together with the operator's response - and a thin or evasive response damages the operator more than the original observation did.

5. The Chief Engineer's role - preparation

Preparation is where the outcome is decided, and almost all of it is ordinary good engineering practice done consistently rather than a special effort made in the week before.

  • Machinery condition and cleanliness. No oil leaks, no oil in save-alls, bilges clean and dry, no hydrocarbon accumulation anywhere, and hot-surface insulation intact, correctly fitted and free of oil - the classic engine-room fire mechanism and one of the most frequently recorded observations of all. No unauthorised or long-standing temporary repairs; no soft patches, no cable-tied fittings, no blanked alarms.
  • Critical equipment. ISM element 10 requires critical equipment and systems to be identified and specific measures, including regular testing of standby arrangements, to promote their reliability. The Chief Engineer must be able to produce that list and the evidence that the tests happen.
  • The inert gas system - the most tanker-specific item in the engineer's scope. Deck water seal level and operation; the pressure-vacuum breaker charged and correct; the non-return and deck isolation arrangements; oxygen analysers calibrated with records; the low-oxygen and high-oxygen alarms, the low-pressure alarm and the automatic trips tested and recorded; scrubber and blower condition.
  • The pump room - gas detection and its calibration; bilge level alarms; ventilation and the interlocks; lighting and emergency lighting; emergency escape route clear; bearing and casing temperature monitoring; and the rescue arrangements and permit system for entry.
  • Cargo-related machinery - cargo pumps and their sealing arrangements, crude oil washing equipment where fitted, tank cleaning arrangements, and the Oil Discharge Monitoring and Control System.
  • Pollution-prevention equipment - the 15 ppm oily-water separating equipment with a functioning alarm and automatic stopping device, and no bypass of any kind; incinerator; sewage plant; ballast water management equipment; and a sludge and bilge account that reconciles with the Oil Record Book Part I. This is the area where an observation is not merely commercial: it is evidence of a potential MARPOL offence.
  • Emergency and safety systems - emergency generator started on load and its starting arrangements proved; emergency fire pump; quick-closing valves, remote stops and fire dampers operated, not merely inspected; fixed fire-extinguishing system and its release arrangements; steering gear, including the changeover to emergency steering and the communication with the bridge.
  • Documentation. Planned maintenance up to date with no unexplained overdue jobs; work orders and permits complete; risk assessments genuine and specific; lubricating and fuel oil analysis reviewed and acted on; critical spares held and the inventory accurate; class survey status and any conditions properly recorded and being met.
  • People. Familiarisation completed and recorded; engine-room drills held; hours of rest records accurate; training records current. Brief the engineers on what a modern inspection asks of them - that they will be asked to show how something is done, and that a straightforward "I will find out" is a far better answer than a confident wrong one.
  • Close out the previous report. ⚠ A repeat observation is the single most damaging finding available, because it says the corrective action was cosmetic. Every machinery observation from the last inspection should be demonstrably closed, with evidence.

6. The Chief Engineer's role - during the inspection

  • Accompany the inspector throughout the machinery spaces, with an officer who can operate the equipment being examined.
  • Control the safety of the visit - safe access, correct personal protective equipment, awareness of any work in progress, and no operation performed unsafely in order to demonstrate it.
  • Produce documents promptly and accurately. Delay in finding a record reads as a records problem, and it usually is one.
  • ⛔ Never falsify, back-date or reconstruct a record, and never conceal a defect. A known defect that is recorded, risk-assessed and being managed is a normal finding. The same defect concealed and then discovered is a finding about integrity, and it is fatal - to the report and potentially to the ship's employment.
  • Answer factually and within your knowledge. Let the responsible engineer demonstrate their own equipment; do not answer over them.
  • Track the observations as they are raised, so that nothing at the closing meeting is a surprise, and clarify any observation that rests on a misunderstanding at the time.
  • Attend the closing meeting with the master and make sure every machinery observation is understood as recorded.

7. The Chief Engineer's role - after the inspection

  • Root cause analysis, not description. "Insulation was oil-soaked" is an observation. "A fuel-pump seal had been leaking for three weeks, the leak was not reported because the reporting route was unclear to the watchkeepers, and the insulation was not renewed because the material was not held" is a root cause - and it is what the operator's published comment must be able to say.
  • Corrective and preventive action, with a named owner and a date, and preventive action that addresses the cause rather than the instance.
  • Feed it into the safety management system. Observations are treated under ISM element 9 as matters to be reported and analysed, and reviewed under element 12. They are also evidence for the company's TMSA position - so a well-handled inspection strengthens the operator's standing as well as the ship's.
  • Verify closure. Confirm that the action was actually taken and is effective, rather than that a form was signed - and consider whether the same finding applies to sister ships in the fleet.
  • Retain the evidence, because the next inspector will look for exactly this.

8. How vetting sits alongside the statutory regime - and what to avoid

  • It confers nothing and removes nothing in law. No certificate is issued, no flag State is engaged, and a poor report cannot detain the ship. What it withdraws is commercial acceptance.
  • It overlaps almost entirely with the SMS. Most vetting observations are, in substance, findings about the safety management system - a procedure that is not followed, a record that is not kept, a defect that is not reported. Treating them as SMS findings under ISM elements 9 and 12 is both the correct handling and the efficient one.
  • ⚠ The failure mode to name explicitly is inspection-driven maintenance - a ship that is painted, cleaned and made presentable before an inspection and neglected between them. It produces exactly the pattern of repeat observations that destroys a vetting record, and it is a safety failure before it is a commercial one. The correct posture is a ship that is always ready for inspection because it is always properly maintained - which is also, precisely, what the ISM Code requires.
  • Do not treat vetting standards as optional because they are not law. Where an industry standard exceeds the convention minimum and the charter or the terminal requires it, it is a contractual obligation, and breaching it has its own consequences.
  • Do not let vetting displace the statutory obligations. A clean SIRE report is not a defence to a MARPOL breach, and an inspector's silence about a defect does not make it lawful.

The one sentence that carries the question

A port State deficiency is rectified and the ship trades on; a pattern of poor vetting reports means no oil major will nominate her, and the ship leaves the trade. Say that early. It explains why owners take vetting so seriously, why the standards sit above the convention minimum, and why the Chief Engineer's preparation matters commercially as well as technically.

Ship, company, and why both are inspected

SIRE inspects the ship; TMSA assesses the office. The industry learnt that a ship presented well for one visit tells you very little, and that the reliable predictor is the shore organisation - its procedures, its resourcing, its follow-up, its maturity. A candidate who names both, and says which is which, has understood the architecture. It also explains why an operator's published response to an observation matters as much as the observation: the response is evidence about the office.

Why a modern inspection cannot be rehearsed

The design of the current generation of the programme is deliberately anti-rehearsal, and knowing why is worth a mark. Questions are drawn from a pool rather than a fixed list, so no two inspections are the same; each is examined for hardware, process and human element, so a working machine with no procedure and an untrained operator still produces a finding; the record is photographic; and the crew are asked to demonstrate. The practical consequence for a Chief Engineer is that the only viable preparation is continuous readiness - which is what the ISM Code was written to produce in the first place.

Common mistakes

  • Describing vetting as a statutory inspection, or confusing it with port State control or class survey. It is commercial.
  • Saying a ship passes or fails a vetting inspection. There is no pass, no fail and no certificate - only a report, and a charterer's decision.
  • Naming SIRE and not TMSA, and so describing the visit rather than the system.
  • Writing the Chief Engineer's role as a cleaning list. The marks are in critical equipment, records that reconcile, systems tested and observations closed out.
  • Omitting the after stage entirely - root cause, corrective action, feeding it into the SMS, verifying closure.
  • Forgetting the inert gas system, which is the most tanker-specific machinery item in the question.
  • Suggesting that records should be tidied or a defect hidden. A recorded, risk-assessed defect is normal; a concealed one is fatal.
  • Treating industry standards as optional because they are not law - where the charter requires them they are contractual.

Understand this first

Admiralty law exists to solve a security problem, and holding that in mind makes the whole question fall out. A shipowner may be a single-ship company in a jurisdiction the claimant will never reach, with unknown directors and no assets - but the ship is right there, identifiable, valuable, and present only until the cargo is discharged. So admiralty allows an action in rem, against the ship herself: arrest her, obtain security, and if necessary have the court sell her with clean title. That is why arrest, not judgment, is the practical objective, and why the maritime lien follows the ship through a change of owner, registration and flag. In India the governing statute is the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, in force 1 April 2018, which swept away the nineteenth-century colonial Acts under which only three High Courts could hear such a claim. Then limb (b), where the real test is hidden. Section 13 does not tell you what an assessor must be. It requires the Central Government to appoint by notification a list of assessors with such qualifications and experience in admiralty and maritime matters - and the nature of their duties and their fees - 'in the manner as may be prescribed', which section 2(1)(j) defines as by rules made by the Central Government. The statute creates the mechanism and delegates the criteria. Say that, then say what the standard contemplates, and you have answered a question most candidates will answer by invention. And keep the assessor distinct from the expert witness: an assessor advises the court and gives no evidence; an expert is called by a party, testifies on oath and is cross-examined. Section 13(2) exists to make clear that having one does not exclude the other.

Model written answer

1. What admiralty law is, and the feature that makes it different

Admiralty law is the body of law by which a court determines maritime claims and exercises a jurisdiction over vessels themselves. Its defining feature - the one that separates it from every other kind of civil litigation - is the action in rem: the claim is brought against the ship, and the ship can be arrested and, if necessary, sold by the court to satisfy the claim.

The commercial reason for this is straightforward. A shipowner may be a single-ship company registered in a jurisdiction the claimant will never reach, its directors unknown and its assets nil. The one asset that is certainly present, identifiable and valuable is the ship - and she is only present for as long as the cargo takes to discharge. An in rem jurisdiction lets a claimant obtain security from the asset in the few days it is within reach, which is why arrest, and not judgment, is the practical objective of most admiralty proceedings.

2. The statute in force, and what it replaced

The governing statute is the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 - Act 22 of 2017, dated 9 August 2017 and brought into force on 1 April 2018. Its long title states its purpose exactly: to consolidate the laws relating to admiralty jurisdiction, legal proceedings in connection with vessels, their arrest, detention, sale and other matters connected therewith or incidental thereto.

What it replaced was colonial and fragmented. Section 17(1) repeals the application in India of the Admiralty Court Act, 1840, the Admiralty Court Act, 1861, the Colonial Courts of Admiralty Act, 1890, the Colonial Courts of Admiralty (India) Act, 1891, and the provisions of the Letters Patent, 1865 so far as they applied to the admiralty jurisdiction of the Bombay, Calcutta and Madras High Courts. Until 2018, in other words, an Indian admiralty claim was governed by nineteenth-century English statutes, and only three High Courts could hear one.

Section 17 also preserves continuity: proceedings pending immediately before commencement continue, but are adjudicated under the 2017 Act, and things done under the repealed enactments are deemed done under the corresponding provisions of the new one.

3. The scope of the jurisdiction - which courts, what waters, what vessels

  • Which courts (section 2(1)(e)). The jurisdiction is High Court jurisdiction, and the Act names them: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras, Karnataka, Gujarat, Orissa and Kerala, and the High Court of Judicature at Hyderabad for Telangana and Andhra Pradesh - or any other High Court as may be notified by the Central Government. That last clause is what allows the list to grow. The extension from three chartered High Courts to a notifiable list of coastal High Courts is one of the Act's principal achievements.
  • What waters (section 3). Jurisdiction over all maritime claims vests in the respective High Courts and is exercisable over the waters up to and including the territorial waters of their respective jurisdictions. The proviso matters: the Central Government may, by notification, extend that jurisdiction up to the limit defined in section 2 of the Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976.
  • What vessels (section 2(1)(l)). 'Vessel' is defined broadly - any ship, boat, sailing vessel or other description of vessel used or constructed for use in navigation by water, whether propelled or not, and expressly including a barge, lighter or other floating vessel, a hovercraft and an off-shore industry mobile unit. The breadth is deliberate: it captures the craft that would otherwise fall outside a narrow definition of 'ship'.
  • Against whom (sections 5 and 6). The Act provides for both action in rem, by arrest of the vessel, and action in personam against the person liable - so a claimant is not confined to one route.

4. What may be claimed - maritime claims, and the maritime liens

Section 4 enumerates the maritime claims over which a High Court may exercise jurisdiction. It is a long, closed list, and its coverage is the practical measure of the Act's scope. It includes disputes over possession or ownership of a vessel and between co-owners over her employment or earnings; mortgages and charges; loss or damage caused by the operation of a vessel; loss of life or personal injury in direct connection with her operation; loss of or damage to goods; agreements for the carriage of goods or passengers and for the use or hire of a vessel, whether in a charterparty or otherwise; salvage, including special compensation where the vessel or her cargo threatened damage to the environment; towage and pilotage; goods, materials, provisions, bunker fuel, equipment including containers, and services supplied to the vessel for her operation, management, preservation or maintenance; her construction, repair, conversion or equipping; port, harbour, canal, dock and light dues; claims by the master or crew for wages, repatriation cost and social insurance contributions - and this one is expressed notwithstanding sections 150 and 151 of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958; disbursements made on behalf of the vessel; particular and general average; disputes over a contract for the sale of the vessel; and insurance premium, including mutual insurance calls.

A maritime lien is a narrower and far more powerful thing. Section 9 recognises five, and gives their inter se priority in this order:

  1. wages and other sums due to the master, officers and other members of the complement, including repatriation costs and social insurance contributions;
  2. loss of life or personal injury, on land or water, in direct connection with the operation of the vessel;
  3. salvage, including special compensation;
  4. port, canal and other waterway dues, pilotage dues and other statutory dues related to the vessel;
  5. claims in tort arising out of loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel, other than loss or damage to cargo and containers carried on her.

Two features of the lien are what make it formidable, and both are examinable. First, it attaches to the vessel and follows her: section 9(2) provides that it continues notwithstanding any change of ownership, of registration or of flag - so a buyer takes the ship with the lien on her. Second, it is time-limited: it is extinguished after one year unless the vessel has been arrested or seized within that period and the arrest has led to a forced sale by the High Court - except for a wage claim, where the period is two years from the date the sum falls due. The period runs continuously from discharge from the vessel for a wage claim and from when the claim arises for the others, with time spent under arrest excluded.

No maritime lien attaches for oil pollution or hazardous and noxious substance damage compensable under other law, or for damage from the radioactive properties of nuclear fuel or waste - section 9(4). And section 10 sets the order of priority of claims overall: first claims secured by a maritime lien, then registered mortgages and charges of the same nature, then all other claims - claims within a category ranking equally, and salvage claims ranking in inverse order of time, the later salvor first.

5. Arrest, security and the protections against its abuse

  • Arrest in rem (section 5(1)). The High Court may order the arrest of a vessel within its jurisdiction to provide security against a maritime claim, where it has reason to believe that the person who owned her when the claim arose is liable and still owns her; or that the demise charterer at that time is liable and is now the demise charterer or the owner; or that the claim is based on a mortgage or similar charge; or that it relates to ownership or possession; or that it is against the owner, demise charterer, manager or operator and is secured by a maritime lien under section 9.
  • Sister-ship arrest (section 5(2)). Another vessel may be arrested in lieu of the one against which the claim is made - except for a claim under section 4(1)(a), a dispute about possession or ownership, which is inherently ship-specific.
  • In personam and its restrictions (sections 6 and 7). The High Court may proceed in personam, but section 7 restricts that route where the claim arises from a collision, from a manoeuvre or its omission, or from non-compliance with the collision regulations made under section 285 of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 - requiring, among other conditions, that the cause of action arise wholly or in part in India.
  • Protection against wrongful arrest (section 11). This is the counterweight and it is real. As a condition of arrest or of maintaining one, the court may require the claimant to give an unconditional undertaking or security for loss or damage the defendant may suffer - expressly including where the arrest was wrongful or unjustified or where excessive security was demanded. The person providing security may apply to have it reduced, modified or cancelled.
  • Abandonment (section 11(3)). If the owner or demise charterer abandons the vessel after arrest, the High Court shall cause her to be auctioned and the proceeds dealt with, within forty-five days of arrest or abandonment - extendable by a further thirty days for reasons recorded in writing. A practical provision: an abandoned ship deteriorates, incurs port dues and endangers her crew.
  • Clean title (section 8). Rights vest on a court sale free of prior claims, which is what makes the court's sale worth anything to a buyer - and therefore what makes the security worth anything to the claimant.
  • Procedure and appeal. The Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 applies so far as not inconsistent (section 12); an appeal lies from any judgment, decree, final or interim order of a single Judge to a Division Bench of the same High Court (section 14); and the Supreme Court may transfer proceedings (section 15).

6. The significance - why the Act matters

  • It ended reliance on repealed colonial English statutes. Indian admiralty jurisdiction had rested on nineteenth-century Acts that England itself had long replaced, supplemented by judicial development. The 2017 Act supplies a modern statutory basis.
  • It widened access from three High Courts to a notifiable list of coastal High Courts, so a claimant is no longer confined to Bombay, Calcutta or Madras and can proceed where the ship actually is.
  • It codified maritime claims and maritime liens, with a stated priority order and stated limitation periods. Financiers, insurers, bunker suppliers, crew and salvors can now assess where they stand before they are owed money rather than afterwards.
  • It aligned Indian law with the international pattern - the structure of the claims list, the liens and the arrest regime follows the model of the modern international arrest and maritime-lien conventions, which makes Indian practice legible to foreign parties even though the alignment is by drafting rather than by treaty.
  • It balanced the claimant's remedy against the owner's protection. Arrest is commercially devastating, and section 11's wrongful-arrest security is what keeps it a remedy rather than a weapon.
  • It strengthens India as a maritime forum. A predictable arrest jurisdiction across the coast supports ship finance, repair, bunkering and supply, because a supplier who can arrest is a supplier who will extend credit.
  • For a ship's officer it is directly practical. Wages carry the first maritime lien and a two-year period rather than one - so an unpaid crew has the strongest claim in the queue, ahead even of the mortgagee bank.

7. Assessors - what section 13 actually provides

Section 13 is headed 'Assistance of assessors', and it has two subsections. It is short, and reading it closely is the whole of limb (b).

  • Section 13(1). Notwithstanding anything contained in any other law for the time being in force, the Central Government shall appoint by notification a list of assessors with such qualifications and experience in admiralty and maritime matters, the nature of the duties to be performed by them, the fees to be paid to them and other ancillary or incidental matters for the purposes of the Act, in the manner as may be prescribed.
  • Section 13(2). The appointment of assessors shall not be construed as a bar to the examination of expert witnesses by any of the parties in any admiralty proceeding.

⚠ Three things follow, and the first is the one the question is really testing.

  1. The Act does not state the qualifications. It requires that the assessors have qualifications and experience in admiralty and maritime matters, and it leaves what those qualifications are to be fixed 'in the manner as may be prescribed'. Section 2(1)(j) defines 'prescribed' as prescribed by rules made by the Central Government under this Act, and the rule-making power is in section 16. The Act creates the mechanism and delegates the criteria. A candidate who recites a list of qualifications as though the statute contained one is describing delegated legislation that the statute does not print.
  2. The appointment is central, not case-by-case. It is the Central Government that appoints, by notification, a list - a standing panel from which a court can draw - rather than each High Court appointing its own expert in each case.
  3. The assessor does not displace the expert witness. Subsection (2) exists precisely to prevent the argument that, an assessor having been appointed, a party may not call its own expert. Both routes remain open.

8. The role of assessors in practice, and how they differ from expert witnesses

An admiralty case turns on questions a judge cannot be expected to have lived: whether a manoeuvre was good seamanship, whether a machinery failure was the result of a defect or of misoperation, whether a salvage service was reasonably rendered, whether cargo damage was caused by a failure of care or by inherent vice, what a vessel is worth. An assessor is a technical adviser to the court, appointed so that the court can engage with such matters properly.

9. Qualifications - what the Act says, and what it therefore contemplates

The honest and complete answer to the qualifications half of limb (b) has three parts, and giving all three is worth more than a confident invented list.

  1. What the Act requires. That the person appointed has qualifications and experience in admiralty and maritime matters. That is the statutory standard, and it is the only one section 13 states.
  2. Where the detail lives. In rules made by the Central Government under section 16, because section 13(1) fixes the qualifications, the nature of duties, the fees and ancillary matters 'in the manner as may be prescribed' and section 2(1)(j) defines 'prescribed' accordingly. The criteria are delegated legislation, not statute.
  3. What the section contemplates. Given a standard expressed as qualifications and experience in admiralty and maritime matters, the panel it contemplates is drawn from those whose professional standing rests on exactly that - master mariners and chief engineer officers with command or charge experience; naval architects and marine engineers; marine surveyors; specialists in salvage, towage, pilotage and port operations; those experienced in marine insurance, average adjusting and ship valuation; and lawyers and academics of standing in maritime law. Seniority, professional certification and demonstrable independence are the natural components of any such rule.

State the third part as what the section contemplates, not as what it enacts. That distinction - between the statutory standard, the delegated detail and the professional reality - is precisely the legal literacy a management-level examination is looking for, and it is also the only defensible position: the rules were not read, and inventing their contents would be worse than saying where they live.

In rem is the whole point

If one idea is carried away from this topic it should be that admiralty exists to solve a security problem, not a liability problem. A single-ship company may be unreachable, but the ship is present, identifiable and valuable - and only for as long as she is discharging. That is why arrest, rather than judgment, is the practical objective, why the maritime lien follows the ship through a change of ownership, registration and flag, and why section 8's clean title on a court sale matters: without it the sale realises nothing and the security is worthless.

Why the wages lien should matter to a candidate personally

Section 9 puts wages first among the five maritime liens - ahead of salvage, ahead of statutory dues, and, through section 10, ahead of the registered mortgage. And the wage lien alone runs for two years rather than one, from the date the sum falls due, with the master's, officers' and crew's repatriation costs and social insurance contributions included. An unpaid crew on an abandoned ship therefore holds the strongest claim in the queue. It is one of the few places where an examination answer describes something an officer may one day rely on.

Common mistakes

  • Answering only scope and never significance. Two words in the stem, two demands.
  • Inventing a statutory list of assessor qualifications. Section 13 states none - it delegates them.
  • Confusing an assessor with an expert witness. An assessor advises the court and gives no evidence; an expert is called by a party, testifies on oath and is cross-examined. Section 13(2) preserves both.
  • Saying an assessor decides the technical issue. The judge decides everything.
  • Confusing a maritime claim with a maritime lien. Section 4 lists many claims; section 9 recognises five liens, and only the liens follow the ship and outrank the mortgage.
  • Saying the jurisdiction extends to the EEZ as of right. Section 3 gives territorial waters; the extension is by Central Government notification under the proviso.
  • Forgetting the protections in section 11 - security for wrongful or unjustified arrest, and the forty-five-day auction on abandonment.
  • Naming only Bombay, Calcutta and Madras. That is the position the Act repealed.

Examiner traps

  • Asking who appoints assessors, expecting the Central Government by notification, not the High Court.
  • Asking whether an assessor can be cross-examined. No - they are not a witness.
  • Asking whether appointing an assessor stops a party calling its own expert. No - section 13(2).
  • Asking for the five maritime liens in order, and the limitation period, expecting one year, two for wages.
  • Asking what happens to a maritime lien when the ship is sold and re-flagged. It continues.
  • Asking which ranks higher, a maritime lien or a registered mortgage. The lien - section 10.
  • Asking which statutes the 2017 Act repealed, expecting the colonial Acts of 1840, 1861, 1890 and 1891 and the Letters Patent of 1865.
  • Asking whether a sister ship can be arrested for an ownership dispute. No - the proviso to section 5(2) excludes a section 4(1)(a) claim.

Understand this first

The EEDI is a design index - grammes of CO2 per tonne-mile that the ship's design will produce - and it is fixed by what the ship is, not by how she is run. That single fact separates it from the CII, which is operational and moves every year. The question is about verification, and the whole of it falls out of one observation: at the design stage every input to the index is already known except one. Capacity, rated power, specific fuel consumption from the engine's NOx Technical File, the carbon factor, the auxiliary load - all of these exist on paper. The one quantity that cannot be known until the ship exists and runs is her speed at 75 % of rated power, and at design that is a prediction from a tank test. So the file is verified twice: once on the prediction, which is what lets construction and trials proceed on an agreed basis, and again on the measurement, after which the file is revised and the index becomes final. Two other things are worth fixing before writing. The Administration verifies; it does not calculate and it does not approve - the shipbuilder calculates and the technical file is the evidence, and the verification may be done by the Administration or by any organization duly authorized by it. And the Annex is deliberately silent on the detail: regulation 22 prints no formula, no coefficient and no reference line, and it never even defines the EEDI technical file. The method lives in Organization guidelines. Knowing that the regulation refers its own method outward is itself worth a mark, and it is why an answer should explain the structure of the calculation rather than attempt the equation.

Model written answer

1. Where the duty comes from, and why verification is in two stages

The Energy Efficiency Design Index is a measure of the CO2 emitted per unit of transport work that a ship's design will produce - grammes of CO2 per tonne-mile. It is a design index: it is fixed by what the ship is, not by how she is operated.

The obligation sits in MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4, in the revised Annex adopted by resolution MEPC.328(76) and in force since 1 November 2023:

  • Regulation 22 - Attained EEDI. An attained EEDI shall be calculated for new ships, for new ships that have undergone a major conversion, and for new or existing ships whose conversion is so extensive that the Administration regards the ship as newly constructed - in each case where the ship falls into one of the ship-type categories the regulation lists. The attained EEDI shall be ship-specific, shall be accompanied by the EEDI technical file containing the information necessary for the calculation and showing the process of the calculation, and shall be verified on the basis of that file.
  • Regulation 24 - Required EEDI. The attained EEDI must not exceed the required EEDI, which is the applicable reference line value reduced by a reduction factor. Table 1 gives the reduction factor by ship type, size band and phase; Table 2 gives the parameters from which the reference line is computed.
  • Regulation 5.4 - Surveys. Ships to which chapter 4 applies are subject to an initial survey verifying the attained EEDI and the SEEMP, a survey after a major conversion of a new ship, and a discretionary initial survey where the conversion is so extensive that the ship is regarded as newly constructed.
  • Regulation 19 - Application. Chapter 4 admits ships of 400 gross tonnage and above, excludes ships trading solely domestically and unpropelled ships and platforms, and expressly disapplies regulations 22 to 25 to ships with non-conventional propulsion.

Why two stages? Because the index depends on the ship's speed at 75 % of rated engine power, and at the design stage that speed is a prediction from tank tests. It cannot be known until the ship exists and runs. So the file is verified once on the prediction - which is what permits construction and trials to proceed on an agreed basis - and again on the measurement, after which it becomes final. The whole two-stage architecture exists to resolve one unknown quantity.

⚠ The trap, and it is a precise one: the Administration does NOT calculate the attained EEDI, and it does NOT approve it. It VERIFIES it. The value is calculated by the shipbuilder and evidenced by the technical file; the verifier's function is to satisfy itself that the calculation is correct and that the file supports it. Writing that the Administration calculates or approves an attained EEDI misstates the whole allocation of duty.

A second point worth making because it surprises candidates: regulation 22 imposes no duty on any ship, owner or company at all. The duties it creates fall on the Administration. The obligation to have a compliant design bites through regulation 24 and through survey and certification, not through regulation 22.

3. The EEDI Technical File - ship, machinery and capacity data

The Annex does not define the EEDI technical file. Regulation 22.1 makes it the evidential basis of verification and requires that it contain the information necessary for the calculation and show the process of the calculation, but the contents themselves come from the Organization's guidelines. What the file must in practice carry is this:

  • Ship particulars - name, IMO number, flag, port of registry, classification society and class notations; ship type and the regulation 2.2 category into which she falls; shipyard, hull number, and the contract, keel-laying and delivery dates that determine whether she is a 'new ship' and which EEDI phase applies to her.
  • Capacity - the deadweight at the summer load draught for most types, or the gross tonnage for passenger and ro-ro passenger ships, with the basis on which it is derived, together with the lightweight, displacement and the summer load line draught.
  • Main engine data - maker, type and number of engines; the rated power (MCR) and rated speed; and the specific fuel consumption at the EEDI power point, taken from the engine's NOx Technical File test report so that it is a certified figure rather than a claimed one. Where a shaft limitation or overridable power limitation is fitted, its arrangement and value.
  • Auxiliary engine data - maker, type, rated power and specific fuel consumption, again from the NOx Technical File.
  • The electrical power table and the derivation of the auxiliary power used in the calculation - the sea-going electrical load, and for a ship with a significant non-propulsion load such as a reefer or a tanker with cargo pumps, the basis on which it is established.
  • Fuel data - the fuel type or types and the corresponding carbon conversion factor; and for a dual-fuel ship, the treatment of each fuel and of pilot fuel.
  • Shaft generator or shaft motor arrangements, where fitted, and how they enter the calculation.

4. The EEDI Technical File - speed, correction factors and the calculation

  • The reference speed. The ship speed at 75 % of the rated installed power, in the maximum design load condition, in deep water and calm weather with no wind and no waves - together with the evidence for it: the tank test report, the model-ship correlation, the power-speed curve, and the identity and quality-system status of the towing tank. At the design stage this speed is an estimate, and saying so is part of the file.
  • The correction factors and their justification. Each factor claimed must be supported, not merely stated - the factors for ship-specific design elements, for ice class and other technical or regulatory capacity limitation, for cubic capacity where volume rather than weight limits the ship, for general cargo ships fitted with cranes, for shaft motors, and the weather factor representing speed reduction in representative sea conditions where it is claimed.
  • Innovative energy efficiency technology. Where waste heat recovery, wind assistance, solar, air lubrication or a similar technology is credited, the file must describe it and evidence the power saved or generated - separated into technologies that reduce the propulsion power required and those that generate electrical power - with the test or measurement basis for the claim. This is the most closely examined part of any file, because it is where an unsupported claim would directly reduce the attained value.
  • The calculation itself. Every input value, each with its source; the process of the calculation set out so that it can be followed and reproduced - which regulation 22.1 requires in terms; and the resulting attained EEDI.
  • The required EEDI for the ship, computed under regulation 24 from her type, size band and phase, and the comparison showing that the attained value does not exceed it.
  • The description of the sea trial - the intended trial condition, the measurement arrangements and the procedure by which the measured speed will be corrected to the reference condition.

Note what is deliberately not reproduced here. Regulation 22.2 refers the entire method of calculation out to Organization guidelines, and regulation 22 prints no formula, no coefficient, no reference-line value and no reduction factor of its own. The structure of the calculation and the inputs it needs can be stated with confidence; the equation belongs to the guidelines.

5. Preliminary verification at the design stage - what it is and when

  • When. At the design stage, before construction is complete and before delivery - as the first part of the initial survey under regulation 5.4.
  • What is submitted. The shipowner or shipbuilder submits the EEDI technical file with its supporting documentation - the tank test report and power curve, the NOx Technical File extracts for main and auxiliary engines, the capacity and load-line documentation, the electrical load table, and the evidence for any correction factor or innovative technology claimed.
  • To whom. To the verifier - the Administration or an organization duly authorized by it, in practice the recognised organization acting for the flag State.
  • The object. To establish, before the ship exists in her final form, that the design as specified will comply - so that a non-compliance is found on paper, where it can be corrected by design change, rather than at the trial, where it cannot.

6. What the verifier interrogates - and why it is only preliminary

  • Applicability and phase. Is the ship within chapter 4 at all - 400 gross tonnage and above, not solely domestic, not unpropelled, and not of non-conventional propulsion, which regulation 19.3 takes outside regulations 22 to 25 entirely? Which regulation 2.2 category is she, and which phase do her contract, keel and delivery dates place her in?
  • The required EEDI. Recomputed independently under regulation 24 - the correct reference line from Table 2 for her type and capacity, and the correct reduction factor from Table 1 for her type, size band and phase.
  • The calculation method. Checked against the current calculation guidelines, including that the correct edition has been used.
  • Each input value. Capacity against the load line and stability information; rated power against the engine documentation and any power limitation arrangement; specific fuel consumption against the NOx Technical File, not against a maker's brochure; the carbon factor against the fuel; the auxiliary power against a defensible electrical load table.
  • The speed prediction. The tank test and the power curve from which the estimated reference speed is derived - the test conditions, the model scale, the extrapolation method, and whether the tank operates under a recognised quality system. The verifier may witness the model test or call for additional data, and this is the item most often queried, because the index is highly sensitive to speed.
  • Correction factors and innovative technology. Each claim examined against its justification; an unsupported factor is removed and the attained value recalculated.
  • The trial plan. That the proposed sea-trial condition, measurement arrangements and correction procedure will actually produce a usable reference speed.
  • Outcome. The verifier confirms the preliminary verification of the attained EEDI on the technical file. It is preliminary for one reason only: the reference speed is still a prediction. Every other input is already final.

7. The sea trial - conduct, measurement and correction

  • Attendance. The trial is conducted by the shipbuilder in the presence of the verifier, who examines the conduct of the trial as well as its result.
  • Condition. The ship should be at the EEDI reference load condition, or at a condition close enough to it that the result can be corrected back; the actual draught, trim and displacement are measured and recorded, and the hull and propeller condition confirmed as clean.
  • Environment recorded - wind speed and direction, sea state and swell, water depth, current, and water temperature and density. Trials are run in deep water where possible, because a shallow-water correction is large and uncertain.
  • Measurement. Speed and shaft power are measured over a set of runs at several power settings spanning the EEDI power point. Runs are made as double runs on reciprocal courses at each setting, so that the effect of current cancels in the mean. Shaft power is taken from a calibrated torsion meter, and speed over ground from satellite positioning, with the run length and steady-state period long enough to be meaningful.
  • Correction to the reference condition. The measured results are corrected by the recognised international speed-power trial analysis procedure for wind, waves, current, shallow water, water temperature and density, and any deviation of displacement and trim from the reference condition. The output is a corrected power-speed curve, from which the speed at 75 % of rated power is read.
  • Series ships. Where a ship is one of a series and is not separately trialled, the guidelines permit the lead ship's trial result to be used, provided the ships are demonstrably identical in the relevant respects and the reliance is justified in the file.

8. Final verification - revision, comparison and certification

  1. Compare measured with predicted. The corrected reference speed from the trial is compared with the estimated speed used in the technical file.
  2. Revise the technical file. ⚠ If they differ - and they generally do - the technical file must be revised. The reference speed is replaced with the measured, corrected value and the attained EEDI is recalculated. Every other input remains as verified at the design stage, so the revision is confined to the one quantity the trial existed to establish.
  3. Verify the trial itself. The verifier examines the trial procedure, the measured data and the corrections applied - not merely the resulting number. A correctly reported result from a badly conducted trial is not verifiable.
  4. Confirm compliance. The verifier confirms that the final attained EEDI does not exceed the required EEDI computed under regulation 24. If it does exceed it, the ship does not comply: the remedy is a design or operational change - a power limitation, a modification, or credited technology - and re-verification. There is no tolerance to be argued.
  5. Certification. With the initial survey under regulation 5.4 complete, the International Energy Efficiency Certificate is issued under regulation 6, and the verified EEDI technical file is retained on board with it as the evidence supporting the certificate.
  6. Reporting. For a ship also subject to regulation 24, the Administration or its authorized organization reports the required and attained EEDI values and relevant information to the Organization, by electronic communication, within seven months of completion of the regulation 5.4 survey.

The design is then fixed. The attained EEDI does not change with operation - a fouled hull or a slow-steaming voyage does not alter it. What changes with operation is the carbon intensity indicator, and confusing the two is the commonest error in this whole subject.

Say that in one sentence and limbs (b) and (c) organise themselves: preliminary verification checks everything and accepts one estimate; final verification replaces that estimate with a measurement and recalculates.

Verify, not calculate, and not approve

The regulation allocates duties precisely and an examiner can test it in one question. The shipbuilder calculates; the verifier verifies. Regulation 22.1 requires the attained EEDI to be verified based on the EEDI technical file, either by the Administration or by any organization duly authorized by it - and the Administration neither calculates the value nor approves it. The one function that is not delegable is the Administration's opinion that a converted ship is to be regarded as newly constructed, which is what brings a converted ship into the calculation cohort at all.

Why the sea trial is a measurement problem, not a formality

The index is highly sensitive to speed, so the credibility of the whole scheme rests on the trial being properly conducted and properly corrected. That is why the answer names the mechanics: double runs on reciprocal courses so that current cancels in the mean; deep water, because shallow-water correction is large and uncertain; measured draught, trim and displacement so the result can be corrected back to the reference condition; a calibrated torsion meter for shaft power; and correction for wind, waves, current, depth, water density and temperature by the recognised analysis procedure. An engineer who can explain why reciprocal runs are used has demonstrated more than one who lists the corrections.

EEDI, EEXI and CII - keep them apart

EEDI is design and applies to new ships and to ships so converted that they count as new. EEXI is the same style of index applied once to existing ships. CII is operational and is recalculated every year from actual fuel consumed and distance sailed. Only the CII changes with how the ship is run - a fouled hull, a slower speed or a longer ballast leg move the CII and do not touch the attained EEDI at all. Mixing them is the commonest error in the topic and is easy for an examiner to expose in one follow-up.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Administration calculates or approves the attained EEDI. It verifies it, on the basis of the technical file.
  • Treating the EEDI as something that changes with operation. That is the CII.
  • Omitting the NOx Technical File as the source of specific fuel consumption, and quoting a maker's brochure figure instead.
  • Describing preliminary verification as a formality. It is where every input except speed is fixed.
  • Forgetting that the technical file must be revised after the trial and the attained EEDI recalculated.
  • Omitting the correction of trial results to the reference condition, or the reason for reciprocal double runs.
  • Failing to say that the attained EEDI must be compared against the required EEDI under regulation 24, and that exceeding it is non-compliance with no tolerance.
  • Forgetting the International Energy Efficiency Certificate at the end, and that the verified file is retained on board.

Examiner traps

  • Asking who verifies the attained EEDI and whether the Administration may delegate it. It may - to any organization duly authorized by it - except the opinion that a converted ship is newly constructed.
  • Asking why the trial uses reciprocal runs, expecting the cancellation of current.
  • Asking what happens if the measured speed is lower than predicted. The file is revised, the attained EEDI recalculated, and if it now exceeds the required value the ship does not comply.
  • Asking where the specific fuel consumption comes from.
  • Asking what document the ship keeps on board afterwards, expecting the IEEC and the verified technical file.
  • Asking whether EEDI applies to an existing ship. Only through EEXI - or through a conversion so extensive that the Administration regards her as newly constructed.
  • Asking whether a ship with non-conventional propulsion must have an attained EEDI. Regulation 19.3 disapplies regulations 22 to 25 to her.
  • Asking what a weather factor or an innovative technology credit must be supported by.

Understand this first

Everything distinctive about a P&I club follows from one fact: the members are the insurers. A club is a mutual, non-profit association of shipowners, charterers and operators - there are no shareholders, the board is drawn from the membership, and the cover is provided at cost. That is why the contribution is a call and not a premium: a member is not buying a product at a fixed price, it is sharing the year's cost with the other members, and if the year turns out worse than estimated it pays the difference through a supplementary call. It also explains why a club can afford an omnibus rule, letting the board meet a claim outside the strict wording but within the spirit of mutuality - a commercial insurer would be giving away its shareholders' money. What the club insures is liabilities, not property: the hull and machinery policy covers the ship, and the club covers what the ship does to other people - the gap nineteenth-century owners created the clubs to fill. And 'unlimited cover' is a credible promise only because of a structure worth naming in four tiers: the club's own retention, then the Pool shared among the International Group clubs, then the Group's collective reinsurance bought as one buyer, and finally the overspill call on every Group member. For limb B, one sentence places any risk correctly: protection is liabilities to people and to property other than cargo; indemnity is cargo and the contract of carriage. The two words are historical - modern rulebooks list numbered classes instead - but the division is real, and saying both things is the complete answer.

1. What a P&I club is - and the gap it exists to fill

A Protection and Indemnity club is a mutual, non-profit-making association of shipowners, charterers and operators, formed to insure the third-party liabilities that arise from owning and operating ships. It is insurance, but it is not insurance as ordinarily understood - and the differences are the first marks in limb A.

The gap it fills. A ship's hull and machinery policy insures the ship herself. It does not insure what the ship does to other people - and historically it did not even cover the whole of that in collision, because the market's collision liability clause left a proportion of the liability with the owner, and covered nothing at all for cargo, crew, pollution or wreck. Nineteenth-century shipowners created the clubs to carry the liabilities the commercial market would not. That origin still explains the shape of the cover: a club insures liabilities, not property.

2. How a club is constituted, and what else it gives a member

  • The rules are the contract. A member's cover is defined by the club rules, and the Certificate of Entry evidences the entry of the ship, the classes of cover and any special terms. There is no policy in the market sense.
  • The board is the membership. Directors are drawn from member shipowners, and it is the board - not a claims department answering to shareholders - that decides discretionary matters.
  • The omnibus rule. Most clubs give the board a discretion to meet a claim that falls outside the strict wording but within the spirit of mutuality. That discretion is only possible because there are no shareholders whose money would be given away.
  • Far more than a payer of claims. A club supplies a worldwide network of correspondents; immediate casualty response; legal and technical advice; condition surveys of older or newly entered ships; loss-prevention work; and - critically for an operating ship - security: a club letter of undertaking given to a claimant will usually secure the release of an arrested ship without cash being put up or a bank guarantee bought.
  • Certification. A club issues the evidence of insurance - commonly called a blue card - on which a flag State issues the certificates required by the liability conventions: oil pollution under the Civil Liability Convention, bunker pollution under the Bunkers Convention 2001, wreck removal under the Nairobi Convention 2007, passenger liability under the Athens regime, and the financial security for abandonment and for death and long-term disability introduced by the 2014 amendments to the Maritime Labour Convention. Without club cover a ship cannot hold those certificates, and without those certificates she cannot trade.
  • A note on Indian law. In an Indian examination the governing marine insurance statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 - not the United Kingdom Act of 1906. And under the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, unpaid insurance premium including mutual insurance calls is expressly a maritime claim, so a club is itself a potential arresting party.

3. The International Group, the Pool and the collective reinsurance

No single club could pay a very large claim from its own resources, and a member needs to know that it could. The answer is a three-tier structure, and understanding it is what makes 'unlimited cover' a credible statement rather than a slogan:

  1. The club's own retention. Each club pays claims up to a retained amount from its own funds.
  2. The Pool. Above that retention, the clubs in the International Group of P&I Clubs share the claim among themselves under the Pooling Agreement, each contributing according to an agreed formula. A single member's disaster is therefore borne by the world fleet.
  3. The collective reinsurance. Above the Pool, the Group buys excess-of-loss reinsurance in the commercial market as one buyer, which secures far greater capacity and a better price than any club could obtain alone.

And above even that sits the overspill call - described in section 4. The Group's clubs between them cover the great majority of the world's ocean-going tonnage, which is why the arrangement works at all.

4. How the funds are collected - the call system

A club does not charge premiums. It makes calls. The distinction is the whole of mutuality: the members do not buy a product at a fixed price, they share the year's cost between them. The forms of call are these.

  • The advance call - sometimes the mutual premium. At the start of the policy year, the club estimates its total outgoings for the year - claims, its share of pooled claims, the reinsurance premium and management expenses - and calls that amount from the members in proportion to their assessed share. It is an estimate, and it is collected before the claims are known. Many clubs split the estimated total call into an advance instalment and a deferred instalment, so a member has budget certainty for most of the year.
  • The supplementary or additional call - and this is the defining feature of a mutual. If the year's claims, reinsurance and expenses exceed the advance calls collected together with investment income, the club calls the difference from the members. A member's cost for a policy year is therefore not finally known until the year is closed. Conversely, a well-run year with good investment returns may see a return of call or a discount at renewal.
  • The release call. A member leaving the club, or transferring a ship out, may pay a release call to buy itself out of liability for future supplementary calls on the open policy years during which it was a member. Without it the departing member remains exposed to years that have not yet closed.
  • The overspill call - the top of the whole structure. If a claim exceeds the limit of the Group's collective reinsurance, the excess is met by an overspill call levied on the members of all the Group clubs, assessed by reference to entered tonnage. It is the ultimate expression of mutuality, and it has in practice never had to be levied.
  • Other elements of a member's cost. The reinsurance element of the call is commonly charged as a separate rate per gross ton by ship type - tankers, dry cargo and passenger ships carrying different rates because they present different pollution and personal-injury exposures. Each claim is also subject to a deductible borne by the member, which keeps small claims out of the mutual system.
  • Fixed premium entries. Clubs also write some business - smaller craft, and much charterers' cover - on a fixed premium basis with no supplementary calls. The trade-off is that such cover is normally subject to a stated limit rather than being unlimited, so it is not mutual cover in the full sense.
  • Policy year accounting. Contributions and claims are accounted by policy year, traditionally running from noon on 20 February. A year stays open while claims develop and is closed when the club is satisfied they are settled - which is why supplementary calls can arrive long after the year in question, and why free reserves accumulated over past years are used to smooth them.

5. What determines a member's contribution, and on what conditions cover rests

  • Rating factors. The entered tonnage; the ship type and age; the trade and geographical range - a United States trade attracts a very different pollution exposure from a coastal one; the crew nationality and terms of employment, which drive the personal-injury exposure; the member's claims record, which is the largest single influence at renewal; the management standard and its inspection and vetting record; and the classes of cover and deductibles chosen.
  • Conditions on which cover rests. The ship must be in class with an approved society and hold her statutory certificates; the member must comply with flag State requirements; and the club must be notified of casualties and claims promptly and its directions followed.
  • The 'pay to be paid' rule. Classically, a club indemnifies - the member must first have discharged the liability before the club reimburses it. That is the historical rule and it is why the club's obligation is an indemnity rather than a promise to a third party; it is displaced in the cases where a convention gives a claimant a direct right of action against the insurer, which the pollution and wreck conventions do.
  • Cover can be prejudiced by conduct - unapproved contractual terms accepted by the member, unlawful trade, want of due diligence, or a failure to disclose material facts.

6. 'Protection' - liabilities to persons and to property other than cargo

⚠ State the division before listing it. The two words are historical, and they still divide the cover accurately in principle even though modern club rules are set out as numbered classes rather than under two headings: 'protection' is the liabilities arising from the ship as a ship - to people, and to property other than the cargo she carries. 'Indemnity' is the liabilities arising from the cargo and the contract of carriage.

Protection risks - people:

  • Crew - illness, injury and death; hospital, medical and funeral expenses; repatriation and substitute expenses; loss of personal effects; and the financial security for abandonment and for contractual claims for death or long-term disability required by the Maritime Labour Convention.
  • Passengers - death, injury, delay and loss of or damage to their effects, under the Athens regime.
  • Others on board or affected - stevedores, pilots, surveyors, supernumeraries and visitors; and persons injured ashore by the ship's operations.
  • Stowaways, refugees and persons rescued at sea - maintenance, repatriation and the associated diversion expenses.
  • Life salvage - awards payable for the saving of life.

Protection risks - property other than cargo, and the ship's effect on the world:

  • Collision liability - the proportion of collision liability to the other ship that is not covered by the hull policy, together with any excess over the hull value, and the cargo and other consequential claims arising out of the collision.
  • Damage to fixed and floating objects - berths, piers, jetties, dolphins, buoys, locks, bridges, submarine cables and pipelines, and terminal equipment. Commonly the largest routine liability an operating ship generates.
  • Pollution - liability for oil and other pollutants, the cost of clean-up and preventive measures, and the associated fines and compensation for consequential loss to fisheries and tourism. This is the single largest exposure the system carries.
  • Wreck removal - the cost of raising, removing, destroying, lighting or marking the wreck of the entered ship where that is compulsory by law, and liability for failing to do so.
  • Towage liabilities arising under approved towage contracts, and liabilities under other approved contracts and indemnities.
  • Quarantine and disinfection expenses arising from an outbreak of infectious disease on board.
  • Fines - for pollution, for customs and immigration infringements including in respect of the crew, for breach of safety or immigration regulations, and for short or over-delivery of cargo. Fines are frequently covered at the board's discretion rather than as of right, and never where the conduct was deliberate.
  • Enquiry, legal and claims-handling costs, and the cost of sue and labour measures taken to avoid or minimise a liability the club would otherwise have carried.

7. 'Indemnity' - cargo and the contract of carriage

  • Loss of, shortage of, damage to, or delay to cargo carried on board the entered ship - the classic indemnity risk, and the reason the second half of the name exists.
  • Liability arising under the contract of carriage - the bill of lading, waybill or charterparty - where the terms are on a basis the club approves. A member who signs up to liabilities more onerous than the standard regime may find that part of the exposure outside cover.
  • The ship's proportion of general average that is not recoverable from cargo because of a breach of the contract of carriage - unseaworthiness being the usual reason.
  • Cargo's proportion of general average or salvage that the member has to bear.
  • Through-transport liabilities where the ship is part of a combined-transport operation.
  • Consequences of misdelivery - cargo discharged at the wrong port, over-carried, or delivered without production of the bill of lading, again subject to the club's rules and often to its prior agreement.
  • The cost of disposing of damaged or unclaimed cargo, and liability for damage to containers and other equipment not owned by the member.

8. The limits, and what is not covered at all

Cover is unlimited for most risks - but not all, and the exceptions matter. The two principal capped classes are oil pollution and passenger and seamen claims, each subject to a limit per event fixed by the International Group and revised from time to time; the passenger limit sits as a sub-limit within the combined passenger-and-seamen figure. The limits are commercial figures, not statutory ones, so a candidate should give them as such and not as though they were treaty limits.

What a club does not cover:

  • Damage to the entered ship herself, and her salvage - that is the hull and machinery policy.
  • Loss of hire or loss of earnings - separate cover, bought if the member wants it.
  • War risks - separately insured, though clubs write supplementary war-risk P&I cover.
  • Cargo owned by the member itself.
  • Crew wages as such - these are a contractual debt, not a liability arising from an incident, although abandonment financial security responds where the Maritime Labour Convention requires it.
  • Liabilities from deliberate or reckless conduct, unlawful trades, or a want of due diligence.
  • Liabilities voluntarily assumed under unapproved contracts.

One negative worth stating, because it is examinable: the HNS Convention 2010 is not in force, so there is no HNS certification regime for a club to support - unlike the oil pollution, bunkers, wreck removal and passenger conventions, which are all in force and all certificated.

Mutuality is the whole of limb A

Everything distinctive about a club follows from one fact: the members are the insurers. There are no shareholders, so there is no profit motive, so the cover can be sold at cost - and so the club can have an omnibus rule letting the board pay a claim that falls outside the wording but within the spirit of mutuality, which no commercial insurer could offer. It is also why the contribution is a call and not a premium: the members are not buying a product at a price, they are sharing a year's cost, and if the cost turns out higher they pay the difference. Say that once and the four kinds of call become obvious rather than a list to memorise.

Why 'unlimited cover' is credible

A single club could not pay a very large claim, so the credibility rests on four tiers: the club's own retention; then the Pool, in which the International Group clubs share the claim under the Pooling Agreement; then the collective excess-of-loss reinsurance the Group buys in the market as one buyer, which secures capacity no single club could; and finally the overspill call on the members of every Group club, assessed on entered tonnage. A candidate who can name all four has explained why a mutual can promise what a commercial insurer cannot.

Why this matters to a ship, not just to an office

Three practical consequences are worth a line each. Without club cover a ship cannot hold her convention certificates - CLC, Bunkers, Nairobi, Athens and the MLC financial security all rest on the club's evidence of insurance - and without them she cannot trade. A club letter of undertaking is what gets an arrested ship released without cash or a bank guarantee. And in India, unpaid mutual insurance calls are themselves a maritime claim under the Admiralty Act, 2017 - so the club that secures your release for someone else's claim can arrest you for its own.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the contribution a premium. It is a call, and the difference is the whole of mutuality.
  • Saying cover is always unlimited. Oil pollution and passenger and seamen claims are capped.
  • Listing risks in limb B without dividing them into protection and indemnity. That is the question.
  • Putting cargo under protection, or collision under indemnity.
  • Saying a club covers damage to the entered ship. That is hull and machinery.
  • Omitting the Pool and the collective reinsurance, and so leaving 'unlimited cover' unexplained.
  • Forgetting the release call and the overspill call - the two that show real understanding of the call system.
  • Citing the UK Marine Insurance Act 1906 in an Indian examination. The Indian statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963.

Examiner traps

  • Asking why it is called a call rather than a premium.
  • Asking what happens if a club's claims for a year exceed the advance calls collected.
  • Asking what a member pays when it leaves the club, expecting the release call.
  • Asking what happens to a claim that exceeds the reinsurance, expecting the overspill call on all Group members by entered tonnage.
  • Asking which side of the division fines fall on - protection, and usually at the board's discretion.
  • Asking whether the club pays before or after the member does, expecting 'pay to be paid' and the convention exceptions giving a direct right of action against the insurer.
  • Asking what document gets an arrested ship released - a club letter of undertaking.

Understand this first

The stem hands you the reason these devices exist, so use it: CO2 is a fixed multiple of fuel burnt, so a percentage of propulsive efficiency is a percentage of emissions - and with the required EEDI tightening by phase, and EEXI and CII applying the same pressure to ships already trading, a device that was once an economy option is now often what lets the design comply. Underneath, one framework organises all four limbs. Quasi-propulsive efficiency is ηD = ηO × ηH × ηR, and a propeller behind a hull loses energy in four ways: axially in the slipstream it accelerates, rotationally in the swirl it leaves behind, in blade friction, and in the tip and hub vortices. Each limb of the question attacks one of them from a different position relative to the disc: (a) straightens the flow going in, raising hull efficiency; (b) twists it the wrong way going in, so the propeller's own swirl is partly cancelled before it is created; (c) untwists what comes out, recovering the swirl and the hub vortex; and (d) improves the disc itself, where the largest single gain of all is simply a bigger propeller turning more slowly. Two management-level points separate a good answer from a list. The gains are not additive - a pre-swirl stator and boss cap fins are chasing the same rotational energy - so a package is designed as one system for one hull. And a saving that has not been measured by a corrected speed trial, and evidenced in the EEDI technical file if it is to be credited, is a marketing figure rather than an engineering one.

Model written answer

1. Why these devices exist, and where the losses actually are

The stem supplies its own driver and it is worth taking seriously. Emission control from ships is now a design constraint with a number attached: MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4 requires a new ship's attained EEDI not to exceed a required EEDI that is the reference line reduced by a factor which tightens by phase; the EEXI applies a comparable index once to existing ships, and the carbon intensity indicator rates a ship on how she is actually operated. Since CO2 is a fixed multiple of fuel burnt, every percentage point of propulsive efficiency is a percentage point of emissions - so a device that was once a fuel-economy option is now, for many designs, what allows the ship to comply at all.

Where the recoverable losses sit. A propeller behind a hull loses energy in four identifiable ways, and each device family attacks one of them:

  • Axial loss - energy left in the accelerated slipstream. Reduced by a larger, more slowly turning propeller.
  • Rotational loss - the propeller leaves the water spinning, and that swirl is wasted work. Attacked before the disc by pre-swirl and after it by post-swirl devices.
  • Frictional and sectional loss - blade drag. Attacked by blade section and area optimisation.
  • Tip-vortex and hub-vortex loss - concentrated vortices shed from the blade tips and the boss. Attacked by tip-loaded blade designs and by boss cap fins.

2. The efficiency framework that classifies every device here

Quasi-propulsive efficiency is conventionally written as the product of three terms, and it is the cleanest way to organise the whole answer:

ηD = ηO × ηH × ηR

  • ηO - open water efficiency. How well the propeller converts delivered power into thrust in uniform flow. Improved by high-efficiency propeller design.
  • ηH - hull efficiency, the ratio (1 - t) / (1 - w), where t is thrust deduction and w is wake fraction. Improved by changing the flow the hull delivers - which is what a wake equalising device does.
  • ηR - relative rotative efficiency. The correction for the propeller working in a non-uniform, rotating inflow rather than in open water. Improved by pre-swirl, and its wasted output partly recovered by post-swirl.

The classification the question uses is therefore not arbitrary. Limb (a) works on the flow into the disc, limb (b) puts rotation into the inflow, limb (c) takes rotation out of the outflow, and limb (d) improves the disc itself. Saying that in two sentences before describing anything makes the whole answer read as a system.

3. Wake Equalizing Devices

The problem. Behind a full-form, single-screw hull the flow into the propeller is strongly non-uniform. There is a pronounced wake peak at the top of the disc where the boundary layer is thickest and the flow slowest, and often separation on the afterbody. The blade therefore sees a violently varying angle of attack and loading once per revolution, which costs efficiency and produces cavitation, pressure pulses, hull vibration and erosion.

The principle. Fit an appendage ahead of the propeller that accelerates and straightens the slow water in the upper disc and suppresses separation, so the inflow is more uniform. Doing so raises hull efficiency by improving the wake fraction, improves the propeller's working environment, and reduces the excitation the hull receives.

Forms:

  • The wake equalising duct - the classic Schneekluth arrangement: a pair of half-ring nozzles mounted on the afterbody either side of the stern post, ahead of the disc, shaped to accelerate flow into the upper quadrants.
  • Flow-guiding fins and stern spoilers - fins fitted on the afterbody, angled to redirect flow and suppress the bilge vortex; the Grothues arrangement is the best known.
  • Stern tunnels and afterbody form changes - a hull-form rather than an appendage solution, available at design stage.
  • Combined duct-and-fin devices - where a wake-equalising duct carries internal pre-swirl fins, the device belongs to both limb (a) and limb (b). The best-known integrated ducts are of this kind, and saying so is worth a mark because it shows the categories are not watertight.

Gain and limits. Indicatively in the region of a few per cent up to the high single figures, and the vibration and cavitation benefit is often worth as much as the fuel. It works best on full-form, high-block-coefficient, single-screw ships at moderate speed - tankers and bulk carriers. On a fine-form, fast ship with a clean wake there is little non-uniformity to correct, and the added appendage drag can exceed the gain.

4. Pre-swirl Devices

The problem. A propeller does not only push water aft; it also spins it. The rotational kinetic energy left in the slipstream is pure loss, and on a heavily loaded single-screw ship it is a significant fraction of the delivered power.

The principle. Give the inflow a rotation opposite to the propeller's own direction of rotation, upstream of the disc. The propeller then has to undo that counter-rotation before it can impart any of its own, so less net swirl leaves the disc and more of the delivered power ends up as axial thrust. The effect appears as an improvement in relative rotative efficiency and, through the modified inflow, in hull efficiency too.

Forms:

  • Pre-swirl stators - a set of fixed radial fins on the hull or stern boss immediately forward of the propeller, each set at an incidence chosen to generate the counter-rotation. They are characteristically asymmetric - more fins, or differently angled fins, on one side - because the wake behind a single-screw hull is itself asymmetric and the rotating propeller meets it differently on the up-going and down-going sides.
  • Pre-swirl ducts - a duct with internal stator fins, combining the wake-equalising effect of limb (a) with the pre-rotation of limb (b) in one appendage. This is the most widely retrofitted energy-saving device on full-form ships.
  • Asymmetric or twisted afterbodies - the same effect produced by hull form rather than by an appendage, available only at design stage.

Gain and limits. Indicatively a few per cent. The device is tuned to a design point - a particular draught, speed and propeller loading - so the benefit falls away in ballast or at part load, and a badly matched stator can cost efficiency. The fins add wetted surface and drag, they must be positioned to avoid exciting blade-rate vibration, and they are a fatigue-critical welded appendage in a region of unsteady loading.

5. Post-swirl Devices

The principle. Whatever swirl the propeller does impart, recover some of it downstream. Post-swirl devices sit in the slipstream and turn its rotation into useful axial thrust, or remove the concentrated hub vortex that carries much of the loss.

Forms:

  • Propeller boss cap fins - small fins fitted on the boss cap, rotating with the propeller, which break up the hub vortex. Indicatively in the low single figures, and among the cheapest of all retrofits. They also reduce hub-vortex cavitation and the associated rudder erosion, and they can usually be fitted in a routine docking.
  • Rudder bulb - a bulb on the rudder leading edge, aligned with and streamlining the flow off the propeller boss. It fills the low-pressure hub region, reduces the hub vortex and the drag associated with it, and is commonly combined with the boss cap fins and a twisted rudder as a matched package.
  • Rudder thrust fins - fins projecting from the rudder into the slipstream, set at an incidence that converts the residual swirl into a forward force. Because the swirl is in opposite senses above and below the shaft line, the fins on each side are angled oppositely.
  • Twisted and asymmetric rudders - the rudder's leading edge twisted to match the swirl angle of the slipstream above and below the shaft, so the rudder meets the flow at a lower angle of attack. This reduces rudder drag and cavitation erosion and improves manoeuvring - a genuine multiple benefit.
  • Free-rotating vane wheels - a larger-diameter freely turning wheel astern of the propeller, acting as a turbine on the inner slipstream and a propeller on the outer. Theoretically attractive, but structurally troublesome in service, and not widely adopted.

Gain and limits. Indicatively low single figures individually, and more when the boss cap fins, rudder bulb and twisted rudder are designed as one system. The devices sit in the most hostile flow on the ship - unsteady, cavitating and erosive - so material selection, cathodic protection and inspection matter, and a lost fin is a hazard to the rudder and the propeller behind which it travels.

6. High-efficiency Propellers

The other three limbs modify the flow around the propeller. This one improves the propeller itself, and it offers the largest gains available.

  • Larger diameter at lower revolutions. The single most effective measure of all. A larger disc accelerates a greater mass of water to a smaller velocity increase, which reduces the axial loss directly and raises open-water efficiency. It is enabled by long-stroke, low-speed engines and by an afterbody designed with the aperture to accept it - and it aligns naturally with slow steaming, since a de-rated engine at lower revolutions suits a larger propeller.
  • Tip-loaded and contracted-tip designs. Blades with end plates or winglets at the tip, curved towards the suction side, which obstruct the flow around the tip and weaken the tip vortex. Because the tip loss is reduced, the tip can carry more load, and the effective diameter is used more fully.
  • Curved-tip designs where the tip is swept smoothly out of the blade plane to the same end, without a discrete plate - a smoother pressure distribution and less cavitation risk.
  • Contra-rotating propellers. Two coaxial propellers turning in opposite directions, the after one recovering the rotational energy of the forward one. The largest gain of any measure here - it addresses the rotational loss almost completely - but it requires coaxial shafting with concentric shafts and a complex sterntube and bearing arrangement, which is why adoption is limited to specialised ships.
  • Ducted propellers. An accelerating (Kort) nozzle around a heavily loaded propeller raises efficiency substantially at high thrust loading and low speed - hence its universal use on tugs and trawlers - while a decelerating nozzle is used where cavitation and radiated noise matter more than efficiency.
  • Blade geometry optimisation - skew to spread the passage through the wake peak over time and so cut pressure pulses and vibration; refined section shape, pitch distribution and blade area ratio chosen for the actual wake field; and, increasingly, a propeller designed for the ship's real operating profile rather than for the contract speed.
  • Blade number and material - chosen against the hull's vibration response, with high-strength alloys allowing thinner, more efficient sections.

The binding constraints are always the same: cavitation margin, blade strength and fatigue, pressure pulses and hull vibration, aperture clearances, off-design behaviour, and class approval of the design.

7. Selection, verification and what happens to the gain in service

  • The devices are not independent, and they are not additive. A pre-swirl stator and a boss cap fin both act on the same rotational energy; fitting both does not add their quoted gains. A matched package must be designed and assessed as one system for the individual hull.
  • Suitability is hull-specific. The wake and swirl devices reward full-form, high-block, single-screw ships at moderate speed. On fine, fast hulls the added drag can exceed the recovery. The right answer to "which device?" is always "for which ship, at which draught and speed".
  • Verification. A claim is established by model tests in a towing tank and cavitation tunnel and by computational prediction, and demonstrated by a speed trial - run as double runs on reciprocal courses to cancel current and corrected to a reference condition for wind, waves, depth and displacement. A gain claimed and not measured is a marketing figure.
  • The regulatory route. Where the benefit is to be credited in the ship's attained EEDI, it must be evidenced in the EEDI technical file, which regulation 22.1 makes the basis on which the attained value is verified. An innovative-technology credit is examined closely for exactly that reason, and it is where an unsupported claim would directly and improperly reduce the attained value.
  • Retrofit economics. Most of these devices can be fitted in a scheduled docking, which is what makes them attractive as an EEXI or CII measure. The business case is fuel saved against installation and added maintenance, and it improves as fuel price and carbon cost rise.
  • In service the gain is not permanent. Fouling of the appendage and of the hull erodes it; the device is outside the design point in ballast or at reduced speed; and structural condition matters - cracking of welded fins, cavitation erosion and failure of cathodic protection are all live risks. These appendages belong on the underwater inspection and planned maintenance schedule, and it is entirely appropriate to treat them as identified equipment within the safety management system.
  • Keep the claim honest. A device improves the attained EEDI, which is a design index and does not change with operation; what improves with a clean hull and a well-chosen speed is the operational carbon intensity. Both matter, and confusing them is the standard error in this subject.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four printed limbs at 4 marks each means four visible, roughly equal regions and a firm discipline about length - it is easy to write three pages on propellers and leave limb (a) with one line. The two decisions that pay are: put one short framing section at the front giving the efficiency breakdown, because it classifies all four limbs and makes them read as a system rather than a list; and answer each limb in the same internal order - problem, principle, forms, gain and limits - so a marker can find each component instantly and nothing is forgotten under time pressure.

The framework that makes the answer a system

ηD = ηO × ηH × ηR is the whole classification. Limb (a) improves hull efficiency by fixing the flow the hull delivers; limbs (b) and (c) work on rotational loss, one before the disc and one after; limb (d) improves open water efficiency, the propeller itself. Say that in two sentences before describing any device and every subsequent paragraph has a place to sit. It also stops the commonest structural error, which is describing four devices without ever saying what loss each one is recovering.

Where the energy actually goes

Four losses, four families. Axial - water left moving aft, cured by a bigger, slower propeller. Rotational - water left spinning, attacked before the disc by pre-swirl and after it by post-swirl. Frictional and sectional - blade drag, attacked by section and area design. Tip and hub vortex - concentrated vortices, attacked by tip-loaded blades and boss cap fins. An answer that names the loss before naming the device is demonstrating understanding; one that lists trade names is not.

Why the gains are not additive - the point that separates a good answer

A pre-swirl stator and propeller boss cap fins are both trying to recover the same rotational energy: one prevents it being created, the other recovers what was. Fit both and you do not get the sum of the two quoted figures, because the first has already removed part of what the second was going to recover. The correct professional statement is that a package must be designed and assessed as one system for the individual hull - and that is also why the honest answer to "which device is best?" is always "for which ship, at which draught and speed?"

The regulatory link the stem hands you

The stem opens with emission control, so use it. CO2 is a fixed multiple of fuel burnt, so propulsive efficiency and emissions are the same quantity. The attained EEDI must not exceed a required EEDI that tightens by phase; EEXI applies the same idea once to existing ships; CII rates operation. Two further points earn marks: a device's benefit is credited only if it is evidenced in the EEDI technical file, which is what the attained value is verified against; and a device improves the design index, while a clean hull and a sensible speed improve the operational one. Do not stray beyond that into later IMO measures.

Common mistakes

  • Listing trade names without explaining the principle or naming the loss being recovered.
  • Giving three limbs properly and one in a sentence. Four limbs at 4 marks each.
  • Confusing pre-swirl and post-swirl. Pre-swirl puts rotation in ahead of the disc; post-swirl takes it out behind.
  • Treating quoted percentages as fixed properties of a device. They are indicative and depend on hull form, draught, speed and loading.
  • Adding the gains of several devices together.
  • Forgetting the penalties - appendage drag, off-design loss, vibration excitation, fatigue and cavitation erosion.
  • Claiming a device improves the CII directly, or that it changes the attained EEDI after the ship is built. The attained EEDI is fixed at verification.
  • Omitting larger diameter at lower revolutions from limb (d), which is the single most effective measure of all.

Examiner traps

  • Asking which way a pre-swirl stator turns the flow - opposite to the propeller's rotation.
  • Asking why pre-swirl stators are asymmetric, expecting the asymmetry of the wake behind a single-screw hull.
  • Asking what a wake equalising duct does besides save fuel - reduces cavitation, pressure pulses and hull vibration.
  • Asking why a wake equalising duct would be a poor choice on a fine-form fast ship.
  • Asking what propeller boss cap fins recover, expecting the hub vortex, and what secondary benefit they give - reduced rudder erosion.
  • Asking why a Kort nozzle suits a tug but not a container ship - high thrust loading at low speed.
  • Asking the drawback of contra-rotating propellers - the coaxial shafting, sterntube and bearing complexity.
  • Asking whether fitting two devices gives the sum of their savings.

Model written answer

1. The accurate starting point - critical habitat, but not a designated area

The Lakshadweep group and the Andaman and Nicobar Islands are without question critical habitat - coral atolls and fringing reefs, seagrass beds, mangrove, turtle nesting beaches and endemic species - and they carry substantial protection under Indian domestic law.

⚠ But neither group is a designated IMO Particularly Sensitive Sea Area. The designations made worldwide are few - fewer than two dozen - and none of them is in Indian waters. No Indian sea area is a MARPOL Special Area either, and none is an Emission Control Area.

That is why limb (a) is written in the conditional - how these islands could be protected - and getting it right is the discrimination the limb rewards. The answer is properly given as a programme: what already protects them under Indian law, what international measures India could seek, and in what order. An answer that assumes a PSSA already exists has misread the question.

2. The Indian statutory and administrative layer, as it stands at this examination

  • Ship-source pollution. The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the operative Indian merchant shipping statute, carrying the prevention and containment of pollution of the sea by oil and the response to it, with the Merchant Shipping Rules made under it as the working detail. It is the vehicle by which India gives effect to MARPOL, and it is administered by the Directorate General of Shipping.
  • Coastal and island zoning. The Island Coastal Regulation Zone (ICRZ) Notification, 2019, made under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, applies specifically to the Andaman and Nicobar and Lakshadweep groups and regulates development, construction and activity in the coastal zone of those islands. It is the instrument that most directly protects the shoreline and the near-shore habitat from land-based and development pressure.
  • Habitat protection. Marine national parks and wildlife sanctuaries declared under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972 - the Mahatma Gandhi Marine National Park in the Andamans being the best-known - give the reefs, the turtles and their species statutory protection independent of the shipping regime. This layer would survive whatever happened at IMO.
  • Jurisdictional reach. The Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and Other Maritime Zones Act, 1976 establishes the zones within which India may act, and it is what makes a measure in the exclusive economic zone legally available at all.
  • Response capability. The Indian Coast Guard, under the Coast Guard Act, 1978, is the central coordinating authority for marine pollution response in Indian waters, and the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan is the framework under which a spill in the islands would actually be fought - with the practical difficulty that these are remote archipelagos and response assets are days rather than hours away.
  • Port and reception facilities. Adequate reception facilities at the island ports, and port State control inspection of ships calling there, close the loop between a paper discharge requirement and the discharge that actually happens. A regime that forbids discharge and provides nowhere to land the waste does not work.

4. What a coherent programme would look like, in sequence

  1. Establish the science and the traffic picture. Habitat surveys, species and reef condition data, and a study of what shipping actually passes - types, tonnages, cargoes and density. No proposal survives without this, and it is also what identifies which reefs matter most.
  2. Apply the domestic controls already available - the ICRZ Notification, the Wild Life (Protection) Act declarations, and enforcement of the pollution provisions under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 and its Rules.
  3. Build the response and reception capability - reception facilities at the island ports, pre-positioned response equipment, and exercised contingency plans under the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan. This must come before or alongside a Special Area proposal, not after it.
  4. Seek the international measures - a PSSA supported by an Area To Be Avoided and mandatory reporting, or Special Area status under the relevant MARPOL Annex, with compulsory pilotage in any confined approach.
  5. Chart, promulgate and enforce. A measure that does not appear on the chart and in the sailing directions does not change what a master does.

5. What a Particularly Sensitive Sea Area is - and what it is not

A Particularly Sensitive Sea Area is an area that needs special protection through action by IMO because of its significance for recognised ecological, socio-economic or scientific reasons, and because it may be vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities. The governing instrument is the Revised Guidelines for the Identification and Designation of Particularly Sensitive Sea Areas, resolution A.982(24), as amended by resolution MEPC.267(68).

Two features define it, and the second is decisive.

  1. It is a designation by IMO, not a declaration by the coastal State. That is what distinguishes it from a national marine protected area, however well protected the latter may be.
  2. ⚠ A PSSA has no legal effect of its own. It is a flag placed on the chart identifying an area as needing care. What actually regulates shipping is the Associated Protective Measure. A PSSA without an APM changes nothing that a ship must do - and understanding that is the single most valuable thing a candidate can say about the concept.

6. The criteria for identification - three groups, and one is enough

The Guidelines set out the criteria in three groups, and an area must meet at least one of them - not all three.

The Indian islands would satisfy several of these without difficulty - critical habitat, fragility, diversity, productivity, breeding grounds, human dependency of indigenous populations, and research value. Meeting a criterion is not the hard part.

7. The vulnerability test - separate from the criteria, and where proposals actually fail

Meeting a criterion is necessary but not sufficient. The proposal must also demonstrate that the area is vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities, and that is assessed on two distinct sets of factors:

  • Vessel traffic characteristics - the operational factors (the kinds of activity conducted there), the types of vessel and their traffic characteristics such as density and routeing, and the harmful substances carried.
  • Natural factors - the hydrographical conditions such as depth, seabed and confined channels; the meteorological conditions such as prevailing wind, current, visibility and cyclone exposure; and the oceanographic conditions that would determine where a spill went and how fast.

This is where the real work of a proposal lies, and it is the reason a beautiful reef with no shipping near it does not become a PSSA. Conversely, for the Indian islands the argument is available and strong: they lie close to major east-west trade routes, laden tankers pass in quantity, and the combination of remoteness, cyclone exposure and confined reef approaches means a casualty would be difficult to reach and its consequences hard to contain.

8. The designation procedure, and the Associated Protective Measure

  1. Proposal. Only a Member Government may propose - a non-governmental body or a scientific institution cannot. The proposal goes to the Marine Environment Protection Committee and must describe the area precisely, show that it meets at least one criterion, demonstrate vulnerability to international shipping activities, and propose at least one Associated Protective Measure.
  2. Every APM must have an identified legal basis. It may be a measure already available under an existing IMO instrument - a routeing measure under SOLAS V/10, a ship reporting system under V/11, a discharge restriction or Special Area under MARPOL, or compulsory pilotage - or a measure not yet available, which must then be adopted by IMO in its own right, or a measure under UNCLOS article 211(6).
  3. Designation in principle. MEPC may grant designation in principle once satisfied on the criteria and the vulnerability.
  4. Adoption of the APM. The proposed measure is referred to the appropriate IMO body - routeing and reporting measures to the navigation sub-committee and then the Maritime Safety Committee; discharge measures to MEPC itself.
  5. Final designation. Once the APM has been adopted, MEPC grants final designation.
  6. Charting and promulgation. The area and its measures are charted and promulgated so that mariners can actually see and comply with them - without which the whole exercise is paper.

The designations are few and they are deliberate. The first was the Great Barrier Reef in 1990; later ones include the Wadden Sea, the Galapagos Archipelago, the Baltic Sea Area, the Canary Islands, the Strait of Bonifacio and the Tubbataha Reefs. None is in Indian waters - which is precisely why limb (a) of this question is written as a question about what could be done rather than what has been.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs at 8 marks each. Limb (a) is a trap disguised as a description: it invites the candidate to assume these islands are already a protected area under some international instrument, and the first mark is for knowing they are not. Answering it as a programme - what exists domestically, what could be sought internationally, and in what order - covers far more ground than a list of good intentions. Limb (b) has two demands, the meaning of the term and the criteria for identification and designation, and the criteria are best given as a three-group table because that is how the Guidelines are structured and it is fast to write.

The one thing to say about a PSSA

A PSSA has no legal effect of its own. It is a designation that identifies an area as needing care; what actually regulates shipping is the Associated Protective Measure attached to it - a routeing measure, a reporting system, a discharge restriction, compulsory pilotage. A PSSA without an APM changes nothing that a ship must do. Say that and the rest of limb (b) follows: it explains why every proposal must propose an APM, why each APM needs an identified legal basis under an existing instrument, and why designation happens in two stages with the APM adopted in between.

Criteria and vulnerability are two different tests

This distinction is worth a clear mark and is regularly missed. The criteria - ecological, socio-economic, scientific - describe why the area is valuable, and only one need be met. The vulnerability test asks something completely different: is it threatened by international shipping?, assessed on vessel traffic characteristics and on natural factors. A pristine, precious, remote reef with no shipping near it fails, and rightly - IMO's jurisdiction is over ships. For the Indian islands both tests are satisfiable, and being able to say why for each is what turns a description into an argument.

The date decides the Indian statute

This examination falls before 15 March 2026, so the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the operative Indian merchant shipping statute and the ship-source pollution limb must be answered on it, with the Merchant Shipping Rules made under it as the working detail. Note carefully, though, that the islands' habitat protection does not come from the shipping statute at all: it comes from the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 and the Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019 made under it, and from the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972. Attributing the reef and turtle protection to a merchant shipping statute is the mirror-image error, and it is just as wrong.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming the islands are a PSSA. They are not, and neither is any other Indian sea area - nor is any Indian water a MARPOL Special Area or an ECA.
  • Saying a PSSA by itself restricts shipping. Only the Associated Protective Measure does.
  • Requiring an area to meet all three criteria groups. One is enough.
  • Treating the vulnerability requirement as part of the criteria. It is a separate and additional test.
  • Proposing an APM with no legal basis. Every APM must rest on an identified instrument, or be adopted by IMO in its own right.
  • Saying anyone can propose a PSSA. Only a Member Government may.
  • Attributing the islands' habitat protection to the merchant shipping legislation rather than to the Environment (Protection) Act, the ICRZ Notification and the Wild Life (Protection) Act.
  • Proposing a Special Area without addressing reception facilities, on which its effect depends.

Examiner traps

  • Asking whether the Andamans are a PSSA.
  • Asking what a PSSA actually prohibits, expecting nothing by itself.
  • Asking how many criteria must be met - at least one.
  • Asking who may propose a PSSA - only a Member Government.
  • Asking for the first PSSA - the Great Barrier Reef, 1990.

None of its own. A PSSA is a designation identifying an area as needing special protection through IMO action; it is a flag placed on the chart. What actually regulates shipping is the Associated Protective Measure attached to it - a routeing measure under SOLAS V/10, a ship reporting system under V/11, a discharge restriction or Special Area under MARPOL, compulsory pilotage, or a measure under UNCLOS article 211(6). A PSSA without an APM changes nothing a ship must do.

It is the single most valuable thing that can be said about the concept and it explains the whole designation procedure.

At least one, not all three. The groups are ecological - uniqueness or rarity, critical habitat, dependency, representativeness, diversity, productivity, spawning or breeding grounds, naturalness, integrity, fragility and bio-geographic importance; social, cultural and economic - social or economic dependency, human dependency and cultural heritage; and scientific and educational - research value, baseline and monitoring studies, and educational value.

Requiring all three is a standard error, and the three-group structure is how the Guidelines are actually written.

The criteria ask why the area is valuable, and only one need be met. The vulnerability test asks a different question - whether the area is vulnerable to damage by international shipping activities - and it is a separate, additional requirement. It is assessed on vessel traffic characteristics, meaning operational factors, vessel types, traffic density and routeing and the harmful substances carried, and on natural factors, meaning hydrographical, meteorological and oceanographic conditions.

A precious reef with no shipping near it fails, because IMO's jurisdiction is over ships - and this is where proposals actually fail.

Only a Member Government may propose. It applies to MEPC, describing the area, showing at least one criterion is met, demonstrating vulnerability to international shipping, and proposing at least one Associated Protective Measure with an identified legal basis. MEPC may grant designation in principle. The APM is then referred to the appropriate body for adoption - routeing and reporting to the navigation sub-committee and the Maritime Safety Committee, discharge measures to MEPC. Once adopted, MEPC grants final designation, and the area is charted and promulgated.

The two-stage structure exists because the APM is what does the work, so designation cannot be final until it is adopted.

Ship-source pollution is governed by the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, with the Merchant Shipping Rules made under it as the working detail. The reefs, turtles and shoreline are protected by different legislation entirely - the Island Coastal Regulation Zone Notification, 2019 made under section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and marine national parks and sanctuaries declared under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972.

Two statutory layers doing different work, and attributing habitat protection to the shipping Act is a standard error.

An area to be avoided, a traffic separation scheme or a no-anchoring area, under SOLAS regulation V/10 on ships' routeing. A mandatory ship reporting system, under SOLAS regulation V/11. A discharge restriction or Special Area designation, under the relevant MARPOL Annex. Compulsory pilotage in defined approaches is a further option, as is a special mandatory measure in the exclusive economic zone under UNCLOS article 211(6).

Every APM must have an identified legal basis, so naming the instrument is as important as naming the measure.

Establish the science and the traffic picture through habitat surveys and a study of vessel types, cargoes and density; apply the domestic controls already available; and above all build the reception and response capability - adequate reception facilities at the island ports, pre-positioned response equipment and exercised contingency plans - because a MARPOL Special Area's effect depends on adequate reception facilities being provided. Only then does a proposal to IMO become credible.

A regime that forbids discharge and provides nowhere to land the waste does not work, and the sequencing is what a management-level answer shows.
