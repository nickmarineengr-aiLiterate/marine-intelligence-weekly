Model written answer

(a) The structure of IMO

1. What IMO is, and the organs that do the work

The International Maritime Organization is the specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and for the prevention of pollution from ships. It works through a two-tier governing structure - an Assembly and a Council - together with five main committees supported by seven sub-committees, all served by a permanent Secretariat headed by the Secretary-General. The governing organs decide policy, budget and membership questions; the technical output that reaches a ship comes almost entirely from the committees.

2. The Assembly - the governing body

  • The highest governing body. All Member States sit in it.
  • Meets in regular session once every two years.
  • Approves the work programme and votes the budget, and determines the financial arrangements.
  • Elects the Council.
  • Receives the reports of the Council and the committees, and adopts Assembly resolutions - recommendations, guidelines and codes.
  • Example of its output: resolution A.1207(34), the HSSC Survey Guidelines 2025, adopted by the 34th Assembly on 3 December 2025.

3. The Council - the executive organ

  • 40 Members, elected by the Assembly for a two-year term, in three categories: (a) ten States with the largest interest in providing international shipping services; (b) ten States with the largest interest in international seaborne trade; (c) twenty States elected to secure geographical representation of all major areas.
  • Between Assembly sessions it performs all the functions of the Assembly, except the function of making recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention, which the Assembly reserves to itself.
  • Supervises the work of the Organization and co-ordinates the activities of the organs.
  • Considers the draft work programme and budget and submits them to the Assembly with its comments.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General, subject to Assembly approval.

4. The five main committees and what each owns

CommitteeWhat it is responsible for
Maritime Safety Committee (MSC)The highest technical body. All matters of navigational safety, construction and equipment, manning from a safety standpoint, casualty investigation, salvage and rescue
Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC)Prevention and control of pollution from ships - MARPOL and its amendments, ballast water, anti-fouling, and greenhouse-gas measures
Legal CommitteeAny legal matter within the scope of the Organization - liability and compensation conventions in particular
Technical Cooperation CommitteeImplementation of the Organization's technical assistance work, particularly for developing States
Facilitation CommitteeElimination of unnecessary formalities and red tape in international shipping - the FAL Convention

All five are open to all Member States. MSC and MEPC are the two that adopt amendments to the mandatory instruments.

5. The seven sub-committees, and the role they perform

The sub-committees are HTW (Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping), III (Implementation of IMO Instruments), NCSR (Navigation, Communications and Search and Rescue), PPR (Pollution Prevention and Response), SDC (Ship Design and Construction), SSE (Ship Systems and Equipment) and CCC (Carriage of Cargoes and Containers).

Their role in supporting the main committees is preparatory and technical. A committee meets for a week or so and cannot itself draft detailed regulation, so it remits the work to the sub-committee with the relevant expertise. The sub-committee develops the draft text, guidelines or unified interpretation, resolves the technical argument, and reports back to its parent committee - MSC, MEPC, or both. A sub-committee adopts nothing. It prepares; the parent committee approves; the committee or the Assembly adopts. That division is what keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands.

(b) Conventions and the other instruments

6. What a convention is

A convention is a formal, legally binding treaty between States. It is negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference or by the Assembly, is then opened for signature, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, and enters into force only when the conditions written into the convention itself are satisfied - typically a stated number of Contracting States representing a stated share of world tonnage. It binds only those States that have become party to it. Example: the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974.

A State may have to be party to bothThe 1978 and 1997 Protocols to MARPOL; the 1988 Protocols to SOLAS and to the Load Lines Convention, which introduced the harmonized system of survey and certification

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks in limb (a) are in the function columns, not the names. Anyone can write 'MSC, MEPC, Legal, TC, FAL'; the mark comes from saying what each one is for. The examiner has also asked one narrow question inside limb (a) - what role the sub-committees play in supporting the main committees - and it is the part most candidates leave out. Step 5 answers it explicitly and states the negative as well as the positive: a sub-committee has no adoption power.

Limb (b) is worth the same eight marks and is far quicker to write, so write it properly. A three-column table with an example in each row banks the marks in about a third of the time limb (a) takes.

The one idea that makes limb (b) easy

Candidates try to memorise which instruments are 'mandatory'. That is the wrong model and it does not survive a follow-up question. The right model is: obligation comes from the treaty route, not from the label. A Code is a container of technical text. It becomes binding when a convention to which a State is party says it is binding. That is why the ISM Code binds - SOLAS chapter IX says so - and why an identically-titled 'Code' with no such hook does not.

Common mistakes

  • Naming only MSC and MEPC as the 'main committees'. There are five, and the question says 'various main committees'.
  • Saying the Assembly meets annually. It meets in regular session every two years.
  • Describing the sub-committees as if they adopt regulation. They do not.
  • Writing that a Code is 'always mandatory' or 'always recommendatory'. Neither is true.
  • Confusing a Protocol with an amendment. An ordinary amendment is adopted under the convention's own amendment article; a Protocol is a separate treaty.
  • Giving no examples in limb (b) when the question expressly asks for at least one of each - that is a straight loss of marks for something the candidate already knows.

Examiner traps

  • The Council's size. An amendment to enlarge the Council from 40 to 52 was adopted in December 2021 but requires acceptance by two thirds of Members. IMO's own convention page still records it as not yet in force (checked 21 September 2026). Write 40, and if the enlargement is mentioned, mention it as an amendment adopted but not in force.
  • The Secretary-General. Arsenio Dominguez has held office since 1 January 2024, for a four-year term. Naming his predecessor dates the script badly.
  • Being asked in the oral 'so is the ISM Code a convention?' The answer is no - it is a Code given mandatory force by SOLAS chapter IX.
  • Being asked which organ adopts MARPOL amendments. MEPC does, by resolution, under the MARPOL amendment article - not the Assembly.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which committee would deal with a new requirement for lifeboat release gear? MSC, remitted to SSE.
  • Who adopted the revised MARPOL Annex VI? MEPC, by resolution MEPC.328(76).
  • What is the difference between adoption and entry into force? Adoption is the decision to make the text; entry into force is when it starts to bind, on the conditions the instrument itself sets.
  • Name a Code that is not mandatory. Part B of the Intact Stability Code.
  • Who appoints the Secretary-General? The Council, subject to Assembly approval.

Model written answer

1. The background - the gap the Convention was written to close

By the late 1990s the international regime for oil pollution was substantial but narrow. The 1992 CLC, backed by the 1992 Fund, applied only to persistent oil carried as cargo in bulk by a tanker. It said nothing about the far more common case of a bulk carrier, container ship or car carrier spilling its own fuel. A large modern non-tanker may carry several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel oil - more than the entire cargo of a small tanker - and a claimant faced with such a spill had no dedicated convention, no guaranteed insurer to sue and no certainty of recovery. The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage was adopted on 23 March 2001 and entered into force on 21 November 2008 to close exactly that gap.

2. Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture

Its relevance is that it completes the map. Taken with CLC and the Fund for tanker cargo, and the HNS Convention for hazardous and noxious substances, the Bunkers Convention brings the fuel of essentially the whole world fleet inside a regime of strict liability, compulsory insurance and direct action against the insurer. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and exclusive economic zone of a State Party, and to preventive measures wherever taken. Its structural weakness, dealt with at step 7, is that it stops there: it creates no limitation fund of its own and no second tier of compensation.

3. (a) Pollution damage

Bunkers article 1.9 defines pollution damage as loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination resulting from the escape or discharge of bunker oil, provided that compensation for impairment of the environment other than loss of profit from that impairment is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken; and it includes the costs of preventive measures and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Against CLC 92: the definition is deliberately the same in substance. The drafters copied the CLC formula so that the two regimes would be interpreted consistently and no claimant would be better or worse off according to which convention applied. The difference lies not in what pollution damage means but in which oil, from which ship, triggers it.

4. (b) Bunker oil

Bunkers article 1.5: any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of such oil. Three features matter. It expressly catches lubricating oil, which candidates routinely forget. It catches residues, so sludge and tank washings are within it. And unlike CLC it is not limited to persistent oil - a marine gas oil spill is bunker oil for these purposes.

Against CLC 92: CLC applies to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil - crude, fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil - carried as cargo, and to such oil in the bunkers of a ship carrying that cargo. So the same drum of lubricating oil is CLC oil in a laden tanker's bunkers and Bunkers Convention oil in a bulk carrier's sump.

5. (c) Time limits for claims

Bunkers article 8. Rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within three years from the date the damage occurred. In no case may an action be brought more than six years from the date of the incident which caused the damage. Where the incident consists of a series of occurrences, the six-year period runs from the date of the first occurrence.

Against CLC 92: substantially identical - CLC article VIII sets the same three-year and six-year periods on the same terms. This is one of the places where the drafters deliberately mirrored CLC. Note the wording: the right is extinguished, not merely barred, so it cannot be revived.

6. (d) Exclusions

Three different things are commonly called exclusions and the answer should separate them:

  1. Excluded damage - the CLC carve-out. Article 4.1: the Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable under it. This is the hinge that stops the two regimes overlapping. A laden tanker's bunker spill is a CLC matter, not a Bunkers matter.
  2. Excluded ships. Article 4.2: it does not apply to warships, naval auxiliaries or other State-owned or operated ships used on government non-commercial service, unless a State Party decides otherwise.
  3. The shipowner's defences - strictly not exclusions at all but a different route to no liability. Under article 3.3 the shipowner is not liable if the damage resulted from an act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection or a natural phenomenon of an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible character; was wholly caused by a third party's act or omission done with intent to cause damage; or was wholly caused by the negligence or wrongful act of a Government or authority responsible for maintaining lights or other navigational aids. Contributory fault of the claimant may also reduce or exonerate.

Against CLC 92: the defences are the same three, in the same terms. The excluded-ship provision is likewise mirrored. The CLC carve-out itself has no CLC equivalent, because CLC is the senior regime.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed question gives you your own headings - (a), (b), (c), (d) - so the structure is free. The mark is lost in a different way: by defining the four terms and never doing the comparison the stem asks for. Every one of steps 3 to 6 therefore ends with an explicit 'Against CLC 92' sentence. That single habit converts a half answer into a full one.

Step 7 exists because the most important difference does not live under any of the four definitions. It is structural - who is liable, what limit applies, and what stands behind it. Putting it in a table at the end banks a large number of comparison marks quickly.

The idea underneath the question

CLC is a cargo regime. Bunkers is a fuel regime. Everything else follows. CLC could afford exclusive channelling to the registered owner and a dedicated fund because the industry paying for the fund - oil receivers - is identifiable and finite. There is no equivalent constituency to levy for bunkers, so the Bunkers Convention got neither a fund nor its own limit, and had to widen the definition of 'shipowner' instead so that a claimant has more than one solvent defendant.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting lubricating oil from the definition of bunker oil. It is expressly in the text.
  • Saying bunker oil must be persistent. It need not be - that is a CLC requirement, not a Bunkers one.
  • Writing that liability is channelled to the registered owner. That is CLC. Under Bunkers it reaches the bareboat charterer, manager and operator as well.
  • Saying the Bunkers Convention has its own limits of liability. It does not - it borrows the LLMC.

Model written answer

1. Why the hull form governs the power the ship needs

Total resistance divides into frictional resistance, which depends on wetted surface area and hull roughness; wave-making resistance, the energy left behind in the wave system; and viscous pressure or form resistance, arising from the way the flow separates and thickens around the hull. For a full-form, relatively slow merchant ship friction dominates, but wave-making and form resistance are the components the designer can actually change by reshaping the hull.

Hull form also sets the second term in the propulsion equation. Delivered power is resistance times speed divided by the propulsive coefficient, and the hull efficiency element of that coefficient - the relationship between wake fraction and thrust deduction - is a direct product of the aftbody shape. The hull therefore appears on both sides of the problem, which is why it is the largest single design lever available.

2. How hull form optimisation is done, and what changed

The modern method is parametric hull modelling coupled to computational fluid dynamics. The hull is described by a set of driving parameters, thousands of variants are generated and evaluated by CFD - potential flow for wave making, viscous RANS solvers for the wake field - and the promising forms are verified by model tank testing. CFD proposes; the tank still confirms.

The decisive change is what the hull is optimised for. Historically a hull was optimised at one design speed and one design draught. Since slow steaming became normal, ships spend their lives away from that point. Optimisation is now carried out over a matrix of speeds and draughts weighted by the ship's real operating profile, which is also why retrofit optimisation of existing ships is worthwhile - many were shaped for a speed they will never see again.

3. (i) Forebody optimisation

Scope. The bulbous bow - its volume, length, shape and vertical position; the waterline entrance angle; the fairing of the forward shoulder; and the stem profile. On existing ships this is the classic bow retrofit: the bulb is cut off and replaced, or removed altogether in favour of a straight stem.

Impact. The forebody governs the wave-making component. A bulb works by generating a wave system in antiphase with the bow wave so that the two partially cancel - but that cancellation is tuned to a particular speed and draught, and off that point the bulb can add resistance rather than remove it. A bulb designed for a laden design speed may be actively harmful in ballast or at slow steaming speed. Reshaping also improves added resistance in waves, which matters because the ship meets real seas rather than calm water, and improves ballast-condition performance where the old bulb sat near the surface.

4. (ii) Aftbody optimisation

Scope. The run of the buttock lines, the choice between U-form and V-form sections, the stern bulb, the shaft bossing and skeg arrangement, and the propeller aperture and tip clearances.

Impact. The aftbody's real job is not resistance but the quality of the water delivered to the propeller. A full stern produces a thick, distorted boundary layer, so each blade passes through a strongly varying inflow once per revolution. That non-uniform wake causes cavitation, pressure pulses, hull vibration and erosion, and lowers propulsive efficiency. Optimisation aims at a more uniform, better organised wake field, which allows a higher blade loading without cavitation and improves the hull efficiency term. There is a genuine trade-off: a wake that is too uniform loses some of the wake gain that makes a single-screw ship efficient in the first place.

Keep the distinction clear. Pre-swirl ducts, stators, fins and boss cap fins are energy saving devices bolted to the hull. They exploit a poor wake; they are not aftbody form optimisation, and an answer that treats them as the same thing has missed what the question asked.

5. (iii) Twin skeg design

Scope. Two separate skegs, each carrying its own shaft line and propeller, in place of a single centreline screw. It suits ships with high block coefficient, large beam and restricted draught, and high installed power - large LNG carriers, ro-ro and car carriers, and bulkers and tankers on draught-limited trades.

Impact - what it buys. On a shallow-draught ship, propeller diameter is the binding constraint. Two smaller propellers permit a larger total disc area and lower revolutions than one propeller squeezed under a flat stern, and open-water efficiency improves accordingly. Each propeller sits in cleaner, more uniform inflow outboard of the centreline, so cavitation and vibration fall. The hull can carry a fuller midbody without the usual penalty, protecting cargo capacity. Redundancy is a real operational benefit.

Impact - what it costs. Two skegs add wetted surface area, and therefore frictional resistance, which on a friction-dominated hull is a significant debit. Two shafts, sterntubes, bearings and sets of machinery raise capital cost and maintenance. Twin skeg is therefore a net gain only where the propulsive efficiency benefit outweighs the added friction - which is precisely the high-block, restricted-draught, high-power case.

6. What limits the gains, and where they are measured

The gains are design-specific and do not simply add: improving the wake field alters the flow the bulb sees, so the hull must be optimised as a whole. Because hull form is a design property it is one of the few genuine levers on attained EEDI for a newbuilding and on attained EEXI for an existing ship, both under MARPOL Annex VI. In service the benefit appears as reduced fuel per unit of transport work and therefore in the operational carbon intensity indicator, in force since 1 January 2023 - which is the commercial reason the subject has become urgent again.

1. What the cylinder oil must do, and why it is once-through

In a two-stroke crosshead engine the cylinder oil is separate from the system oil. It maintains the oil film between rings and liner, neutralises the acid formed from the fuel's sulphur, keeps the ring pack and piston crown clean, helps the rings seal, and carries debris down to the scavenge space. It is a total-loss (once-through) system: every drop is burnt or scraped down and none returns. The quantity injected is therefore both a running cost and the principal wear variable - which is why the question asks how to optimise it.

2. The system layout - what the sketch must show

3. How the dose is timed and controlled

Unlike the old mechanical lubricator, the modern unit is electronically timed and independently metered. The control unit knows the crank position and injects a measured quantity through the non-return valves as the piston rings pass the quills on the upward stroke, so the oil goes into the ring pack rather than onto bare liner. Delivery is intermittent - for instance every 2, 4 or 8 revolutions - with the frequency set from engine speed and fuel index. Above a part-load breakpoint the feed follows engine load; below it, engine speed - so the dose can follow load, fuel sulphur and oil BN instead of a mechanical drive.

4. Why sulphur content drives both the base number and the quantity

Fuel sulphur burns to sulphur dioxide and trioxide. Where the liner wall is below the acid dew point, water vapour condenses and the oxides dissolve in it as sulphuric acid on the liner. Unneutralised, this is cold corrosion - corrosive wear of liner and rings, very rapid in bad cases.

The oil carries an alkaline reserve (overbased detergent), measured as its Base Number (BN). Acid produced is proportional to the sulphur burnt - fuel consumption times sulphur percentage - so the alkali delivered must track the sulphur burnt. Alkali delivered is essentially BN times quantity: the engineer has two independent knobs, the grade and the feed rate. A high-BN oil at a low rate can match a lower-BN oil at a higher rate, which is why the grade is chosen before the rate is set.

5. Setting the optimum feed rate in service

The procedure is: choose the grade, then set the quantity, then prove it.

  • Select the BN grade for the fuel in use - a low-BN oil for fuel of up to 0.50% sulphur, a high-BN oil for high-sulphur fuel (ships with exhaust gas scrubbers). The switching valve puts the selected grade into the line.
  • Enter the sulphur figure from the bunker delivery note on the operating panel - the system cannot measure it.
  • Set the feed. On high-sulphur fuel the feed is proportional to load and to sulphur through the maker's feed-rate factor (formerly the ACC factor). On low-sulphur fuel the sulphur-proportional figure falls below the minimum, so the minimum feed-rate setting governs and is optimised from the cylinder condition.
  • Respect the maker's minimum feed rate - below it the film fails whatever the acid calculation says.
  • Optimise downward in small steps, only after confirming good cylinder condition and acceptable iron in the drain oil; hold and monitor between steps - never one large reduction, never during unstable load or running-in.

7. The traps - changeover, over-lubrication and the minimum

  • Changing between high- and low-sulphur fuel without changing the setting. A high-BN oil left in use on low-sulphur fuel over-delivers alkali and builds deposits; a low-BN oil on high-sulphur fuel invites cold corrosion. Grade selection and the sulphur entry are part of the fuel changeover procedure.
  • Over-lubrication is not a safe error. Excess alkaline oil leaves hard calcium deposits on the top land and in the ring grooves, causes bore polishing that destroys the liner's oil-retaining honing, sticks rings and raises cost.
  • Never go below the maker's minimum, whatever a calculation suggests.
  • The low-sulphur trap. Little acid forms, but the oil is still needed for film strength and cleaning: use a suitable lower-BN grade at an adequate rate, not simply less of a high-BN oil.

8. Technical extension - automatic grade supply, dual-fuel operation and maker differences

Technical extension - beyond the core answer. Credit-earning detail for a 'new generation' question; write it only after steps 1 to 7 are complete.

Automatic grade supply. Carrying two grades raises a practical question: how does the right one reach the lubricator? Everllence offers two supply systems:

  • ACOS2 - automated cylinder oil switching (the standard system on most engines). It selects one of two oils through the three-way valve, controlled by the engine control system - in Auto mode the engine control system selects automatically between the two grades according to the engine's control configuration; High BN and Low BN modes use that grade only - and fails safe to the high-BN oil. The maker shows the valve either on the ship side ahead of one heating tank or, on GI engines, on the engine behind separate low-BN and high-BN inlets. The maker describes it as securing fully acceptable conditions where a longer changeover period is available.
  • ACOM Mk III - automated cylinder oil mixing (an option for engines that burn high-sulphur fuel; not applicable to low-sulphur-only engines). It mixes two fully formulated cylinder oils in a small tank to the BN that the control system orders from the fuel sulphur, giving a faster change of BN. Since Category II BN 40 oils became available, the maker no longer recommends mixing for low-sulphur operation.

Neither system meters the oil or sets the feed rate - the Alpha lubricator and its control do that. BN selection and feed rate are separate control decisions: the supply system decides how much alkali each drop carries; the lubricator control decides how many drops arrive. The maker's name for the feed control is Alpha ACC (Adaptive, or Adaptable, Cylinder oil Control); its sulphur-proportional factor is now called the feed-rate factor.

A different maker. Switching, mixing, blending and metering - four different jobs

ACOS2 switches between two finished oils. ACOM mixes two fully formulated cylinder oils in a small unit on the supply line, to a BN that the engine control system orders from the fuel sulphur. WinGD's Pulse Jet system (called the Pulse Lubricating System in WinGD's installation manuals) is a genuinely different design, not a variant of the Alpha lubricator. Common mistakes

  • Describing a mechanical, camshaft-driven lubricator when the question says 'new generation'. The point of the modern system is electronic timing and metering.
  • Treating feed rate as the only variable and never mentioning base number.
  • Assuming over-lubrication is harmless. It causes deposits, bore polishing and stuck rings.
  • Forgetting that the sulphur figure is entered by the engineer, not measured by the engine.
  • Omitting the maker's minimum feed rate - the single most important operational limit in the whole answer.
  • Confusing cylinder oil with system oil, or suggesting cylinder oil is filtered and returned. It is total-loss.
  • Calling the Alpha lubricator a 'common-rail' system, or giving the ME engine's lubricator an accumulator. Neither is in the maker's description of the ME arrangement; accumulators belong to the older MC/MC-C Alpha lubricators.
  • Treating ACOS2 or ACOM as the lubricator. They deliver the right oil to it; the lubricator meters it.
  • Calling ACOM 'blending on board'. ACOM mixes two finished cylinder oils in the engine's supply system to an ordered BN; a blending-on-board unit makes cylinder oil from system oil (or a low-BN oil) and a high-BN product and fills a day tank. Different equipment, different principle.
  • Applying the sulphur-proportional factor on low-sulphur fuel. On the maker's current guidance it governs only on high-sulphur fuel; otherwise the minimum feed-rate setting does.
  • Quoting a feed rate or BN figure as if it were universal. Name the maker and say the figures come from its current service letter for that engine; do not transfer them to another maker.

Examiner traps

  • 'You have reduced the feed rate and iron in the scrape-down has risen. What now?' Restore the previous rate immediately, then investigate - the reduction has passed the point where the film or the alkali is adequate.
  • 'Residual BN in the scrape-down is very high. Is that good?' No - it means alkali is being thrown away unburnt and the rate is above what the fuel needs.
  • 'Why not just run maximum feed always?' Cost, deposits, bore polishing, stuck rings and higher emissions of unburnt oil.
  • 'What happens on entering an ECA?' The fuel changes to 0.10% sulphur and the sulphur entry must be changed with it. On an engine already running a low-BN oil for 0.50% fuel, the maker's guidance does not call for a change of oil; a change of grade is needed when the ship moves between high-sulphur fuel and low-sulphur fuel.
  • 'The ship has ACOM. Why not order one mid-range BN all the time?' Because the BN should follow the fuel's sulphur. For low-sulphur fuel the maker now recommends a single Category II BN 40 oil rather than a mixture.
  • 'Is the ACC factor still how the feed is set?' Only on high-sulphur fuel, where the maker now calls it the feed-rate factor. On low-sulphur fuel and gas the minimum feed-rate setting governs.
  • Being asked for numbers. State the principle and the basis, and say that the actual figures come from the engine maker's and oil supplier's current recommendation for that installation.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is cold corrosion and where on the liner does it occur? Corrosive wear from condensed sulphuric acid where the wall is below the acid dew point, typically in the upper part of the liner.
  • What is bore polishing and why does it matter? Loss of the honed surface pattern, so the liner no longer retains oil - it follows over-lubrication and deposit build-up.
  • How many quills per liner, and why several? The number depends on the engine type; several round the circumference so that oil reaches the whole ring pack in the time available.
  • What does iron in the scrape-down tell you? Wear rate, and the form of the iron indicates the wear mechanism.
  • Where does the cylinder oil finally go? Burnt with the charge, or scraped down to the scavenge drain.
  • What does the ACOS2 valve do if control air or its signal is lost? A spring returns it to the high-BN oil.
  • What is a 'sulphur equivalent'? The sulphur content of the fuel actually burnt in dual-fuel operation, which the control system calculates from engine load, the amount of pilot fuel and the pilot fuel's sulphur.
  • What triggers and meters the Alpha lubricator on an ME engine? The cylinder control unit commands a solenoid valve (a proportional valve on the Mk 2) that activates each stroke, and the lubricator's small multi-piston pumps meter and inject the cylinder oil. It is mounted on the hydraulic cylinder unit, which is supplied with 300 bar system oil, but the maker's current documents do not state the medium that actuates the lubricator - say so rather than guess.

It is electronically timed and independently metered. The control unit injects a measured quantity through non-return valves as the piston rings pass the quills on the upstroke, intermittently - for instance every 2, 4 or 8 revolutions - setting the frequency from engine speed and fuel index. The question says 'new generation'; describing a camshaft-driven lubricator answers a different question.

Sulphur burns to SO2 and SO3. Where the liner wall is below the acid dew point, water vapour condenses and the oxides dissolve to form sulphuric acid on the liner, corroding liner and rings. The cylinder oil carries an overbased alkaline reserve, measured as its Base Number, which neutralises that acid.

The mechanism, not the slogan. Everything about grade selection follows from it.

Excess alkaline oil forms hard calcium deposits on the piston top land and in the ring grooves, causes bore polishing which destroys the liner's oil-retaining honing pattern, sticks rings, increases cost and increases emission of unburnt oil.

The most common wrong assumption on this question.

Choose the BN grade for the fuel in use and enter the sulphur from the bunker delivery note. On high-sulphur fuel the feed follows the feed-rate factor times sulphur; on low-sulphur fuel and gas the minimum feed-rate setting governs. Reduce in small steps only after good condition and acceptable drain-oil iron are confirmed, holding between steps. Prove it by residual BN and iron, scavenge port inspection, daily consumption checks and liner wear.

This is the marks-bearing operating procedure and the part that shows shipboard judgement.

Alkali is being delivered in excess of what the fuel's sulphur requires and is being wasted. Provided iron content and liner condition are satisfactory and the maker's minimum is respected, the feed rate can be reduced in a controlled step and the result monitored before any further reduction.

Tests whether the candidate can interpret the analysis rather than merely name it.

ACOS2 SWITCHES between two cylinder oils through a three-way valve controlled by the engine control system, and fails safe to the high-BN oil; it is the standard system and suits a longer changeover period. ACOM Mk III MIXES two fully formulated oils to the BN ordered from the fuel sulphur, for a faster change; it is an option for high-sulphur operation. Neither meters the oil - the Alpha lubricator does.

Switching, mixing and metering are three different jobs; examiners reward keeping them apart.

Only on high-sulphur fuel, where the feed is the factor times the sulphur percentage, never below the minimum. On fuel of up to 0.50% sulphur and on gas or methanol the product falls below the minimum, so the minimum feed-rate setting governs and is optimised from inspection and drain-oil results.

The commonest out-of-date statement in answers to this question.

Not on the maker's current guidance - the same low-BN Category II oil serves gas and fuel of up to 0.50% sulphur. The pilot fuel's sulphur must still be entered so that the system can calculate the sulphur equivalent. A change of oil is needed when moving to or from high-sulphur fuel.

Model written answer

(a) The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification

1. What the HSSC is, and the problem it solved

Before harmonization each convention imposed its own surveys, its own certificate validity and its own expiry date. A single ship therefore held certificates falling due at different times, and had to be surveyed on several separate occasions by several separate attendances - with the ship out of service each time. The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification aligns the survey requirements and certificate validity periods of the principal statutory instruments into one common cycle with one common anniversary date, so that the ship can be surveyed for all of them at essentially the same time.

2. The instruments that carry it, and the edition in force

The HSSC was introduced by the 1988 Protocol to SOLAS 1974 and the 1988 Protocol to the Load Lines Convention 1966, by resolution MEPC.39(29) for MARPOL and MEPC.132(53) for MARPOL Annex VI, and by corresponding amendments to the IBC, IGC and BCH Codes; the Ballast Water Management Convention included it from the outset. The Guidelines now extend across SOLAS 74/88, the Load Lines Convention 66/88, MARPOL, the BWM Convention, the IBC, IGC and BCH Codes, the Polar Code and, since the 2025 edition, the IP Code.

The operative edition at this sitting is resolution A.1207(34), the Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2025, adopted 3 December 2025, which revoked A.1186(33) and takes into account amendments entering into force before or on 31 December 2025.

3. The key objectives behind its establishment

  1. One survey occasion instead of many - align the surveys required by different instruments so they can be carried out together.
  2. A common certificate cycle and anniversary date, so all statutory certificates run and expire together.
  3. Minimise the time the ship is out of service and the commercial disruption that repeated attendances cause.
  4. Reduce the administrative burden on Administrations, recognized organizations and shipowners alike.
  5. Provide flexibility - a window within which a survey may be done - without lowering the standard being surveyed to.
  6. Remove conflict and duplication between the survey requirements of different instruments, so that a single coherent regime applies.

4. How the system works - survey types, cycle and time window

The Guidelines describe seven types of survey: initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal, inspection of the outside of the ship's bottom, and additional. The harmonized system provides for a one-year standard interval between surveys based on those types as appropriate - with the exception of MARPOL Annex IV, which is based on initial and renewal surveys only.

Certificates run for a maximum of five years on a common anniversary date. Flexibility is built in: the renewal survey may be completed within three months before the expiry date with no loss of the certificate's period of validity, and annual and intermediate surveys have a six-month time window - from three months before to three months after the anniversary date. The Guidelines also set out the conditions for extending a certificate, for revalidation, for surveys after transfer of flag, and - in this edition - for the application of remote surveys, which were brought in following the experience of the pandemic and advances in technology.

(b) The HSSC and port State control

5. (b) How the HSSC helps port State control

Efficiency - the officer meets one coherent certificate set on one anniversary date, in harmonised forms with a standard record of surveys and endorsements. The initial document check that begins every inspection is therefore faster and can be done consistently by officers in different regions. Because the endorsements are standard, an officer does not need to know each convention's separate survey vocabulary to read them.

Effectiveness - alignment makes a gap visible. If one certificate is unendorsed or a survey is overdue against its window, that stands out immediately against the others, and gives the officer clear grounds to move from a document check to a more detailed inspection under SOLAS regulation I/19 and the port State control procedures, which at this sitting are those of resolution A.1206(34), Procedures for Port State Control, 2025. It also removes the situation in which a ship was technically in order on one certificate and lapsed on another, which previously allowed genuine deficiencies to hide in the mismatch. Fewer, better-documented surveys mean the officer can target effort rather than duplicate what the flag has already verified.

(c) Implementation

6. (c) The challenges and complexities of implementation

  • Getting an existing ship onto a single anniversary date in the first place, which requires certificates to be shortened or extended and is administratively awkward.
  • The window may not suit the ship. A trading pattern or a drydock booking may not place the ship where a surveyor can attend within the three-month-either-side window.
  • Surveyor availability - the world fleet is global and recognized organizations are not equally present in every port.
  • Multiple recognized organizations. Where a flag delegates different certificates to different societies, the coordination the HSSC was meant to simplify returns in another form.
  • Keeping the Guidelines current. Nine parent instruments are amended on their own timetables, so the Guidelines themselves must be reissued repeatedly - each edition has revoked its predecessor after about two years (A.1186(33) in 2023, A.1207(34) in 2025).
  • Consistency of interpretation between Administrations and between recognized organizations, which determines whether the same standard is actually applied.
  • A survey is a snapshot. Alignment does not stop condition deteriorating between attendances.

Model written answer

1. The statute, and what abandonment changes

At the date of this examination the governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 (Act 24 of 2025). It received assent on 18 August 2025 and the Central Government, by notification S.O. 1244(E) dated 10 March 2026, brought its provisions into force on 15 March 2026. Section 324(1) repeals the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 (except its Part XIV, not including section 411A), and section 324(2) saves appointments, orders and pending proceedings. Four Parts of the 2025 Act engage on these facts:

PartSubjectWhat it gives on these facts
Part VII (s.131 onward)Prevention and containment of pollution from vessel and responseReporting, notice to the owner, direct action by the Central Government, cost recovery - and an express rule for an ABANDONED vessel
Part X (ss.223-230)Marine incident and emergency responseA notified nodal authority, the owner, master and seafarers as primary response party, powers of direction and requisition - worked out in the Merchant Shipping (Marine Incident and Emergency Response) Rules, 2026 (G.S.R. 354(E))
Part XI (s.231 onward)Investigation and inquiries on marine casualtiesA vessel stranded or abandoned on or near the coast is a marine casualty for investigation
Part XII (ss.233-246 and s.247 onward)Wreck and salvageReceiver of wreck, hazard determination, marking and removal at the owner's cost, compulsory insurance, salvage

What abandonment changes, and what it does not. When the owners walk away, the practical initiative passes to the State. It does not end the owner's liability: the Act lets the authorities act and then recover the cost from the owner, charges it on the owner's other ships, and allows claims directly against the insurer.

2. Immediate action - life first, then report

  • Safety of life comes first. While anyone remains on board, the master and seafarers are, with the owner, the primary response party (s.226(1)) and must comply with the nodal authority's directions (s.226(2)). The Indian Coast Guard coordinates search and rescue.
  • Report the casualty. Where a maritime casualty results in a wreck, the master, owner or operator must report it without delay to the Director-General - under the 2025 Act the Director-General of Maritime Administration (s.2; appointed under s.7), successor to the Director-General of Shipping - and to the Maritime Rescue Coordination Centre or Sub-Centre of the Indian Coast Guard (s.236(1)).
  • Report pollution. Where oil or other harmful substances escape, or are likely to, the master must report the incident (s.138(1)).
  • Contain on board while the crew remain: sound tanks, assess hull damage, stop leaks, transfer oil away from damaged tanks where safe, and prevent the ship working further on the ground.

3. The pollution powers - Part VII, section 138

This is the provision that answers the question most directly where the owner has abandoned the ship.

  1. Notice to the owner (s.138(2)). On receiving the report the Central Government may serve notice on the owner, agent, master, charterer, operator, lessee or licensee to: prevent the escape of harmful substances; remove the cargo or oil; move the vessel; clean up slicks; prohibit removal of the vessel or her cargo; and take any other necessary action.
  2. The abandoned-vessel rule (s.138(3)). If the vessel is abandoned, or her report is incomplete, the Central Government fixes responsibility for the incident on the master or owner. Abandonment therefore does not dissolve responsibility; the Act assigns it.
  3. Direct action (s.138(4) and (5)). Where there is grave and imminent danger to the coast or coastal waters, or where a notice is not complied with, the Central Government may itself take the measures needed to prevent, mitigate or eliminate the threat. On these facts - owners gone - this is the operative power.
  4. Cost recovery (s.138(6)). The expenditure is a debt due to the Central Government, recoverable from the persons served, and a charge on any vessel they own, which may be detained and, if the debt is not paid, sold.
  5. Other vessels (s.139). The Government may, by written order, direct the owner of any Indian vessel or equipment to provide services or assistance.

Operationally the oil spill response is run under the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan, with the Indian Coast Guard as coordinating authority for response at sea.

4. Emergency response - Part X and the MIER Rules 2026

  • The Central Government appoints by notification one or more nodal authorities to administer and supervise marine incidents and the maritime emergency response (s.225). No s.225 notification was located as at 21 September 2026. The Directorate General of Maritime Administration describes itself as the national nodal authority, but the Act requires a notification, so the answer names the office by its statutory description.
  • The nodal authority may issue written directions to Central and State bodies, requisition officers, and requisition pollution response equipment (s.228).
  • The Merchant Shipping (Marine Incident and Emergency Response) Rules, 2026, G.S.R. 354(E) of 12 May 2026, made under s.230 read with s.228(4), are in force from their publication in the Gazette. The nodal authority must coordinate and supervise the response and may issue directions to the master, owner, managers, salvors, insurers, ports and others - including removal or containment of hazards and pollution control (r.3(1)-(3)). Where directions fail it may enter premises, assume control of the ship and arrange salvage and pollution response itself (r.3(4)). For an abandoned ship the decisive rule is r.4(1): the nodal authority must initiate immediate response where the primary response party delays or is unable to act, and may hand control back once that party is ready (r.4(2)). The Director-General ensures overall coordination of all responders and communicates with the owner and the flag, port and coastal States (r.3(6)); the Principal Officer enforces compliance (r.3(5)). Costs incurred by those complying with directions, by affected landowners and by the Government are recoverable from the ship owner (r.5).
  • Every owner of an Indian vessel must maintain insurance or other financial security for liability under this Part and carry evidence of it on board (s.229).

5. The wreck regime - Part XII, Chapter I

  • Receiver of wreck (s.235). Appointed by notification to take possession of wreck, give notices, enforce the obligations of the owner, operator or insurer, and act to remove the wreck by the most practical and expeditious means consistent with safety and the marine environment.
  • Hazard (ss.234 and 237). The Central Government decides whether the wreck is a hazard - a danger or impediment to navigation, or a threat of major harm to the marine environment, coastline or related interests - against listed criteria including the oil on board, proximity to shipping routes, protected areas and ports.
  • Marking (s.238). The owner or operator must mark a hazardous wreck at his own cost and keep it marked until removed.
  • Removal (s.242). The Central Government informs and consults the flag State and the registered owner; the registered owner or operator must remove a hazardous wreck (s.242(3)) and produce evidence of insurance (s.242(4)); a removal plan is submitted and approved with a time limit (s.242(5)-(6)). If the owner does not remove it in time - or where immediate action is needed - the Government or the receiver removes it at the owner's expense (s.242(7)-(8)).
  • Liability and insurance (ss.243 and 244). The registered owner is liable for the cost of locating, marking and removing the wreck, subject to narrow defences (war or exceptional natural phenomenon, third-party intent, negligence of the authority maintaining navigational aids), and may limit liability under s.162. Indian vessels of 300 GT and above must carry compulsory insurance, and a claim may be brought directly against the insurer.
  • Owner's claim and time limit (ss.245 and 246). An owner may reclaim wreck or proceeds within one year on paying salvage and charges; cost-recovery claims must be made within three years of the hazard determination and six years of the casualty.

6. Salvage, place of refuge and investigation

  • Salvage. Part XII Chapter II applies to salvage operations within India's territory including coastal waters (s.247). Where a wreck-removal measure amounts to salvage, the salvage provisions apply (s.242(9)). With the owner gone, the State may have to procure salvage itself and recover the cost.
  • Place of refuge. A request to move the ship to sheltered water is assessed under the Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, resolution A.1184(33). A coastal State is under no obligation in international law to grant one.
  • Investigation. A vessel lost, abandoned, stranded or materially damaged on or near the coast of India is a marine casualty for the purposes of Part XI (s.231(1)(a)), as is pollution or its threat in coastal waters (s.231(1)(f)). Separately, the marine safety investigation follows the Casualty Investigation Code (MSC.255(84)), which seeks to prevent recurrence and does not apportion blame.

Why this answer changed from the February 2024 version

This question was set, word for word, in February 2024 (QP2402-Q8). That answer was correct for its sitting and was built on the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 - Part XA (ss.356J-356L) and Part XIII (ss.390-404). The engineering and response logic carries over; every statutory citation had to be rebuilt from the 2025 text.

The one provision to remember

Section 138(3): if the vessel is abandoned, the Central Government fixes responsibility on the master or owner. It is the Act's own answer to the word 'abandoned' in the question, and it is new in form compared with the 1958 scheme.

Examiner traps

  • Citing 1958 section numbers. ss.356J-356L and 390-404 belong to a repealed Act. Name the 2025 Act.
  • Saying abandonment ends the owner's liability. It does not - ss.138(3), 138(6), 243 and 244 all keep it on the owner and the insurer.
  • Forgetting the insurer. Compulsory insurance and direct action (s.244(5)) are what actually pay for a wreck removal.
  • Confusing the two investigations. The Part XI inquiry and the Casualty Investigation Code safety investigation have different purposes.
  • Naming the nodal authority without authority. s.225 provides for one or more notified nodal authorities. The Directorate's own casualty page describes it as the national nodal authority, but no s.225 Gazette notification was located, so say 'the nodal authority notified under s.225 (the Directorate describes itself as the national nodal authority)' rather than asserting a notification.

The question asks about the Act, so the Convention is context, not the answer.

Model written answer

(a) The IMO and ILO human element regimes

1. (a) The human element, and how IMO and ILO divide the work

IMO's own term is the human element, and resolution A.947(23) treats it as a complex multi-dimensional issue affecting maritime safety, security and marine environmental protection, involving the entire spectrum of human activity - ships' crews, shore-based management, regulatory bodies, recognized organizations, shipyards and legislators. It is not simply 'crew error'.

The two organisations divide the work along a clean line: IMO regulates the seafarer as an operator of the ship - competence, safe operation, fitness for duty. ILO regulates the seafarer as a worker - employment, living and working conditions, health and welfare. Together they cover the same person from two directions.

2. The IMO regime - competence, safe operation and fitness for duty

  • STCW 1978, as amended by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force 1 January 2012 - the standards of training, certification and watchkeeping. Regulation I/6 on training and assessment, I/14 on company responsibilities, and chapter VIII with section A-VIII/1 on fitness for duty and minimum hours of rest.
  • The ISM Code, mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX - safety management, dealt with in limb (b).
  • Resolution A.1047(27), Principles of Minimum Safe Manning - the framework by which an Administration determines the manning a ship must carry.
  • MSC.1/Circ.1598, Guidelines on fatigue, of 24 January 2019, which address fatigue across the whole system rather than only on board.
  • Resolution A.947(23), the human element vision, principles and goals, which is the policy statement underlying all of the above.
  • The III Code and the mandatory Member State Audit Scheme, which audit whether a flag State actually gives effect to these obligations.

3. The ILO regime - MLC 2006, the fourth pillar

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 consolidated a large body of earlier ILO maritime instruments and is called the fourth pillar of international maritime regulation, alongside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW. Its structure is distinctive: Articles and Regulations setting out the obligations, and a Code in two parts - Part A, mandatory Standards, and Part B, Guidelines to which due consideration must be given.

It is arranged in five Titles: 1 minimum requirements for seafarers to work on a ship; 2 conditions of employment; 3 accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering; 4 health protection, medical care, welfare and social security protection; and 5 compliance and enforcement.

Title 5 is what makes it real. A ship carries a Maritime Labour Certificate and a Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance - Part I drawn up by the competent authority, Part II by the shipowner and certified by the authority or an authorised recognized organization - and is subject to flag State inspection, port State control, and on-board and onshore complaint procedures.

The position at this sitting. In force: the 2014 amendments (18 January 2017, financial security for repatriation and shipowners' liability), 2016 (8 January 2019), 2018 (26 December 2020) and 2022 (23 December 2024). The 2025 amendments - approved by the International Labour Conference in June 2025, on shore leave, protection against violence and harassment, and recognition of seafarers as key workers - are adopted but not yet in force. On the IMO side, a comprehensive review of STCW is in progress.

4. Where the two organisations act together

  • Medical fitness - joint IMO/ILO guidelines on medical examinations of seafarers, where the STCW medical certificate and the MLC health requirements meet.
  • Fair treatment of seafarers in the event of a maritime accident - joint guidelines addressing detention and criminalisation.
  • Abandonment of seafarers, addressed jointly and reflected in the MLC financial security requirements.
  • Hours of rest is the clearest overlap: STCW section A-VIII/1 sets rest hours for watchkeeping safety, and MLC Title 2 sets hours of work and rest as a labour standard. The two must be read together, and where they differ the more demanding applies.

(b) The human factor and the ISM Code

5. (b) The ISM Code - what it is actually trying to do

The ISM Code exists because a series of casualties were traced not to defective equipment but to defective management - unclear responsibility, commercial pressure on the master, and shore organisations that did not know what was happening on their own ships. The Code therefore regulates a system, not a component. Its objectives are to ensure safety at sea, prevention of human injury or loss of life, and avoidance of damage to the environment; and to that end it requires safeguards against all identified risks and continuous improvement of the safety management skills of personnel ashore and aboard.

That is why the human factor is not one topic within the Code - it is what the Code is made of. A safety management system is a set of human commitments written down.

6. Where the human factor decides whether the Code works

7. Why implementation fails, and what makes it succeed

Where it fails:

  • Paper compliance. Checklists completed in advance, drills recorded but not run - the documentation is in order and the practice is not.
  • A system the crew do not own. An SMS written by an outside consultant, in a register the users do not read comfortably, describing a ship's operation that its own crew would not recognise.
  • Documentation overload. Excessive paperwork consumes the very hours the rest-hour rules protect, and fatigue then degrades the operation the system was meant to make safe.
  • Blame culture. If a report produces punishment, reporting stops - and element 9, the Code's learning mechanism, dies quietly while the certificate remains valid.
  • A DPA without independence, or a master whose overriding authority is understood by everyone to be career-limiting to use.
  • Familiarisation skipped on short crew turnarounds, and language barriers on multinational crews.

What makes it succeed:

  • A just culture that separates honest error from wilful violation, so that near-miss reporting is safe and therefore happens.
  • A proportionate system, written for the ship and in the users' working language, that the crew recognise as their own.
  • Visible leadership - the master and Chief Engineer using the system themselves, and the Company demonstrably accepting a safety decision that costs money.
  • A closed feedback loop, so crews see their reports produce change. Nothing sustains reporting like evidence that reporting works.
  • Management review that acts on trends rather than closing findings individually - which is where the latent organisational failures are caught, as distinct from the active failures at the sharp end.

In short: the ISM Code can require a system, but only people can make it a real one. That is how the human factor plays its role in implementation.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is a broad overview - breadth and correct attribution, not depth. Four short steps with named instruments earn the eight marks; two paragraphs on STCW rest hours do not. Limb (b) is worth the same eight marks and is the harder half, because it asks for analysis. Listing the twelve elements is not analysis. Steps 6 and 7 do the analytical work by tying each element to the human decision it depends on, and then evaluating honestly why systems fail.

The idea underneath the question

IMO regulates the seafarer as an operator; ILO regulates the seafarer as a worker. That single line organises the whole of limb (a) and lets you place any instrument correctly under pressure. For limb (b): the ISM Code regulates a system, and a system is made of human commitments. Everything in the Code that works, works because someone chose to make it real.

Common mistakes

  • Attributing the MLC to IMO. It is an ILO Convention.
  • Describing MLC Part B as mandatory. Part A carries the mandatory Standards; Part B is Guidelines to which due consideration must be given.
  • Answering limb (b) by listing the twelve ISM elements. The question asks how the human factor plays a role in implementing the Code.
  • Omitting the designated person ashore and the master's overriding authority - the two most explicitly human safeguards in the Code.
  • Treating the ISM Code as an IMO convention. It is a Code, given mandatory force by SOLAS chapter IX.
  • Saying nothing about why implementation fails. The analysis marks are there.
  • Getting the MLC amendment position wrong for the sitting. See the trap below.

Examiner traps

  • The MLC amendment position. At this sitting the amendments in force are those of 2014, 2016, 2018 and 2022. The 2025 amendments have been adopted but have NOT entered into force. Saying they were in force is the specific error this question is built to catch, and 'adopted' is not 'in force'.
  • Who draws up the DMLC? Part I by the competent authority; Part II by the shipowner, certified by the authority or an authorised recognized organization. The two are routinely inverted.
  • Is the ISM Code a convention? No - a Code made mandatory by SOLAS chapter IX.
  • What is the DPA for? Direct access to the highest level of management, so that safety information reaches the top without passing through the people it might reflect on.
  • Being asked to distinguish active and latent failures. Active failures are the unsafe acts at the sharp end; latent failures are the organisational conditions lying dormant until they combine with an active failure.
  • Being asked what the master may do under element 5. Exercise his overriding authority and responsibility to take any action he considers in the best interests of safety and pollution prevention, and request the Company's assistance.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Name the five Titles of the MLC. Minimum requirements; conditions of employment; accommodation, recreation, food and catering; health protection, medical care, welfare and social security; compliance and enforcement.
  • Which ISM element covers reporting of near misses? Element 9 - non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences.
  • Why is a common working language required? So that instructions and safety information are actually understood - element 6.
  • How is fatigue addressed by the two organisations? STCW section A-VIII/1 as a watchkeeping safety standard, MLC Title 2 as a labour standard, with IMO fatigue guidance alongside.
  • What certificate does MLC compliance produce? The Maritime Labour Certificate with the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance.

Model written answer

(a) Immediate actions of the Chief Engineer

1. Immediate actions

Steering has failed with the ship about to enter port, so time and sea room are short. The priorities are: the Master knows at once, the standby system is tried at once, and the ship is made safe in parallel rather than after diagnosis.

  1. Inform the Master immediately and confirm what the bridge sees - rudder angle indicator, alarms, which system was in use. The navigational decisions - stopping or reducing speed, anchoring, calling tugs, exhibiting not-under-command signals and informing the pilot and VTS - are the Master's.
  2. Start the standby power unit and change over to the other pump and control system. Most failures are confined to one power unit, one pump or one control circuit, and the duplicated arrangement exists for exactly this.
  3. Send an engineer to the steering flat at once. Check the alarm panel, hydraulic oil level, pump running, electrical supply and control signal. If remote control has failed, transfer to local control in the steering gear compartment with a reliable communication link to the bridge and a rudder angle indicator in view.
  4. If both systems fail, use the emergency steering arrangement and confirm the emergency or alternative power supply is available. SOLAS chapter II-1 requires, for larger rudder stocks, an alternative power supply that comes on automatically within 45 seconds.
  5. Prepare for the worst case in parallel: anchors ready forward, engine on standby with a second generator on line, tugs requested early, SOPEP equipment ready, and the time of each event logged for the report.

(b) Long-term actions

2. Long-term actions

  • Investigate the root cause and report it as a non-conformity or hazardous occurrence under ISM Code element 9 - not simply 'replaced relay', but why it failed and why it was not caught.
  • Treat the steering gear as critical equipment under ISM element 10.3: planned maintenance of pumps, valves, control and feedback units; hydraulic oil analysis and filter condition; critical spares carried; changeover and alarm functions proven.
  • Make the SOLAS tests real. SOLAS regulation V/26 requires the steering gear to be checked and tested within 12 hours before departure, and emergency steering drills at least once every three months, including direct control from the steering gear compartment, the communications procedure with the bridge and, where applicable, the alternative power supply. These should exercise the full changeover, not be signed off on paper. A pre-arrival check before entering confined waters is good practice.
  • Train and familiarise engineers and ratings who would operate emergency steering, with posted changeover instructions in the steering flat.
  • Share the lesson fleet-wide through the Company and the designated person ashore, and review whether the design or maker's service information needs to be applied.

(c) Legal consequences of contact with the jetty and a minor spill

The ship is in a foreign port, so the port State's own law applies, together with the conventions that State is party to. Several consequences arise at the same time and independently of one another:

3. Pollution liability and reporting

  1. Reporting and the pollution offence. The discharge must be reported to the coastal State without delay under MARPOL (Protocol I). A discharge is a MARPOL violation for which the port State may impose penalties under its own law; the defence available for a discharge resulting from damage to the ship applies only if all reasonable precautions were taken after the damage and there was no intent or recklessness.
  2. Civil liability for pollution damage. Clean-up costs and third-party loss are claimed from the owner. If the ship is a tanker carrying oil as cargo, CLC 1992 applies where the port State is a Party; for any other ship, a spill of bunker oil falls under the Bunkers Convention 2001 where the port State is a Party. Both impose strict liability on the owner, require compulsory insurance and permit direct action against the insurer. Where the State is not a Party, its national law governs.

4. Damage to the jetty and the ship's exposure

  1. Damage to the jetty. The port authority claims for repair and loss of use. The owner may be entitled to limit liability for damage to harbour works under the limitation regime applicable in that State, and the pilot's presence on board does not shift the ship's liability to the port.
  2. Arrest and detention. The claimants may arrest the ship as security, and the port authority or port State control may detain her until the damage is surveyed, the steering gear is proven, class has attended and security - normally a P&I letter of undertaking - is provided.
  3. Flag and class. The casualty is reported to the flag State and class; class may impose a condition of class, and the flag State may investigate. A marine safety investigation may follow under the Casualty Investigation Code.

5. The people

  1. Personal exposure of master and officers. Some States prosecute individuals for pollution offences. The IMO/ILO guidelines on fair treatment of seafarers in the event of a maritime accident bear on their treatment.
  2. Company obligations. ISM reporting, preservation of the VDR and records, notification to P&I and hull underwriters, and cooperation with surveyors.

Model written answer

(a) Ongoing developments - technical and operational measures

1. The frame

IMO's work is framed by the 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships, resolution MEPC.377(80), adopted 7 July 2023. Its ambitions are to cut CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 against 2008; to raise zero or near-zero GHG energy to at least 5%, striving for 10%, by 2030; and to reach net-zero GHG emissions by or around 2050, with indicative checkpoints of at least 20% (striving for 30%) by 2030 and at least 70% (striving for 80%) by 2040 in total annual emissions.

2. Technical measures in force

  • EEDI (MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4) - a new ship's attained design index must not exceed the required value, which tightens in phases.
  • EEXI (regulations 23 and 25), in force 1 January 2023 - the same design approach applied once to existing ships, commonly met by engine or shaft power limitation or energy-saving devices.

3. Operational measures in force

  • SEEMP (regulation 26) in three Parts; Part III is the plan to achieve the required CII. The operative guidance is the 2024 SEEMP Guidelines, MEPC.395(82), as amended by MEPC.401(83) and, at MEPC 84, by MEPC.413(84); the CII calculation guidelines (G1) were amended at the same session by MEPC.412(84).
  • CII (regulation 28), ships of 5,000 GT and above - an annual A-to-E rating against a required value that tightens each year. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E in one year, must develop a plan of corrective actions (regulation 28.7).
  • Data collection system (regulation 27) - the fuel-consumption data on which every measure depends; the amendments in MEPC.385(81), in force 1 August 2025, brought enhanced data granularity from 1 January 2026.

4. What is ongoing at September 2026

  • Review of the short-term measures. The first phase of the CII/SEEMP/EEXI review was completed in 2025 and set the reduction factors to 2030; the second phase, covering the deeper questions including the metric, corrective action and enforcement, began in 2026 and is due to finish in 2028.
  • Life-cycle (well-to-wake) accounting under the LCA Guidelines, which underpins any fuel-based measure.
  • Other GHGs and new fuels - work on methane and nitrous oxide from engines, and MSC's safety framework for alternative fuels and new technologies.
  • The mid-term measures - see limb (b); these are the largest open item.

(b) The basket of candidate measures

5. What the Strategy puts in the basket

The 2023 Strategy calls for a basket of candidate mid-term GHG reduction measures to be developed and agreed, comprising both:

  • a technical element - a goal-based marine fuel standard regulating the phased reduction of the marine fuel's GHG intensity; and
  • an economic element - a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism.

The two work together: the standard sets the trajectory, the price makes falling short dearer than compliance and raises revenue. Both are assessed well-to-wake and must be accompanied by a comprehensive assessment of impacts on States, with attention to developing countries, and must support a just and equitable transition. The Strategy's timetable envisaged adoption in 2025 and entry into force about 16 months later.

6. The Net-Zero Framework - the basket written as draft law

The basket was turned into the IMO Net-Zero Framework, approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 as draft amendments adding a new chapter 5 to MARPOL Annex VI. As approved in draft it would:

  • apply to ships of 5,000 GT and above;
  • require an annual GHG Fuel Intensity (GFI), well-to-wake, against two tiers - a base target and a stricter direct compliance target;
  • require a ship that falls short to acquire remedial units (the pricing element), and let a ship that over-achieves earn surplus units;
  • channel revenue into an IMO Net-Zero Fund to reward low-emission ships and support a just transition.

7. The position at this examination

The Framework is approved but NOT adopted, and nothing in it is in force. The extraordinary session convened to adopt it (MEPC/ES.2) adjourned on 17 October 2025 for one year. MEPC 84 (27 April - 1 May 2026) did not adopt it and set up an intersessional working group. IMO has announced that the extraordinary session will resume on 4 December 2026, subject to confirmation by MEPC 85 (30 November - 3 December 2026). No entry-into-force date exists. Regional schemes such as the EU Emissions Trading System are not IMO measures.
