1
How do you compose the engine room watch (watchkeeping levels)?
STCWWATCHKEEPING

15-Second Answer

Watch composition is set under STCW Chapter VIII (Section A-VIII/2, Part 3-2 engineering watch): the CE, in consultation with the Master, determines a watch adequate at all times for safe operation, considering machinery state and mode (manned/UMS), automation and alarm fitness, weather/ice/traffic and emergencies, qualifications and fatigue (rest-hour compliance, A-VIII/1), special operations (bunkering, manoeuvring, restricted waters), and the Minimum Safe Manning Document floor. On a UMS ship the "watch" becomes a duty engineer arrangement with the machinery-space alarm/monitoring fit (SOLAS II-1/Reg.46-53 automation provisions) proven operational.

60-Second Answer

There is no universal table — the answer is the decision process. The CE composes the watch so it can: monitor and control main/auxiliary machinery, respond to alarms and emergencies, and maintain safety of the ship. Factors from STCW A-VIII/2: (1) mode — continuously manned ER (traditional 4-on-8-off watches: watchkeeping engineer + rating(s) as needed) vs UMS (Administration-accepted unattended operation with a designated duty engineer on call, bridge/cabin alarm extensions, dead-man/patrol-man system, and the automation surveyed operational); (2) machinery condition — defects, manual operation of any critical system forces manning up; (3) operational phase — manoeuvring/standby, bunkering, restricted visibility, heavy weather, entering/leaving port typically require the ER manned regardless of UMS notation; (4) people — competency (certificates and familiarisation), experience mix (never an unaccompanied trainee), and rest hours per STCW A-VIII/1 / MLC (minimum 10 h in any 24 / 77 h in any 7 days); (5) floor — never below the Safe Manning Document. The CE documents the standing arrangement in standing orders and adjusts by written order for special conditions — and the Master must be informed of any change affecting ship safety.

Governing instrument

STCW Section A-VIII/2 Part 3-2 (engineering watch composition) with Part B-VIII/2 guidance; ISM Code Sec. 6; the ship's Minimum Safe Manning Document under SOLAS V/14.

Decision Factors — the Checklist to Recite

  • Mode: manned vs UMS; automation/alarm system status.
  • Machinery: defects, critical equipment on manual, redundancy state.
  • Operation: manoeuvring, bunkering, heavy weather, restricted waters, cargo ops needing ER support.
  • People: certification, experience, fatigue/rest-hour compliance.
  • Floor: Minimum Safe Manning Document (SOLAS V/14).

Trap point

Quoting a fixed watch composition is the fail — it is a CE determination under A-VIII/2 against machinery state, mode (manned/UMS) and conditions, never below the Safe Manning Document floor.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Simon wants the CE's decision logic, not a crew list. Structure: "adequate at all times" test → the five factors → UMS vs manned distinction → the Safe Manning floor → rest hours. Quote 10/24 and 77/7 unprompted. The failing answer gives a fixed roster ("one engineer and one oiler") with no conditions under which it changes.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

This is literally the CE's daily authority: standing orders define the default watch; night orders/manning-up decisions for weather, defects and port approaches are the CE's written record that the A-VIII/2 factors were actually applied — the document an investigator reads after an incident.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "UMS ship, purifier room flooding alarm at 0300 — who responds?" A: The designated duty engineer immediately, with the dead-man system active; the CE mans up if the situation exceeds one-man response. Q: "Can you run UMS with the main alarm printer/extension defective?" A: No — UMS is conditional on the surveyed automation being operational; revert to manned watches and record it. Q: "Cadet alone on watch?" A: Never — trainees keep watch only under supervision of a qualified officer.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Reciting a fixed roster with no factor logic; forgetting the Safe Manning floor; not knowing the rest-hour numbers; treating UMS as unconditional; omitting that manoeuvring/bunkering normally mans the ER regardless of notation.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Rest: 10 h / 24 h (divisible into max 2 periods, one ≥6 h) and 77 h / 7 days; UMS provisions SOLAS II-1 Regs.46–53; Safe Manning SOLAS V/14; STCW A-VIII/2 Part 3-2.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Fatigue-linked machinery casualties in MAIB/ATSB reports — single watchkeepers exceeding rest limits during port-intensive schedules — are why rest-hour compliance is a composition factor, not an HR formality.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: Compose the ER watch → what changes it → UMS conditions → duty engineer vs watchkeeper (bridging to Q2) → rest hours → standing/night orders → who informs the Master.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel, UMS notation: sea passage on duty-engineer rotation with alarm extensions and dead-man system; ER manned for arrival/departure, bunkering, canal transits and any critical-equipment defect — the changeover recorded in the ER log against the CE's standing orders.

Who is an independent watchkeeper?
STCWWATCHKEEPING

15-Second Answer

An independent watchkeeper is an engineer certified and assessed as competent to take sole charge of an engineering watch without supervision — i.e. holding at least the STCW Reg. III/1 officer-in-charge of an engineering watch certification (or III/7 EOOW-supporting ratings under direction), completed shipboard familiarisation, and formally signed off by the CE/company as fit for unsupervised watch on that ship's plant. The contrast is the supervised watchkeeper — a cadet/junior standing watch only under a qualified officer.

60-Second Answer

The term captures the transition every junior engineer makes: from keeping watch with someone to keeping watch alone, in sole charge. Requirements layer up: (1) certification — STCW Reg. III/1 (officer in charge of an engineering watch in a manned engine-room, or designated duty engineer in a periodically unmanned engine-room — the certificate wording itself covers both roles); (2) ship-specific competence — ISM Sec.6 familiarisation with this plant: main engine type, power plant, automation, emergency procedures — documented in the familiarisation checklist; (3) formal release — the CE assesses and authorises the officer for independent watch/duty-engineer rotation, recorded per the SMS (many companies use a written "watchkeeping release"); (4) fitness — medically fit, rest-hour compliant. For ratings, STCW III/4 (watch rating) and III/5 (able seafarer engine) contribute to the watch but do not keep it independently; an engine cadet never does. In UMS operation "independent watchkeeper" maps onto the designated duty engineer able to respond alone to alarms. The concept the examiner is probing: sole-charge responsibility — the independent watchkeeper answers directly to the CE for the machinery during his watch and must know when his independence ends: call for assistance and inform the CE when any situation exceeds safe one-person response (A-VIII/2 duty to call the CE in doubt — doing so is competence, not weakness).

Governing instrument

STCW Reg III/1 (officer in charge of an engineering watch) and Section A-VIII/2 sole-charge standards; the company's assessment/release procedure under ISM Code Sec. 6.

Build-Up to Independence

  • Certificate: STCW III/1 minimum (III/2 for senior watch on larger plants as assigned).
  • Familiarisation: ISM Sec.6.3 — before being assigned, documented.
  • Release: CE's assessment and written authorisation for sole charge / UMS duty.
  • Limits: knows the call-the-CE triggers; never delegates the watch to an uncertified person.

Trap point

An 'independent watchkeeper' is not simply any certificated engineer — the CoC is the floor; sole-charge release additionally requires company assessment and documented familiarisation.

STCW Reg. III/1 (A-III/1)Officer in charge of an engineering watch / duty engineer in a periodically unmanned ER — the base certificate
STCW A-VIII/2 Part 3-2Watch duties — sole-charge responsibilities and the duty to call the Chief Engineer when in doubt
ISM Code Sec.6.3 & 6.5Familiarisation before assignment and identification of training needs
STCW III/4 & III/5Watch rating / able seafarer engine — support the watch, never keep it independently
SOLAS V/14 (Safe Manning)The manning document listing the certificated watchkeepers the arrangement relies on
CE Oral Tip (Simon): Define it in one line — "certified, familiarised and formally released to keep the watch in sole charge" — then the three layers (STCW III/1 → ISM familiarisation → CE's release). Simon's follow-up on a container session is the UMS mapping: independent watchkeeper = the designated duty engineer answering alarms alone. The weak answer stops at "someone with a CoC" and misses ship-specific familiarisation and the CE's authorisation step.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Releasing a new 4/E for independent watch is a CE decision with legal weight: the assessment record is what shows due diligence if that engineer's watch later features in a casualty. Structured release — witnessed rounds, alarm-response drills, emergency-scenario questioning — is the professional standard.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "New 4/E joins with a valid III/1 CoC — can he take tonight's watch?" A: Not until ship-specific familiarisation is completed and he is released — the certificate is necessary, not sufficient (ISM 6.3). Q: "Can an electro-technical officer keep the engineering watch?" A: No — ETO (III/6) is not an engineering watchkeeping certificate; he supports, the watch needs a III/1 holder. Q: "Does calling the CE at night mean the watchkeeper isn't independent?" A: The opposite — A-VIII/2 obliges it in doubt; knowing the limit is part of the competence.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Equating certificate with competence (skipping familiarisation/release); not knowing III/4-III/5 ratings can't hold the watch; unable to map the concept onto UMS duty-engineer practice; forgetting the sole-charge accountability to the CE.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

STCW Regs: III/1 (EOOW/duty engineer), III/2 (C/E & 2/E ≥3000 kW), III/4/III/5 (ratings), III/6 (ETO), III/7 (electro-technical rating); ISM familiarisation: Sec.6.3, before assignment.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Engine-room fires and blackouts where an unfamiliarised officer misdiagnosed the specific plant (documented across MAIB reports) are the standing argument for the familiarisation-plus-release model over certificate-only manning.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: Who is an independent watchkeeper → certificate vs competence → familiarisation requirements → how you as CE release someone → when independence ends (call the CE) → UMS duty engineer mapping.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk practice: computer-based plus walked familiarisation checklists per rank, and the CE's documented handover/assessment before a joining engineer enters the UMS duty roster — typically after a supervised period matched to plant complexity.

What is Risk Based Planning?
ISMRISK

15-Second Answer

Risk-based planning means prioritising and designing work, maintenance and operations according to assessed risk (likelihood × consequence) rather than fixed routine alone. Its ISM anchor is Section 1.2.2.2 — "assess all identified risks to ships, personnel and the environment and establish appropriate safeguards." In practice: risk assessments before non-routine jobs, a risk matrix driving controls to ALARP, and risk-based maintenance (criticality-ranked PMS, condition-based intervals under class-approved schemes) instead of purely calendar-based routines.

60-Second Answer

The traditional model plans by fixed rules — calendar overhauls, standard checklists. Risk-based planning inverts it: identify hazards, evaluate likelihood and severity, and allocate effort/controls where risk is highest. Framework: hazard identification → risk evaluation (matrix) → controls by the hierarchy (eliminate → substitute → engineering → administrative → PPE) → residual risk to ALARP → implement, monitor, review. Where the CE applies it: (1) operations — every non-routine or high-hazard job (enclosed space, hot work, HV work, working aloft/overside) gets a documented risk assessment linked to the PTW system, per ISM 1.2.2.2 and Sec.7; (2) maintenance — criticality analysis ranks equipment by failure consequence; critical equipment (ISM Sec.10.3) gets tighter intervals, redundancy testing and standby verification, while low-criticality items can move to longer or condition-based intervals — the class-approved PMS/CBM survey arrangements are risk-based planning institutionalised; (3) voyage/resource planning — spares, manning-up decisions, weather routing. Benefits: resources follow consequence, hidden high-risk tasks surface, and deviations become visible decisions. Boundary: risk-based never overrides statutory/class minimums — you may tighten beyond them, extending beyond them needs the approving authority (class/flag).

Governing instrument

ISM Code Sec. 1.2.2.2 (assess all identified risks to ships, personnel and the environment — 2010 amendments, MSC.273(85)); the company's risk-assessment procedures implementing it.

The Matrix — Say It Concretely

  • Likelihood bands × consequence bands (people/environment/asset) → risk ranking (typically 5×5 in company SMS forms).
  • ALARP: reduce until further reduction is grossly disproportionate to the gain — then manage the residual.
  • Review triggers: incident, near-miss, change of method/equipment/people (Management of Change).

Trap point

Treating risk assessment as permit paperwork is the trap — ISM 1.2.2.2 requires assessment of ALL identified risks as a system property, feeding maintenance, manning and operational planning.

ISM Code Sec.7 & 10.3Shipboard operations planning · critical equipment identification and standby testing
Res. A.1118(30)Revised ISM implementation guidelines — risk assessment expectations in practice
Class PMS/CBM schemesApproved machinery survey arrangements — risk/condition-based maintenance recognised by class
MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 (FSA)Formal Safety Assessment — IMO's own risk-based method for rule-making (the concept at regulatory level)
CE Oral Tip (Simon): Anchor it to ISM 1.2.2.2 verbatim, then give one operational and one maintenance example from your own plant: "risk assessment + PTW before entering the scavenge space" and "criticality-ranked PMS with class-approved CBM on purifier bearings." Close with the boundary — risk-based can tighten, never relax, statutory/class minimums without approval. The weak answer defines a matrix in the abstract with no shipboard application.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE signs the risk assessments that matter most onboard — combustion-space entry, HV isolation, retarded maintenance decisions — and owns the criticality register behind the PMS. When deferring a job for spares, the risk assessment is what converts "we couldn't" into a defensible managed decision.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Condition monitoring looks good — extend the overhaul?" A: Only within a class-approved CBM/PMS scheme; outside it, class agreement first — risk-based planning works inside the approved envelope. Q: "Difference between risk assessment and PTW?" A: The RA analyses and sets controls; the PTW is the administrative control that enforces them for that job — the PTW references the RA. Q: "What is ALARP?" A: As Low As Reasonably Practicable — reduce risk until further measures are grossly disproportionate to the benefit.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Matrix-only answers with no application; confusing RA with PTW; suggesting risk assessment can override statutory intervals; not knowing ISM 1.2.2.2 as the anchor; no mention of criticality/critical equipment (10.3).

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

ISM 1.2.2.2 (risk), 7 (operations), 10.3 (critical equipment); hierarchy of controls (5 levels); typical SMS matrix 5×5; review triggers: incident / near-miss / change.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Enclosed-space fatalities — still among the most repeated shipboard killers — almost always show a missing or paper-only risk assessment; they are the standing case for risk-based planning as practice, not paperwork.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: What is risk-based planning → ISM anchor → matrix and ALARP → hierarchy of controls → application to maintenance (CBM/criticality) → who approves deviations → Management of Change.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk SMS: electronic risk-assessment library with job-specific templates, PTW integration, and a criticality-coded PMS; CBM data (vibration, LO analysis) feeds interval decisions within the class-approved machinery survey arrangement — the CE reviews the criticality register at each internal audit.

4
Contents of the training manual.
SOLASLSAFIRE

15-Second Answer

Two statutory training manuals exist. LSA training manual (SOLAS III/35): instructions and information, in easily understood terms and illustrated wherever possible, on the ship's life-saving appliances and the best survival methods — kept in each crew mess/recreation room. Fire training manual (SOLAS II-2/15.2.3): instructions on fire safety practice, fire-fighting operations and the ship's systems — same accessibility rule. Both must be ship-specific, and any part may be provided as audio-visual aids instead of text.

60-Second Answer

SOLAS III/35 (LSA training manual) — contents include, for the ship's actual equipment: donning of lifejackets, immersion suits and anti-exposure suits; muster at assigned stations; boarding, launching and clearing survival craft and rescue boats, including use of marine evacuation systems where fitted; method of launching from within the craft; release from launching appliances; use and deployment of protection in launching areas; illumination in launching areas; use of all survival equipment (rations, water, pyrotechnics, EPIRB/SART); use of detection equipment; assistance-rendering with radio LSA; drogues; engine and accessories of motor lifeboats; recovery of craft; hazards of exposure, hypothermia and first-aid; best use of survival craft facilities for survival; methods of retrieval including helicopter rescue strops/baskets and breeches buoy; and all other functions in the muster list and emergency instructions. SOLAS II-2/15.2.3 (fire training manual) — the fire-safety operational counterpart: general fire safety practice and precautions (smoking, electrical hazards, flammables, common hazards), general instructions on fire-fighting activities and procedures — reporting a fire, use of manual call points — meaning of alarms, operation and use of fire-fighting systems/appliances, operation and use of fire doors and fire/smoke dampers, and escape systems and appliances. Companions, not substitutes: the maintenance manuals (III/36 for LSA; II-2/14.2.2 fire maintenance plan) cover upkeep, while the training manuals cover use; drills (III/19) are where the manual's content is exercised.

Governing instrument

SOLAS III/35 (LSA training manual) and SOLAS II-2/15.2.3 (fire training manual) — contents as those regulations specify, ship-specific in detail.

Memory Skeleton

  • LSA manual = wear it, board it, launch it, survive in it, get rescued from it — for this ship's specific gear.
  • Fire manual = prevent, report, respond, operate systems, escape.
  • Format: easily understood, illustrated, ship-specific; A/V aids acceptable in lieu; a copy in every crew mess/recreation room (or each cabin).

Trap point

'The training manual' singular is the trap — there are two statutory training manuals (LSA III/35; fire II-2/15.2.3), and SOLAS III/36 maintenance instructions are a different document again.

CE Oral Tip (Paul): First disambiguate — "two statutory training manuals: LSA (III/35) and fire (II-2/15.2.3)" — that alone beats most candidates. Then the skeleton (wear-board-launch-survive-rescue / prevent-report-respond-operate-escape) with two or three concrete items each, the location rule (every mess/recreation room), and the trap distinction from the maintenance manuals. Reciting a memorised list without knowing it must be ship-specific is the common shortfall.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The engine department's emergency duties (fire parties, emergency generator, fire pumps, quick-closing valves) must be reflected in the ship-specific fire training manual; the CE checks his systems are correctly described after any modification — an outdated manual is a PSC/audit finding.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Where must the training manual be kept?" A: In each crew mess room and recreation room, or in each cabin (III/35.2). Q: "Training manual vs maintenance manual?" A: Use vs upkeep — III/35 vs III/36 for LSA; II-2/15.2.3 vs the II-2/14 maintenance plan for fire. Q: "Can it be a video?" A: Yes — any part may be provided as audio-visual aids in lieu of the manual.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Merging the two manuals into one vague "safety manual"; not knowing the location rule; confusing training with maintenance manuals; generic contents with no ship-specific requirement; no link to drills where the content is exercised.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

LSA training manual: SOLAS III/35; fire training manual: II-2/15.2.3; maintenance: III/36 / II-2/14; drills: III/19 (abandon-ship and fire drill monthly for cargo-ship crew, within 24 h of port departure if >25% crew changed).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Lifeboat-drill fatalities (on-load release accidents across multiple flags) exposed crews trained on generic material rather than their own hooks — driving both the ship-specific manual emphasis and the LSA release-gear amendments.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Paul: Training manual contents → where kept → LSA vs fire manuals → training vs maintenance manual → drill requirements → who updates it after equipment change.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: both manuals ship-specific in the crew mess and electronically in the document system; engine-room emergency-equipment sections (EDG, emergency fire pump, quick-closing valves, CO₂ release) verified by the CE at internal audit and after any system modification.

What are the safety manuals kept onboard?
ISMSOLASMARPOL

15-Second Answer

Group them by origin: (1) SMS documentation under ISM Code Sec. 11 — safety & environmental policy, procedures manuals, shipboard operations and emergency-preparedness manuals; (2) statutory SOLAS manuals — LSA training manual (III/35), LSA maintenance instructions (III/36), fire training manual (II-2/15.2.3), fire safety operational booklet (II-2/16.2), maintenance plan (II-2/14); (3) MARPOL/emergency plans — SOPEP/SMPEP (Annex I Reg 37 / Annex II Reg 17), garbage management plan (Annex V), VOC plan where applicable; (4) cargo/operations manuals — Cargo Securing Manual (VI/5.6 & VII/5), stability booklet, loading manual; plus SSP under ISPS (restricted).

60-Second Answer

A CE-level answer surveys the statutory manual landscape rather than reciting a random list. SMS family (ISM Code): the company's documented system — policies, shipboard procedures, planned maintenance documentation, emergency preparedness (Sec. 8), all controlled documents under Sec. 11 with current versions onboard and obsolete ones removed. SOLAS family: the two training manuals (LSA III/35, fire II-2/15.2.3), their maintenance counterparts (III/36 instructions for onboard maintenance; II-2/14 maintenance plan), the fire safety operational booklet (II-2/16.2), damage control plans/booklet (II-1/19), and for the engine room the engine-room manuals and UMS documentation the Administration accepts. MARPOL family: SOPEP or SMPEP, Garbage Management Plan, Ballast Water Management Plan (BWM Convention Reg B-1), SEEMP (Annex VI Reg 26). Cargo family: Cargo Securing Manual, grain loading manual where applicable, stability/loading manuals approved by the Administration or class. Each manual has an owner, an approval basis (flag, class, or company) and a controlled-document status in the SMS — that framing is what distinguishes a management-level answer.

Governing instrument

ISM Code Sec. 11 (SMS documentation); SOLAS III/35–36 and II-2/14–16 (training and maintenance manuals, fire safety operational booklet); MARPOL manuals — SOPEP (Annex I Reg 37), SMPEP (Annex II Reg 17), SEEMP (Annex VI Reg 26).

Memory Skeleton

  • Four families: SMS (ISM) · SOLAS (train/maintain/operate) · MARPOL (pollution plans) · Cargo (securing/stability).
  • Each manual = owner + approval basis + document control.
  • Ship-specific wherever the regulation says so (training manuals, SOPEP, CSM, stability booklet).

Trap point

Merging SMS manuals with statutory manuals — and inside SOLAS, confusing the fire training manual (II-2/15.2.3) with the fire safety operational booklet (II-2/16.2) is the classic slip.

⚖ Regulatory References
ISM Code Sec. 11Documentation — SMS document control; valid documents onboard, obsolete promptly removed
SOLAS III/35, III/36LSA training manual; instructions for onboard maintenance of LSA
SOLAS II-2/14, 15.2.3, 16.2Fire maintenance plan; fire training manual; fire safety operational booklet
MARPOL Annex I Reg 37 / Annex II Reg 17SOPEP / SMPEP shipboard pollution emergency plans
MARPOL Annex V Reg 10 / Annex VI Reg 26Garbage management plan; SEEMP
SOLAS VI/5.6 & VII/5Cargo Securing Manual — approved by the Administration
CE Oral Tip (Paul): Don't dump a list — give the four-family structure first, then one or two examples per family with their regulation. Paul often follows with "who approves that manual?" — know that CSM and stability booklet are flag/class-approved, SOPEP flag-approved, SMS manuals company-controlled under ISM document control.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE is document-owner for engine-room SMS procedures, the maintenance plan entries for fire and LSA machinery items, SEEMP Part measures affecting machinery operation, and the engine-room pages of SOPEP (bunkering, transfer procedures). Audit findings frequently trace to obsolete manual revisions still in circulation.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is SOPEP an ISM document?" A: It is a statutory MARPOL plan, flag-approved — but it is also controlled within the SMS. Q: "Fire training manual vs fire safety operational booklet?" A: Training manual (II-2/15.2.3) teaches the crew; the operational booklet (II-2/16.2) contains information and instructions for the safe operation of the ship and cargo-handling in relation to fire safety. Q: "Where is the SSP kept?" A: Restricted access under ISPS — not open in the mess room.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Unstructured lists; confusing training with maintenance manuals; not knowing the approval authority for each manual; forgetting BWMP and SEEMP as newer statutory plans; claiming the SSP is freely available.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

III/35 & III/36 (LSA train/maintain); II-2/14, 15.2.3, 16.2 (fire maintain/train/operate); Annex I Reg 37 (SOPEP); Annex II Reg 17 (SMPEP); Annex VI Reg 26 (SEEMP); ISM Sec. 11 (document control).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

PSC concentrated inspection campaigns repeatedly find outdated SOPEP contact lists (Annex I Reg 37 requires current coastal-State contacts) — a small document-control failure that becomes a detainable deficiency in a real spill.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Paul: Safety manuals onboard → who approves each → training vs maintenance manual → SOPEP contents → document control under ISM → what happens to obsolete documents.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: electronic document management with controlled master list; paper copies of SOPEP, training manuals and muster documentation at stations; CE verifies engine-room manual revisions at each internal audit.

6
What are the ISM manuals? Describe the SMS documentation structure.
ISMSMS

15-Second Answer

ISM Code Sec. 11.3: SMS documentation is kept in a form the Company considers most effective, typically a Safety Management Manual. In practice it is a hierarchy: Level 1 — policy manual (safety & environmental protection policy, Sec. 2; company responsibility/authority, Sec. 3; DPA, Sec. 4); Level 2 — procedures manuals (shipboard operations Sec. 7, emergency preparedness Sec. 8, reporting Sec. 9, maintenance Sec. 10); Level 3 — work instructions, checklists, forms and records — all under Sec. 11 document control.

60-Second Answer

The Code deliberately does not prescribe manual titles — Sec. 1.4 lists the functional requirements the documentation must deliver: policies, instructions and procedures for safe operation, defined levels of authority and communication, reporting of accidents and non-conformities, emergency preparedness, and internal audit/review procedures. The conventional company structure maps those onto a pyramid. The policy level states commitments and the organisation — masters' overriding authority (Sec. 5.2), DPA link to highest management (Sec. 4). The procedure level holds the fleet manuals: shipboard operations (key operations and special operations, mooring, bunkering, watchkeeping), emergency preparedness with drill programmes (Sec. 8), maintenance system procedures including critical equipment identification (Sec. 10.3), and personnel/familiarisation procedures (Sec. 6). The working level is the ship-specific material: checklists, standing orders, forms, records — the objective evidence auditors sample. Sec. 11.2 requires valid documents at all relevant locations, review and approval by authorised personnel, and prompt removal of obsolete documents; Sec. 12 closes the loop with internal audits and management review feeding revisions back down the pyramid.

Governing instrument

ISM Code Secs. 1.4 (functional requirements — mandatory in substance) and 11.3 (form free); certification under Sec. 13 (DOC/SMC cycles).

Memory Skeleton

  • Pyramid: Policy → Procedures → Work instructions/records.
  • Sections to anchor: 2 policy · 4 DPA · 5 master's authority · 7 shipboard ops · 8 emergency · 10 maintenance · 11 documentation · 12 audit & review.
  • Form is free (11.3), function is mandatory (1.4).

Trap point

Claiming ISM prescribes a manual structure is the fail — Sec. 11.3 leaves the form free; what is mandatory is function (Sec. 1.4's functional requirements), evidenced at audit.

QB4_J · Q6 · v1.0

Difference between ISM survey and ISM audit.
ISMAUDIT

15-Second Answer

Strictly, ISM verification is by audit, not survey. An audit is a systematic, independent examination of whether the safety management system conforms to the ISM Code and is effectively implemented — it produces DOC/SMC certification and non-conformity findings. A survey is the physical examination of the ship's structure, machinery and equipment against convention/class requirements — it produces statutory and class certificates. Different object (system vs ship), different verifier competence, different output.

60-Second Answer

The trap in this question is the premise: there is no "ISM survey" in the Code — verification of the SMS is done by audit (external audits by the flag Administration or its RO for the DOC and SMC, and the Company's own internal audits under Sec. 12). The auditor samples documents, records and people — interviews, evidence of drills, non-conformity close-outs — asking "does the system work?". A surveyor, by contrast, examines the physical ship: hull thickness, boiler internals, LSA/FFA condition, machinery trials — asking "does the ship comply?". The two interact: a surveyor finding poorly maintained equipment may report objective evidence that Sec. 10 (maintenance) is not implemented, which can trigger an additional ISM audit; conversely an audit non-conformity in maintenance procedures may prompt closer survey attention. Certification also differs — audits sustain the DOC (annual verification) and SMC (intermediate verification), while surveys sustain Safety Construction, Safety Equipment, Load Line, MARPOL certificates and class. A major non-conformity — a serious threat to safety or serious lack of effective implementation — can invalidate the SMC and thereby detain the ship even when every survey certificate is clean.

Governing instrument

ISM Code Sec. 13 with Res. A.1118(30) (revised ISM implementation guidelines); the RO Code for organisations performing the audits; physical survey regimes sit separately under class/statutory rules.

Memory Skeleton

  • Audit = management system (paper, people, practice) → DOC/SMC.
  • Survey = physical ship (steel, machinery, equipment) → statutory/class certificates.
  • Bridge: bad hardware is objective evidence of a Sec. 10 system failure.

Trap point

Saying 'ISM survey' is itself the trap — ISM verification is by audit of the system; surveys examine the physical ship; a major non-conformity is defined by the Code, not by the surveyor's discretion.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Open by correcting the premise politely — "ISM works by audit; surveys are the physical-examination regime" — then give the object/verifier/output triple. Simon (LNG background) likes the interaction case: a surveyor's finding as objective evidence in an audit. Finish with the major non-conformity definition; that is his standard follow-up.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE faces both: surveyors for CSM/boiler/shaft surveys and auditors for the SMS. Presenting maintenance records that satisfy the auditor (system evidence) and machinery that satisfies the surveyor (physical condition) are distinct preparations — conflating them leaves one exposed.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Define major non-conformity." A: An identifiable deviation that poses a serious threat to the safety of personnel or the ship or a serious risk to the environment and requires immediate corrective action; includes lack of effective and systematic implementation of a Code requirement. Q: "Can the ship sail with a major NC?" A: The SMC/DOC is affected — the major NC must be downgraded to an NC with an agreed corrective-action plan before certification continues per flag procedures. Q: "Who audits the Company office?" A: Flag or RO for the DOC; the Company audits itself internally under Sec. 12.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Accepting the "ISM survey" premise; not knowing DOC vs SMC audit cycles; unable to define major non-conformity; missing that internal audits are a Code requirement, not company goodwill.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

DOC annual verification; SMC intermediate between 2nd and 3rd anniversary; both certificates 5-year validity; interim DOC 12 months, interim SMC 6 months (extendable by flag).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Post-casualty investigations (e.g. multiple MAIB/ATSB reports) repeatedly show ships with valid survey certificates but hollow SMS implementation — the gap this question probes, and the reason audits sample effectiveness, not paperwork existence.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk: annual internal audit per ship plus external SMC audits; CE prepares maintenance-system evidence (PMS compliance, critical-equipment tests) for audits and physical readiness for class/statutory surveys — two parallel checklists.

QB4_J · Q7 · v1.0

What are the different types of leadership?
STCWHELM

15-Second Answer

Classic styles: autocratic (leader decides — right in emergencies), democratic/participative (team consulted — best for planning and buy-in), laissez-faire/delegative (leader hands over — only with competent, motivated specialists). Broader models: transactional (task-and-reward, procedures and compliance), transformational (vision and development of people), and situational leadership — matching style to the follower's competence and commitment. STCW anchors this under management-level competence: leadership and managerial skills, Table A-III/2 (HELM).

60-Second Answer

The examiner wants two things: the taxonomy, and the judgement of when each style is correct in the engine room. Autocratic: engine-room fire, blackout recovery, flooding — one voice, closed-loop orders, no debate; the cost is suppressed input, so it must be temporary. Democratic: planning a main-engine unit overhaul or a risk assessment — drawing on the 3/E's and fitters' knowledge produces better plans and ownership; the cost is time. Laissez-faire: a proven electro-technical officer on a familiar task — micromanaging him wastes the CE and demotivates the specialist; the failure mode is abdication when competence was overestimated. Situational leadership formalises the choice: direct low-competence/high-commitment newcomers, coach, support, then delegate as competence grows — which is precisely watchkeeper development under an engine-room training programme. Transactional leadership underpins the SMS itself — procedures, compliance, corrective action — while transformational behaviour (explaining why, developing juniors, modelling standards) is what turns a compliant department into a resilient one. The STCW hook: Reg III/2 and Section A-III/2 require "leadership and managerial skills" including task and workload management, resource management, decision-making techniques — delivered through HELM(M) courses, and assessable in the oral.

Governing instrument

STCW Table A-III/2 (management level) — leadership and managerial skills competence, introduced by the 2010 Manila amendments; company HELM(M) training implementing it.

Memory Skeleton

  • A-D-L: Autocratic = emergency · Democratic = planning · Laissez-faire = proven specialist.
  • Transactional = run the system; Transformational = grow the people.
  • Situational = match style to competence & commitment — direct → coach → support → delegate.

Trap point

Naming one 'best' style is the fail — the management-level answer is situational fit (direct → coach → support → delegate), with autocratic decision-making correct in emergencies.

STCW Sec. A-III/1Operational level — application of leadership and teamworking skills
STCW Sec. A-VIII/2Watchkeeping principles — resource management context
ISM Code Sec. 6Resources and personnel — Company's duty to ensure competent, motivated crew
CE Oral Tip (Paul): Name three classic styles with one engine-room situation each — emergency, overhaul planning, trusted specialist — then close with situational leadership as "the answer to which style: it depends on the person and the task". Paul's follow-up is usually "which style during a blackout?" — autocratic, closed-loop communication, then debrief democratically afterwards.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE's daily reality is switching styles within one watch: directive during manoeuvring stations, participative in the morning planning meeting, delegative with the 2/E's maintenance domain — and knowing the switch is deliberate is what A-III/2 assesses.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Which is the best leadership style?" A: None universally — situational; the skill is diagnosis. Q: "Difference between leadership and management?" A: Management administers systems (planning, organising, controlling); leadership influences people toward the goal — the CE needs both. Q: "Where does STCW require this?" A: Management level Table A-III/2 — leadership and managerial skills; HELM(M) is the standard delivery.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Reciting styles with no shipboard application; claiming democratic is always best; not linking to STCW/HELM; missing that laissez-faire fails when competence is overestimated.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

STCW Table A-III/2 (management), A-III/1 (operational teamworking); situational model: 4 modes — direct, coach, support, delegate.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Investigations from Herald of Free Enterprise to modern engine-room fires cite failures of challenge-and-response and one-man error chains — the human-element findings that put leadership, teamwork and resource management into STCW via the 2010 Manila amendments.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Paul: Types of leadership → which in an emergency → leadership vs management → how you develop a weak 4/E → where STCW requires it → ERM link.

What does MLC say about war risk? Can a seafarer refuse to sail into a war zone?
MLCWAR RISK

15-Second Answer

Answer honestly in two layers. MLC text: the 2018 amendments (in force 26 Dec 2020) amended Standards A2.1 and A2.2 so that a seafarer's employment agreement continues to have effect and wages and entitlements continue to be paid throughout captivity by pirates or armed robbers, even after the SEA would otherwise expire — and repatriation cannot lapse during captivity. Right to refuse a war zone: that right is NOT in the MLC text — it lives in collective bargaining agreements (IBF/ITF Warlike Operations Areas): typically the right to refuse to sail, repatriation at company cost, and bonus/doubled compensation for those who sail.

60-Second Answer

Candidates who claim "MLC gives the right to refuse war zones" fail on precision. What the Convention itself does: Regulation 2.1/Standard A2.1 (SEA) and Standard A2.2 (wages) as amended in 2018 address the piracy/armed-robbery captivity gap exposed off Somalia — before the amendment, owners could treat a hijacked seafarer's contract as expired mid-captivity. Now the SEA continues to have effect while the seafarer is held captive on or off the ship, wages and allotments continue, and entitlement to repatriation (Reg 2.5) survives. Definitions of piracy and armed robbery follow UNCLOS Art. 101 and IMO usage. What the Convention does not do: grant a general statutory right to refuse entry into warlike areas. That protection is contractual — IBF and national CBAs designate Warlike Operations Areas and High Risk Areas; within them seafarers typically get the right to refuse to sail with repatriation at the owner's expense, compensation bonuses (commonly 100% of basic wage for the transit period), and death/disability doubled. The employer's overarching MLC duties still bite: Reg 4.3 (occupational safety and health — risk assessment applies to security threats), Reg 2.5 and Standard A2.5.2 (financial security), and flag-State war-risk insurance requirements. So the exam-safe formula: MLC 2018 = wages and SEA protected during captivity; refusal rights = CBA layer, not MLC text.

Governing instrument

MLC 2018 amendments to Stds A2.1 and A2.2 (in force 26 Dec 2020) — SEA and wages continue through piracy captivity; refusal-to-sail rights arise from CBA/IBF Warlike Operations Area machinery, not the MLC text.

Memory Skeleton

  • MLC 2018 amendments: SEA + wages + repatriation continue during piracy/armed-robbery captivity (A2.1, A2.2, in force 26 Dec 2020).
  • Refusal right = CBA (IBF/ITF Warlike Operations Areas), not MLC text.
  • Supporting MLC duties: Reg 4.3 OSH risk assessment; Reg 2.5 repatriation.

Trap point

Claiming the MLC text grants a right to refuse to sail into a war-risk area is the trap — that right lives in the CBA/IBF Warlike Operations Area machinery; MLC 2018 secures the SEA and wages during captivity.

Transits of designated High Risk Areas (Gulf of Aden, Red Sea) put the CE inside the ship-security plan: engine-room hardening, safe-muster/citadel readiness with independent comms and machinery control considerations, fuel and speed planning for group transits — the technical face of the same risk the MLC amendments address.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

QB4_J · Q9 · v1.0

What is seafarer abandonment? What protection does MLC provide?
MLCABANDONMENT

15-Second Answer

Under MLC Standard A2.5.2 (2014 amendments, in force 18 Jan 2017) a seafarer is abandoned when the shipowner: (a) fails to cover the cost of repatriation; or (b) leaves the seafarer without necessary maintenance and support; or (c) otherwise unilaterally severs ties, including failing to pay contractual wages for at least two months. Every ship must carry a financial-security certificate (insurance/P&I) giving seafarers direct access to cover: up to four months outstanding wages and entitlements, repatriation costs, and essential needs (food, accommodation, water, fuel, medical care) until arrival home.

60-Second Answer

Abandonment is the ugly end of shipowner failure — crews left unpaid on arrested or laid-up ships for months. The 2014 amendments closed the gap with a compulsory financial-security system. Definition (A2.5.2.2): the three limbs above; "necessary maintenance and support" means adequate food, accommodation, drinking water, essential fuel for survival on board, and necessary medical care (A2.5.2.5). The instrument: a certificate or other documentary evidence of financial security issued by the provider (typically the P&I club) must be carried on board and posted where seafarers can see it, in English, covering all seafarers on the ship. The cover (A2.5.2.9): outstanding wages and other entitlements limited to four months, all reasonable repatriation expenses, and essential needs from abandonment until arrival home; the seafarer has direct access to the provider — no need to sue the owner first. The system sits alongside Regulation 2.5 repatriation proper (entitlement after maximum service periods under 12 months, at no cost to the seafarer for standard circumstances) with the fallback chain when the owner defaults: flag State → port State → labour-supplying State, with the paying State entitled to recover from the owner including by ship arrest. Cases are logged in the joint ILO/IMO database on abandonment of seafarers — cite it; examiners know incidents from it. Parallel financial security for death/long-term disability sits in Standard A4.2 from the same amendment package.

Governing instrument

MLC Std A2.5.2 (2014 amendments, in force 18 Jan 2017) — abandonment definition and compulsory financial security, certificates carried and posted onboard; the ILO/IMO abandonment database.

Memory Skeleton

  • Three limbs: no repatriation · no maintenance & support · ties severed / ≥2 months unpaid.
  • Certificate on board, posted, English — direct access to provider.
  • Cover: 4 months wages + repatriation + essential needs.
  • Fallback: flag → port → labour-supplying State; ILO/IMO database.

Trap point

Reducing abandonment to failed repatriation is the trap — the definition has three limbs including ≥2 months' unpaid wages, and the financial security is capped at four months' wages.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Lead with the three-limb definition and the two-months wage trigger, then the certificate and the four-month cap — those four numbers-and-limbs are the whole answer. Simon's follow-up is reliably "where would I see proof on board?" — the financial-security certificate posted in a conspicuous place, alongside the MLC certificate and DMLC.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

On an arrested or laid-up ship the CE keeps generators, fresh water and sanitation alive — "essential fuel for survival on board" in A2.5.2.5 is literally his fuel calculation; knowing the crew's direct-access rights also makes the CE the informed senior officer when wages stop.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is the wage cover unlimited?" A: No — outstanding wages and entitlements limited to four months (A2.5.2.9). Q: "Two months of what triggers abandonment?" A: Failure to pay contractual wages for a period of at least two months under the severed-ties limb. Q: "Who repatriates if the owner and flag both fail?" A: The port State or labour-supplying State may arrange it and recover costs from the owner — including detention/arrest of the ship.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

2014 amendments in force 18 Jan 2017; wage trigger ≥2 months; cover cap 4 months wages; max service period before repatriation entitlement <12 months; certificates posted in English.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

The ILO/IMO database recorded record abandonment numbers in the 2020s (hundreds of ships per year at peak); long-running cases such as MT Iba (crew unpaid ~32 months, Umm Al Quwain) illustrate exactly the limbs and the P&I direct-access remedy the amendments created.
