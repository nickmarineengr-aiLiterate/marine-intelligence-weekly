🎯 60-Second AnswerGood morning, Examiner. I would like to narrate an incident involving a localized electrical fire in the main engine room ventilation VFD panel onboard a Maersk G-class container vessel. While under peak electrical load during a tropical transit, the ECR panel triggered a Group Automation Alarm indicating a VFD earth fault, followed immediately by a smoke detection alarm in the purifiers flat nearby. As Chief Engineer, I immediately stood up the emergency response team, verified that the automated safety interlocks had tripped the upstream circuit breaker, and ordered a manual isolation of the distribution board. The ship's fire squad made entry using appropriate PPE and EEBD, confirmed a class C fire within the enclosure, and extinguished it using a portable CO_2 extinguisher. We initiated a comprehensive Root Cause Analysis (RCA) under our SMS, identifying dielectric degradation in the DC-link capacitor bank due to harmonic heating. This necessitated short-term containment measures via auxiliary fans to maintain positive engine room pressure, followed by a permanent component modification and revised thermographic assessment protocols across the fleet.

1. Chronological Sequence of the Incident

  • Detection & Alarm: At 1420 hours, during transit through the Malacca Strait, the engine room monitoring system registered a high-priority "VFD Cubicle Earth Fault." Concurrently, the optical smoke detectors in the auxiliary machinery space activated the main fire alarm panel on the bridge and ECR.
  • Initial Assessment & Isolation: Smoke was observed emanating from the Port ER Ventilation Fan VFD panel. The duty engineer immediately attempted to trip the fan remotely, but the control loop was burnt out. I ordered the manual tripping of the 440V main feeder circuit breaker at the Main Switchboard (MSB) to electrically isolate the unit.
  • Emergency Containment: The engine room was placed under emergency status. The Bridge team was notified, and the vessel's speed was adjusted to manage apparent wind and smoke dispersion. The ER ventilation was temporarily configured to extract smoke via the starboard systems.
  • Extinguishment: The fire squad donned full firefighter's outfits and entered with a 5 kg CO_2 extinguisher. After ensuring complete electrical isolation through lock-out/tag-out (LOTO), the panel door was carefully cracked open, and the extinguishing medium was injected. The fire was declared extinguished within 8 minutes of the initial alarm.

2. Root Cause Analysis (RCA) & Technical Investigation

A detailed investigation was conducted once the panel cooled down:

  • Primary Cause: The intermediate DC-link capacitor bank inside the VFD suffered a catastrophic dielectric breakdown.
  • Contributing Factors: High ambient engine room temperatures (>45^circC) combined with unmitigated harmonic distortion (THD) from neighboring non-linear loads accelerated the thermal degradation of the capacitor's polypropylene film.
  • Escalation: The failure resulted in a localized short circuit, melting the surrounding busbar insulation and igniting the plastic casing of the adjacent control cards.

Root Cause vs Proximate Cause — the Distinction the Insurer Applies

The analysis above ends at the root cause: the engineering answer to “why did this component fail?” — here dielectric breakdown of the DC-link capacitor bank, driven by ambient heat and harmonic loading. That is the finding that drives the corrective action and the PMS change, and it is what the ISM Code Section 9 investigation exists to produce.

Proximate cause is a different test, applied by a different party. It is the insurance and legal question of the dominant, effective cause of the loss, and it decides which policy responds. Under the Marine Insurance Act 1906, s.55(1), the insurer “is liable for any loss proximately caused by a peril insured against” — and not for one that is not. On this incident the root cause is capacitor dielectric failure, while the proximate cause is the fire, a named peril under the hull policy, which is why H&M engages at all. Had the same failure been traced to a documented want of due diligence in maintenance, the proximate-cause analysis would put the claim outside cover even though the root cause was identical.

One line if pressed: the root cause is what I fix; the proximate cause is what the underwriter pays on.

3. Short-term Containment & Permanent Corrective Actions

  • Short-term Action: To maintain positive pressure in the engine room and satisfy the main engine combustion air requirements, the port fan was bypassed, and the standby starboard axial fan was run at maximum capacity.
  • Fleet Rectification: A technical report was raised to Maersk fleet managers. The failed capacitor banks were replaced with higher thermal-grade, explosion-protected variants.
  • Preventive Measure: The vessel's Planned Maintenance System (PMS) was updated to mandate monthly infrared thermographic checks on all high-power VFD enclosures, rather than the previous six-monthly interval.

VGM (Verified Gross Mass) — who issues, who verifies, where it is mentioned, and the two methods of determination
SOLASCargo
⏱ 15-Second AnswerThe Verified Gross Mass (VGM) of a packed container must be obtained and communicated by the shipper. It is verified against the ship/terminal planning system by the Master and the terminal operator before the container is loaded. It is mandated under SOLAS Chapter VI, Regulation 2. Two methods are permitted: Method 1 (weighing the packed container) and Method 2 (weighing all contents/packing materials and adding tare mass).
🎯 60-Second AnswerGood morning, Examiner. VGM stands for Verified Gross Mass, the mandatory declaration of a packed container's total weight introduced after the SOLAS amendments effective 1 July 2016, following incidents of container stack collapses and mis-declared weights. The shipper is the party responsible for obtaining and declaring the VGM — this obligation cannot be delegated to the carrier or forwarder. Verification that a valid VGM has been received happens jointly through the Master or his representative and the terminal representative, who confirm the container will not be loaded aboard without it. The requirement is contained in SOLAS Chapter VI, Part A, Regulation 2, supported by IMO's MSC.1/Circ.1475 Guidelines. There are two permitted methods: Method 1 — weighing the entire packed container using calibrated and certified weighing equipment; and Method 2 — weighing all individual packages, cargo items, and dunnage/securing material, then adding the container's own tare mass, using a certified method approved by the competent authority of the state in which packing was completed.

1. Issuing and Verifying Responsibility

  • Issuing/Declaring Party: The shipper (named on the bill of lading) is solely responsible for obtaining the VGM and providing it in the shipping instructions, in advance of the ship's stowage plan being prepared. This obligation is non-transferable.
  • Verifying Party: The Master (or his representative) and the terminal representative jointly ensure a signed VGM is on file before a container is loaded. If VGM is not provided, SOLAS requires the container is not loaded onto the ship.

2. Regulatory Location

  • SOLAS Chapter VI, Part A, Regulation 2: The primary source — packed containers shall not be loaded unless the shipper has provided the VGM.
  • IMO MSC.1/Circ.1475: Guidelines regarding the VGM of a container carrying cargo, detailing the two permitted methods and documentation format.
  • CTU Code (Code of Practice for Packing of Cargo Transport Units): Provides supplementary practical guidance on weighing and packing.

3. Two Methods of Determining VGM

A. Method 1 — Weighing the Packed Container

The entire packed and sealed container is weighed as a single unit using calibrated, certified weighing equipment (weighbridge, lifting equipment with load cells, or terminal gantry scales) that meets the accuracy standards set by the competent authority.

B. Method 2 — Sum of Constituent Weights

All packages and cargo items, including pallets, dunnage, and other securing/packing material to be packed in the container, are individually weighed, and the container's own tare mass (from the CSC Safety Approval Plate) is added to the sum. This method must be carried out using a certified method approved by the competent authority.

Csc convention full explanation, including testing and certification, type of containers and difference in testing reefer containers
Surveys
⏱ 15-Second AnswerThe International Convention for Safe Containers (CSC 1972) establishes uniform structural regulations to ensure the safety of human life during the handling and transport of freight containers. It mandates strict manufacturing design type testing, independent classification approval, and the permanent attachment of a Safety Approval Plate. For specialized units like reefer containers, structural testing is paired with localized insulation, frame modifications, and thermal integrity checks to handle asymmetric loads and weight distribution from integrated refrigeration units.
🎯 60-Second AnswerGood morning, Examiner. The CSC Convention was adopted by the IMO to achieve two main goals: maintaining high safety standards for container transport through formalized structural requirements and facilitating international container traffic via uniform safety test procedures. Under the convention, every container must undergo design type testing—including stacking, lifting, transverse racking, and longitudinal restraint testing—before receiving a CSC Safety Approval Plate. Containers are checked under either a Periodic Examination Scheme (PES), starting 5 years after manufacture and repeating every 30 months, or an Approved Continuous Examination Programme (ACEP). For reefer containers, the structural testing must account for the offset center of gravity and localized frame stress caused by the heavy integrated compressor unit, requiring specialized corner post and rear-frame deflection criteria that differ from standard dry vans.

1. Objectives and Scope of the CSC Convention

The CSC 1972 convention maintains two fundamental pillars:

  • Safety of Life: Formulating structural testing criteria to prevent catastrophic container failures during sea transport, crane lifts, and terminal stacking.
  • Harmonized International Trade: Creating uniform global regulations so that a container approved in one signatory state can move seamlessly through foreign ports without redundant structural inspections.

2. Testing Protocols for Design Type Approval

Before any container series enters service, a prototype must be subjected to rigorous structural tests witnessed by an authorized administration or classification society:

  • Lifting Tests:
  • Lifting from Top Corner Fittings: The container is loaded to 2 times R (where R is the maximum operating gross mass) and lifted vertically, ensuring no permanent structural deformation.
  • Lifting from Bottom Corner Fittings: Loaded to 2 times R with forces applied at specified angles (30^circ to the horizontal).
  • Stacking Test: Simulates the massive downward vertical loads encountered in a ship’s cellular guides. The container is subjected to a vertical load equivalent to the maximum allowable stack weight (typically rated up to 192,000 kg or 9-high stacking) distributed on the four corner posts.
  • Transverse Racking Test: Simulates the dynamic rolling forces of the ship. A dynamic lateral force of 150 kN is applied to the top corner fittings to test the frame's shear resistance.
  • Longitudinal Restraint (Dynamic Shock Test): Simulates acceleration shocks during rail/road shunting. The container floor structure is subjected to longitudinal thrusts equal to 2 g.

3. The CSC Safety Approval Plate & Maintenance Schemes

Every approved container must bear a permanent, fireproof Safety Approval Plate containing:

  • Country of approval and approval reference.
  • Date of manufacture.
  • Maximum gross weight (R) and allowable stacking weight for 1.8 g.
  • Racking test load value.
  • Maintenance Schemes:
  • Periodic Examination Scheme (PES): The first inspection must take place within 5 years of manufacture. Subsequent structural examinations must be conducted at intervals not exceeding 30 months. The next examination date (NED) is stamped directly on the plate.
  • Approved Continuous Examination Programme (ACEP): A dynamic scheme used by large lines like Maersk, where containers are continuously inspected by depot staff during every repair, wash, or turnaround cycle. The plate features an ACEP registration number, removing the need for an NED stamp.

1. Regulatory Origin and Scope of application

⏱ 15-Second AnswerThe Multimodal Transportation of Goods Act provides a legal framework for transporting cargo across multiple modes of transport (e.g., sea, rail, road) under a single contract and a single Multimodal Transport Document (MTD). It establishes a single point of liability for the Multimodal Transport Operator (MTO), standardizing cargo claims while shifting structural risks between owners, charterers, and cargo interests.
🎯 60-Second AnswerGood morning, Examiner. The Multimodal Transportation of Goods Act—historically established in India in 1993 and aligned with international shipping rules—governs the transport of goods from an inland origin to an international destination using at least two different modes of transport under a single contract. For shipowners and charterers operating as Multimodal Transport Operators (MTOs), this act introduces balanced commercial impacts. The primary advantage is commercial efficiency: it allows them to offer end-to-end logistics solutions, use a single Multimodal Transport Document (MTD), and optimize supply chain networks. The main disadvantage is the significant expansion of their financial liability, as the MTO accepts direct, continuous liability for cargo loss or damage occurring anywhere along the supply chain—whether at sea, in an inland rail yard, or during road transit—unless a specific statutory exclusion applies.

1. The Core Legal Concept of the Multimodal Act

  • Single Contract Framework: Traditionally, a shipper had to execute separate contracts with trucking firms, rail networks, port authorities, and ocean liners. The Multimodal Act simplifies this by introducing the Multimodal Transport Operator (MTO), who executes a single contract for the entire journey.
  • The Multimodal Transport Document (MTD): The MTO issues a single MTD that serves as a negotiable instrument, replacing individual bills of lading and consignment notes, and acting as proof of receipt of the goods.

2. Advantages for the Shipowner / Charterer (Operating as an MTO)

  • Comprehensive Logistics Offerings: Large container operators (such as Maersk) can expand beyond port-to-port shipping to provide end-to-end door-to-door logistics, capturing higher commercial margins.
  • Improved Asset Utilization: Integrated control over inland container depots (ICDs), rail networks, and ocean vessels allows for optimized container turnaround times, reducing empty container repositioning costs.
  • Simplified Claims Settlement: Standardized liability limits under the Act provide a predictable framework for handling cargo claims, avoiding complex jurisdictional disputes between different modes of transport.

3. Disadvantages for the Shipowner / Charterer (Operating as an MTO)

  • Expanded Liability Profile: The MTO assumes through-transport liability. If cargo is damaged during an inland trucking segment far from the ship, the carrier remains legally liable to the shipper, forcing them to pay the claim and then seek recovery from the third-party sub-contractor.
  • Increased Insurance Overhead: To cover through-transport liabilities across rail and road networks, operators must maintain specialized, expensive insurance cover (such as TT Clubs), which increases their operational overhead compared to standard port-to-port P&I cover.
  • Complex Network Management: The carrier faces operational exposure from inland logistical bottlenecks—such as rail strikes or highway infrastructure failures—that can trigger delays and cargo claims, even if their ocean vessels operate on schedule.

Where it comes from

  • Statute: section 4 of the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 (Act No. 24 of 2025), which received assent on 18 August 2025 and was brought into force on 15 March 2026. The Board is not new — it was established on 1 March 1959 under s.4 of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and the provision was carried forward. In the 2025 Act it is referred to throughout as the “Shipping Board”.
  • Rules: the National Shipping Board Rules, 2026 — G.S.R. 379(E) of 20 May 2026, Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways, made under s.6(1) and s.6(2)(a) read with s.4(8) and (9), in supersession of the National Shipping Board Rules, 1960. In force from the date of Gazette publication.

Composition — s.4(2)

  • Six members of Parliament — four elected from amongst themselves by members of the House of the People, two by members of the Council of States. Their term ends the moment they cease to be members of the House that elected them (s.4(3)).
  • Not exceeding sixteen other members, appointed by the Central Government to represent (i) the Central Government, (ii) ship owners, (iii) seafarers, and (iv) such other interests as it thinks should be represented — at least four of whom shall be women, and with an express proviso that the Board shall include an equal number of persons representing ship owners and seafarers.

Function — and the boundary that matters

Section 4(6) is short and worth knowing in its own words: the Board shall advise the Central Government “(a) on matters relating to Indian shipping including the development thereof; and (b) on such other matters arising out of this Act as the Central Government may refer to it for advice.” It has power to regulate its own procedure (s.4(7)).

It is an advisory body and nothing more. It issues no certificate, detains no ship, holds no inquiry and makes no rule. Regulation is DGMA’s. Under the 2026 Rules its decisions requiring action are recorded as recommendations forwarded to the Ministry — which is exactly the language of a body that advises.   • Term and continuity. Constituted for two years in the first instance and re-established every two years. That six-month cap is new, and it exists because gaps between Boards were a real problem.
  • Electronic communication. Notices and all communications to members may be sent by hand, speed post, or electronic means including e-mail as approved by the Board. This is the headline modernisation over the 1960 Rules.

It is also the cleanest example in Indian maritime law of the difference between an advisory body and a regulatory one, and examiners use it to test exactly that distinction.

⚠ Trap Questions

“Does the Board issue your Certificate of Competency?” — No. It advises; DGMA administers and certifies. “How many members?” — Up to 22: six MPs plus not more than sixteen others; say “up to”, because the Government appoints as many of the sixteen as it thinks fit. “Is the Chairperson an MP?” — Not necessarily; the Central Government nominates one of the members, of whatever category. “Which Act — 1958 or 2025?” — The 2025 Act, in force since 15 March 2026; the 1958 Act is repealed and is historical framing only. “Are the 1960 Rules still in force?” — No. The 2026 Rules supersede them and, under rule 1(2), come into force on the date of their publication in the Official Gazette; the notification is dated 20 May 2026 and the gazette copy is digitally signed 23 May 2026, so quote the notification date and the publication rule rather than treating the two as the same thing. “What happens if the Government forgets to reconstitute it?” — The existing Board carries on, but for a maximum of six months. “How many make a quorum?” — Eight — and note that an adjourned meeting is valid even without one.

✗ Common CE Failures

Describing the Board as a regulator, or crediting it with DGMA’s functions. Citing the 1958 Act or the 1960 Rules as current. Confusing it with the Seafarer’s Welfare Board under s.5, or with the Maritime State Development Council. Stating a fixed membership figure instead of a maximum. Above all: inventing a recent meeting, a date, or an outcome. The minutes are published; a fabricated resolution is checkable and fatal.

🔢 Numbers & Regulations to Memorise

Established 1 March 1959 under MS Act 1958 s.4; now MS Act 2025 s.4, Act 24 of 2025, assent 18 Aug 2025, in force 15 March 2026. National Shipping Board Rules, 2026 — G.S.R. 379(E), 20 May 2026. Composition: 6 + up to 16 = max 22; MPs 4 Lok Sabha + 2 Rajya Sabha; at least 4 women; ship owners and seafarers equal. Term 2 years; continuity cap 6 months; resignation effective at 30 days if not accepted sooner; removal after 3 consecutive absences; meets every 3 months; notice 15 days; extraordinary meeting on 5 members’ requisition within 15 days; quorum 8; working year 1 April to 31 March. Headquarters New Delhi.

⚓ Casualty Link

There is no casualty behind this question, and saying so is better than manufacturing one. The Board’s connection to safety is upstream and structural: it is where owner and seafarer representation reaches national policy before it becomes rule. The nearest thing to a “failure” in its own record is administrative and is minuted in plain sight — action points on a permanent office, on advance reconstitution of successive Boards, and on inviting the Maritime State Development Council were all carried as pending with the Ministry, with no update provided, over successive meetings.

🔗 Examiner Chain (Kochi MMD)

In the August 2026 sittings this arrived as a single ask covering both limbs — the Board and its recent meeting — which is itself the warning: the panel is checking whether you follow Indian maritime policy or only the conventions. Expect it to run into the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 transition, into DGMA’s role as the contrast, and possibly into Maritime Amrit Kaal Vision 2047, which is one of the Board’s standing agenda items.

🚢 On My Vessel (Maersk Container)

The honest CE answer is about the chain rather than the ship: your company’s association — INSA for Indian owners — sits on the Board, and the seafarer side is represented by NUSI and MUI, whose positions on manning, training and welfare reach the Ministry through it. If you sail under an Indian flag, that is the route by which policy affecting your CoC revalidation, your ship’s coastal licensing and your green-fuel obligations is shaped. If your ship is foreign-flagged, say that plainly and answer on the Indian regime you are certificated under.
