MARPOL Annex V latest amendments?
MARPOLSURVEY
15-Second AnswerRecent MARPOL Annex V amendments introduce stricter controls on garbage management, requiring a Garbage Management Plan for ships ≥ 100 GT and, since 1 May 2024 under Resolution MEPC.360(79), a Garbage Record Book for ships ≥ 100 GT (lowered from the former 400 GT threshold). It establishes a total ban on plastic discharges anywhere at sea and designates strict zero-discharge Special Areas.
60-Second AnswerMARPOL Annex V has been progressively tightened to eliminate marine plastic pollution and enhance garbage tracking. Under current regulations—reinforced by resolutions like MEPC.277(70) and subsequent revisions—plastics are completely banned from sea disposal globally. The compliance framework lowers operational thresholds, mandating a Garbage Management Plan for ships ≥ 100 GT and, since 1 May 2024 under Resolution MEPC.360(79), a Garbage Record Book (Parts I and II) for all ships ≥ 100 GT and passenger vessels — this replaced the earlier 400 GT GRB threshold. In strict Special Areas—such as the Baltic, North, Red, and Mediterranean Seas—discharges of all garbage, including cargo residues and food waste, are subject to zero-discharge limits or strict distance parameters.

Core Regulatory Shifts and Classifications

The focus of modern MARPOL Annex V amendments is to enforce rigorous record-keeping and eliminate synthetic discharges:

  • Structural Categorization: Garbage is classified into strict categories (Categories A through K), splitting operational plastics, food wastes, domestic wastes, cooking oil, incinerator ashes, and cargo residues.
  • Absolute Plastic Prohibition: Plastics of any form—including synthetic ropes, fishing nets, and plastic garbage bags—are subject to a permanent, global ban and can never be discharged into the sea under any circumstances.
  • E-Garbage Record Book (E-GRB): Electronic logging of garbage operations is fully harmonized, allowing entries to be managed via certified digital software frameworks with secure electronic signatures.

Tonnage Thresholds & Mandatory Shipboard Documents

Compliance requirements are strictly tied to gross tonnage (GT) criteria:

Document / Equipment RequirementTonnage Threshold (GT)Operational Scope
Garbage Placards≥ 12 m (Length Overall)Must display clear discharge layout rules to crew and passengers.
Garbage Management Plan (GMP)≥ 100 GT and passenger ships Written procedures for minimizing, collecting, storing, processing, and discharging garbage.
Garbage Record Book (GRB)≥ 100 GT (per MEPC.360(79), eff. 1 May 2024; lowered from former 400 GT) and passenger ships. Split into Part I (All crews/spaces) and Part II (Cargo residues for bulk carriers).

Special Area Regulations

Within designated MARPOL Annex V Special Areas (including the Baltic Sea, North Sea, Mediterranean Sea, Red Sea, Black Sea, Gulf Area, and Antarctic Area), discharge rules are extremely strict:

  • General Garbage: Zero discharge permitted for all domestic, operational, and structural wastes.
  • Food Waste: Can only be discharged if comminuted/ground ( ≤ 25 mm screen) and the vessel is not less than 12 nautical miles from the nearest land or ice shelf.
  • Cargo Residues: Discharge of cargo hold washing water containing residues is prohibited unless the substances are verified as non-Harmful to the Marine Environment (HME) and no port reception facilities are available at both departure and destination ports.

Oil tanker amendment package?
MARPOLSOLASSTCWMLC
15-Second AnswerRecent oil tanker amendments enforce stricter structural and survey compliance, including SOLAS Chapter II-2 (MSC.520(106)) for flashpoint safety and MARPOL Annex VI for EEXI/CII carbon controls. They tie directly into STCW for specialized tanker endorsements and MLC 2006 for mandatory financial security certificates.
60-Second AnswerModern oil tanker operations are governed by a complex matrix of updated international instruments. Under SOLAS (MSC.520(106)), strict pre-bunkering flashpoint declarations are mandatory to mitigate ignition risks. Environmental controls are stepped up under MARPOL Annex VI, enforcing strict EEXI and Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) operational ratings for tankers. On the human element side, STCW mandates specialized, tiered tanker training endorsements for officers, while the MLC 2006 financial security amendments require valid certificates covering shipowner liability for repatriation and abandonment across all trading tankers.

Multi-Convention Regulatory Matrix for Tankers

Operating an oil tanker demands compliance across environmental, structural, and human element frameworks:

1. MARPOL Environmental & Carbon Controls

  • MARPOL Annex I & ESP Code: Tankers are subject to the Enhanced Survey Programme (ESP Code), requiring intensive, close-up structural thickness measurements of tank boundaries, transverse bulkheads, and deck plating to monitor accelerated localized corrosion.
  • MARPOL Annex VI (EEXI & CII): Effective for tankers, vessels must calculate their attained Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index (EEXI) and maintain an active Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) operational profile. Tankers must log their annual fuel consumption to receive an operational carbon rating from A to E, with a Corrective Action Plan required for poorly performing vessels.

2. SOLAS Fire and Safety Amendments

  • Flashpoint Enforcement (Resolution MSC.520(106)): Enforces pre-bunkering supplier safety declarations and explicit flashpoint records on the Bunker Delivery Note (BDN) to eliminate volatile fuel vapors near product storage areas.
  • Fixed Gas Detection: Mandates fixed hydrocarbon gas detection systems in the pump rooms and double-hull ballast spaces of oil tankers to provide early detection of cargo boundary leakage.

3. STCW Training Framework

  • Tiered Endorsements: Mandates a two-tiered certification structure for cargo handling teams:
  1. Basic Training for Oil and Chemical Tanker Cargo Operations (Section A-V/1-1-1).
  1. Advanced Training for Oil Tanker Cargo Operations (Section A-V/1-1-2) for Masters, Chief Engineers, Mates, and Second Engineers who have direct responsibility for loading, discharging, and care of cargo in transit.

4. MLC 2006 Seafarer Protections

  • Financial Security Amendments: Tankers must carry valid insurance or financial security certificates under Title 2 and Title 4 of the MLC. This ensures that if a company suffers financial distress or abandons a vessel, seafarers are guaranteed up to 4 months of outstanding wages, repatriation costs, and essential provisions.

Hong Kong Convention — IHM three parts and CE role?
MARPOLSURVEYIACS
15-Second AnswerThe Hong Kong Convention (HKC) ensures that ships are recycled safely without endangering human health or the environment. Flag states must mandate, verify, and certify an Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM) for all vessels ≥ 500 GT, tracking onboard hazardous materials from construction to demolition.
60-Second AnswerThe Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships entered into force on 26 June 2025. Its primary purpose is to ensure that ship recycling does not pose unnecessary risks to human health or the environment. Under this framework, Flag State duties include mandating and certifying an Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM) for all ships ≥ 500 GT, issuing a Statement of Compliance, and verifying the Ship Recycling Plan before issuing a Ready for Recycling certificate. The IHM is split into three parts: Part I tracks materials in the ship's structure and equipment, Part II logs operational waste, and Part III lists stores and consumables compiled immediately prior to recycling.

Core Purpose of the Convention

The Hong Kong Convention (HKC) 2009 aims to facilitate safe and environmentally sound ship recycling practices globally. It addresses concerns around occupational safety at recycling yards, the management of hazardous materials onboard, and the prevention of marine pollution during dismantling.

Flag State Duties and Enforcement

Flag States carry out regulatory oversight through a structured certification process:

  • IHM Verification: Ensure every vessel flying its flag maintains an accurate, updated Inventory of Hazardous Materials and issue an International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials (or Statement of Compliance).
  • Recycling Authorization: Prior to recycling, the Flag State must review the yard-specific Ship Recycling Plan and issue a Ready for Recycling (RFR) Certificate.
  • Facility Control: Ensure that ships flying its flag are recycled exclusively at authorized Ship Recycling Facilities (SRFs) that maintain strict environmental and safety controls.

Structure of the Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM)

The IHM is a living document that stays with the vessel throughout its life cycle, identifying the location and approximate quantities of hazardous substances (such as asbestos, PCBs, ozone-depleting substances, and heavy metals):

IHM PartContent & ScopeResponsibility & Timing
Part IHazardous materials utilized within the ship’s structure, machinery, and equipment (e.g., insulation, paints). Compiled at delivery by the shipyard; updated by the Chief Engineer during operations when equipment is modified or replaced.
Part IIOperationally generated wastes present onboard. Compiled by the ship's crew immediately prior to the vessel being taken out of service for recycling.
Part IIIStores and consumables present onboard. Logged concurrently with Part II at the end of the vessel's operational life.

GESAMP — role and MARPOL Annex II link?
MARPOLSURVEY
15-Second AnswerGESAMP is a joint UN advisory body of independent scientific experts that evaluates marine pollution hazards. It provides the scientific assessments used by the IMO to classify hazardous chemicals under MARPOL Annex II and evaluate ballast water treatment systems.
60-Second AnswerGESAMP stands for the Joint Group of Experts on the Scientific Aspects of Marine Environmental Protection. Founded in 1969, it is an independent UN advisory body sponsored by multiple organizations, including the IMO, FAO, and UNEP. GESAMP does not pass laws itself; instead, it provides the technical and scientific foundation for regulatory decisions made by the MEPC. Its core maritime work includes establishing the GESAMP Hazard Profiles to classify noxious liquid substances into Categories X, Y, or Z under MARPOL Annex II, evaluating anti-fouling system impacts, and auditing active substances in ballast water treatment technologies.

Definition and Institutional Role

GESAMP is a multidisciplinary UN advisory body composed of independent scientists who provide specialized expertise on marine environmental health. It serves as a scientific resource for UN sponsoring agencies, ensuring that international maritime pollution legislation is backed by peer-reviewed research.

Primary Regulatory Work in the Maritime Industry

GESAMP's scientific evaluations directly influence the structure of several key IMO environmental conventions:

  1. MARPOL Annex II (Noxious Liquid Substances in Bulk): The GESAMP/EHS Working Group maintains a comprehensive composite list evaluating the environmental, toxicological, and safety hazards of bulk chemical cargoes. These scientific "GESAMP Hazard Profiles" are used to categorize substances into Categories X, Y, or Z, dictating their permissible discharge controls and stripping requirements.
  1. Ballast Water Management (BWM) Convention: The GESAMP-Ballast Water Working Group reviews and evaluates the safety and environmental impacts of ballast water treatment systems that utilize "Active Substances" (such as ozone, chlorine, or peracetic acid), ensuring they do not harm the local marine environment when discharged.
  1. Anti-Forming Systems (AFS Convention): Evaluates the ecotoxicological profiles of biocides used in hull coatings to assist the IMO in banning harmful compounds, similar to the historical ban on tributyltin (TBT).
  2. Marine Litter and Plastics (MARPOL Annex V): Conducts global studies on microplastics and sea-based sources of litter to provide the data that supports tighter garbage discharge standards.

Environmentally Sound Management (ESM) in shipping?
MARPOLISM
15-Second AnswerEnvironmentally Sound Management (ESM) means taking all practicable shipboard steps to minimize, manage, and dispose of hazardous waste safely to protect human health and the environment. It integrates compliance across the Basel Convention, Hong Kong Convention, and all MARPOL Annexes under a single operational standard.
60-Second AnswerEnvironmentally Sound Management, or ESM, is a structural concept originally derived from Article 2(8) of the Basel Convention. In the maritime regulatory framework, it represents a commitment to managing all shipboard generated wastes, hazardous materials, and operational systems in a manner that protects human health and prevents marine pollution. For a Chief Engineer, ESM is implemented practically through strict adherence to the Hong Kong Convention for certified ship recycling, tracking structural and operational hazardous items via the IHM, and managing operational discharges—including ODS recovery under MARPOL Annex VI, sewage treatment under Annex IV, and garbage segregation under Annex V.

Regulatory Definition and Origin

  • Basel Convention Foundation: The term "Environmentally Sound Management" (ESM) originates from Article 2(8) of the Basel Convention on the Control of Transboundary Movements of Hazardous Wastes and their Disposal.
  • Core Definition: It means taking all practicable steps to ensure that hazardous wastes or other wastes are managed in a manner which will protect human health and the environment against the adverse effects which may result from such wastes.
  • Application to International Shipping: In a maritime context, ESM serves as an overarching compliance standard, linking global shore-side waste management treaties with shipboard operational codes and conventions.

Practical Shipboard Implementation by the Chief Engineer

Executing ESM onboard requires the engineering team to manage multiple overlapping regulatory areas systematically:

  1. Ship Recycling and the Living IHM: Ensuring the ship is recycled only at authorized, ESM-certified facilities in compliance with the Hong Kong Convention. The Chief Engineer must continuously maintain and update the Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM) Part I during operations to log structural changes.
  1. Ozone-Depleting Substances (ODS) Controls: Strict adherence to MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 12, ensuring that any ODS gases (such as Halon or HCFCs) are captured and recovered during maintenance using dedicated reclamation units, rather than being vented to the atmosphere.
  1. Sewage and Graywater Management: Maintaining the type-approved sewage treatment plant or comminuting system in strict accordance with MARPOL Annex IV, preventing illegal discharges in restricted or coastal waters.
  1. Rigid Garbage Segregation: Executing the ship's Garbage Management Plan under MARPOL Annex V, including the sorting of domestic and operational wastes, logging ash from incinerators, and securing port reception facility receipts.
  1. Bilge and Sludge Accountability: Operating the Oil Water Separator (OWS) and logging all sludge generation and oil transfers in the Oil Record Book (ORB) Part I, ensuring no hazardous oily waste bypasses the certified processing line.

The Hong Kong Convention is now in force. Why is the EU Ship Recycling Regulation still required?
MARPOLRECYCLING
15-Second Answer

Because the EU SRR is stricter, and enforced differently. Regulation (EU) No 1257/2013 came into being in 2013, when the Hong Kong Convention had no prospect of early entry into force, and the EU took the view that the Convention's standards were not enough on their own. Its two hard edges remain: an EU-flagged ship may be recycled only at a yard on the European List, and the EU's hazardous-materials list is wider than the Convention's. A yard holding a HKC Statement of Compliance is not thereby on the European List. So the Convention sets a global floor; the Regulation keeps a higher regional ceiling.

60-Second Answer

Sir, the Hong Kong Convention entered into force on 26 June 2025, and the natural question is why a regional instrument survives it. Three reasons. First, history: the Regulation was adopted in 2013 precisely because the Convention was stalled — its entry-into-force conditions on tonnage and recycling capacity looked unreachable — so the EU front-ran it rather than wait. Second, stringency: the EU prohibits or restricts a longer list of hazardous materials than the Convention does, and it operates a positive list of approved yards. An EU-flagged ship may only be recycled at a facility on the European List, and inclusion is decided by the Commission against EU criteria — a yard can hold a Convention Statement of Compliance and still not be listed. In practice that operates as a bar on beaching, which the Convention itself does not prohibit outright. Third, enforcement: the Regulation is directly applicable EU law with penalties and port-State inspection behind it, whereas the Convention depends on each Party's implementation. And the reach is not only EU flags — a non-EU flagged ship calling at an EU or EEA port must carry an Inventory of Hazardous Materials and a statement of compliance, so the IHM obligation follows the trade, not the flag.

1. The two instruments in one line each

  • Hong Kong Convention 2009 — the global instrument. In force 26 June 2025. Requires an Inventory of Hazardous Materials for ships of 500 GT and above, a Ship Recycling Plan, and recycling at an authorised facility in a Party State. Certificates: the International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials, and the International Ready for Recycling Certificate.
  • Regulation (EU) No 1257/2013 — the regional instrument, in force since 2013 and fully applicable to the recycling of EU-flagged ships since 31 December 2018. Same architecture, tighter content, plus the European List.

2. Where the EU regime is actually harder

  • The European List. This is the structural difference. An EU-flagged ship may be recycled only at a facility on a list maintained by the European Commission, and the Commission assesses facilities against EU criteria — including containment, worker safety, downstream waste management and the ability to demonstrate it. Facilities outside the EU can be and are listed, but listing is not automatic and a Convention Statement of Compliance does not confer it.
  • Wider hazardous-materials scope. The Convention's Appendices set the baseline; the EU adds further substances to its own Annexes, so an IHM prepared to the EU standard covers more than a Convention-only IHM. Where the two differ, the EU list is the binding one for a ship in scope.
  • Beaching. The Convention regulates ship recycling but does not itself outlaw the intertidal beaching method. The EU does not ban it by name either — it achieves the same end through listing criteria that a beaching yard has, in practice, found very difficult to satisfy.
  • Enforcement. An EU Regulation is directly applicable in every Member State with national penalties behind it, and its inspection regime sits alongside the Port State Control Directive. Convention enforcement depends on each Party's own transposition and on the reach of its flag and port administrations.

3. Who is caught by which

  • EU-flagged ship — the Regulation governs the whole recycling event, wherever in the world it happens. This is what stops an owner reflagging at the end of life … except that reflagging shortly before sale for recycling is exactly the well-known avoidance route, and it is the Regulation's most criticised gap.
  • Non-EU flagged ship calling at an EU or EEA port — must carry an IHM and a statement of compliance and have it available for inspection. The recycling obligations do not follow, but the inventory obligation does.
  • Everyone else — the Convention, through their own flag State.

4. Basel, and why it is still mentioned

A ship destined for recycling can be waste under the Basel Convention and its Ban Amendment, and the EU implements Basel through its Waste Shipment Regulation. The EU SRR was drafted partly to give ships a purpose-built regime instead of a general waste one, and it amended the Waste Shipment Regulation accordingly. If the examiner pushes on “which convention makes a ship waste”, Basel is the answer, and the EU SRR is the lex specialis that displaces it for ships in its scope.

5. What the Chief Engineer actually does

The recycling event may be someone else's problem, but the IHM is not. Part I is a living document maintained throughout the ship's service life, and it is the Chief Engineer who makes it live or lets it rot: every new item of equipment, every spare, every drum of chemical and every repair with new material carries a Material Declaration and a Supplier's Declaration of Conformity that must be checked against the inventory before the item comes aboard. A Part I that has not been updated since delivery is the standard finding, and it is an engineering-department finding. Parts II and III — operationally generated waste, and stores — are compiled when the ship is being prepared for recycling.

Basel Convention and the EU Waste Shipment RegulationThe general hazardous-waste regime a ship for recycling can fall under. The EU SRR was written in part to give ships a dedicated regime instead, and amended the Waste Shipment Regulation to that end.
Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 (India)India's implementing statute and its yard authorisation regime, covered at QB3_G#q3. Relevant because Alang yards have pursued both HKC compliance and European List inclusion, and the two are separate processes.
CE Oral Tip: The question is why both, so do not describe the two instruments in turn — contrast them. The one-sentence answer that gets the mark is: the Convention is a global floor and the Regulation is a higher regional ceiling, enforced through a positive list of yards. Then give the three hard differences: the European List, the wider hazardous-materials scope, and directly applicable EU enforcement. Have the date ready — HKC in force 26 June 2025 — because a candidate who still says the Convention is “not yet in force” has dated himself badly. If he asks the obvious follow-up about how owners get around it, the honest answer is reflagging before the final voyage, and saying so shows you understand the Regulation rather than just its text.
CE Relevance, Traps, Failures & Exam Strategy
CE Relevance

The Chief Engineer owns IHM Part I in practice, because it is maintained by controlling what comes aboard. Every stores requisition, spare and chemical needs its Material Declaration checked before acceptance, and asbestos is the recurring problem — gaskets, brake linings and insulation still arrive in supply chains long after the ban, and an asbestos-containing spare fitted in service is both an IHM entry and a SOLAS II-1/3-5 issue.

Trap Questions
  • Examiner: “Is the Hong Kong Convention in force?” Yes — 26 June 2025. Getting this wrong makes everything after it sound stale.
  • Examiner: “Does the EU ban beaching?” Not in those words. It requires EU-flagged ships to be recycled at European List facilities, and the listing criteria have in practice been very difficult for intertidal beaching yards to meet. Say it that way — it is both accurate and more impressive than the slogan.
  • Examiner: “My ship is Panama-flagged. Does the EU Regulation touch me?” Yes, in part. Calling at an EU or EEA port you must carry an IHM and a statement of compliance. The recycling provisions do not apply to you; the inventory does.
  • Examiner: “If a yard has a HKC Statement of Compliance, can an EU ship go there?” Only if it is also on the European List. The two are separate assessments.
  • Examiner: “Which convention makes a ship waste?” Basel, with the EU implementing it through the Waste Shipment Regulation. The EU SRR is the dedicated regime that displaces it for ships in scope.
Common CE Failures

Describing the HKC and the EU SRR separately and never comparing them — the question was the comparison. Saying the Convention is not yet in force. Claiming the EU ban applies to all ships in EU waters; the recycling limb follows the EU flag, not the port. Not knowing the European List exists, which is the single structural difference. Confusing the three IHM parts, or thinking Parts II and III are maintained throughout service life.

Numbers to Memorise

26 June 2025 — HKC entry into force. 2013 — Regulation (EU) No 1257/2013. 31 December 2018 — EU-flag recycling confined to European List facilities. 500 GT — IHM threshold. Three IHM parts. 26 June 2030 — the outer date by which existing ships must hold the certificate, or before going for recycling if earlier.

Casualty Link

Not a casualty so much as a continuing record: the fatality and injury rate at intertidal recycling yards, and the exposure of workers to asbestos, PCBs and residual hydrocarbons, is what drove both instruments. The EU's impatience with the pace of the Convention is the whole explanation for why a regional regime exists at all.

On My Vessel

IHM Part I is treated as a controlled document with a named owner, and no chemical or spare is accepted aboard without its Material Declaration checked against it. The failure mode is not dramatic: it is two years of small acceptances nobody recorded, discovered at once when the ship is finally surveyed for its certificate.

Incinerator regulations — what governs shipboard incineration, and what may and may not be burned?
MARPOLANNEX VI
15-Second Answer

Sir, MARPOL Annex VI regulation 16. Incineration only in a shipboard incinerator, with one exception. Six substances prohibited — Annex I/II/III cargo residues and contaminated packing, PCBs, garbage above trace heavy metals, refined products with halogen compounds, sludge not generated on board, and scrubber residues. PVC only if the unit holds an IMO Type Approval Certificate.

60-Second Answer

Regulation 16 works in four moves. Where: only in a shipboard incinerator, except as paragraph 4 allows. What not: six prohibited substances, plus PVC except in incinerators holding IMO Type Approval Certificates — a conditional prohibition, not an absolute one. The exception: sewage sludge and sludge oil generated during normal operation may also be burned in the main or auxiliary power plant or boilers, but then not inside ports, harbours or estuaries. The hardware: every incinerator on a ship constructed, or installed, on or after 1 January 2000 must meet appendix IV and be approved by the Administration. Those units need a manufacturer’s manual kept with the unit, trained operators, and continuous gas outlet temperature monitoring: continuous-feed must not be fed below 850°C; batch-loaded must reach 600°C in five minutes then stabilise at not less than 850°C.

The instrument, and its currency

  • MARPOL Annex VI, regulation 16 — “Shipboard incineration”. The consolidated Annex VI in force was adopted by resolution MEPC.328(76), in force 1 November 2022.
  • Regulation 16 is unamended. No amendment in the held in-force chain touches it, so the current text is the MEPC.328(76) text. That is worth one sentence in the room, because a panel testing currency expects you either to name a recent amendment or to know confidently that there is not one.
  • Note what regulation 16 is not: it is not the garbage rule. What you may discharge is Annex V; what you may burn is Annex VI reg. 16. Candidates routinely answer this question out of Annex V.

Paragraph 2 — the six outright prohibitions, in the Code’s own order

  • .1 residues of cargoes subject to Annex I, II or III, or related contaminated packing materials;
  • .2 polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs);
  • .3 garbage, as defined by Annex V, containing more than traces of heavy metals — note the qualifier: it is not all garbage, it is garbage above trace heavy-metal content;
  • .4 refined petroleum products containing halogen compounds;
  • .5 sewage sludge and sludge oil neither of which is generated on board the ship — the discriminator is origin, not substance. Your own sludge is fine; sludge received from another ship or from shore is not;
  • .6 exhaust gas cleaning system residues — the scrubber limb, and the one most recently relevant to a modern ship.

Paragraph 3 — PVC, the conditional prohibition

  • Verbatim: “Shipboard incineration of polyvinyl chlorides (PVCs) shall be prohibited, except in shipboard incinerators for which IMO Type Approval Certificates have been issued.”
  • So the correct answer to “can you burn PVC?” is “only if the incinerator has an IMO Type Approval Certificate” — and then the honest second half: “I would check the unit’s certificate before I did.” Saying flatly that plastics can never be burned is the common wrong answer and it is wrong on the text.

Paragraph 4 — the one place incineration happens outside the incinerator

  • Sewage sludge and sludge oil generated during normal operation of the ship may also be burned in the main or auxiliary power plant or boilers — that is the routine practice of burning your own sludge in the boiler, and it is expressly permitted.
  • But in those cases it shall not take place inside ports, harbours or estuaries. Note precisely what is restricted: this restriction attaches to the power-plant/boiler route, not to the incinerator generally. There is no blanket MARPOL prohibition on using the incinerator in port — local port and terminal rules commonly impose one, and many do, but that is local law or terminal condition, not regulation 16. Say which is which.

Paragraph 5 — the saving clause

  • Regulation 16 does not affect the incineration-at-sea prohibitions of the London Convention 1972 as amended and its 1996 Protocol, and does not preclude alternative design shipboard thermal waste treatment devices that meet or exceed regulation 16.

Paragraphs 6 to 9 — approval, manual, training and temperature

  • 6.1 Each incinerator on a ship constructed on or after 1 January 2000, or installed on board on or after 1 January 2000, shall meet appendix IV and shall be approved by the Administration taking into account IMO’s standard specification for shipboard incinerators. Two limbs to the trigger — ship construction date or installation date.
  • 6.2 The Administration may allow exclusion from 6.1 for an incinerator installed before 19 May 2005, provided the ship is solely engaged in voyages within waters under the sovereignty or jurisdiction of its own flag State.
  • 7 A manufacturer’s operating manual, retained with the unit, specifying how to operate within the limits of appendix IV paragraph 2.
  • 8 Personnel responsible for operation shall be trained to implement the guidance in that manual.
  • 9 Combustion chamber gas outlet temperature monitored at all times the unit is in operation. Continuous-feed: waste shall not be fed when the temperature is below 850°C. Batch-loaded: designed so the temperature reaches 600°C within five minutes after start-up and thereafter stabilises at not less than 850°C.

CE Relevance, Traps, Failures & Exam Strategy
CE Relevance

The incinerator is one of the few pieces of machinery where the Chief Engineer’s operating decision is itself the regulatory act. Nobody surveys what you put in the chamber; the control is that you know the six prohibitions, you know your unit’s approval status, and the Oil Record Book and Garbage Record Book say what actually happened. Three things are practically yours: the sludge route decision — incinerator or boiler, and if boiler then not in port, harbour or estuary; the temperature discipline, because feeding a continuous-feed unit below 850°C is a breach and not merely bad practice; and the scrubber residue rule, which is newer than most candidates’ reading and is absolute.

Trap Questions

“Can you burn plastic?” — PVC only in an incinerator with an IMO Type Approval Certificate; that is a conditional prohibition, not a ban. “Can you burn sludge in the boiler?” — yes if it is your own sewage sludge or sludge oil, but not inside ports, harbours or estuaries. “Can you run the incinerator in port?” — regulation 16 does not prohibit it; the port-limit words attach to the boiler/power-plant route. Local rules very often do prohibit it — say that, and say it is local. “Whose sludge?” — reg. 16.2.5 turns on origin: sludge not generated on board may not be burned at all. “What temperature?” — 850°C feed floor continuous-feed; 600°C in five minutes then 850°C batch. “Scrubber sludge?” — prohibited, reg. 16.2.6.

Common CE Failures

Answering out of Annex V because the subject sounds like garbage. Saying plastics can never be incinerated. Saying incineration is always prohibited in port. Listing three or four of the six prohibitions and stopping. Missing exhaust gas cleaning system residues entirely, which is the limb a modern scrubber-fitted ship most needs. Not knowing the unit needs Administration approval and an appendix IV specification. Quoting 850°C without saying which type of unit it applies to, or giving the batch-loaded figures the wrong way round. Forgetting that the manual must be kept with the unit and that operators must be trained.

Numbers to Memorise

Six prohibited substances in reg. 16.2 · PVC conditional on an IMO Type Approval Certificate · 1 January 2000 — appendix IV and Administration approval, on ship construction or incinerator installation · 19 May 2005 — cut-off for the flag-waters exclusion · 850°C — continuous-feed floor for feeding waste · 600°C in 5 minutes, then not less than 850°C — batch-loaded · MEPC.328(76), Annex VI in force 1 November 2022, reg. 16 unamended.

Casualty Link

The honest link here is enforcement rather than casualty, and it should be stated soberly. The prohibitions in reg. 16.2 exist because incinerating the wrong material converts a waste-handling problem into an air-emission and dioxin problem that leaves the ship as stack gas and cannot be recovered — which is precisely why the rule is a list of substances and a temperature, rather than a discharge limit. The halogen limb and the PVC limb are the same chemistry: burn chlorinated material at the wrong temperature and you form the compounds the rule exists to prevent, which is also why the 850°C floor and the type approval sit alongside the prohibition rather than instead of it.

Examiner Chain

Asked at Kochi in the August 2026 sittings simply as “Incinerator regulations”, on a panel with Simon and Srivastava, immediately before ODMCS calibration and cargo slop discharge — so the panel was working through the MARPOL machinery items in sequence. A bare two-word ask wants the instrument first, then the prohibitions, then the operating limits. Expect follow-ups on plastics, on burning sludge in the boiler, on running in port, and on what temperature. From there it commonly moves to the Garbage Record Book and Annex V, or to the sludge side and the Oil Record Book — see QB3_F Q10.

On My Vessel

Answer from your own unit. Say whether it is continuous-feed or batch-loaded, because the temperature answer depends on it; whether it holds an IMO Type Approval Certificate and where that certificate is filed; where the manufacturer’s manual is kept relative to the unit; and how sludge reaches it — settling and service tank route, decanting, and whether your ship burns sludge in the incinerator or in the auxiliary boiler. The practical CE detail worth offering is the preparation: sludge that is too wet will not burn and will drop the chamber temperature below the feed floor, so dewatering and the sludge tank heating regime are the real operating controls behind the 850°C number. If your ship has a scrubber, say explicitly that its residues go ashore and are never burned.

CE Oral Tip: Open with the instrument — “Sir, shipboard incineration is MARPOL Annex VI regulation 16” — because half the candidates answer from Annex V and the examiner is listening for which Annex you reach for. Then give the structure in four words: where, what not, the exception, the hardware. Have the six prohibitions ready as a list and include exhaust gas cleaning system residues, which is the one that separates a current answer from a memorised one. Handle PVC as a conditional: “prohibited unless the incinerator has an IMO Type Approval Certificate”. And when the port question comes, be precise rather than absolute: the regulation restricts the boiler route in ports, harbours and estuaries, and anything stricter is local law — distinguishing the two is the mark the examiner is actually giving.
