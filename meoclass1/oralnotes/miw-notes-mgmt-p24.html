SEEMP Part I — Planning, EEOI and Carbon Conversion Factors

Reg. 26 · SEEMP Parts I–III · EEOI (voluntary) · CF · Draft GFI context Uday pp. 576–584 Examiner: Simon ⭐ High Frequency

📖 Definition

The Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan (SEEMP) is the ship-specific plan that every ship of 400 GT and above subject to MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 4 must keep on board under regulation 26. It is developed and reviewed taking into account the IMO guidelines, currently the 2024 SEEMP Guidelines, MEPC.395(82) as amended. It has three parts. Part I is the ship management plan to improve energy efficiency: a continuous cycle of planning, implementation, monitoring and self-evaluation and improvement. Part II is the fuel oil consumption data-collection plan for ships of 5,000 GT and above. Part III is the operational carbon intensity plan for CII ships of 5,000 GT and above. The Energy Efficiency Operational Indicator (EEOI) is a voluntary IMO indicator (MEPC.1/Circ.684). It expresses the CO2 emitted per unit of transport work actually performed, and is one of the tools a ship may use to monitor Part I. CO2 is obtained from fuel mass using the carbon conversion factor CF, in tonnes of CO2 per tonne of fuel.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • SEEMP Part I applies to every ship of 400 GT and above to which MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 4 applies, subject to the exclusions in regulation 19. It is verified at the IEE survey, and a PSC inspector will ask to see it; PSC treatment of a missing or invalid SEEMP is covered in Part 7.
  • Most Part I measures sit in the engine room or on the bridge: hull and propeller condition, speed, trim and ballast, voyage execution. The Chief Engineer supplies the monitoring data that shows whether the plan works.
  • The EEOI is the natural voyage-level check on a measure's effect, because it uses actual cargo carried. The CII/AER uses deadweight capacity and annual totals. Knowing which indicator answers which question avoids misreading a rating.
  • The carbon conversion factor turns every tonne of fuel into tonnes of CO2 in the EEDI, EEXI, CII, DCS and EEOI calculations alike. A wrong CF corrupts all of them.

⚗️ Carbon Conversion Factor CF

CF converts the mass of fuel burned into the mass of CO2 produced, in t-CO2 per t-fuel. It is the fuel's carbon mass fraction multiplied by the molecular-mass ratio CO2/C = 44/12 ≈ 3.67. The SEEMP Part II form calls it the 'emission factor'. The ratio 44/12 alone is CO2 per unit of carbon, not per unit of fuel.

🛂 Where the SEEMP Sits in the IEE Survey Regime
  • Initial survey of a new ship (reg. 5.4.1): verifies that the attained EEDI meets the requirement and that the SEEMP is on board. The IEE Certificate supplement records this.
  • Existing ships (reg. 5.4.4): SEEMP on board verified at the first intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2013. Since 1 January 2023 their attained EEXI is also verified (reg. 5.4.7) — see Part 23 Topic 2.
  • Part II and Part III (reg. 5.4.5, 5.4.6): a Confirmation of Compliance is issued and kept on board.
  • Company audits and verification (reg. 26.3.3) apply to the SEEMP as a whole.
  • The IEE Certificate itself, its supplement and its validity are taught in Part 23 Topic 1.

EEXI has no phases: the required EEXI uses a single reduction factor per ship type and size (regulation 25, Table 3). Phases belong to the EEDI. ⚠️ Draft IMO GHG Fuel Intensity — Context Only

DRAFT — NOT ADOPTED — NOT IN FORCE. The source page on a 'GFS (Global GHG Fuel Standard)' and 'GFI (Global GHG Fuel Intensity)' describes a measure that, at the time of writing (October 2026), exists only as draft amendments to MARPOL Annex VI, the IMO Net-Zero Framework. The full adoption status is taught in Part 22 Topic 3 and is not repeated here.

  • Correct term: GHG Fuel Intensity (GFI), not 'Global GHG Fuel Intensity'. In the draft it is the life-cycle GHG emitted per unit of energy used on board, in gCO2eq/MJ, on a well-to-wake basis.
  • Draft mechanism: each ship ≥ 5,000 GT would calculate an attained annual GFI (energy-weighted across its fuels) and compare it with a two-tier target. That is a fuel standard, distinct from the CII (tank-to-wake, per transport work).
  • Correct ambition: the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy aims at net-zero GHG emissions by or around, i.e. close to, 2050, with indicative checkpoints of at least 20% (striving for 30%) reduction by 2030 and 70% (striving for 80%) by 2040, against 2008. The source's 'complete decarbonisation by 2030' is wrong.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the SEEMP, which regulation requires it, and to which ships does it apply?
The Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan is a ship-specific plan that MARPOL Annex VI regulation 26 requires every ship to which Chapter 4 applies to keep on board — ships of 400 gross tonnage and above, other than ships solely on domestic voyages, ships not propelled by mechanical means, and platforms including FPSOs, FSUs and drilling rigs regardless of propulsion. It was introduced as regulation 22 by MEPC.203(62) in 2011 and has been regulation 26 since 1 November 2022. It is developed and reviewed taking into account the 2024 SEEMP Guidelines, MEPC.395(82) as amended by MEPC.401(83) and MEPC.413(84). It may form part of the ship's Safety Management System.
Q2. Describe the three parts of the SEEMP and how each is checked.
Part I is the ship management plan to improve energy efficiency, required for all Chapter 4 ships; its presence on board is verified at the initial IEE survey, or for existing ships at the first intermediate or renewal survey after 1 January 2013. Part II, for ships of 5,000 GT and above, is the fuel oil consumption data-collection plan describing the methods and reporting processes for the regulation 27 data; the Administration confirms it with a Confirmation of Compliance. Part III, for ships of 5,000 GT and above in the CII categories, is the operational carbon intensity plan containing the CII method, the required CII for the next three years, an implementation plan and a self-evaluation procedure, plus a corrective action plan if the ship is rated D for three years or E once; it too carries a Confirmation of Compliance.
Q3. Explain the four-step cycle of SEEMP Part I with examples.
Planning identifies ship-specific measures such as speed optimisation, weather routeing, hull maintenance and propeller polishing; company-specific measures such as just-in-time arrival agreed with ports; human resource development through awareness and training ashore and afloat; and a measurable goal. Implementation sets up a system with responsible persons, dates, procedures and records. Monitoring tracks performance with an IMO indicator such as the AER, cgDIST, EEOI or CII, on consistent data. Self-evaluation and improvement assesses whether each measure worked and feeds the next planning cycle, so the plan improves continuously.
Q4. Define the EEOI, give its formula and explain how ballast voyages are treated.
The Energy Efficiency Operational Indicator is a voluntary IMO indicator under MEPC.1/Circ.684, defined as the mass of CO2 emitted per unit of transport work. For a voyage it equals the sum over fuel types of fuel consumed times its carbon conversion factor, divided by cargo mass times distance sailed; for a period it is the sum of all voyages' CO2 divided by the sum of their transport work, usually in grams of CO2 per tonne-nautical mile. Ballast and other non-cargo voyages are included: their fuel is added to the numerator, but their transport work is zero because no cargo is carried. Voyages to secure the ship's safety or save life are excluded.
Q5. How does the EEOI differ from the AER used for the CII?
The EEOI is voluntary and uses actual cargo carried and the distance it was carried, per voyage or as a rolling average, so it reflects real utilisation. The AER is the mandatory CII metric for most cargo ships of 5,000 GT and above; it uses deadweight capacity and total annual distance as a supply-based proxy for transport work and produces the annual A to E rating under regulation 28. A ship sailing part-loaded may look poor on the EEOI and good on the AER, so each answers a different question.
Q6. What is the carbon conversion factor and what are its values for the common marine fuels?
C_F is the non-dimensional factor that converts the mass of fuel consumed into the mass of CO2 emitted, equal to the fuel's carbon mass fraction multiplied by 44/12. The IMO values are 3.206 for diesel or gas oil, 3.151 for light fuel oil, 3.114 for heavy fuel oil, 3.000 for LPG propane and 3.030 for butane, 2.750 for LNG and 1.375 for methanol. The same table is used in the EEDI, EEXI, DCS, CII and EEOI calculations.

Situation

A Supramax on a regular coal trade has 'propeller polishing every 12 months' as a ship-specific measure in SEEMP Part I. The company wants evidence before approving a shorter interval. The Chief Engineer is asked to show the effect.

Method

Use voyages on the same route and loading. Calculate the EEOI from the voyage reports for the three laden-plus-ballast round trips before the polish and the three after, with the fuel from flow meters as defined in SEEMP Part II and the CF values for HFO and MGO. Count the ballast-leg fuel but not ballast transport work. Record the shaft power and rpm at a fixed service condition as an engine-side check on propeller condition (Topic 2).

Result and SEEMP action

Suppose the rolling EEOI falls by about four per cent after the polish, and the rpm at constant power rises by about one per cent. The improvement is entered under self-evaluation, the measure is retained, and the planning stage is updated with a proposed nine-month interval. The figures are illustrative; the point for the examiner is that Part I is a closed loop, and the monitoring indicator must be computed correctly, or the evaluation is meaningless.

Propeller–Engine Matching — Layout and Load Diagrams, Margins, De-rating and Heavy Running

Propeller law · Layout L1–L4 · SM / EM / LRM · mep De-rating · MAN B&W Load Diagram · Heavy Running Uday pp. 587, 589–599 Examiner: Simon ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Maker-specific values. The percentages in this topic come from the MAN B&W standard load diagram (MAN Energy Solutions, now branded Everllence: Project Guide section 199 06 11-0.1 'Engine layout and load diagram', edition 2025-06-16, and Basic principles of ship propulsion). They are maker guidance for MAN B&W two-stroke engines, not IMO rules, and other makers or engine series may differ. Corrections in this topic. (1) Light running margin. The source gives both '3–7% usual' and '4–10% recommended by MAN'. MAN recommends 4–7%, up to 10% in special cases. The EEDI link is that lower installed power shrinks the absolute margin, so MAN recommends a higher relative margin for EEDI Phase 3 power-reduced designs. (2) Sea margin covers average wind and waves. Fouling and heavier weather are covered by the light running margin, not the sea margin. (3) Engine margin is historically 10% and typically 10–30%. 'SP = 90% of MP' holds only for a 10% margin. (4) SMCR is the Specified MCR, not 'Service' MCR. It may be placed anywhere in the L1–L2–L3–L4 area. (5) De-rating means lowering the mean effective pressure of the SMCR, not choosing among speed/power combinations. A de-rated engine cannot run back to NMCR (L1) in service, even 'with overload'. The source's speed-reduction threshold for choosing de-rating has no maker basis and is not taught. (6) Load-diagram lines. Line 3 is the maximum rpm for continuous operation, not a 'sea-speed limit'. Line 9 is the sea-trial limit: 110% of SMCR rpm but not more than 107% of L1 rpm, not a flat '107%'. The overspeed trip setting is maker- and class-specific and is not quoted. (7) Zones. The red area is the time-limited overload area. Heavy-running ('torque-rich') operation lies left of line 1 towards line 4. (8) Line 4 is limited mainly by thermal and bearing load, with an air-excess limiter on electronic engines. It is not the stoichiometric limit, and engines never run near stoichiometric. The source's air–fuel ratio figures for distillate fuel and for the excess-air supply have no supporting maker or chemistry basis and are not reproduced. (9) Causes of heavy running. Fouling, the main in-service cause, was missing from the source's list. One unsupported symptom in the source's list is dropped. (10) Slow steaming saves fuel and CO2; it is not a NOx-compliance measure. NOx Tier limits are in g/kWh. Source notes: PDF 592 duplicates PDF 591. PDF 598 is the author's 'rough note' citing an unidentified video; nothing here rests on that video. 📖 Definition

Propeller–engine matching is the selection of a main engine rating and a propeller so that the ship reaches its design speed and can still operate safely when the hull fouls, the weather worsens or the ship accelerates. For a fixed-pitch propeller driven directly by a two-stroke engine, the propeller demands power roughly in proportion to the cube of its speed. The designer starts from the propeller design point (PD) on the light propeller curve (clean hull, calm water). The designer then adds a sea margin for average weather and an engine margin so the engine is not run at full rating in service. Finally the curve is shifted by a light running margin to allow for future heavy running. The result is the specified maximum continuous rating (SMCR), which must lie inside the engine type's layout diagram (L1–L4). The installed engine then works inside the load diagram built around its SMCR, which sets its limits for continuous and overload operation. De-rating places the SMCR at a lower mean effective pressure than the engine's maximum, to reduce specific fuel consumption.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Every heavy-running complaint — high exhaust temperatures, an engine that will not reach rpm, an overload alarm in head seas — is the operating point moving left on the load diagram. Reading the diagram is how a Chief Engineer explains it.
  • The light running margin is a design reserve that fouling consumes over time. Tracking it is a practical way to decide when to clean the hull or polish the propeller.
  • De-rating and power limitation are common EEXI and EEDI responses (Part 23 Topic 2). Their consequences for acceleration and heavy weather show up in the engine room, not on the certificate.
  • Examiners use the load diagram to test whether a candidate understands the difference between a power limit, a torque limit and a speed limit.

📈 The Propeller Law and Logarithmic Diagrams

For a fixed-pitch propeller at moderate speeds, resistance rises with the square of ship speed. Power (resistance × speed) therefore rises with the cube: P ∝ n³ (and ∝ V³). MAN stresses that this 'law' is an assumption valid at lower Froude numbers. Faster ships can have exponents of four or more. Engine power at constant mean effective pressure rises in direct proportion to speed: P ∝ n.

That is why engine layout and load diagrams are traditionally drawn on logarithmic scales: propeller curves become parallel straight lines, and the constant-mep lines become straight lines of slope 1.

NCR, the normal continuous rating (point S, the service propulsion point SP), is the power needed in service including the sea margin and light running margin. It is sometimes called the continuous service rating, because the engine margin is not in it. With a 10–15% engine margin, NCR is about 85–90% of SMCR. That is a typical range, not a rule.

🔧 De-rating — An mep Concept

A de-rated engine has its SMCR at a lower mean effective pressure than the maximum-mep line L1–L3. It keeps its maximum cylinder pressure, so the lower mep gives a lower specific fuel oil consumption. Because mep is constant along lines parallel to L1–L3, moving the SMCR parallel to those lines gives no de-rating. Lowering the SMCR speed at the same power actually raises mep. Ship-speed and shaft-generator needs often mean de-rating is achieved by choosing a larger engine, for example one more cylinder, rather than by cutting power.

  • De-rating is built in: shims under the piston raise the compression ratio; the turbocharger is matched to the reduced gas flow; fuel valves, cooling, lubrication, pumps and coolers are dimensioned for the SMCR.
  • Consequence: a de-rated engine cannot operate at NMCR (L1) in service. Above its SMCR it has only its own load diagram's overload area. A later up-rating is possible only if the ship was designed as 'dual rated' and the EEDI permits it.
  • Slow steaming lowers the operating point on an existing engine; de-rating changes the engine's rating. EEXI de-rating and power limitation are covered in Part 23 Topic 2.

🌊 Heavy Running — 'Torque-Rich' Operation

When resistance rises, a fixed-pitch propeller needs more torque at the same rpm, so the operating point moves left, from the light curve (6) towards and past the heavy curve (1/2), into the yellow area and towards the torque/speed limit (4). That is 'heavy running', loosely called 'torque-rich' in shipboard language. It is not the red area, which is the time-limited overload area.

What the maker guidance supports
CausesHull and propeller fouling (the main in-service cause, growing between cleanings and dry-dockings); heavy weather and seas; shallow water, including squat; ice; acceleration from low speed (the bollard-pull condition)
Typical indicationsHigher fuel index for the rpm achieved; higher exhaust gas temperatures; smoke; the engine cannot reach its normal rpm at the power limit
Remedies — immediateFPP: reduce rpm to bring the point back inside the diagram. CPP: reduce pitch. On ME (electronically controlled) engines the control system can also limit fuel against torque and a minimum air-excess ratio (dynamic limiter function).
Remedies — lastingHull cleaning and propeller polishing; dry-docking when in-water cleaning no longer restores the margin

Why line 4 is not a stoichiometric limit. A diesel engine always runs with a large air excess. The stoichiometric air–fuel ratio is only about 14:1 by mass for residual fuel, and slightly higher for distillate. Line 4 protects the engine from thermal and bearing overload at low speed and high torque. Electronic engines add a limiter that holds fuel injection above a minimum air-excess ratio, to avoid smoke and overheating long before stoichiometric conditions are reached.

Using the diagram to decide on cleaning. In calm weather, the extent of heavy running indicates the need for hull cleaning or propeller polishing. Plot measured shaft power and rpm against the light and heavy curves. A point drifting from near the light curve towards the heavy curve shows the light running margin being consumed. MAN gives the in-service check as LR = [(n / nSMCR) × (PSMCR / P)1/3 − 1] × 100%.

⚖️ EEDI and the Light Running Margin

The EEDI pushes designers towards lower installed power. With the same relative light running margin, a lower SMCR brings the torque limit closer, in absolute terms, to the propeller curve. Acceleration and heavy-weather capability then fall. MAN therefore recommends a higher relative light running margin for EEDI Phase 3 power-reduced plants. Its example raises the margin from 5% to about 7% to keep the same absolute margin. This is achieved by lowering the SMCR speed or reducing propeller pitch, not by adding power.

🎓 CE Oral Tip (Simon): Simon will hand you a blank page and say 'draw the load diagram'. Draw it on log axes. Number lines 1 to 9 correctly. Shade green (1–3–7), yellow (1–4–5) and red (4–5–7–8). Say the red area is overload, one hour in twelve. Then explain why line 2 sits to the left of line 6: that gap is the light running margin, MAN 4–7%, up to 10%, and fouling eats it. If he asks about de-rating, say lower mep at SMCR for lower SFOC, physical changes to the engine, cannot go back to L1. Attribute every percentage to MAN B&W guidance, not to IMO. Two answers fail candidates: that line 4 is where the air runs out at stoichiometric, and that the sea margin covers a fouled hull.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the propeller law and why are engine diagrams drawn on logarithmic scales?
For a fixed-pitch propeller at moderate speed the power absorbed is approximately proportional to the cube of propeller speed, because resistance rises with the square of speed and power is resistance times speed. MAN describes this as an assumption valid at lower Froude numbers; at higher speeds the exponent can be four or more. On logarithmic scales any power law becomes a straight line whose slope is the exponent, so propeller curves become parallel lines of slope three and constant-mep lines become lines of slope one, which makes layout and load diagrams easy to read.
Q2. Explain the engine layout diagram, NMCR and SMCR.
The layout diagram of an engine type is bounded by L1 to L4. L1 to L3 and L2 to L4 are lines of maximum and minimum mean effective pressure, the lower typically 75 to 80 per cent of the maximum, and L1 to L2 and L3 to L4 are constant-speed limits. L1 is the nominal maximum continuous rating, the most power that engine type with that number of cylinders can deliver. The specified maximum continuous rating, point MP, is the rating ordered for the ship and may be placed anywhere within the area; its position determines how far the engine is de-rated.
Q3. Describe the sea margin, engine margin and light running margin and how they lead to the SMCR.
Starting from the propeller design point on the light propeller curve, the sea margin, historically 15 per cent, is added for expected average wind and waves so that design speed is kept in average conditions. The engine margin, historically 10 per cent, keeps the engine below full rating in normal service for economy and reserve. The light running margin, recommended by MAN at 4 to 7 per cent and up to 10 per cent in special cases, shifts the curve in rpm so that fouling and heavy weather can make the propeller heavier without leaving the load diagram. The result is the engine layout curve and the SMCR. The normal continuous rating includes sea margin and light running margin but not engine margin.
Q4. What is engine de-rating and what are its consequences?
De-rating is placing the SMCR at a lower mean effective pressure than the maximum-mep line of the layout diagram. The engine keeps its maximum firing pressure, so specific fuel consumption falls. It is achieved by choosing the SMCR below the L1 to L3 line, often by selecting a larger engine for the same power, and it involves physical changes such as shims under the piston for a higher compression ratio and turbocharger matching. Moving the SMCR parallel to the constant-mep lines gives no de-rating. A de-rated engine cannot be run back to its nominal rating in service; above its SMCR only the time-limited overload area of its own load diagram is available.
Q5. Explain the lines and operating areas of the MAN B&W standard load diagram.
Line 1 is the engine layout curve through 100 per cent SMCR, coinciding with line 2, the heavy propeller curve. Line 6 is the light propeller curve for a clean hull in calm weather. Line 3 is the maximum rpm for continuous operation and line 9 the maximum rpm at sea trial. Line 4 is the torque/speed limit, line 5 the maximum mean effective pressure, line 7 the maximum continuous power, line 8 the overload limit, and line 10 the PTO layout limit. The area between lines 1, 3 and 7 is for continuous operation without limitation. The area between 1, 4 and 5 is for shallow water, heavy weather and acceleration. The area between 4, 5, 7 and 8 is overload, one hour in every twelve.
Q6. What is heavy running, what causes it and what does a Chief Engineer do about it?
Heavy running is the operating point moving left of the engine layout curve because the propeller needs more torque at a given rpm. The main in-service cause is hull and propeller fouling, together with heavy weather, shallow water including squat, ice and acceleration. Indications are a higher fuel index for the rpm achieved, higher exhaust temperatures and smoke, and the engine reaching its power limit below normal rpm. In the short term rpm is reduced on a fixed-pitch plant or pitch on a controllable-pitch plant. In the longer term the light running margin is tracked from calm-weather power and rpm readings, and hull cleaning, propeller polishing or dry-docking is planned when the margin has been consumed.

Situation (illustrative — assumed values)

A bulk carrier has a MAN B&W engine with SMCR 10,000 kW at 80 rpm and a design light running margin of 6%. At sea trial, in calm weather, 7,000 kW gave 75.3 rpm: LR = [(75.3/80) × (10,000/7,000)1/3 − 1] × 100 ≈ 6.0%.

Eighteen months later

In calm weather and laden, the same 7,000 kW now gives only 73.0 rpm: LR ≈ 2.8%. More than half the margin has gone. In a head sea the operating point would be pushed past line 2 into the yellow area. Full power would be reached well below SMCR speed, with high exhaust temperatures and a high fuel index.

What the Chief Engineer does

Reports the trend through the SEEMP monitoring step with the supporting readings, and recommends a propeller polish at the next port. After the polish, 7,000 kW gives 74.0 rpm (LR ≈ 4.2%), showing that the propeller accounted for part of the loss. An in-water hull clean then restores 75.0 rpm (LR ≈ 5.6%). The residual shortfall against the sea-trial 6% is noted for the dry-dock specification. The lesson: the load diagram is not only a design drawing; read with measured power and rpm, it is a condition-monitoring tool, and the light running margin is the quantity to watch.
