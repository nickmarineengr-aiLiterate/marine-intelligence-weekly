📖 Definition

The human element is the set of human factors (how people perceive, decide, communicate, work together, tire and learn) that shapes how safely a ship is run. IMO calls it 'a complex multi-dimensional issue that affects maritime safety, security and marine environmental protection' (resolution A.947(23)). This topic teaches the people skills behind it, which STCW calls non-technical competences: human nature (the eight aspects in the MCA guide The Human Element); empathy and emotional intelligence; the five team skills (Salas, Sims & Burke, 2005); motivation; the balance of authority and assertiveness; and situational awareness, which Endsley (1995) defines as the perception of elements in the environment, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future. It ends with how a Chief Engineer develops people: task and workload management, culture, formal and informal learning, mentoring and coaching, and Bloom's levels of learning.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Senthil tests the Chief Engineer as a leader, not a mechanic. 'How do you motivate your crew?', 'What is situational awareness?' and 'What is the two-challenge rule?' are standing oral questions, and the source marks several of these pages 'Oral Q' and 'written Q'.
  • STCW makes these skills a competence. Since 2012, Table A-III/2 ('Use leadership and managerial skills') and Table A-III/1 expect engine-room resource management, assertiveness and leadership including motivation, situational awareness, and task and workload management (Topic 2).
  • Most engine-room incidents have a human chain behind them. Typical links are a missed cue, a wrong mental picture, a junior who did not speak up, or a tired watchkeeper. The candidate must name the mechanism, not just say 'human error'.
  • The C/E owns the engine department's people. That means training junior engineers, mentoring them, and keeping a team that monitors and backs each other up. This is assessed directly under 'knowledge of shipboard personnel management and training'.
  • Wrong labels lose marks. Examples are 'assertiveness is pushing your idea whether right or wrong', 'Bloom alone in 1956', and 'IMO requires the two-challenge rule'.

💞 Empathy and Emotional Intelligence

Empathy. The MCA guide calls empathy 'a particular and powerful form of human imagination': putting yourself in another's shoes. It is what makes teamwork possible, because seeing the task from a colleague's perspective tells you how to support the shared goal. 'Communication is the mechanism by which we express empathy.' (The source writes 'another man's shoes'; the guide says 'another's shoes'.)

Emotional intelligence (EI). Goleman (1998) defines it as the capacity for recognising our own feelings and those of others, for motivating ourselves, and for managing emotions well in ourselves and in our relationships. 'EQ' is popular shorthand; Goleman writes EI. The source adds that it is critical to managing one's own behaviour, handling social situations and making critical choices.

The guide and Salas add three coordinating mechanisms that hold the five skills together: shared mental models (everyone has the same picture of the plan and the plant), mutual trust, and closed-loop communication (order, read-back, confirmation). An examiner who asks 'why do teams fail?' is often looking for these three.

🛠️ Resource-Management Training (ERM/BRM) — Non-Technical Skills

The source links communication failures in accidents to resource-management training. The correct regulatory position is this (P27-C005). STCW does not mandate a named 'ERM course'. Since the 2010 Manila amendments, knowledge of engine-room resource management (Table A-III/1) and bridge resource management (Table A-II/1), together with leadership and teamworking skills, have been mandatory STCW competences. Administrations normally require an approved ERM, BRM or leadership course to demonstrate them. The STCW detail is in Topic 2.

  • Non-technical by design (course practice). Resource-management courses teach human-factors skills: communication, assertiveness, leadership, situational awareness and workload. They do not teach machinery. That is a course-design norm, not a regulation.
  • Mixing ranks and departments. Courses put ranks and disciplines in the same classroom, with the same content, terminology and objectives. The aim is to break down barriers between people, between departments and between ship and shore, and to build a common safety culture. Technical training, by contrast, usually keeps deck and engine groups apart.
  • ERM principles (STCW Table A-III/1, as reproduced in MIN 654): (1) allocation, assignment and prioritisation of resources; (2) effective communication; (3) assertiveness and leadership; (4) obtaining and maintaining situational awareness; (5) consideration of team experience.

⚖️ Authority and Assertiveness

The source sets authority as the leader's attribute and assertiveness as the follower's, and says the C/E needs both. This balance is a standard resource-management topic, and STCW lists 'assertiveness and leadership' in the ERM knowledge.

Assertiveness, correctly defined (P27-C017): the ability to state one's observations, concerns or opinions clearly, confidently and respectfully, with supporting facts, and to persist until they are acknowledged, regardless of rank. It is not pushing an idea 'whether right or wrong'. STCW expresses it as 'questionable decisions and/or actions result in appropriate challenge and response'.

🚧 Barriers to SA, What Goes Wrong, and the Two-Challenge Rule

Common barriers to situational awareness (corrected list, P27-C026; Endsley 1995, Jones & Endsley 1996):

  • faulty perception or information processing (missed or misread cues);
  • incorrect or incomplete mental models;
  • high workload (overload), or low workload leading to complacency;
  • fatigue and stress (Topic 3);
  • poor communication.
LevelWhat can go wrong (source, consistent with Jones & Endsley 1996)
1 — PerceptionPerception is our mental picture of reality and is coloured by past experience and expectation, so we see what we expect to see.
2 — ComprehensionUsing a poor, incomplete or wrong mental picture; deficient observation; poor knowledge or experience; misunderstanding perceived information.
3 — ProjectionOver-reliance on the mental picture, and failing to recognise that it needs to change.

Losing SA: the two-challenge rule (source: 'Oral Q / written Q'). If a team member fails to respond adequately to two or more challenges about omissions or questionable actions, that person is assumed to have lost situational awareness, and action is required: escalate, or take over if necessary. The rule comes from aviation crew resource management and is used in the AHRQ TeamSTEPPS programme. It is a resource-management practice, not an IMO or STCW rule. The STCW counterpart is the criterion that questionable decisions or actions 'result in appropriate challenge and response'.

📋 Task and Workload Management — and SWOT

Task and workload management (STCW Tables A-III/1 and A-III/2): (1) planning and coordination; (2) personnel assignment; (3) time and resource constraints; (4) prioritisation. In plain words: planning, organising and controlling people, resources and procedures so the task is done safely (P27-C029 replaces the source's generic project-management wording).

It is a general management tool, not a maritime regulation; its origin is disputed and is not credited here. Strengths and weaknesses are internal; opportunities and threats are external.

🌐 Culture, Learning and How Trainees Learn

Culture (general definition): the values, beliefs, underlying assumptions, attitudes and behaviours shared by a group of people. A ship has a national mix, a company culture and a safety culture. Safety culture under the ISM Code is covered in Part 13 · Topic 2 (ISM Code).

How trainees learn practical skills. The MCA guide says people learn practical skills by progressively extending and fine-tuning their physical capabilities. That is why supervised practice, repeated under guidance, works better than a single demonstration.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is situational awareness? Explain its levels with engine-room examples.
Situational awareness is an accurate understanding of what is going on around you. Endsley's 1995 definition is the perception of the elements in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future. Level 1, perception: gathering cues with the senses on rounds and focusing on the important ones, such as a drip from a fuel pipe, a temperature above normal, a hot motor drive end, a wobbling belt or hammering in a line. Level 2, comprehension: matching those cues with knowledge and experience to build an accurate mental picture. For example, a rising jacket temperature together with a falling expansion-tank level means a leak, not a faulty sensor. The picture must be kept up to date. Level 3, projection: using the picture to predict what will happen and act first, for example changing over before the engine slows down in a channel. Projection is the most valuable level because it prevents the incident. SA is lost through faulty perception, a wrong or outdated mental model, overload or complacency, fatigue and stress, and poor communication. STCW requires the ability to obtain and maintain situational awareness in Tables A-III/1 and A-III/2.
Q2. What is the two-challenge rule, and where does it come from?
The two-challenge rule says that if a team member fails to respond adequately to two or more challenges about an omission or a questionable action, that person is assumed to have lost situational awareness, and the challenger must take stronger action: escalate to a senior officer or, where necessary, take over. It comes from aviation crew resource management and is used in the TeamSTEPPS programme. It is a resource-management practice, not an IMO or STCW rule. STCW expresses the same idea as an evaluation criterion: questionable decisions and/or actions result in appropriate challenge and response. On board, the C/E should make it clear in his standing orders and briefings that juniors are expected to challenge, and that a second unanswered challenge is a trigger to call him.
Q3. Name and explain the five team skills essential to team effectiveness.
These are the 'Big Five' of teamwork identified by Salas, Sims and Burke in 2005, and summarised in the MCA guide The Human Element. One, team leadership: directing and coordinating members' activities, motivating them, and assessing and developing their knowledge and skills. Two, mutual performance monitoring: members keep track of each other's work, spot mistakes and give feedback. Three, backup behaviour: understanding each other's tasks well enough to anticipate needs and take over when necessary. Four, adaptability: adjusting to changes in the environment that affect the team's plans, such as a generator trip in port. Five, team orientation: seeing yourself as part of the team and putting team goals above personal ones. Three coordinating mechanisms hold them together: shared mental models, mutual trust and closed-loop communication. In the engine room these are exactly what STCW's engine-room resource management competence asks for.
Q4. Distinguish authority from assertiveness. How would you, as Chief Engineer, handle an inspection observation you disagree with?
Authority belongs to the leader: the right and duty to decide and direct. Assertiveness is expected of every team member: the ability to state observations, concerns or opinions clearly, confidently and respectfully, with supporting facts, and to persist until they are acknowledged, regardless of rank. It does not mean pushing an idea whether right or wrong. A good C/E uses authority without suppressing his juniors' assertiveness, and is himself assertive towards superiors and outside parties. STCW lists assertiveness and leadership, including motivation, in its resource-management knowledge, and expects questionable actions to meet appropriate challenge and response. With an inspector, I would first make sure I understood the observation, then present the facts calmly: the record, the test result or the class approval. I would ask whether, on that evidence, the observation could be reconsidered or reworded. I would not argue in front of the crew or offend the inspector. If it stands, I record my comment and inform the company. This is illustrative practice, not a procedure from any rule.
Q5. How do you motivate your engine-room crew? Bring in a motivation theory.
I start from needs. Maslow's 1943 hierarchy runs from physiological needs (food, water, sleep), through safety, love and belonging, and esteem, to self-actualisation. A man who is not getting his rest or who fears for his safety will not respond to talk of promotion, so I first make sure rest hours, food, cabins and safe working conditions are right. Then I use the higher levels. I share the company's and the ship's goals with each person and communicate regularly. I recognise good work and celebrate successes. I give challenging tasks and opportunities to grow. I support new ideas, empower people to own their equipment, and manage each person individually. I avoid boredom by rotating tasks, and I keep healthy competition and team spirit. These points are good shipboard practice, not a regulatory list. STCW Table A-III/2 expects 'assertiveness and leadership, including motivation', so motivation is part of the C/E's assessed competence.

Situation (illustrative)

At 0300 in a UMS ship, the duty 3/E is called to a high-temperature alarm on No. 2 generator. He has been on board for one week. The 2/E, also called out, tells him the sensor has been faulty before and asks him to reset the alarm. The 3/E has felt the cylinder-head cover and it is much hotter than No. 1's. He says so once; the 2/E repeats 'it's the sensor' and goes to start the purifier. The 3/E says again, 'the head is genuinely hot — I want to take it off load', and gets no answer.

Analysis using this topic

Situational awareness. The 3/E has Level 1, perception: a physical cue, the hot head. The 2/E is working from an outdated mental model ('sensor fault before'). That is a Level 2 comprehension failure and the classic Level 3 trap of over-reliance on a picture that should change. Fatigue at 0300 is a barrier for both men (Topic 3). Assertiveness and the two-challenge rule. The 3/E has challenged twice with facts and had no adequate response. Under the two-challenge practice, he should assume the 2/E has lost situational awareness and escalate: call the C/E and, if the generator's safety requires it, take it off load according to the standing orders. Team skills. Mutual performance monitoring worked, because the junior saw the problem. Backup behaviour and closed-loop communication did not. Leadership afterwards. The C/E thanks the 3/E publicly for persisting. He coaches the 2/E on keeping his mental model updated, and reviews the standing orders so that a second unanswered challenge is a defined trigger for calling the C/E.

The lesson for the examiner

Candidates who say only 'human error' do poorly. Candidates who name the SA level that failed, show assertiveness as challenge with facts, call the two-challenge rule a practice (not STCW), and link it to STCW's 'appropriate challenge and response' and to a C/E who mentors and coaches score well.

📖 Definition

IMO's human element vision (resolution A.947(23), 2003) is 'to significantly enhance maritime safety, security and the quality of the marine environment by addressing human element issues to improve performance'. Three instruments carry most of that work. The STCW Convention and Code set minimum standards of competence for seafarers. Since the 2010 Manila amendments these include non-technical competences: resource management, 'Application of leadership and teamworking skills' at operational level, and 'Use leadership and managerial skills' at management level. The ISM Code (SOLAS chapter IX) requires each company to run a Safety Management System that depends on people at every level, from 'commitment from the top' to near-miss reporting on board. The ILO Maritime Labour Convention 2006 covers hours of work and rest and seafarers' welfare (Topic 3; Part 6 · Topic 27 (MLC 2006)).

🔐 Certificates, Uniform Interpretation, Model Courses and STCW-F
  • Certificate authenticity. STCW requires Parties to keep registers and make information on the status of certificates available, so that others can check the validity and authenticity of a seafarer's certificate (regulation I/2). 🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. How is the human element addressed in the STCW Convention and Code?
IMO treats the human element as a key element of safety, and its vision under resolution A.947(23) is to enhance safety, security and the marine environment by addressing human element issues. STCW 1978 was the first convention to set international minimum standards for training, certification and watchkeeping. The 1995 amendments rewrote it around standards of competence, created the STCW Code with a mandatory Part A and a guidance Part B, and added enforcement through the reporting system under regulation I/7. The Manila amendments, adopted in 2010 and in force from 1 January 2012, strengthened the human side further: rest raised from 70 to 77 hours in any 7 days, stronger anti-fraud measures, updated medical fitness standards with new drug and alcohol abuse prevention requirements, marine-environment awareness, training for new technology such as ECDIS, updated tanker requirements, new able-seafarer and ETO certificates, modern training methods, and updated revalidation and refresher training. Most importantly, Manila made non-technical skills mandatory competences: 'Application of leadership and teamworking skills' at operational level, engine-room and bridge resource management, and 'Use leadership and managerial skills' at management level in Table A-III/2, with task and workload management, assertiveness and leadership including motivation, situational awareness and decision-making. Chapter VIII adds fitness for duty and rest hours. Regulation I/2 lets certificates be verified, and IMO model courses support training. The UK calls its course HELM, but that is a course name, not STCW wording.
Q2. State the scope of STCW Tables A-III/1, A-III/2 and A-III/6.
Table A-III/1 specifies the minimum standard of competence for officers in charge of an engineering watch in a manned engine-room, or designated duty engineers in a periodically unmanned engine-room, on ships powered by main propulsion machinery of 750 kW or more. Table A-III/2 covers chief engineer officers and second engineer officers on ships of 3,000 kW propulsion power or more. Ships between 750 and 3,000 kW come under regulation III/3, using Table A-III/2 with permitted variations. Table A-III/6 covers electro-technical officers on ships of 750 kW or more; this certificate was introduced by the Manila amendments. On the deck side, Table A-II/1 covers officers in charge of a navigational watch and Table A-II/2 masters and chief mates, both on ships of 500 GT or more, with regulation II/2 certifying in two tiers. Each table sets the competence, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency, the methods of demonstrating competence, and the criteria for evaluating it.
Q3. Analyse the human factor in the ISM Code. How does it affect implementation?
The ISM Code grew directly out of human-element failures. Accidents in the 1980s, including the Herald of Free Enterprise in 1987, were put down to human error with management faults behind it. IMO responded with A.596(15), then the 1989 guidelines A.647(16), the revised A.680(17), and in 1993 the ISM Code itself, A.741(18), made mandatory by SOLAS chapter IX from 1998 and 2002. Its aim is a standard for safe management, operation and pollution prevention through an SMS, and every element of that system depends on people. The preamble says commitment from the top is the cornerstone. Section 6 requires qualified, certificated and medically fit crew, familiarisation, training and communication in a working language. Section 7 needs procedures that crew actually follow. Section 8 requires drills. Section 9 depends on crew reporting near misses and the company finding root causes. Section 10.3 depends on engineers maintaining critical equipment. Sections 5.1.5 and 12 need the master and the company to review honestly. If people are not motivated, trained, rested and willing to report, the SMS becomes paperwork, so ISM succeeds only with involvement from top to bottom.
Q4. Discuss the IMO and ILO regimes relating to the human element.
On the IMO side, the policy umbrella is resolution A.947(23), the human element vision, principles and goals, adopted in 2003 and still current. Its principles include honouring the seafarer's opinion, investigating the causal chain of casualties, ensuring that a single human or organisational error cannot cause an accident, writing simple and clear rules, and recognising that crew endurance depends on many factors. Under it, STCW sets competence standards, including leadership, teamwork and fitness for duty with rest hours in chapter VIII. The ISM Code under SOLAS chapter IX makes companies manage safety through people. IMO guidance such as MSC.1/Circ.1598, the Guidelines on Fatigue, supports both. STCW-F covers fishing-vessel personnel, revised from 1 January 2026. On the ILO side, the Maritime Labour Convention 2006 covers hours of work and rest under Standard A2.3 (minimum 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 in any 7 days, or maximum hours of work of 14 in 24 and 72 in 7 days), along with seafarers' welfare and living conditions. The STCW and MLC rest minima are the same figures (Topic 3).
Q5. What is HELM, and how does it relate to STCW?
HELM stands for Human Element, Leadership and Management. It is the title of the UK Maritime and Coastguard Agency's courses, run at operational and management level, through which UK candidates show the STCW leadership competences. It is not an STCW term. STCW's own wording, introduced by the 2010 Manila amendments, is 'Application of leadership and teamworking skills' at operational level, in Tables A-II/1 and A-III/1, and 'Use leadership and managerial skills' at management level, in Tables A-II/2 and A-III/2. STCW sets the competence and its evaluation criteria; each Administration decides how it is demonstrated, usually through an approved course.

Situation (illustrative)

During an internal audit, the auditor finds that the emergency fire pump failed its weekly test three weeks running. Each failure was 'fixed' by priming and was not reported. A newly joined 3/E did not know the priming arrangement, and the planned-maintenance entry shows the pump's foot valve overdue. The company's draft corrective action reads 'crew reminded to report defects'.

What the Chief Engineer's analysis covers

ISM section 9 (reporting and root cause). 'Remind the crew' treats the symptom. The root causes are organisational: no familiarisation on the pump for new joiners (ISM section 6); critical equipment not maintained at its interval (section 10.3); and a reporting culture where 'we fixed it' replaces a report. STCW competence. The 3/E holds a valid A-III/1 certificate, but competence on paper is not familiarisation with this ship. The C/E's own competence under Table A-III/2 ('Use leadership and managerial skills') includes personnel training and task and workload management. Human element. Was the test done at the end of a long day? Did juniors feel able to report a failed test without blame? Corrective action. Add the pump to the joining familiarisation checklist. Complete the overdue foot-valve maintenance and tighten critical-equipment tracking. The C/E briefs the engine department that a failed test is a reportable event. Verify effectiveness at the next master's review (5.1.5) and audit (12).

The lesson for the examiner

Candidates who answer 'ISM requires reporting' do poorly. Candidates who trace the failure through people (competence, familiarisation, communication, fatigue, reporting culture) and map each link to the right ISM section and STCW competence show they understand the human factor in ISM.

📖 Definition

IMO MSC.1/Circ.1598, 'Guidelines on Fatigue' (24 January 2019), defines fatigue as: 'A state of physical and/or mental impairment resulting from factors such as inadequate sleep, extended wakefulness, work/rest requirements out of sync with circadian rhythms and physical, mental or emotional exertion that can impair alertness and the ability to safely operate a ship or perform safety-related duties.' Fatigue is a hazard: it affects everyone regardless of skill, knowledge and training. It is managed in two ways. The first is guidance: Circ.1598's six modules on causes, effects and mitigation for companies, seafarers, trainers, designers and Administrations. The second is mandatory limits: STCW regulation VIII/1 with Section A-VIII/1, and ILO MLC 2006 Standard A2.3. These require a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24 hours and 77 hours in any 7 days (under MLC, alternatively, maximum hours of work).

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define fatigue and name the current IMO guidance on it.
IMO's Guidelines on Fatigue, MSC.1/Circ.1598 of 24 January 2019, define fatigue as a state of physical and/or mental impairment resulting from factors such as inadequate sleep, extended wakefulness, work and rest requirements out of sync with circadian rhythms, and physical, mental or emotional exertion, which can impair alertness and the ability to operate a ship safely or perform safety-related duties. The circular was approved at MSC 100 in December 2018 after review by the HTW Sub-Committee. It superseded MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001, whose title was Guidance on fatigue mitigation and management. That old title is still used in exam questions, but the answer is Circ.1598. It is guidance, not mandatory; the mandatory limits are in STCW chapter VIII and MLC Standard A2.3.
Q2. Describe the structure of MSC.1/Circ.1598 and who it is written for.
It has six modules and two appendices. Module 1 covers fatigue in general: definition, causes, effects and related instruments. Module 2 is fatigue and the company. Module 3 is fatigue and the seafarer, including masters, officers and ratings. Module 4 is fatigue, awareness and training, for training providers. Module 5 is fatigue and ship design, for naval architects. Module 6 is fatigue, the Administration and port State authorities. Appendix 1 gives examples of sleep and fatigue monitoring tools, and Appendix 2 an example of fatigue event report information. It addresses all stakeholders. Unlike the 2001 circular, it has no separate pilot or tugboat module. It urges companies to take fatigue into account in their ISM safety management systems.
Q3. What are the causes of fatigue on board?
Circ.1598 lists six primary causes: lack of sleep, meaning inadequate restorative sleep; poor quality of sleep and rest; working or sleeping at the wrong time of the body clock; staying awake for long periods; stress; and excessive workload, meaning prolonged mental or physical exertion. It groups the contributing factors into five categories. Seafarer-specific factors: sleep quantity and continuity, body clock, boredom, loneliness, diet, fitness, stress, medication, alcohol and caffeine, age, shift work. Management factors ashore and aboard: manning, scheduling, rest between work periods. Ship-specific factors: design, automation, accommodation. Environmental factors: noise, vibration, light, ship motion, temperature, humidity and ventilation. Operational factors: inspections, surveys, audits and other extra tasks. In the engine room, the typical combination is UMS call-outs breaking sleep, port stays with bunkering and surveys, and noise and heat reaching the cabins.
Q4. State the hours-of-rest requirements under STCW and MLC, including how rest may be split and the exceptions.
STCW regulation VIII/1 and Section A-VIII/1 require a minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. Rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which must be at least 6 hours long, and the interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 hours. Musters and drills are to be conducted so as to minimise disturbance of rest and not induce fatigue. Administrations may allow exceptions, provided rest is not less than 70 hours in any 7-day period and the exception to the weekly figure lasts no more than two consecutive weeks; rest may then be divided into no more than three periods, one of at least 6 h and neither of the others less than 1 h; the interval between consecutive rest periods must not exceed 14 h; such exceptions to the daily figure may not extend beyond two 24-hour periods in any 7 days; and the interval between two periods of exception to the weekly figure must be not less than twice the duration of the exception. MLC Standard A2.3 lets the flag State fix either the same minimum rest, 10 in 24 and 77 in 7, or maximum hours of work of 14 in any 24 hours and 72 in any 7 days, with the same splitting rules. The two MLC options are not equivalent over a week: 72 hours of work leaves 96 hours of rest, not 77. STCW itself sets rest only. The Manila amendments raised the weekly minimum from 70 to 77 hours.
Q5. As Chief Engineer, how would you mitigate fatigue in your department?
First, compliance and planning. I make sure the watch and day-work plan meets 10 hours in 24 and 77 hours in 7, with the two-period, 6-hour and 14-hour rules. I check the records honestly, and I re-plan ahead of port stays, bunkering, surveys and repairs rather than afterwards. Second, scheduling. I avoid hazardous jobs such as crankcase entry or high-pressure fuel work during the circadian low, roughly 3 to 5 in the morning, where practicable. I plan drills and routine maintenance to minimise disturbance of rest, rotate monotonous tasks, and make sure joining engineers are adequately rested before taking a watch, using rested personnel to cover. Third, culture. Anyone can tell me they are too tired without recrimination, fatigue events are discussed at the safety meeting, and I lead by example. Fourth, conditions. Air conditioning, ventilation, food, water and cabin noise are kept in order; naps of about 20 minutes are allowed where safe; and healthy habits are encouraged. Finally, if rest cannot be achieved with the manning available, I report it to the master and the company, because Circ.1598 makes resources and manning a company responsibility within the SMS.

Situation (illustrative)

A UMS container ship has three port calls in four days. The 2/E has supervised bunkering at night in port 1, attended a class survey by day in port 2, and is down for the 0000–0400 UMS duty before arrival at port 3, where a PSC inspection is expected. His rest record for the last 7 days shows 74 hours. In the last 24 hours he has rested 4 h + 3 h + 2 h. The company's crewing office has also sent a relief 3/E who arrives after 30 hours of travel and is rostered on the duty that evening.

What the Chief Engineer does

Check the rules. 4 + 3 + 2 = 9 h, in three periods with none of 6 h. That fails 10 h in 24 hours, the two-period rule and the 6-hour rule. 74 h in 7 days is below 77 h. An exception is only possible within the national provisions under A-VIII/1.9 (not less than 70 h, for no more than two consecutive weeks); a busy rotation does not make a non-compliant roster lawful. Re-plan. The C/E takes the 0000–0400 duty himself or reassigns it to a rested engineer, gives the 2/E an unbroken rest of at least 6 hours, and moves non-urgent maintenance out of the port window. Joining 3/E. Under Circ.1598 he must be adequately rested before assuming duties. He is not put on that evening's duty; a rested engineer covers. Hazard timing. The planned fuel-valve change is moved out of the 0300–0500 window. Record and report. Rest records are kept truthfully. The master and the company are told that the rotation, as manned, cannot meet the rest limits, which is a company resourcing issue under the SMS. The event is discussed at the next safety meeting as a fatigue learning point.

The lesson for the examiner

Candidates who recite '10 and 77' do poorly. Candidates who test a real roster against all the rules (10/77, two periods, 6 h, 14 h, the exception floor), apply the Circ.1598 measures (rested on joining, circadian lows, no-blame reporting), and escalate a manning shortfall through the SMS show the judgement Senthil is looking for.
