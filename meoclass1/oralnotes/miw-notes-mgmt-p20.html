Cargo Liability Regimes in Operation — Clause Paramount, Jurisdictional Scope and the Due Diligence Defence

🔎 Verification note: Three corrections. (1) Wrong Article cited for limitation. The draft twice attributed the 666.67 SDR / 2 SDR per kilogram limits to "Article IV bis". They are in Article IV, rule 5(a). Article IV bis is a different provision — inserted by the Visby Protocol — which extends the carrier's defences and limits to claims framed in tort and to the carrier's servants and agents (the "Himalaya" provision), and removes the limit where damage resulted from an act or omission done with intent to cause damage or recklessly with knowledge that damage would probably result. Both citations have been corrected and the Article IV bis content added, because the recklessness carve-out is itself examinable. (2) "Ship's rail" used as the liability boundary. Article I(e) of the Hague-Visby Rules defines carriage of goods as covering the period from the time the goods are loaded on to the time they are discharged from the ship. "Tackle-to-tackle" is the accepted shorthand for that period, but the ship's rail is not the statutory test and the phrase was deleted from Incoterms in 2010 for the same reason. Corrected throughout. (3) Deck cargo comparison row. The draft's table said deck cargo is excluded under Hague-Visby "unless explicitly agreed or in container ships". More precisely: Article I(c) excludes from the definition of "goods" cargo which by the contract of carriage is stated as being carried on deck and is so carried. Containers carried on deck on a purpose-built containership are generally treated as within the Rules because that carriage is not a departure from the contract. Reworded.
📖 Definition

A cargo liability regime allocates risk between carrier and cargo interest by fixing a floor of non-excludable carrier duties, a defined list of defences, and a ceiling on financial exposure. Part 19 Topic 5 dealt with how these regimes came into being and what they say. This topic deals with the operational question that follows: how does a regime actually attach to a given voyage, and what evidence does the engine room have to produce when a claim lands?

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • A charterparty is a private contract — no cargo regime applies to it automatically. The Clause Paramount is the mechanism that brings the Rules in, and with them the carrier's defences and limits. Without it, the owner is exposed on ordinary common-law terms.
  • The liability period defines what you are answerable for. Under tackle-to-tackle, damage on the quay before loading is not the carrier's problem; under port-to-port it may be. That single boundary decides whether a claim is even admissible.
  • The due diligence defence is proved almost entirely from engineering records — ultrasonic hatch cover tests, hold bilge alarm function tests, daily sounding logs, ventilation and dew point records. These are not paperwork chores; they are the evidence file.
  • Inherent vice is the carrier's strongest defence on wet bulk cargo damage, but it only works if the ventilation record proves the crew did everything right. A missing ventilation log converts a shipper's problem into an owner's liability.

📎 The Clause Paramount

A Clause Paramount is a contractual clause in a bill of lading or charterparty that expressly incorporates a cargo liability regime — normally the Hague or Hague-Visby Rules — into the contract, and provides that the incorporated Rules prevail over any inconsistent term.

It exists because the Rules apply compulsorily only in defined circumstances. A charterparty is a freely negotiated private contract and is expressly excluded from compulsory application. Without a Clause Paramount, the owner has neither the statutory defences of Article IV rule 2 nor the limitation of Article IV rule 5 — the exposure reverts to whatever the contract and the governing common law provide.

Is the regime enacted in the country of SHIPMENT? ├── YES ──> applies compulsorily to outbound shipments └── NO │ Is the regime enacted in the country of DESTINATION? ├── YES ──> may apply compulsorily to inbound shipments └── NO │ Does the contract contain a CLAUSE PARAMOUNT? ├── YES ──> the regime applies contractually └── NO ──> local law / common law governs

That decision tree is a teaching model of how municipal enactments bite, not a uniform IMO hierarchy. The detail varies by enacting state — for India, the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 applies to ships carrying goods from any port in India to any other port, whether in or outside India, and requires bills issued in India to state expressly that they take effect subject to the Rules.

🧮 SDR Mechanics and the Limitation Calculation

The Special Drawing Right is an international reserve asset created by the International Monetary Fund, valued against a basket of five currencies — US dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi, Japanese yen and pound sterling. Using SDR rather than a national currency insulates the limit from the inflation and devaluation that destroyed the £100 gold limit of 1924.

Maximum liability: Lmax = the greater of (N × 666.67) and (W × 2), in SDR

where N is the number of packages or units and W the gross weight in kilograms of the goods lost or damaged. The higher figure applies, so the claimant in practice selects whichever basis favours them. For heavy, low-count cargo the weight basis wins; for numerous light packages the package basis wins.

Worked check. 100 drums, total gross weight 40,000 kg, all damaged. Package basis: 100 × 666.67 = 66,667 SDR. Weight basis: 40,000 × 2 = 80,000 SDR. The weight basis is higher, so the limit is 80,000 SDR. Reverse the numbers — 500 cartons of 20 kg each, total 10,000 kg — and the package basis (333,335 SDR) far exceeds the weight basis (20,000 SDR).

Container enumeration (Art. IV r.5(c)). Where the bill of lading enumerates the packages inside a container, each enumerated package counts. Where it does not, the container itself is one package. Loss of the limit (Art. IV bis r.4). The carrier cannot limit at all where the damage resulted from an act or omission done with intent to cause damage, or recklessly and with knowledge that damage would probably result — a high threshold, but one that a documented decision to sail with a known unrepairable defect can reach.

🔍 Building the Due Diligence Evidence File
  • Hatch cover weathertightness. Ultrasonic leak detection or hose test results dated before loading, with the tested compartments identified. Physical condition record of rubber packing, compression bars, drain channels and non-return valves.
  • Hold bilge system. Function test of each hold bilge high level alarm, with date and result per hold. Strum box condition, suction valve operation, stripping pump capacity test.
  • Daily soundings. Every hold bilge well, every day, recorded with an actual figure — not "dry" as a standing entry. A flat, plausible record with no spike through heavy weather is affirmative proof that no ingress occurred.
  • Ventilation and dew point. Ambient and cargo temperatures, dew points, and the ventilation decision taken with the reason. This is what defeats a moisture-damage claim.
  • PMS and defect status. No overdue safety-critical jobs on the systems implicated; open defects risk-assessed and reported.

Ventilation rules for hygroscopic bulk cargo. The Dew Point Rule: ventilate only when the dew point of the outside air is lower than the dew point of the air in the hold. The Three Degree Rule: ventilate when the outside air temperature is at least 3 °C below the average cargo temperature at loading. Either rule is defensible if applied consistently and logged; what is not defensible is ventilating on instinct and recording nothing.

Inherent vice. If the pre-shipment quality certificate shows grain loaded above its safe storage moisture content — for maize commonly cited around 14 to 14.5% — self-heating and mould are a property of the cargo, not a failure of the ship. That defence under Article IV rule 2(m) becomes available only if the ventilation record shows the crew did their part.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. How would you demonstrate due diligence to a surveyor after a cargo damage claim alleging hold flooding?
I would produce a chronological technical file proving the vessel was seaworthy and cargoworthy before and at the beginning of the voyage. First, hatch cover integrity: ultrasonic leak detection or hose test reports dated before loading, identifying the compartments tested, together with the inspection record of rubber packing, compression bars, drain channels and non-return valves. Second, maintenance history: planned maintenance system records showing hatch gasket renewals, cleat overhauls and drainage servicing carried out on schedule with no overdue safety-critical items. Third, bilge management: daily sounding logs for each hold bilge well with actual figures, function test records for the hold bilge high level alarms, and maintenance entries for suction valves and stripping pumps. Fourth, operational data: engine room logs and automated records showing pumping frequency, confirming no abnormal accumulation attributable to mechanical failure. The point I would make to the surveyor is that a flat, credible sounding record through the heavy weather period is positive evidence that no ingress occurred, not merely an absence of evidence.
Q2. What is inherent vice, and how does the engine department's documentation support that defence?
Inherent vice is a quality or defect within the cargo itself that causes it to deteriorate without any external operational fault — classically bulk grain loaded above its safe storage moisture content, which self-heats and moulds when the cargo passes through changing climatic zones. It is a listed carrier exception under Article IV rule 2(m) of the Hague-Visby Rules. The engine department supports the defence with the ventilation record: daily ambient and hold dew points, cargo temperatures, and the ventilation decision taken with its reason, showing that ventilation was applied or suspended correctly under the Dew Point Rule or the Three Degree Rule. Combined with the pre-shipment quality certificate showing the loaded moisture content, that record shows the damage was a property of the cargo rather than a failure of care. Without the ventilation log the defence generally fails, because the carrier cannot show it discharged its Article III rule 2 duty to care for the goods.
Q3. Distinguish tackle-to-tackle from port-to-port liability, and explain why it matters to the P&I club.
Tackle-to-tackle is the Hague-Visby period of responsibility defined in Article I(e): from the time the goods are loaded on to the time they are discharged from the ship. Damage occurring on the quay before loading begins, or after discharge is complete, falls outside the statutory regime. Port-to-port is the Hamburg Rules scope, covering the whole period the goods are in the carrier's charge at the port of loading, during carriage, and at the port of discharge, including shore-side storage and transhipment. It matters to the P&I club because the port-to-port regime substantially widens the owner's exposure to risks the ship's staff do not control — stevedore handling, terminal storage, transhipment custody — which changes both the premium and the documentation the club will require. Note that the older textbook phrasing of "the ship's rail" is not the statutory test; the Rules speak of loading on and discharging from the ship.
Q4. Under Hague-Visby, when can the carrier lose the right to limit liability altogether?
Under Article IV bis rule 4, the carrier is not entitled to the benefit of the limitation of liability if it is proved that the damage resulted from an act or omission of the carrier done with intent to cause damage, or recklessly and with knowledge that damage would probably result. This is a deliberately high threshold — ordinary negligence, even serious negligence, does not break the limit. What can reach it is a documented decision taken with knowledge: sailing with a hatch cover known to be leaking and known to be over that cargo, or operating a system that has been formally condemned. It is worth noting that Article IV bis also extends the Rules' defences and limits to claims brought in tort and to the carrier's servants and agents, which is why the same limits protect the crew personally.

The claim

A 65,000 DWT Panamax discharges 60,000 tonnes of yellow corn at Rotterdam. The consignee finds microbiological degradation, caking and mould across the top 1.5 metres of Hold No. 3 and alleges unseaworthiness — specifically that defective hatch cover sealing admitted seawater during the North Atlantic passage. Rotterdam is a Hague-Visby jurisdiction, so the Rules apply compulsorily.

Step 1 — secure the record before anything else

On notification, print, sign and stamp the engine room logbook extracts, the PMS history for hatch cover and bilge systems, and the automated data logger records for hold bilge pumping. Doing this immediately, and dating it, forecloses any later allegation that records were reconstructed after the claim was known.

Step 2 — hatch cover integrity

Retrieve the pre-loading ultrasonic leak detection report for Hold No. 3 and confirm it identifies that hatch specifically. Physically inspect rubber packing for permanent set, cracking and compression, the compression bars for distortion, the drain channels for blockage, and the non-return drain valves for correct seating. Photograph everything before any remedial work.

Step 3 — bilge evidence

Extract the daily Hold No. 3 bilge well soundings. If the readings are flat and low through the heavy weather period, with no step change, seawater ingress through the hatch covers is effectively excluded — water entering a hold does not stay in the cargo, it drains to the well. Support this with the high level alarm function test record and the stripping pump capacity check.

Step 4 — the ventilation case

Produce the daily ventilation log with ambient dew point, hold dew point, cargo temperature and the ventilation decision. Damage confined to the top 1.5 metres with no bilge evidence is the classic signature of ship's sweat — condensation on the underside of the hatch and upper strakes dripping onto the cargo surface — which points to the cargo's own moisture, not to ingress.

Step 5 — inherent vice

Obtain the pre-shipment quality certificate. If the corn was loaded above its safe storage moisture content, the self-heating and condensation cycle is a property of the cargo. Combined with a clean ventilation record, that establishes the Article IV rule 2(m) defence and shifts liability to the shipper.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

The container analogue is the reefer claim. The same logic applies: continuous supply and return air temperature logs, power interruption events with durations, and defrost cycles are what separate "the cargo was already deteriorating" from "the ship failed to care for it". Damage patterns tell the story — uniform deterioration across a consignment points to pre-shipment condition; damage concentrated at one plug position points to the ship.

Bills of Lading and Charter Party Operational Management — On-Hire Surveys, Bunker Disputes and Bareboat Handover

On/Off-Hire Surveys · Bunker ROB Disputes · VCF · Bareboat Handover Uday pp. 484–492 (approx.) Examiner: Senthil ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Two corrections and one clarification. (1) e-B/L legal basis. The draft stated that "digital signatures and blockchain-backed tokens carry the same legal weight as traditional paper endorsements". As corrected in Part 19 Topic 2, legal equivalence comes from MLETR-based national legislation — for example the UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 — together with the platform rulebook and, commercially, from the International Group of P&I Clubs' approval of the specific system. The full sequence is given below. (3) Clarification. The draft describes the on-hire dispute as arising "transitioning from a Bareboat Charter to a fixed-term Time Charter". Bunker ROB disputes arise on any delivery or redelivery where bunkers change hands for value — the procedure below applies to all of them, not only to bareboat-to-time transitions.
📖 Definition

Charter party operational management is the day-to-day work of making the ship's technical reality match the contract's commercial assumptions — and of proving it when the two are alleged to differ. Part 19 Topic 3 set out the structure of the three chartering regimes. This topic covers the two places where that structure produces disputes the Chief Engineer personally owns: the bunker quantity on delivery and redelivery, and the condition of the ship at handover.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The on-hire and off-hire bunker figure is money changing hands on the strength of a measurement the Chief Engineer takes. A 180-tonne discrepancy on VLSFO is a six-figure argument settled by soundings, temperature and correction tables.
  • The independent surveyor's figure is not automatically right. Surveyors routinely miss unpumpable dead bottoms and fuel held in treatment piping, and misapply or omit temperature correction. Signing their statement without checking transfers the error to the owner.
  • A bareboat redelivery survey is the most exhaustive technical audit a Chief Engineer will face outside a renewal survey, because the whole point is to establish whether the returning charterer degraded the asset beyond fair wear and tear.
  • Under a time charter, the same records that defend a bunker figure also defend the speed and consumption warranty. One evidence discipline serves both.

📑 B/L Points That Bite Operationally
  • Prima facie versus conclusive. As between carrier and shipper, the bill is prima facie evidence of what was received — the carrier can prove the contrary. Once it is transferred to a third party acting in good faith, Article III rule 4 makes it conclusive. That is why a clean bill signed over visibly damaged cargo cannot be unwound later.
  • Shipper's guarantee. Under Article III rule 5 the shipper guarantees the accuracy of quantity and weight and indemnifies the carrier — which is the legal reason draft surveys and tank dips matter, since they are the carrier's independent check on the figure it is being asked to certify.
  • "To order" versus named consignee. A "to order" bill is negotiable by endorsement and delivery and is what makes mid-voyage resale possible. A straight bill names the consignee and is not negotiable. Presentation of an original remains the safe operational rule for both (see Part 19 Topic 2 and The Rafaela S).
  • Misdelivery. Delivery without production of an original bill is outside standard P&I cover. Commercial pressure from a charterer does not change that, and a letter of indemnity moves the risk onto the indemnifier's balance sheet rather than removing it.
⛽ The Bunker Quantity Dispute — Full Procedure

Scenario. On an on-hire survey a 300,000 DWT VLCC shows a 180 tonne discrepancy in VLSFO between the outgoing figure and the incoming charterer's independent surveyor. The allegation is either concealed fuel or miscalibrated flow meters. The dispute is settled by measurement, not argument.

  • Step 1 — sound everything, manually. Every fuel oil bunker tank, settling tank, service tank, overflow tank, drain tank and sludge tank, using the vessel's certified sounding tapes and water-finding paste. Manual soundings override automated gauge readings, which drift and are frequently uncalibrated. Record trim and list at the time of sounding and apply the correct sounding table corrections.
  • Step 2 — correct for temperature. Measure the actual temperature of each tank. Take the observed volume from the tables, apply the volume correction factor from ASTM Table 54B to obtain the volume at 15 °C, then multiply by the density at 15 °C from the Bunker Delivery Note. For metric tonnes in air, use density at 15 °C minus 0.0011.
  • Step 3 — account for what cannot be pumped. Unpumpable dead bottoms below the suction bellmouth, fuel held in the transfer and treatment piping, in the purifiers and heaters, and in the service system. This is the single commonest cause of a surveyor's figure reading low, and it is a legitimate part of the quantity on board.
  • Step 4 — check the metering. Calibration certificates for the Coriolis mass flow meters, physical integrity of anti-tamper seals on the fuel system, and consistency between the daily engine room logs, the flow meter totals and the Oil Record Book Part I entries.
  • Step 5 — resolve or protest. Present the audited tank-by-tank spreadsheet, jointly signed if the surveyor agrees. If not, sign the statement of quantity under protest, annexing the tank-by-tank differences, and issue a Letter of Protest to the owner and the P&I club the same day.

Why bareboat redelivery is the hardest. Under time and voyage charters the owner has retained technical management, so the machinery history is the owner's own record. Under bareboat the charterer has run the ship — crewing, maintenance, spares, possibly a different Class and flag. Fair wear and tear is accepted; degradation beyond it is a claim.

🎓 CE Oral Tip (Senthil): Senthil's version of the bunker question is deliberately confrontational: "the surveyor says you are 180 tonnes short — what do you do?" He is testing whether you defend the ship with method or with assertion. Lead with the measurement sequence — manual soundings of every tank including sludge and overflow, temperature of each tank, ASTM 54B correction to 15 °C, density at 15 °C less 0.0011 for weight in air, then dead bottoms and system hold-up. Only after that, the protest: sign under protest with tank-by-tank annexure and issue a Letter of Protest the same day. Never say you would refuse to sign anything at all — refusing outright delays delivery and creates a separate claim.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. The charterer's surveyor records a bunker ROB significantly below your engine room figures at an on-hire survey. What do you do?
I would not sign the surveyor's figure as presented, and I would not simply refuse to sign either, since that delays delivery and creates a separate claim. I would request a joint re-measurement and conduct it methodically. First, manual soundings of every tank on board using the ship's certified sounding tapes and water-finding paste — bunker tanks, settling, service, overflow, drain and sludge tanks — with trim and list recorded and the sounding table corrections applied. Second, take the temperature of each tank and correct the observed volume to 15 degrees using the volume correction factor from ASTM Table 54B, then apply the density at 15 degrees from the Bunker Delivery Note, less 0.0011 for weight in air. Third, account separately for unpumpable dead bottoms below the suction bellmouths and for fuel held in the transfer and treatment piping, purifiers, heaters and service system — this is usually where a surveyor's figure goes low. Fourth, produce the flow meter calibration certificates and confirm the anti-tamper seals are intact. If the surveyor still will not align, I sign the statement of quantity under formal protest with a tank-by-tank annexure of the differences, and issue a Letter of Protest to the owner and the P&I club the same day.
Q2. When does a bill of lading stop being merely prima facie evidence of what was received?
Under Article III rule 4 of the Hague-Visby Rules, the bill of lading is prima facie evidence of receipt of the goods as described, meaning that as between the carrier and the original shipper the carrier may prove the contrary — for example that the actual weight differed from the declared figure. However, proof to the contrary is not admissible once the bill has been transferred to a third party acting in good faith. At that point the description becomes conclusive against the carrier. This is the practical reason a Master clauses a bill rather than signing clean over visibly damaged or short cargo: once the bill is endorsed to a good-faith purchaser, the opportunity to contest the description is gone. Article III rule 5 gives the carrier a counterbalance, in that the shipper is deemed to guarantee the accuracy of the marks, number, quantity and weight and indemnifies the carrier against inaccuracies — but that is a claim against the shipper, not a defence against the endorsee.
Q3. Why is a bareboat redelivery survey more demanding than a time charter off-hire survey?
Because the technical history of the ship during the charter is not the owner's. Under a time or voyage charter the owner retains technical management and crewing, so the maintenance record and machinery condition are the owner's own and continuous. Under a bareboat charter the charterer has taken possession and run the vessel entirely — its own crew, its own planned maintenance system, its own spares policy, and often a different Class society and flag. The redelivery survey therefore has to establish the actual condition of the asset from scratch: open up machinery to assess wear against running hours, inspect tank internals and coating condition, review the complete maintenance and class records for gaps or deferred work, and verify spares and consumables inventories. The commercial purpose is to distinguish fair wear and tear, which the owner accepts, from degradation beyond it, which is a claim against the charterer. It is also a high-risk PSC period, because a change of manager and ISM Company is a targeting factor.
Q4. Give the calculation sequence from a sounding to a bunker figure in metric tonnes.
Take the manual sounding or ullage with the tape, note trim and list, and enter the tank calibration table to obtain the observed volume, applying the trim and list corrections. Measure the tank temperature. Apply the volume correction factor for the observed temperature and density, from ASTM Table 54B for generalised products, to convert observed volume to volume at the standard temperature of 15 degrees Celsius. Multiply that corrected volume by the density at 15 degrees taken from the Bunker Delivery Note. If the figure is required as metric tonnes in air, which is the commercial convention, subtract 0.0011 from the density at 15 degrees before multiplying. Finally, add the quantities held in the treatment and transfer system and identify unpumpable dead bottoms separately, so the total on board and the pumpable quantity are both clear on the statement.

The four usual explanations

First, temperature. VLSFO in a heated tank at 50 °C occupies materially more volume than the same mass at 15 °C. If the surveyor read observed volume and applied a density figure without the volume correction factor, or applied it in the wrong direction, a large apparent difference appears with no fuel having moved. Second, dead bottoms. On a VLCC the volume below the suction bellmouth across all bunker tanks is substantial and is genuinely on board even though it cannot be pumped — it belongs in the total, identified separately. Third, system hold-up: transfer lines, settling and service tanks, purifier bowls, heaters and the supply and circulating system hold a real and calculable quantity. Fourth, tables and trim: an uncorrected sounding at significant trim on a long tank produces a large error.

What almost never explains it

Concealment. The allegation is made because it is the only explanation a surveyor can offer for a difference they cannot reconcile, but it requires the fuel to physically exist somewhere unmeasured. Sounding every tank on board, including sludge, overflow and drain tanks, closes that argument definitively — which is why the audit must be exhaustive rather than confined to the bunker tanks in dispute.

Making the audit stand up

Present the result as a tank-by-tank spreadsheet: tank, sounding, trim/list correction, observed volume, temperature, VCF, volume at 15 °C, density at 15 °C, mass in air, and a remarks column for dead bottom or system content. Invite the surveyor to sound jointly and co-sign each line. A jointly signed spreadsheet ends the dispute at source; a unilateral one still stands as evidence, but only if it was produced on the day.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

Liner container tonnage rarely sees full bareboat handovers, but redelivery and delivery bunker surveys on period charters are routine, and the same discipline applies. The practical habit worth building is to keep a standing, maintained bunker calculation spreadsheet with the tank tables, VCF logic and system hold-up figures already embedded, so that on the day of the survey the calculation takes minutes rather than being improvised under commercial pressure.

📖 Definition

The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification (HSSC) is the IMO framework that aligns the survey intervals and certificate validity periods of SOLAS, Load Lines, MARPOL and the associated mandatory codes into a single five-year cycle with common windows and terminology. Before it, each convention ran its own schedule and the ship was interrupted repeatedly through the year. Continuous Machinery Survey (CSM) and Planned Maintenance System (PMS) survey arrangements then distribute the machinery examination burden across that cycle instead of concentrating it at renewal.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The survey calendar is the Chief Engineer's operational calendar. Every window has a hard edge, and a certificate that ceases to be valid stops the ship — commercially this is indistinguishable from a machinery breakdown.
  • CSM is the difference between a manageable rolling workload and a crushing renewal-year overhaul programme. Managing the CSM ledger is a planning skill examiners test directly.
  • Survey credit for work done at sea is real money and real time saved, but only if the documentation package — measurements, photographs, running hours, logbook entries — is assembled at the time of the overhaul, not reconstructed for the surveyor.
  • Safety equipment deficiencies are the highest-frequency detention items in the engine department's area: emergency fire pump, lifeboat engine, quick-closing valves.

🛠 Bottom Inspections — Drydock and In-Water Survey

A minimum of two inspections of the outside of the ship's bottom must be carried out within each five-year certificate period, and the interval between any two consecutive inspections must not exceed 36 months.

  • Drydocking survey. Hull plating condition and thickness, rudder and pintle clearances, propeller and shaft condition, stern tube bearing wear (poker gauge), sea chest gratings and internal coatings, sacrificial anode wastage, anchor cables ranged and gauged.
  • In-water survey (IWS). Carried out afloat by approved divers or ROV with real-time video and a surveyor in attendance. Class rules generally permit an IWS to substitute for one of the two bottom inspections, typically for ships below about 15 years of age, provided the ship carries the appropriate class notation. The usual notation prerequisites are a high-durability underwater coating system, clear external hull markings referencing internal frames and bulkheads so that findings can be located precisely, and arrangements allowing sea inlets and underwater valves to be blanked and examined from outside while afloat.
  • Age limit caveat. The 15-year threshold is a classification society rule, not convention text, and differs between societies. State it as class practice, not as SOLAS.
⚙ Continuous Machinery Survey and PMS Survey

Under a CSM arrangement, the machinery items that would otherwise all be examined at renewal are distributed across the five-year cycle so that roughly 20% are surveyed each year. The surveyor attends annually to audit completed items and witness key open-ups. The alternative is a PMS survey arrangement, in which the ship's approved computerised planned maintenance system, audited periodically, provides the basis for credit against running hours and condition rather than against a fixed list.

Learn the substance — attended open-up, Chief Engineer's survey, approved alternative — and check the coding used by the ship's own society rather than quoting a code letter that may not exist in that society's system.

🧯 Safety Equipment Survey and Form E

Cargo ships of 500 GT and above must hold a Cargo Ship Safety Equipment Certificate issued under SOLAS Chapter I Regulation 8, supplemented by the Record of Equipment, Form E. Form E lists the exact type, quantity and specification of the equipment the ship is required to carry, and functions as the checklist against which both the survey and any PSC inspection are conducted. Any discrepancy between Form E and the physical equipment on board is a statutory deficiency.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. An auxiliary engine piston is pulled at sea. How do you obtain survey credit under a CSM arrangement?
Class rules generally allow a qualified and approved Chief Engineer to carry out the examination of certain machinery items at sea, with the attending surveyor granting credit at the next visit on the strength of the documentation. To secure that credit I would assemble the package at the time of the overhaul, not afterwards. First, an official entry in the planned maintenance system and the engine room logbook recording the date, the running hours at overhaul and the reason. Second, complete measurement records taken with calibrated instruments — liner wear at the defined measuring positions, piston ring vertical and radial clearances, gudgeon pin and bearing dimensions, all against the manufacturer's new and maximum-wear figures. Third, dated photographs of the components before cleaning and again after measurement, showing crown condition, ring groove condition and liner cross-hatching. Fourth, the part numbers and batch details of everything fitted. I would then present the compiled package at the next annual attendance so the item is credited without opening the unit again. The exact code letter used to record the credit differs between classification societies, so I would confirm the notation with our own society.
Q2. What is Form E and how does it relate to the Safety Equipment Survey?
Form E is the Record of Equipment for Cargo Ship Safety Equipment, the mandatory supplement to the Cargo Ship Safety Equipment Certificate issued under SOLAS Chapter I Regulation 8 to cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage and above. It lists the exact type, quantity and specification of the life-saving appliances, fire-fighting appliances, navigational equipment and signalling equipment the ship is required to carry. During the Safety Equipment Survey and during any Port State Control inspection it is the primary checklist: the inspector works down Form E and verifies each item physically. Any discrepancy between the record and what is actually on board — a missing immersion suit, an expired pyrotechnic, a lifebuoy light that does not work — is a statutory deficiency, and because Form E is a certificate supplement rather than a ship's document, the discrepancy goes directly against the certificate.
Q3. Explain the requirements for bottom inspections and when an in-water survey may substitute for drydocking.
A minimum of two inspections of the outside of the ship's bottom must be carried out within each five-year certificate period, and the interval between two consecutive inspections must not exceed 36 months. Classification society rules generally permit an in-water survey to substitute for one of those two inspections, typically for ships below about fifteen years of age, provided the ship carries the appropriate in-water survey class notation. The notation prerequisites are usually a high-durability underwater coating system in good condition, external hull markings that reference internal frames and bulkheads so any finding can be located precisely, and arrangements permitting sea inlets and underwater valves to be blanked and examined from outside while afloat. The survey itself is conducted by approved divers or an ROV with real-time video and a surveyor in attendance, covering hull plating, rudder and pintle clearances, propeller condition and sea chest gratings. For older ships both inspections normally have to be carried out in dry dock. The age threshold is class rule, not SOLAS text, and differs between societies.
Q4. What happens if an annual survey window is missed?
Under SOLAS Chapter I Regulation 14, the annual survey must be carried out within three months before or after each anniversary date of the certificate. If the survey is not completed within that period, the certificate ceases to be valid. It becomes valid again on satisfactory completion of the survey. What it does not automatically do is trigger a complete re-survey and re-issue — whether anything beyond the outstanding survey is required is a decision for the flag State on the facts, and there are limited harmonisation and extension provisions in the HSSC guidelines for specific circumstances such as a ship not in a port where a survey can be carried out. The practical position is that the ship should not sail on a certificate that has ceased to be valid; Port State Control treats an invalid statutory certificate as a detainable deficiency, and the P&I and hull cover may also be affected.

The problem

A 180,000 DWT Capesize enters its fifth anniversary window with fifteen CSM line items outstanding, a drydock booked, and a Safety Equipment Certificate to renew. The failure mode is predictable: the CSM ledger has drifted, the heavy items were left to last because they need the ship stopped, and the yard period is not long enough to absorb them all.

Sequencing

Split the outstanding items into three groups. Items that can only be done in dock — tailshaft, rudder, sea valves, anodes, hull. Items that can be done alongside with the plant down — main air compressors, turbochargers, coolers. Items that were already done at sea and only need the documentation presented — these should never be occupying yard time at all. Clear the third group in the pre-arrival submission, so the surveyor's attendance in dock is spent on the first group.

Under-water clearance measurements

Rudder stock palm and pintle clearances by dial gauge against the class wear limits; record the datum and the measuring positions so the next docking is comparable. Stern tube bearing wear down by poker gauge, taken at the same relative shaft angular position each time — a wear-down record is only useful if it is measured consistently. Propeller blade edges, cavitation erosion, and cone and cap security. Sea chest internal coatings and grating security, and anode wastage expressed as a percentage so the next docking interval can be planned from it.

The safety demonstration

The surveyor will want to see systems work, not read that they were tested. Simulate the dead-ship condition and time the emergency generator onto the emergency switchboard — it must be inside 45 seconds. Run the emergency fire pump from cold and deliver the required jets at the required pressure. Function-test the fixed fire system alarms and the ventilation dampers and closing appliances together, because the failure is usually in the interlock rather than in either component.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

The discipline that prevents the whole problem is monthly review of the CSM ledger against the remaining months of the cycle, not annual review. If the ledger is reviewed monthly, every item that could have been done at sea is done at sea, and the yard period is spent only on what genuinely requires the ship out of the water.
