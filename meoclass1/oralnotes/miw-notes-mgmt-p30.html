Cyber Risk on Ships — Definitions, IT, OT and IIoT, Relevant Systems, Attack Types and Stages, and the CIA Impact Model

📖 Definition

Maritime cyber risk is the risk that a ship's computer-based systems — the business IT that handles information and the OT that monitors and controls machinery and physical processes — are compromised, disrupted or manipulated, whether by a deliberate cyber attack or by an accidental cyber incident, with consequences for the safety of the ship, the crew and the environment. IMO defines a cyber incident as 'an occurrence or a sequence of occurrences, which actually or potentially results in adverse consequences to a CBS or to the information that they process, store or transmit, and which may require a response action to mitigate the consequences' (MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4). Its impact is judged against confidentiality, integrity and availability — and on board, availability and integrity of OT are safety issues.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The engine room is now a network. Main-engine control, the alarm and monitoring system, power management, ballast and boiler controls are computer-based OT. A cyber event can stop propulsion or blind the watch just as a mechanical failure can.
  • The Chief Engineer owns most of the OT. Vendor laptops, service engineers' USB sticks, remote-diagnostic links and IIoT gateways are all engine-room entry points — and the CE decides who plugs in what.
  • Examiners test the distinctions. Incident vs attack, IT vs OT, untargeted vs targeted, and why OT ranks availability and integrity above confidentiality — a candidate who blurs them loses the question.
  • The threat is real and recent. NotPetya cost Maersk roughly USD 250–300 million in 2017, and a deep-draft ship arriving in New York in February 2019 had its network degraded by malware (USCG Marine Safety Alert 06-19).

🔎 Cyber System, Cyber Incident and Cyber Attack

Cyber system. Uday's definition is the BIMCO glossary's: 'any combination of facilities, equipment, personnel, procedures and communications integrated to provide cyber services' — business systems, control systems and access-control systems are examples. IMO and IACS now use the term computer-based system (CBS): a programmable electronic device, or a set of them, organised to achieve a purpose. On board, CBSs include both IT and OT.

Cyber incidentCyber attack
IntentMay be intentional or accidentalAlways deliberate — an 'offensive manoeuvre'
Data breach?Not necessarily — it may only affect availability or integrityMay or may not steal data
RelationshipEvery successful attack causes an incidentNot every incident is an attack
Source of definitionIMO MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4 para 2.1BIMCO/ICS Guidelines glossary (IMO does not define it)

Accidental incident — the corrected example. The source's case of a service technician whose boiler-panel software update 'crashed' is a cyber incident only if there is a cyber cause. IACS UR E26 excludes plain system failures. The classic accidental case is a service engineer's infected USB stick or laptop plugged into the panel during the update: nobody intended harm and no data is stolen, but the control software is corrupted.

🏭 IT, OT and IIoT — Where the Engine Room Sits
What it isShipboard examples
IT — information technologyHardware, software and communications that store and process data as information; it does not control physical processesE-mail, crew payroll and certificates, cargo documents, the company ERP
OT — operational technologyHardware and software that directly monitor or control physical devices and processes (IMO's example: main-engine oil temperature sent to the control room)ME control and safety system, alarm and monitoring, power management, boiler control panel, ballast and steering controls

Uday's two examples are good ones once labelled: a stand-alone boiler control panel with on-site access only is isolated OT, although it can still be infected through removable media; reefer temperature monitoring from shore is IIoT. BIMCO's rule for IIoT is blunt: because OT often cannot be patched, the OT-to-IT interface needs a firewall as a minimum.

IoT as a technology — its architecture, advantages and drawbacks — is in Part 31 · Topic 1; this topic treats it only as an attack surface.

🎯 Untargeted and Targeted Attacks — Techniques Corrected

BIMCO divides threats into untargeted — the ship or company is one of many potential victims, or collateral damage (NotPetya is BIMCO's shipping example) — and targeted, where the ship or company is the intended target, usually after reconnaissance. Uday's grouping of techniques follows BIMCO's 2020 edition; v5 (2024) lists the same techniques without assigning them to either group.

🪜 The Four Stages of a (Targeted) Cyber Incident
SURVEY / RECONNAISSANCE → DELIVERY → BREACH → PIVOT open sources, social phishing, USB, exploit a use the compromised media, crew lists fake sites, weakness; system to reach others vendor updates depth set by — e.g. IT network → OT the defences

These are BIMCO's stages for a targeted attack. The fourth is pivot, not 'affect': the attacker uses the first compromised system to reach otherwise inaccessible ones — which is why a flat network that joins the office PC to the engine-room systems is dangerous. How far a breach goes depends, as Uday says, on the firewall and the other security measures in place.

⚖️ Impact Assessment — the CIA Model, Read for OT
Loss of …MeaningShip example
ConfidentialityUnauthorised access to or disclosure of informationCrew, cargo or passenger data stolen
IntegrityImproper modification or destruction of information or systemsA manipulated engine sensor value; altered ECDIS chart data; a changed cargo or stability figure
AvailabilityLoss of timely and reliable access to information or systemsA frozen alarm and monitoring system; ECDIS unavailable; PMS servers encrypted by ransomware

The engineer's reading. Office IT usually ranks confidentiality first. Shipboard OT puts safety first, then availability and integrity, with confidentiality least critical (NIST SP 800-82r3; BIMCO v5). Stolen engine data is a commercial problem; a false lube-oil pressure reading or a frozen alarm system is a safety problem.

🛠️ What It Means in the Engine Room — Practical Controls

Uday's notes stop at definitions. The controls below are what IMO now lists as minimum controls (MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4, Protect), what IACS UR E26 requires on ships contracted from 1 July 2024, and what BIMCO recommends. The source document is named for each, because their legal force differs (see Topic 2).

  • Segment OT from IT — firewalls and separate zones; navigation and machinery systems not on the office network (IMO Rev.4 2.2.2 and Protect; E26 security zones).
  • Removable media — no unscanned USB sticks on OT; scan or validate software before loading (IMO Protect; E26 makes a removable-media policy mandatory on new ships).
  • Remote and vendor access — no remote session until accepted on board, ability to terminate it from the ship, every session logged; MFA for access from untrusted networks (E26; BIMCO).
  • Accounts — unique log-ins, no shared generic accounts, least privilege, accounts removed when people leave (IMO Protect; BIMCO).
  • Patching with care — OT updates only after supplier testing and onboard agreement; OT often cannot be patched quickly, and a change may need class re-approval (E26; BIMCO; NIST).
  • Backups and recovery — current, tested backups of critical configurations, and a hard-copy incident response plan on board (IMO Recover; E26).
  • People — annual basic cyber training for all, OT-specific training for OT users, familiarisation on joining (IMO Rev.4 Protect — recommendatory).
🎓 CE Oral Tip (Simon): Simon asks 'What is the difference between a cyber incident and a cyber attack?' Answer in one line each — an incident is any occurrence with actual or potential adverse consequences to a computer-based system, intentional or not; an attack is a deliberate offensive act — and give an accidental example: the service engineer's infected USB stick in the boiler panel. If he moves to IT and OT, say IT handles information and OT controls machinery, name three OT systems in your own engine room, and finish with the priority: 'On OT, availability and integrity are safety; confidentiality comes last.' If he asks for the functional elements, there are now six, starting with Govern (Topic 2).
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Distinguish a cyber incident from a cyber attack, with a shipboard example of each.
IMO defines a cyber incident as an occurrence, or a sequence of occurrences, that actually or potentially results in adverse consequences to a computer-based system or to the information it processes, stores or transmits, and that may require a response action. It can be intentional or accidental, and it need not involve any data breach — it may only make a system unavailable or unreliable. A cyber attack, in the industry guidelines' wording, is an offensive manoeuvre that targets IT and OT systems or networks and attempts to compromise, destroy or access company and ship systems and data; it is always deliberate. Every successful attack produces an incident, but not every incident is an attack. An accidental incident: a service engineer connects an infected USB stick to the boiler control panel during a software update and corrupts the control software. An attack: a phishing e-mail to the ship's office PC installs ransomware that encrypts the planned-maintenance and stores servers.
Q2. What are IT, OT and IIoT? Why does the difference matter to the Chief Engineer?
Information technology is the hardware, software and communications that store and process data as information — e-mail, payroll, cargo documents — and it does not control physical processes. Operational technology is the hardware and software that directly monitor or control physical devices and processes — the main-engine control and safety system, alarm and monitoring, power management, boiler and ballast controls. The industrial internet of things is instrumentation and connected sensors on machinery; on board it means OT that is connected through IT or satellite links so that it can be monitored or supported from shore, such as reefer temperatures or engine performance. The difference matters because OT is where a cyber event becomes a safety event, because OT often cannot be patched or rebooted like office IT, and because every IIoT link is a path from the internet into the machinery, so it must be firewalled and segmented.
Q3. Describe the stages of a cyber incident.
The industry guidelines describe four stages of a targeted attack. First, survey or reconnaissance: the attacker gathers information from open sources and social media about the company, the ship and the crew. Second, delivery: the attacker tries to get into the systems — through phishing e-mails, infected removable media or software updates, fake websites or the company's online services. Third, breach: a vulnerability is exploited; how far the attacker gets depends on the firewalls and other defences, and at this point data may be copied or altered or a system taken over. Fourth, pivot: the attacker uses the compromised system to reach other systems that were not directly accessible — for example from the office network into OT. That is why segmentation between IT and OT is the single most important engine-room defence.
Q4. Explain the CIA model as applied to a ship's OT.
The CIA model assesses the impact of a cyber event on confidentiality, integrity and availability. Loss of confidentiality is unauthorised access to or disclosure of information, such as crew or cargo data. Loss of integrity is improper modification of information or systems, such as a manipulated engine sensor value or altered chart data. Loss of availability is the loss of timely and reliable access, such as a frozen alarm and monitoring system or encrypted servers. For office IT, confidentiality usually comes first. For shipboard OT, safety comes first, then availability and integrity, with confidentiality least critical, because a false reading or a dead control system can cause a casualty while stolen engine data cannot.
Q5. Name the shipboard systems IMO now lists as relevant to cyber risk.
MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4 lists nine: bridge systems, including navigation, safety and communications; cargo, bunkering, lubrication, ballast and other pumping and management systems; propulsion, fuel and machinery management and power control; security, access control and surveillance; passenger and crew servicing and management; public networks for passengers, crew and contractors; administrative and crew welfare systems; ship–port interfaces; and ship-to-shore integrated systems such as remote control or MASS. The older 2017 list had eight 'vulnerable systems' and did not include the last two.

Situation (illustrative)

At anchorage a maker's service engineer boards to update the alarm and monitoring software. He brings his own laptop and a USB stick with the update, and asks for the engine-room network password so that he can download a patch from the maker's server.

What the Chief Engineer checks

Authorisation. Is the visit and the software change planned under the company's management-of-change procedure, with the superintendent and the maker confirming the version? Media. The USB stick is scanned on the ship's stand-alone scanning station before it goes anywhere near OT; the update file's integrity or digital signature is checked. Network. No connection of the laptop to the OT network beyond the system being serviced, and no internet bridge through the engine-room network — if a download is unavoidable, it goes through the controlled IT route. Fallback. A current backup of the system configuration exists, and the engine can be run on local or backup control during the work. Record. The session is logged, the old and new software versions are recorded, and the system is tested before the engineer leaves.

Lesson

Most shipboard cyber incidents need no hacker — only a well-meaning person and an uncontrolled device. The Chief Engineer's controls are procedural as much as technical, and they belong in the SMS (Topic 2).

📖 Definition

Cyber risk management in the SMS is the requirement — set by IMO resolution MSC.428(98) and enforced through ISM audits — that a company's Safety Management System treat cyber risk like any other identified risk under the ISM Code: assessed, safeguarded against, assigned to responsible people, trained for, covered by emergency procedures, reported and corrected. The recommended method is IMO's Guidelines on maritime cyber risk management (MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4), built on six functional elements — Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. For new ships, IACS UR E26 and E27 add class requirements for the cyber resilience of the ship and its equipment.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Senthil asks where cyber lives in the SMS. The answer is the ISM clause map — and an examiner who knows the code will notice if you put company policy under 3.2.
  • Layer discipline wins marks. MSC.428(98) and MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 are IMO recommendatory instruments working through the mandatory ISM Code; E26/E27 are class requirements for newbuildings; the DGS circular is an Indian flag rule; BIMCO is industry guidance. Calling all of them 'IMO requirements' is the commonest error.
  • The guidance has moved twice since the notes were written. Rev.3 (2025) added Govern; Rev.4 (May 2026) is current. 'Five functional elements' is now out of date.
  • Insurance shapes the risk. The Chief Engineer should know that malicious cyber damage to the ship may fall outside the H&M policy unless it is bought back or covered separately.

📜 MSC.428(98) — What the Resolution Actually Says

Resolution MSC.428(98), Maritime Cyber Risk Management in Safety Management Systems, was adopted by the Maritime Safety Committee on 16 June 2017. Its four operative paragraphs:

VerbWhat it does
AFFIRMSthat an approved SMS should take into account cyber risk management in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code
ENCOURAGESAdministrations to ensure that cyber risks are appropriately addressed in SMS no later than the first annual verification of the company's DOC after 1 January 2021
ACKNOWLEDGESthe precautions that may be needed to preserve the confidentiality of certain aspects of cyber risk management
REQUESTSMember States to bring the resolution to the attention of all stakeholders

Legal force — say it precisely. The resolution does not amend the ISM Code or SOLAS. It records IMO's view that the mandatory ISM Code — which already requires every identified risk to be assessed and safeguarded against — covers cyber risk. In practice it bites through flag and RO ISM audits: a missing cyber element is raised as an ISM non-conformity. The 2021 date is not 'from 1 January 2021' but the first annual DOC verification after it.

🧭 MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3 — Now Six Functional Elements (Rev.4)

The guidelines Uday cites date from 5 July 2017. They have been revised four times: Rev.1 (June 2021), Rev.2 (7 June 2022, reference standards), Rev.3 (4 April 2025, a major rewrite) and Rev.4 (28 May 2026, current). Uday's five elements, 'IPDRR', are the 2017–2022 text. Since Rev.3 there are six, listed in para 3.5 as concurrent and continuous, not sequential:

The guidelines say of themselves that they are high-level recommendations; the 'Govern' element mirrors the NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024). Uday's other list — that cyber risk management should identify roles, identify critical systems, implement measures and exercise a contingency plan — is from the industry BIMCO/ICS Guidelines (v5 adds third parties, regulatory compliance and incident reporting under the SMS), not from the IMO text.

The original April 2022 versions were withdrawn and never came into force. E26/E27 do not apply to existing ships, except as voluntary guidance, and they do not replace the company's ISM obligation: they are a class layer. IMO's Rev.3/Rev.4 guidelines list them as reference standards, which does not make them IMO requirements. The system-level vs component-level distinction is in Current Topics · Q6.

🇮🇳 India — DGS Engg. Circular No. 06 of 2017

The Indian instrument is DGS (ISM Cell) Engg. Circular No. 06 of 2017, dated 6 November 2017 (corrigendum 20 November 2017), Implementation of Cyber-security risk mitigation measures on board Indian Flag Ships. It is an audit and verification procedure within the ISM regime, not a technical standard, and DGMA still listed it as current on 8 October 2026. No later ship-cyber circular was found.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What does resolution MSC.428(98) require, and what is its legal force?
MSC.428(98), Maritime Cyber Risk Management in Safety Management Systems, was adopted by the Maritime Safety Committee on 16 June 2017. It affirms that an approved safety management system should take into account cyber risk management in accordance with the objectives and functional requirements of the ISM Code, and it encourages Administrations to ensure that cyber risks are appropriately addressed in safety management systems no later than the first annual verification of the company's Document of Compliance after 1 January 2021. It does not amend SOLAS or the ISM Code; it is IMO's interpretation that the mandatory ISM Code, which already requires a company to assess all identified risks and establish safeguards, covers cyber risk. It is therefore enforced through flag and recognised-organisation ISM audits, where a missing cyber element is raised as a non-conformity.
Q2. What are the functional elements of cyber risk management in the current IMO guidelines?
The current guidelines are MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.4 of 28 May 2026. Since Rev.3 of April 2025 they list six functional elements, which are concurrent and continuous rather than sequential. Govern: establish and monitor the risk-management strategy and policies, define roles and responsibilities and ensure business continuity. Identify: determine the current cyber risk to the ship and its ship–port interfaces, with an inventory of systems and a risk assessment. Protect: implement risk controls and contingency planning — unique credentials, multi-factor authentication, segmentation of OT from IT, control of removable media, training and backups. Detect: detect a cyber incident in time. Respond: practised plans to provide resilience and restore systems, including reporting to the Administration. Recover: restore onboard systems and networks and find the root cause. The original 2017 guidelines had only the last five.
Q3. List the ISM Code clauses under which cyber risk is incorporated into the SMS.
Risk assessment and safeguards under 1.2.2.2; the safety and environmental-protection policy under 2.1; responsibility, authority and interrelation of personnel under 3.2; adequate resources and shore support for the designated person under 3.3; training under 6.5, with familiarisation under 6.3; procedures for key shipboard operations that rely on OT under 7; emergency procedures for cyber incidents under 8.1, exercised under 8.2; reporting of cyber incidents under 9.1; corrective action under 9.2; identification of critical OT and measures to promote its reliability under 10.3; and integration of those measures, such as backups, into the maintenance routine under 10.4. The widely copied industry table puts policy under 3.2 and responsibility under 3.3, which is wrong.
Q4. Explain IACS UR E26 and E27 and how they relate to MSC.428(98).
UR E26 covers the cyber resilience of the ship as a whole — security zones and segmentation of OT, control of remote access and removable media, an incident response plan on board, backup and restore, and a cyber-resilience test procedure verified by class. UR E27 covers the security capabilities of the individual computer-based systems and equipment provided by suppliers, with type approval through class. Both are at Rev.1 of 2023 and apply to ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024; for existing ships they are only guidance. They are class requirements for the design and construction of new ships, whereas MSC.428(98) concerns the company's management system on every ship. One does not replace the other.
Q5. Is a shipowner insured if a cyber attack damages the ship?
Not automatically. Hull and machinery policies have long carried the Institute Cyber Attack Exclusion Clause, CL.380, which excludes loss caused by the malicious use of a computer, software or virus as a means of inflicting harm, so damage from a hacked system may fall outside the H&M cover, while accidental cyber losses were not excluded by that clause. Newer wordings exclude malicious cyber and affirm non-malicious cyber. P&I is different: International Group cover has no general cyber exclusion, so third-party liabilities caused by a cyber event are covered unless the attack falls within the war or terrorism exclusion. The owner can close the hull gap by buying back the exclusion or buying an affirmative cyber hull product; one example is the Cyber Hull Cover marketed by SIGCo, the Bermuda company originally set up in 1996 for OPA 90 COFR guaranties.

Situation (illustrative)

During the ship's intermediate SMC audit the auditor asks the Chief Engineer to show how cyber risk is managed in the engine room. The company's SMS has a one-page 'IT policy' covering e-mail use.

What the auditor looks for

Govern and responsibility. Who is accountable for cyber on board and ashore (ISM 3.2), and does the DPA have shore IT and OT support (3.3)? Identify. An inventory of the ship's OT and IT systems and a cyber risk assessment (1.2.2.2, 10.3). Protect. Procedures for removable media, vendor laptops and remote access, and separation of OT from the office network (7). Training. Records of cyber awareness training and familiarisation (6.5, 6.3). Respond and recover. A cyber contingency in the emergency procedures, exercised (8.1, 8.2); recent backups of critical configurations (10.4). Reporting. Cyber events reported and investigated like any non-conformity (9.1, 9.2).

Outcome and lesson

An e-mail policy alone does not meet MSC.428(98): the auditor raises a non-conformity. For an Indian-flag ship the company's DOC compliance with Engg. Circular 06/2017 is also checked. The fix is not new software but an SMS that treats cyber like fire or flooding — assessed, assigned, drilled and reported.
