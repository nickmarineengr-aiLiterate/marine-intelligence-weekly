Part 22 · Topic 1

Commercial Vetting Frameworks — SIRE 2.0, CDI-Marine and TMSA-3

SIRE 2.0 Architecture · Graded Responses · CDI · TMSA-3 KPIs Uday pp. 526–533 (approx.) Examiner: Senthil ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Two clarifications, no factual reversals. (1) SIRE 2.0 status and timing. The draft presents SIRE 2.0 as "modern" against VIQ7 as "legacy" without dating the transition. SIRE 2.0 was developed by OCIMF over several years and phased into full operation during 2024, replacing the static VIQ7 questionnaire regime. Candidates should say when, not just what. (2) Question counts. The draft's "~300 static questions" for VIQ7 is an approximation of the order of magnitude and is retained as such; the SIRE 2.0 set is compiled dynamically per inspection from a much larger question library, so no fixed count exists and none is stated here. (3) TMSA version. TMSA-3 (13 elements) is presented as current. OCIMF revises TMSA periodically and the self-assessment is tightly coupled to the SIRE regime, so the applicable edition should be confirmed against the company's own submission before an examination. Carried forward from Part 21 Topic 3: vetting is a commercial risk-assessment practice, not a statutory requirement — no IMO instrument mandates SIRE, CDI or TMSA.
📖 Definition

Commercial vetting is the process by which cargo interests, charterers and terminals satisfy themselves that a ship and its operator are an acceptable risk, independently of statutory compliance. SIRE (Ship Inspection Report Programme), operated by OCIMF since 1993, is the dominant scheme for tankers. CDI serves the chemical trades. TMSA is the operator's own structured self-assessment of its shore management, verified against what an inspector finds on board. A ship can hold every statutory certificate and still be commercially unemployable on a poor vetting record.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • SIRE 2.0 grades the crew, not only the equipment. A correctly maintained system operated by an engineer who cannot explain it now scores "Not As Expected" — which changes what preparation means.
  • The question set is no longer predictable. Rotational and campaign questions mean the engine room cannot be prepared to a checklist; it has to be in order across the board.
  • TMSA is the company's declaration of its own maturity. A SIRE inspector cross-checks shipboard reality against that declaration, so a gap between office claims and engine room practice damages the operator far beyond the single ship.
  • The findings that end vetting acceptance are almost all engine department items: bypassed interlocks, unauthorised jumpers, temporary pipe repairs, an OWS with a questionable history.

🧩 SIRE 2.0 Architecture

SIRE 2.0 replaced a fixed paper questionnaire with a dynamically compiled, risk-targeted question set delivered on an inspector's handheld device. Three consequences follow, and each is examinable.

[OCIMF question library + vessel factors, history, operator TMSA data] │ dynamically compiled per inspection │ ┌───────────────────┬───────────────────┬───────────────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ CORE QUESTIONS ROTATIONAL QS CAMPAIGN QS every inspection vary between industry-wide themes (critical safety inspections selected by OCIMF and pollution) (unpredictable) └───────────────────┴───────────────────┘ │ each question assessed across FOUR elements, graded not binary: HUMAN · PROCESS · HARDWARE · PHOTOGRAPH
  • Core questions — mandatory on every inspection; the critical safety, pollution-prevention and statutory checkpoints where failure carries the highest consequence.
  • Rotational questions — drawn dynamically from the wider library and varied between inspections, so the crew cannot anticipate the checklist and must maintain readiness across all systems.
  • Campaign questions — selected by OCIMF to probe an industry-wide trend or a recurring failure mode identified from the aggregated data.

The four elements. Each question is assessed against: Human — crew familiarity, training and actual execution; Process — whether the company's SMS procedure is adequate and was followed; Hardware — physical condition, certification and maintenance; Photograph — photographic evidence substantiating what was found. The mnemonic H-P-H-P is a revision aid, not an OCIMF term.

Graded responses. Observations are recorded as As Expected, Not As Expected or Better Than Expected, with supporting narrative for any variance in either direction. This is the structural change that matters: the old binary yes/no recorded whether a thing existed; the graded response records how well it was being operated. A fully compliant, fully certificated system operated by an engineer who cannot explain its logic can now be graded Not As Expected.

🧪 CDI and TMSA
  • CDI-Marine. Operated by the Chemical Distribution Institute, a non-commercial trade association, for bulk chemical tankers. It produces a detailed technical report against a standardised questionnaire, used by chemical companies for risk assessment. It is more prescriptive and hardware-focused than SIRE 2.0.
  • MPCAS. The Marine Packed Cargo Audit Scheme extends CDI's approach to packaged chemicals moving in containers — packing, securing, documentation and the terminal logistics interface.
  • TMSA-3. The operator self-assesses its shore organisation and fleet against 13 elements, scoring each across maturity levels 1 to 4. The result is filed with OCIMF and becomes part of the data the SIRE inspection is compiled against.

The engine department's direct connection is TMSA Element 4, Vessel Reliability and Maintenance. That element is where the company declares its planned maintenance discipline, critical equipment identification, defect management and spares policy — and it is the shipboard PMS, defect list and critical spares inventory that a SIRE inspector uses to test whether the declared maturity level is real.

🔧 The Engine Room in a Vetting Inspection
  • Before: pre-inspection questionnaire and certificate data uploaded and accurate; PMS current with no unexplained overdue items; defect list live, with each open item risk-assessed, dated and owned; critical spares inventory verified against the declared list, not against the software.
  • During: the Chief Engineer escorts the inspector throughout — never allow unescorted movement through machinery spaces. Where the inspector photographs a finding, take an identical photograph showing the surrounding context and any immediate action taken, so the ship's record matches the inspector's.
  • Known high-scrutiny items: oily water separator and the 15 ppm alarm, with no bypass arrangement and a coherent Oil Record Book that reconciles to soundings; bilges visibly clean and dry; emergency generator and emergency fire pump on demand; steering gear including the emergency changeover drill; fixed firefighting release arrangements and their alarms and dampers.
  • After: if a finding is factually wrong, present the approved technical manual or system drawing at the time, not afterwards. If it is correct, acknowledge it, take immediate mitigating action if safety is affected, and log it into the SMS so root cause analysis and corrective action begin with the DPA.

The findings that end vetting acceptance are rarely worn components. They are evidence of deliberate circumvention: an electrical jumper across a trip, a bypassed machinery interlock, a temporary soft patch on a pressure line, a wire-tied quick-closing valve. Each of these tells the inspector that the ship's safety systems are negotiable — and each will also attract an expanded PSC inspection at the next port.

🎓 CE Oral Tip (Senthil): Senthil's opening on this topic is almost always the boundary question — is vetting a statutory requirement? Say plainly that it is not: no IMO instrument mandates SIRE, CDI or TMSA, and they are commercial risk-assessment tools operated by cargo interests and underwriters, which have nonetheless become a practical prerequisite to trading. Then show you understand the SIRE 2.0 shift: from binary hardware checking to graded assessment across Human, Process, Hardware and Photograph, with core, rotational and campaign questions compiled per inspection. The sentence that lands is: under SIRE 2.0 a perfectly maintained system can still be graded Not As Expected if the watchkeeper cannot explain it — because that is the change the industry actually made.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. A SIRE 2.0 inspector grades an engine room observation "Not As Expected". What do you do?
I would first ask to review the observation with the inspector on the device while we are still in the space, so that the factual basis is clear before it is finalised. If the grading arises from a misunderstanding of our specific machinery configuration, I would present the approved technical manual and the system drawing to show the design arrangement, and ask that the narrative reflect it. If the observation is correct, I acknowledge it without argument, take immediate mitigating action if safety is affected, and photograph the item myself showing the surrounding context and the action taken, so the ship's record matches the inspector's. I would then log the observation into the safety management system so that root cause analysis and a corrective action plan are initiated with the Designated Person Ashore, rather than treating it as a one-off repair. The important thing is that under SIRE 2.0 a negative variance carries a narrative, not just a mark, so the quality of the explanation recorded at the time affects how the finding reads to every charterer who later opens the report.
Q2. Explain the difference between core, rotational and campaign questions in SIRE 2.0.
Core questions are the critical safety, pollution-prevention and statutory checkpoints that carry the highest consequence of failure, and they are generated automatically for every single inspection regardless of ship or operator. Rotational questions are drawn dynamically from the much larger OCIMF question library appropriate to that ship type and vary from one inspection to the next, so that the crew cannot predict the checklist and must maintain readiness across all systems rather than preparing to a fixed list. Campaign questions are selected by OCIMF to target a specific industry-wide trend or a recurring failure mode identified from the aggregated inspection data, so they change as the industry's problems change. The combined effect is that the inspection is compiled for that ship on that day, which is the essential difference from the VIQ7 regime it replaced in 2024, where every ship faced the same static set of roughly three hundred questions.
Q3. How does TMSA-3 affect what you do on board?
TMSA-3 is the operator's own structured self-assessment of its shore management and fleet against thirteen elements, each scored across maturity levels one to four, filed with OCIMF. It matters on board because the SIRE inspection tests whether the declared maturity is real. The element that bears directly on my department is Element 4, Vessel Reliability and Maintenance, where the company declares its planned maintenance discipline, how critical equipment is identified, how defects are managed and what its critical spares policy is. The inspector will use my planned maintenance system records, my defect list and my critical spares inventory to test those declarations. So if the office has declared a high maturity level and my PMS shows unexplained overdue jobs, or my critical spares list does not match what is physically in the store, the damage is not confined to this ship — it undermines the operator's whole self-assessment across the fleet. In practice that means my records have to be accurate rather than merely complete.
Q4. Why is vetting sometimes commercially more damaging than a PSC detention?
A Port State Control detention is a public but discrete event: the deficiencies are rectified, the ship is released, and while the detention affects the flag and company performance record and may raise the ship's targeting factor, the ship can trade again immediately. A poor vetting report is different in kind. It sits in the OCIMF or CDI database, accessible to every charterer, trader and terminal operator for its validity period, and each of them applies their own acceptance criteria to it. A ship that fails to meet those criteria is simply not fixed — there is no appeal to compliance, because vetting is a commercial judgement about acceptable risk, not a legal finding. For a tanker operator, losing acceptance with a major oil company can remove a substantial part of the ship's addressable market, and regaining it requires a subsequent clean inspection plus evidence that the underlying management issues have been corrected. That is why findings suggesting deliberate circumvention of safety systems are so serious: they speak to culture rather than condition.

Why checklist preparation no longer works

Under VIQ7 a competent Chief Engineer could work the questionnaire in the week before an inspection and close the gaps. SIRE 2.0 removed that possibility deliberately: the rotational element means the specific questions are unknown until the inspector is on board, and the campaign element means the emphasis shifts across the industry without notice. The only viable preparation is a plant and a set of records that are continuously in order.

The human element is the real change

Grading crew performance means the inspector will ask a watchkeeper to explain a system, not just show it. The failure mode is a well-maintained installation that nobody on the current crew fully understands — common after a crew change, and common with equipment retrofitted between dockings. The defence is familiarisation that is genuinely completed rather than signed: walkthroughs on the actual plant, with the responsible engineer explaining the system logic aloud, recorded as training.

Photographic discipline

Every finding is photographed by the inspector and the photograph travels with the report. A close-up of an oil-stained tank top conveys something quite different from a wide shot showing a clean machinery space with one localised leak under repair. Taking a matching contextual photograph at the moment the inspector takes theirs is not defensive gamesmanship; it is how the ship ensures its own record of the same fact exists.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

Container tonnage is outside the SIRE and CDI regimes, but the same logic has migrated into charterer and terminal inspections and into the company's own internal audits. The transferable discipline is the graded mindset: assume the question is not "does this work?" but "can the duty engineer explain why it works, show me the record, and tell me what happens when it fails?" A department that can answer that consistently does not need to prepare for inspections at all.

Global Climate Accords and the Legal Genesis of Maritime Decarbonisation — Kyoto, Doha and Paris

Kyoto Art. 2.2 · Annex A Gases · CDM/JI/ET · Paris NDCs Uday pp. 533–542 (approx.) Examiner: Simon ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: One significant addition and two clarifications. (1) The missing link. The draft explains Kyoto and Paris but never states why a marine engineer studies them. The connection is Kyoto Protocol Article 2, paragraph 2, under which Annex I Parties agreed to pursue limitation or reduction of greenhouse gases from aviation and marine bunker fuels working through ICAO and the IMO respectively. That single provision is the legal origin of the IMO's mandate over shipping GHG, and it is the reason international shipping sits outside national inventories. It has been added, because without it the chapter is climate history rather than maritime regulation. (2) Paris and shipping. The Paris Agreement does not expressly mention international shipping or aviation — the reference in the draft text of the agreement was removed before adoption. International shipping emissions therefore fall outside Nationally Determined Contributions, which is precisely why the IMO route matters and why the point is examinable. Added. (3) Doha Amendment entry into force. The draft describes the second commitment period without noting that the Doha Amendment did not enter into force until 31 December 2020 — the day the period it governed ended. Added, because it explains why the second period had little practical effect.
📖 Definition

The UNFCCC (1992) is the framework convention; the Kyoto Protocol (1997) was its first binding implementing instrument, imposing quantified targets on developed countries only; the Paris Agreement (2015) replaced that architecture with universal participation through self-determined national pledges. For a marine engineer their significance is narrow and specific: Kyoto assigned international shipping emissions to the IMO rather than to national inventories, and Paris left that assignment untouched. Everything in MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 4 traces back to that assignment.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Kyoto Article 2.2 is why the IMO — and not the flag State's environment ministry — regulates ship GHG. Every subsequent instrument, from EEDI to CII to the Net-Zero Framework, sits on that foundation.
  • International shipping falls outside national NDCs. That structural gap explains both the IMO's slow consensus-based progress and the willingness of the EU to act unilaterally through the ETS and FuelEU Maritime.
  • Kyoto's Annex I / non-Annex I split created the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities, which collides directly with the IMO's own principle of no more favourable treatment. That collision is the substantive reason IMO GHG negotiations are difficult, and it is a strong oral answer.
  • The Kyoto market mechanisms — CDM, JI and emissions trading — are the conceptual ancestors of every maritime carbon pricing proposal now on the table.

Party categories. Annex I — developed countries and economies in transition, which accepted quantified targets. Annex II — a subset of Annex I (developed countries excluding the economies in transition), additionally obliged to provide finance and technology transfer. Non-Annex I — developing countries, with no binding quantified commitments. India is non-Annex I.

Commitment periods. First, 2008–2012: a collective reduction of about 5% against a 1990 baseline across participating Annex I Parties, with differentiated national figures — the EU accepting 8% and Germany 21%. Second, 2013–2020, established by the Doha Amendment with an 18% target against 1990 for participating Parties. India accepted the Doha Amendment on 8 August 2017 as the 80th Party to do so. The amendment did not enter into force until 31 December 2020, the final day of the period it governed, which is why the second period is of limited practical significance.

  • Clean Development Mechanism (Art. 12). An Annex I Party finances an emission-reduction project in a developing country and earns Certified Emission Reductions (CERs), one CER equalling one tonne of CO₂ equivalent, usable against its own target.
  • Joint Implementation (Art. 6). The same project-based logic between two Annex I Parties, generating Emission Reduction Units (ERUs).
  • International Emissions Trading (Art. 17). Parties that overachieve may sell surplus assigned amount to Parties that fall short — the first genuine international carbon market.
  • Sinks. Land use, land-use change and forestry, allowing sequestration to be counted against emissions.

Why the omission matters. Because international shipping is not in any NDC, no State's national target is affected by what its flagged fleet emits. That removes the domestic political driver that operates in every other sector, and leaves progress dependent on consensus at the IMO — where the Kyoto-derived principle of common but differentiated responsibilities is pressed by developing States against the IMO's own long-standing principle of no more favourable treatment, under which a ship is regulated by where it trades rather than by whose flag it flies. That tension is the substantive explanation for the pace of IMO GHG regulation, and it produced the October 2025 adjournment discussed in Topic 3.

India's position — worth having precisely for a Kochi MMD oral. Ratified the Paris Agreement on 2 October 2016. At COP 26 (Glasgow, 2021) announced: reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45% below 2005 levels by 2030; 500 GW of non-fossil energy capacity by 2030; meet 50% of energy requirements from renewables by 2030; reduce projected emissions by one billion tonnes by 2030; and achieve net-zero by 2070. Forest and tree cover expansion supports the additional carbon sink commitment.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the legal basis for the IMO regulating greenhouse gas emissions from ships?
It derives from Article 2, paragraph 2 of the Kyoto Protocol, under which the Annex I Parties agreed to pursue limitation or reduction of emissions of greenhouse gases not controlled by the Montreal Protocol from aviation and marine bunker fuels, working through the International Civil Aviation Organization and the International Maritime Organization respectively. That provision assigned international shipping emissions to the IMO rather than to national emissions inventories, and it is the origin of everything that followed — the 2011 energy efficiency amendments introducing EEDI and SEEMP into MARPOL Annex VI, the Data Collection System, EEXI and CII, and the Initial and Revised GHG Strategies. The Paris Agreement did not disturb that assignment: it does not expressly address international shipping at all, so the sector remains outside Nationally Determined Contributions and the IMO route remains the only global one. That structural fact is also why regional measures such as the EU Emissions Trading System extension and FuelEU Maritime emerged — regional actors filling the gap left by the pace of global consensus.
Q2. List the Kyoto Protocol Annex A gases and identify those with direct shipboard relevance.
The original six controlled gases are carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide, hydrofluorocarbons, perfluorocarbons and sulphur hexafluoride. Nitrogen trifluoride was added as a seventh for the second commitment period by the Doha Amendment of 2012. Four have direct shipboard relevance. Carbon dioxide is the dominant emission and the subject of the entire EEDI, EEXI and CII framework. Methane matters because of methane slip from low-pressure dual-fuel LNG engines, which is significant in well-to-wake accounting and can materially erode the apparent carbon benefit of LNG. Hydrofluorocarbons are the refrigerants in the provision plant and air conditioning system, which is why refrigerant leakage control and log-keeping have become an environmental compliance matter rather than a purely technical one. Sulphur hexafluoride is the insulating medium in high-voltage switchgear, relevant on ships with high-voltage installations. Nitrous oxide is a minor combustion product but is included in carbon dioxide equivalent accounting. Perfluorocarbons are industrial process gases and are not a normal ship emission.
Q3. Compare the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement in structure.
Kyoto was top-down and differentiated. Binding quantified emission limitation targets were negotiated and assigned to Annex I Parties only — developed countries and economies in transition — while non-Annex I developing countries took no binding commitments. Compliance was supported by three market mechanisms: the Clean Development Mechanism generating Certified Emission Reductions from projects in developing countries, Joint Implementation generating Emission Reduction Units between Annex I Parties, and International Emissions Trading in surplus assigned amounts. Paris is bottom-up and universal. Every Party prepares, communicates and maintains a Nationally Determined Contribution stating its own targets, reviewed on a five-year ratchet under which each successive contribution must represent a progression. The legal obligation attaches to the process of submitting and maintaining an NDC rather than to achieving the number in it. The overall goal shifted from an aggregate percentage against 1990 to a temperature outcome — well below two degrees, pursuing efforts towards one and a half. For shipping the critical structural difference is that Kyoto expressly assigned international bunker fuel emissions to the IMO under Article 2.2, whereas Paris does not mention international shipping, leaving that assignment in place by default.
Q4. Why is progress on IMO greenhouse gas measures so slow compared with other MARPOL amendments?
Because two legitimate principles collide. From the UNFCCC and Kyoto comes common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities — the position that developed States, having caused the historical accumulation, should bear the greater burden, and that developing States should not have their development constrained. From the IMO comes no more favourable treatment, the principle that a ship is regulated according to where it trades rather than whose flag it flies, which is what makes MARPOL enforceable at all — without it, tonnage would migrate to whichever register imposed the least. A measure that differentiates by flag or by the nationality of the owner undermines enforceability; a measure that treats all ships identically is resisted as ignoring differentiated responsibility. Every carbon pricing proposal at the IMO has had to navigate that, usually by treating ships uniformly while redistributing the revenue differentially. The October 2025 extraordinary session of the MEPC, which adjourned adoption of the Net-Zero Framework by a vote of 57 to 49 with 21 abstentions, is the clearest demonstration of how unresolved that tension remains.

  • CBDR (UNFCCC) vs No More Favourable Treatment (IMO) — the structural tension behind every GHG negotiation.
  • India: Paris ratified 2 Oct 2016 · net-zero 2070 · 45% intensity cut vs 2005 by 2030 · 500 GW non-fossil by 2030.

The gap

If international shipping is outside every national inventory and outside every NDC, then no State can improve its own reported position by regulating the ships that call at its ports. The only lever is the IMO, and the IMO moves by consensus among States with sharply divergent economic interests. That gap — a sector nobody counts, regulated by a body that cannot move quickly — is what regional measures were designed to close.

What the EU did

Two instruments, deliberately complementary. The EU Emissions Trading System was extended to maritime transport from 2024, requiring surrender of allowances against verified emissions on a phased basis — a proportion in the first years rising to full surrender — covering voyages within the EU in full and voyages into and out of the EU at half. Separately, FuelEU Maritime (Regulation (EU) 2023/1805) applies from 1 January 2025 and limits the greenhouse gas intensity of the energy used on board, on a well-to-wake basis against a 2020 fleet baseline of 91.16 gCO₂eq/MJ, tightening on a defined schedule.

The distinction that must not be blurred

These are separate regulatory tracks with different accounting boundaries, and conflating them is a known examination trap. CII under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 28 is tank-to-wake: it counts what comes out of the funnel, using fixed carbon factors per fuel type, and takes no account of how the fuel was produced. FuelEU Maritime and the IMO's proposed GHG Fuel Intensity metric are well-to-wake: they count the whole fuel lifecycle including production and distribution. A fuel can therefore look excellent under CII and mediocre under a well-to-wake metric, which is exactly the case for fossil LNG. Never write a causal sentence connecting a CII outcome to a FuelEU outcome without checking the boundary.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

For a European-trading container operator these regimes overlap in practice: the same voyage generates an ETS allowance liability, a FuelEU intensity balance and a CII rating contribution, computed on different boundaries from the same bunker data. The engine room consequence is that fuel consumption reporting has to be accurate at a granularity none of these schemes required ten years ago — by fuel grade, by voyage leg, and reconciled to bunker delivery notes — because three separate compliance calculations now depend on the same underlying figures.

📖 Definition

The IMO's greenhouse gas regime has three layers. A strategy setting the ambition — the Initial Strategy of 2018, replaced by the Revised Strategy adopted at MEPC 80 in July 2023. A set of in-force technical and operational measures in MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 4 — EEDI for new ships, EEXI for existing ships, the SEEMP, and the CII operational rating. And a proposed economic layer, the Net-Zero Framework, which combines a GHG fuel intensity standard with carbon pricing — approved in April 2025 but not yet adopted, with the next scheduled decision point a resumed extraordinary session on 4 December 2026.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • EEDI is fixed at design; EEXI is a one-off calculation usually satisfied by power limitation; CII is the one that recurs every year and is driven by how the ship is operated — which makes it the Chief Engineer's measure.
  • A D rating for three consecutive years, or a single E, forces a corrective action plan into SEEMP Part III. That is a commercial and administrative penalty arising directly from engine room and voyage performance.
  • Waste heat recovery, shaft generators, hull and propeller condition and auxiliary load management are the levers that move CII within a given trade pattern — they are engineering decisions with a regulatory rating attached.
  • The Net-Zero Framework, if adopted, would shift the metric from carbon intensity per transport work to GHG intensity per unit of energy on a well-to-wake basis. That changes which fuels look good, and it is why the tank-to-wake versus well-to-wake distinction has to be understood rather than memorised.

Two points that gain marks. First, the 2030 and 2040 figures are indicative checkpoints, not binding limits — the binding instruments are the MARPOL measures beneath the strategy. Second, the Revised Strategy did not simply raise the numbers: it changed the type of target, from a carbon-intensity metric (emissions per transport work, which can improve while total emissions rise) to absolute GHG reduction, which cannot.

The three horizons of candidate measures. Short-term — the technical and operational measures now in force (EEXI, CII, SEEMP Part III), subject to review. Mid-term — a technical element, being a goal-based marine fuel standard regulating a phased reduction of fuel GHG intensity, and an economic element, being a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism. These two together form the Net-Zero Framework. Long-term — full decarbonisation through zero-emission fuels and propulsion by or around 2050.

⏸ The Net-Zero Framework — Current Status

Status as at this Part: approved but NOT adopted, and not in force. The Framework was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 as draft amendments creating a new Chapter 5 of MARPOL Annex VI. It has two limbs: a goal-based GHG fuel intensity standard requiring a phased reduction in the well-to-wake GHG intensity of the energy used on board against a fleet-average baseline, and an economic mechanism under which ships exceeding the required intensity acquire remedial units while ships outperforming it may earn surplus units — a pricing and reward structure rather than a simple levy.

The reference baseline discussed at MEPC 83 for the GHG Fuel Intensity metric is a 2008 fleet-average figure of 93.3 gCO₂eq/MJ. That figure belongs to the draft IMO instrument and is not the same as the FuelEU Maritime baseline of 91.16 gCO₂eq/MJ, which is a 2020 EU fleet-average under Regulation (EU) 2023/1805. The two are different regimes with different baselines, different scopes and different compliance mechanics, and conflating them is a documented examination trap.

Momentum since the adjournment. The October 2025 vote to adjourn was itself close — 57 to 49, with 21 abstentions. By MEPC 84 in April–May 2026, reporting suggests the balance of States had shifted towards accepting the April 2025 text as the basis for moving forward, though a market-readiness camp remains, and no agreement was actually reached. The direction of travel is informative for an oral, but it is not a substitute for the fact that the instrument remains unadopted.

How to state this in an oral. Say the Framework is approved but not adopted; give the October 2025 adjournment (57/49/21); note that MEPC 84 in April–May 2026 discussed it again and still reached no final agreement; and give the current decision point — a resumed MEPC/ES.2 on 4 December 2026, immediately after MEPC 85. Say that if adopted, entry into force would follow the tacit acceptance sequence, so the sector should plan for it without treating it as law. Presenting it as being in force, or citing the original "October 2026" reconvening date as still current, are both factual errors an examiner will test for.

🔥 Shipboard Carbon Reduction — the Engineering Levers
  • Waste heat recovery. Exhaust gas economiser raising steam for a turbo-generator, and where fitted a power turbine on the exhaust bypass, displacing auxiliary diesel generator load. Output degrades with soot fouling of the economiser tube nest, with air cooler and turbocharger fouling reducing exhaust energy quality, and with steam system leakage — all of which are maintainable causes, not inevitable decline.
  • Shaft generator / PTO. Producing electrical power from the main engine at its far better specific fuel consumption instead of running an auxiliary engine, wherever the load profile and the propulsion mode allow.
  • Hull and propeller condition. The single largest recoverable loss on most ships. Fouling raises the power required at a given speed continuously between cleanings, and the penalty compounds.
  • Air path and combustion. Turbocharger and air cooler cleanliness govern charge air density; injector condition and correct timing govern combustion quality. Both act directly on specific fuel consumption at every load.
  • Thermal insulation. Exhaust manifolds, steam lines and heated tanks — cheap to restore, and the loss is continuous.
  • Auxiliary load management. Avoiding unnecessary parallel running of pumps and compressors, and using variable frequency drives where fitted rather than throttling.
  • Wind assistance and solar. Rotor sails, suction wings and photovoltaic arrays — real but ship- and route-specific; treat as a design or retrofit decision rather than an operational lever.

Measuring honestly. Trend specific fuel oil consumption against corrected shaft power rather than against speed over ground, so that current, wind and draught are removed from the comparison. Only then can a claimed improvement be attributed to the intervention. That same corrected dataset is what supports a hull cleaning business case, a charterparty performance defence and the CII narrative — one measurement discipline serving three purposes.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Compare the Initial and Revised IMO GHG Strategies.
The Initial Strategy, adopted at MEPC 72 in 2018, set a carbon intensity target of at least a forty per cent reduction in CO2 emissions per transport work by 2030 while pursuing efforts towards seventy per cent by 2050, and a target of reducing total annual greenhouse gas emissions from international shipping by at least fifty per cent by 2050, both against a 2008 baseline, with a vision of phasing out emissions entirely within this century. The Revised Strategy, adopted at MEPC 80 in July 2023, retained the forty per cent carbon intensity target for 2030 but changed the character of the headline goal from intensity to absolute reduction. It sets net-zero greenhouse gas emissions from international shipping by or around 2050, with indicative checkpoints of at least twenty per cent striving for thirty per cent absolute reduction by 2030, and at least seventy per cent striving for eighty per cent by 2040, both against 2008. It also adds a technology target that zero or near-zero greenhouse gas emission technologies, fuels and energy sources should represent at least five per cent, striving for ten per cent, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030. The important conceptual point is that an intensity target can be met while total emissions rise if trade grows; an absolute target cannot.
Q2. What is the current status of the IMO Net-Zero Framework?
It is approved but not adopted, and it is not in force. The Framework was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 as draft amendments that would insert a new Chapter 5 into MARPOL Annex VI, combining a goal-based greenhouse gas fuel intensity standard on a well-to-wake basis with an economic pricing and reward mechanism, under which ships exceeding the required intensity acquire remedial units and ships outperforming it may earn surplus units. It was submitted for formal adoption at the second extraordinary session of the Marine Environment Protection Committee, held in London from the fourteenth to the seventeenth of October 2025. The session did not adopt it: a motion to adjourn for one year carried by fifty-seven votes to forty-nine with twenty-one abstentions. The Committee returned to it at MEPC 84, held from the twenty-seventh of April to the first of May 2026 and preceded by the twenty-first Intersessional Working Group on GHG emissions, but again reached no final agreement — reporting describes a continuing split between states wanting the original text adopted with minimal change and states wanting a more market-ready approach, although the balance of support was reported to have shifted towards adoption since the October 2025 vote. Work continues through two further intersessional working groups scheduled for September and November 2026, reporting to MEPC 85, which is planned for the thirtieth of November to the third of December 2026, immediately followed by a one-day resumption of the extraordinary session on the fourth of December 2026 — that date is the current decision point, superseding the October 2026 date originally announced in 2025. So the correct statement is that it is a draft instrument that operators should plan for but which has no legal effect, and any figure associated with it, including the 93.3 grams of CO2 equivalent per megajoule reference baseline discussed at MEPC 83, is a draft figure.
Q3. Which of the energy efficiency measures do you actually influence as Chief Engineer, and how?
EEDI is fixed at design and construction and I cannot change it; my only concern there is that the ship-specific EEDI Technical File is maintained on board and that no machinery or structural modification takes the ship outside its approved design assumptions. EEXI is a one-off calculation for existing ships, most commonly satisfied by engine or shaft power limitation, so my role is to ensure the limitation arrangement is correctly installed, sealed, and that the override arrangement and its recording are understood, since misuse of the override is a compliance failure. The measure I genuinely influence is the CII, the operational carbon intensity rating under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 28, which is recalculated every year from reported fuel consumption and distance sailed and produces a rating from A to E against a reduction factor that tightens annually. The levers are hull and propeller cleanliness, which is usually the largest single recoverable loss; waste heat recovery output, which degrades with economiser soot fouling and steam leakage; turbocharger and air cooler cleanliness, which govern charge air density and therefore combustion quality; injector condition and timing; use of the shaft generator in place of auxiliary engines where the load profile allows; thermal insulation of exhaust and steam systems; and auxiliary load discipline. The SEEMP Part III is where those measures are formally recorded, and if the ship is rated D for three consecutive years or E once, a plan of corrective actions must be developed and incorporated into it.
Q4. Explain the difference between tank-to-wake and well-to-wake accounting and why it matters.
Tank-to-wake accounting counts only the emissions produced on board from the fuel as burned, using fixed carbon conversion factors for each fuel type. That is the basis of the CII under MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 28: fuel consumed multiplied by its carbon factor, divided by transport work. It takes no account of how the fuel was produced, extracted, refined or transported. Well-to-wake accounting counts the entire lifecycle, including upstream production and distribution emissions, and is expressed as greenhouse gas intensity per unit of energy in grams of CO2 equivalent per megajoule. That is the basis of FuelEU Maritime under EU Regulation 2023/1805, and of the greenhouse gas fuel intensity metric in the draft IMO Net-Zero Framework. The distinction matters because a fuel can look very different on the two bases. Fossil LNG performs well tank-to-wake because of its favourable carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, but performs considerably less well well-to-wake once upstream methane leakage and any onboard methane slip are counted. Blue and green fuels can show the reverse pattern. The practical rule is that the two are separate regulatory tracks with separate baselines — the draft IMO figure of 93.3 grams of CO2 equivalent per megajoule is a 2008 fleet average, while the FuelEU baseline of 91.16 is a 2020 EU fleet average — and no causal statement should be made across them without checking which boundary applies.

Symptom

A Post-Panamax bulk carrier built to EEDI Phase 3 shows progressively falling turbo-generator output on a steady-state laden passage. Eventually the electrical load can no longer be carried and a second auxiliary diesel generator is brought on line, raising daily fuel consumption and worsening the CII contribution of the voyage. The engine parameters look broadly normal, which is why the problem is allowed to develop.

Systematic elimination

The output of an exhaust gas economiser depends on exhaust mass flow, exhaust temperature and the heat transfer available. Take them in order. Exhaust temperature and mass flow: check turbocharger speed against load and against the shop test curve, scavenge air pressure and temperature, air cooler differential pressure and cooling water inlet temperature, and air filter condition. A fouled air path reduces charge density, which raises exhaust temperature at the turbine inlet but reduces mass flow, and the net effect on recoverable energy is usually negative. Heat transfer: economiser soot deposits on the tube nest, established from the gas-side differential pressure trend and from the record of soot blowing frequency and effectiveness; water-side scaling from feedwater quality; and any indication of past soot fire, which permanently degrades the nest. Steam side: leakage across the system, drains passing, unlagged or damaged insulation on steam lines, feedwater pump performance, and dump condenser operation. Electrical side: turbo-generator excitation, governor response and load-sharing settings — a generator that is healthy but incorrectly load-shared will drop off unnecessarily.

The likely cause and the fix

In most cases of progressive rather than sudden loss, the answer is gas-side fouling of the economiser combined with air-path fouling upstream. The remedy is soot blowing restored to correct frequency and pressure, water washing at the next opportunity with the correct chemistry and full drainage, air cooler cleaning, and turbocharger cleaning. The critical precaution is soot fire risk: never water wash without confirming the plant condition, following the manufacturer's procedure, and ensuring drainage is clear, because a wet soot deposit at temperature is exactly the condition that initiates an economiser fire.

Closing the loop on compliance

Record the intervention against corrected specific fuel consumption and against turbo-generator output at comparable load, so the improvement is demonstrable rather than asserted. Enter the measure and its effect in SEEMP Part III as an implemented energy efficiency action. If the ship is on a charter with a CII clause, the same evidence supports the owner's position on responsibility for the rating.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

On container tonnage the equivalent exposure is larger because service speed is dictated by schedule and cannot be traded for efficiency. That concentrates everything on the recoverable losses — hull and propeller condition, air path cleanliness, waste heat recovery availability and reefer-driven auxiliary load. The department that trends these continuously has an argument to make when the CII rating is questioned; the department that does not has only an opinion.
