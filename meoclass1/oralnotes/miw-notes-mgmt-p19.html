Part 19 · Topic 1

General Average — Principles, Security Mechanisms and the York-Antwerp Rules 2016

GA Principles · YAR 2016 · Lien & Security · Average Adjuster Uday pp. 451–457 (approx.) Examiner: Nair ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Three corrections were applied to the source draft. (1) Time bar: the draft stated that the York-Antwerp Rules "do not universally prescribe a fixed, global time-bar". That is true of YAR 1994, but not of YAR 2004 or YAR 2016. Rule XXIII of YAR 2016 expressly extinguishes rights to GA contribution — including rights under GA bonds and guarantees — unless action is brought within one year of the date the GA adjustment is issued, with an absolute six-year long stop from termination of the common maritime adventure; the periods are subject to any mandatory time limitation in the applicable law, may be extended by agreement made after termination of the adventure, and do not apply as between a GA party and its own insurers. The draft's internal contradiction (its own black-letter list correctly described Rule XXIII as imposing limitation windows) has been resolved in favour of the verified rule text. (2) Apportionment formula: retained but explicitly kept labelled as a pedagogical model — it is not algebraic text appearing in the Rules. (3) Overlap: Part 18 Topic 4 already covered General Average at the level of the New Jason Clause and the YAR framework; this topic is the deeper operational treatment (security enforcement, the adjuster, Rule VII machinery damage) and is deliberately not a repeat.
📖 Definition

General Average (GA) is a principle of private maritime law under which all parties to a common maritime adventure proportionally share any loss arising from a voluntary, intentional sacrifice of part of the ship or cargo, or from an extraordinary expenditure, incurred for the purpose of preserving the remaining property from a real and imminent common peril. Its purpose is to ensure that the party whose property is deliberately sacrificed for the common good does not bear that loss alone — which in turn frees the Master to make fast, objective decisions in an emergency without weighing individual cargo-owner claims against the safety of the venture.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • GA is the reason a Master can order jettison, voluntary stranding, or destructive firefighting without hesitating over which cargo owner will sue — the economics are settled afterwards, not during the emergency.
  • The Chief Engineer is the technical custodian of the evidence on which the entire adjustment rests. If bunker consumption during the extraordinary act cannot be isolated from routine consumption, that expenditure may simply not be allowed in GA.
  • Rule VII is one of the few places in commercial maritime law where a deliberate engineering decision — running the main engine beyond its design limits — converts directly into a recoverable claim, but only if two narrow conditions are satisfied.
  • The version of YAR incorporated into the charterparty or B/L (1994, 2004 or 2016) changes the answer to real questions — most sharply on the time bar. GA is driven by contract, not by a mandatory global convention.

🧮 Apportionment — the Contributory Value Mechanism

Settlement rests on the contributory value of each interest — ship, cargo and freight at risk — assessed at the place and time the adventure ends. Each party contributes in the ratio its saved value bears to the total value of all property that benefited, including the value of the property sacrificed (the sacrificed property contributes towards making good its own loss).

Contribution of party i: Ci = (Vi ÷ Vtotal) × LGA

Where Vi is that party's net contributory value, Vtotal the aggregate of all contributory values, and LGA the total of allowed sacrifices and extraordinary expenditure. This is a teaching model, not rule text — no such equation appears in the York-Antwerp Rules. Say so if you write it on the board; presenting a self-made formula as convention text is exactly the trap Nair looks for.

🔒 Lien on Cargo and the Four Security Mechanisms

The shipowner is usually the largest single contributor because the saved ship is the most valuable interest, but the shipowner also holds the leverage. Where cargo interests will not commit to contribute, the owner exercises a possessory lien on the cargo — a contractual right expressly written into the charterparty and bill of lading. The cargo is not released until acceptable security is in hand.

⚖ The Average Adjuster

One party — normally the shipowner — appoints the Average Adjuster, a specialist in marine insurance and GA practice. The appointment does not make the adjuster that party's advocate.

  • Collects all evidence following the GA act — logs, soundings, surveys, repair accounts, port-of-refuge invoices.
  • Prepares the statement of general average, allocating allowed losses and calculating each interest's contribution.
  • Collects GA security from each interest and administers deposits.
  • Issues the adjustment — the document that starts the Rule XXIII one-year clock running.

The CMI Guidelines Relating to General Average state that the adjuster is expected to act impartially and independently so as to act fairly to all parties in the adventure, regardless of who appointed and pays them. A proposal to write that duty expressly into the 2016 Rules was considered and not adopted; it lives in the Guidelines, not the Rules. That distinction is a precise answer if pushed.

⚙ Rule VII — Damage to Machinery and Boilers

The rule allows in general average damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety; but where a ship is afloat, no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers is in any circumstances made good as general average.

  • Condition 1 — ashore and in peril. The vessel must actually be aground and in danger. Thrashing the engine to claw off a lee shore while still afloat is excluded, however extreme the weather and however genuine the peril.
  • Condition 2 — actual intention to refloat for the common safety. The machinery must have been deliberately worked beyond normal limits with that specific intent. This is why the engineer's contemporaneous technical report matters: it is the primary evidence of intention.
[Machinery worked beyond normal limits] │ ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐ Ship AGROUND & in peril Ship AFLOAT │ │ Intent to refloat for EXCLUDED in all the common safety? circumstances ├── YES ──> ALLOWED (Rule VII) (ordinary wear / └── NO ──> EXCLUDED operational loss)

The asymmetry is deliberate. Working the engine while afloat is what a ship is for; the resulting wear is an ordinary operating cost, not an extraordinary sacrifice. Once aground, the same act becomes extraordinary because the machinery is being used for a purpose, and at a stress level, outside its intended service.

🎓 CE Oral Tip (Nair): Nair's standard follow-up on Rule VII is the mirror case: "and if the ship had been afloat?" The expected answer is excluded in any circumstances — those words are in the rule. If you then volunteer that the YAR version matters, and that under YAR 2016 Rule XXIII the adjustment itself starts a one-year clock with a six-year long stop, you have moved from a memorised answer to a working one. Do not claim the Rules are silent on time bars — that was true only up to YAR 1994.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. The vessel is aground and the Master orders maximum astern power to refloat. The main engine suffers scored liners and a cracked cylinder head. Is this damage allowed in General Average?
Yes, under York-Antwerp Rules Rule VII (Damage to Machinery and Boilers), provided two conditions are satisfied: the ship was ashore and in a position of peril, and the damage arose from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety. Both are met here — the vessel was hard aground and the engine was deliberately worked beyond its design limits specifically to refloat. The damage is therefore an intentional sacrifice rather than accidental wear, and the repair cost is made good in GA and shared by all contributing interests. Had the vessel been afloat, Rule VII excludes such damage in any circumstances, even if the engine was being worked hard to escape a genuine peril.
Q2. Cargo interests at the discharge port refuse to provide a General Average Bond or deposit. What happens, and what is your part in it?
The shipowner exercises the possessory lien on cargo conferred by the charterparty and bill of lading, and does not release the cargo until acceptable security is provided — a GA bond, an underwriter's average guarantee, a bank guarantee, or a cash deposit paid into a separate joint-name account under Rule XXII. My part is technical support to the Master in maintaining the ship's custody of that cargo: continuing to run and log reefer power, hold ventilation and bilge monitoring so that no cargo-care claim arises during the detention, keeping auxiliary machinery available for the extended stay, and recording the additional fuel and stores consumed so those costs can be presented to the adjuster. I would not unilaterally isolate cargo-gear power — enforcement of the lien is the Master's and the owner's decision, and I act on the Master's instruction.
Q3. Who pays the engine room crew's wages while the vessel is detained at a port of refuge for emergency repairs?
Under YAR Rule XI, the wages and maintenance of the master, officers and crew, together with fuel and stores consumed during the extra period of detention at a port of refuge, are allowed in general average, provided the detention was necessary for the common safety or to enable the vessel to complete the voyage safely. The cost is not borne by the shipowner alone: the average adjuster pools it into the total GA losses and it is distributed across all interests in proportion to their contributory values under Rule XVII. YAR 2016 specifically reinstated the allowance for wages and maintenance during detention at a place of refuge that the 2004 Rules had restricted.
Q4. A GA adjustment was issued 14 months ago and the owner has only now started proceedings against a cargo interest. Is there a problem?
Yes, if YAR 2016 or YAR 2004 is incorporated into the contract of carriage. Rule XXIII extinguishes the right to GA contribution — including any right to claim under a GA bond or guarantee — unless action is brought within one year of the date the adjustment was issued, subject to any mandatory time limitation in the applicable law. The right is extinguished, not merely barred as a remedy. There is also an absolute six-year long stop running from termination of the common maritime adventure, and the periods can be extended only by agreement made after the adventure terminated. If YAR 1994 is the incorporated version, Rule XXIII does not exist and national limitation law governs instead — which is why the first question in any GA dispute is which version the charterparty incorporates.

Chronology

0200 LT — fuel line rupture in the auxiliary engine room on a laden container vessel; severe fire. 0215 — Master releases the fixed CO₂ system; fire contained but all main propulsion and auxiliary power lost, vessel adrift in a narrow channel. 0330 — tidal set drives the unpowered vessel hard onto a mud bank. 0500 — Master declares General Average. 0800 — Chief Engineer prepares the main engine for maximum astern to refloat before the next low water exposes the hull to excessive stress. 0930 — engine run significantly past thermal limits for 45 minutes; liner scoring, bearing damage and a cracked cylinder head result; the vessel refloats.

Which losses fall where

CO₂ discharge and any water or foam damage to ship and cargo: Rule III. The grounding itself was accidental, not voluntary, so Rule V (voluntary stranding) does not apply — a common trap in this scenario. The machinery damage from the refloating attempt: Rule VII, because the ship was ashore and in peril and the engine was worked with actual intention to refloat. Port of refuge expenses and crew wages during detention for repairs: Rules X and XI.

Chief Engineer's evidence checklist

Verify and lock the CO₂ battery release counters and timestamps. Sound every fuel tank immediately before and after the refloating attempt to isolate extraordinary consumption. Isolate and preserve the cracked cylinder head, damaged bearings and scored liner segments for the surveyor and the adjuster — do not scrap or send them for reconditioning. Extract and duplicate engine control room data logger parameters covering 0800–1015 LT. Draft a technical report stating why the engine had to be worked beyond normal limits — this is the primary evidence of the intention required by Rule VII.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

If the ECR data logger dies with the power, designate an engineer to keep a handwritten log of jacket water temperature, exhaust temperatures, scavenge pressure and lube oil pressure at fixed intervals. A contemporaneous manual log in the duty engineer's handwriting is accepted evidence; a reconstruction written up two days later is not, and the adjuster will treat it accordingly.

Bills of Lading — Legal Framework, Operational Typologies and the Electronic B/L

Triple Function · B/L Typologies · Endorsement · e-B/L Uday pp. 457–463 (approx.) Examiner: Nair ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Four corrections were applied. (1) The draft repeatedly referred to India's "Bills of Lading Bill, 2025" as pending legislation. It is no longer a Bill: the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 received Presidential assent on 24 July 2025 and was published in the Gazette Extraordinary the same day, repealing the Indian Bills of Lading Act, 1856. 📖 Definition

A Bill of Lading (B/L) is a transport document issued by or on behalf of the carrier to the shipper, recording the type, quantity, apparent condition and destination of goods received for carriage by sea. It is the pivot around which the seller, buyer, carrier and financing bank all operate — it is what allows cargo to be sold, resold and financed while it is physically in the middle of an ocean.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The B/L is the document against which a cargo claim is measured. Every technical record the engine room keeps — bilge soundings, hold ventilation, reefer power logs — exists partly to defend the carrier's position against what the B/L says about apparent condition on loading.
  • A Master signing a clean B/L for visibly damaged cargo converts a shipper's problem into the owner's liability. Understanding why the Master clauses a bill explains why the Chief Engineer's records of hold condition and tightness testing are demanded before loading.
  • Misdelivery — releasing cargo without the original B/L — is one of the few exposures a P&I club will not cover as of right. It is a contractual and criminal risk, not merely a commercial one.
  • e-B/L adoption is now moving fast under MLETR-based law; the shipboard consequence is that data-transfer protocols and documentary timings change, while the underlying liability principles do not.

🔺 The Triple Function
┌──────────────────────────────┐ │ THE B/L TRIAD │ └──────────────┬───────────────┘ ┌─────────────────────────────┼─────────────────────────────┐ ▼ ▼ ▼ [DOCUMENT OF TITLE] [RECEIPT FOR GOODS] [EVIDENCE OF CONTRACT] Entitles the lawful Records quantity, date Proves the terms agreed holder to delivery and apparent order and before loading — but is at the discharge port condition on loading NOT the contract itself
  • Document of title. The lawful holder presenting an original B/L is entitled to delivery from the carrier. Note the precision: it confers the right to possession from the carrier, not ownership. Ownership passes under the sale contract between buyer and seller, on entirely separate terms.
  • Receipt for goods. It records the date of receipt, place of issue, load and discharge ports, and quantity or weight — and, critically, apparent order and condition, which is the phrase that makes clausing matter.
  • Evidence of the contract of carriage. It is evidence, not the contract. The contract is concluded earlier, at booking or under the charterparty. If cargo is damaged after loading but before the B/L is issued, the shipper can still sue on the pre-existing contract.

There is one important refinement: as between the carrier and a third-party endorsee who took the bill in good faith, the B/L generally becomes the contract itself rather than mere evidence of it, because the endorsee was never party to the original booking and can only look to the document in their hands. Between the original shipper and the carrier it remains evidence.

Note on the straight bill. Older texts state that a straight B/L need not be presented at delivery because the consignee is named and can be identified. The House of Lords in The Rafaela S [2005] UKHL 11 held that a straight bill is a document of title requiring presentation, and that the Hague-Visby Rules apply to it. In the oral, the defensible answer is: practice varies by jurisdiction and by the wording of the bill, but presentation of an original is the safe operational rule, and delivering without presentation exposes the carrier to a misdelivery claim that the P&I club may decline to cover.

Endorsement mechanics. A full (special) endorsement names the new party — "deliver to X" — and is signed and stamped by the current holder. An endorsement in blank is a signature and stamp on the reverse with no named party, which converts the bill into one transferable by delivery alone. Blank endorsement is convenient and is the reason a lost or stolen original is a serious fraud exposure.

💻 The Electronic Bill of Lading

An e-B/L is a functional electronic equivalent of the paper bill, issued and transferred on an approved platform. Its legal effect does not come from the technology: it comes from MLETR-based national legislation where enacted, and from the contractual rulebook that binds every user of the platform to treat electronic transfer as equivalent to endorsement and delivery. Distributed-ledger or centralised-registry technology is how uniqueness and exclusive control are enforced — it is the mechanism, not the source of legal validity.

  • Speed. Transfer is effectively instantaneous, removing the courier leg that routinely delays cargo release and drives demand for letters of indemnity.
  • Cost. Removes printing, courier and re-issue costs, and the LOI exposure created when cargo arrives before the paper does.
  • Security. Cryptographic control prevents duplicate originals and retroactive alteration — the two classic paper-B/L frauds.
  • Constraint. Recognition is jurisdiction-dependent. An e-B/L is only as good as the law at the place of delivery and the P&I club's approval of the platform.
[Paper] Shipper ─> Courier ─> Bank ─> Courier ─> Consignee ─> Master (days; risk of loss; LOI often needed if cargo beats the paper) [e-B/L] Shipper ─> [Approved platform — exclusive control transferred] ─> Consignee (minutes; no LOI; validity depends on MLETR-based law at delivery)

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Explain the difference between a Clean and a Claused Bill of Lading, and why a Master would refuse to sign clean.
A clean B/L states that the carrier received the cargo in apparent good order and condition, with no notation of defect or shortage. A claused or "dirty" bill carries an express remark recording damage, shortage, staining, wet packaging or similar. The Master has a duty to ensure the bill accurately reflects the apparent condition of the goods as received. Signing clean for visibly damaged cargo binds the carrier to deliver in that stated good condition, so any damage found at discharge becomes the carrier's liability even though it existed before loading. The Master therefore clauses the bill, or refuses to sign it clean. Shippers commonly offer a letter of indemnity to obtain a clean bill — accepting one to conceal known damage is a misrepresentation to the bank and the LOI is generally unenforceable and outside P&I cover.
Q2. A shipper says the contract of carriage is fully contained in the Bill of Lading. Evaluate that statement.
It is inaccurate as between the original parties. The B/L is evidence of the contract of carriage, not the contract itself; the contract is concluded earlier, at booking or under the governing charterparty. The proof is that if cargo is damaged after loading but before the bill is issued, the shipper can still claim under the pre-existing contract. There is a refinement worth stating: once the bill is transferred to a third-party endorsee who took it in good faith, the terms on the face of the bill generally become the contract as against that endorsee, because they were never party to the original booking and can only rely on the document they hold.
Q3. What is the legal basis for an electronic Bill of Lading, and what makes it as secure as paper?
The legal basis is domestic legislation giving electronic transferable records the same effect as paper documents — the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records of 2017, enacted for example by the UK Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 — together with the contractual rulebook that binds all users of an approved platform. The technology, whether distributed ledger or a centralised registry, provides the two properties the law requires: a reliable method of identifying the record as unique so there cannot be two originals, and of establishing exclusive control so that transfer of control functions as endorsement and delivery. The Rotterdam Rules also provide for electronic transport records, but they are not in force and cannot be relied on as operative law.
Q4. What are your specific duties as Chief Engineer in relation to what is written on the Bill of Lading?
My duties are evidential. Before loading, I confirm hold bilge systems, bilge alarms and hold ventilation are tested and logged, and support hold tightness and hatch cover ultrasonic testing so the vessel can demonstrate cargo spaces fit to receive the cargo. Tank soundings are taken and recorded immediately before and after loading so that no shipboard liquid transfer can be alleged to have affected cargo weight or condition. During the voyage I log reefer power continuity, any interruption and its duration, and the daily bilge soundings for every hold. All bunker delivery notes and fuel change-over records are retained. The purpose is to establish that the carrier exercised due diligence and cared properly for the cargo — which is what preserves the liability limits and immunities under the carriage regime that the B/L incorporates.

The commercial pressure

On short sea legs and in oil trades, the vessel routinely arrives before the original bills have completed the banking chain. The receiver cannot present an original, and demurrage starts accruing. The standard fix is a letter of indemnity from the charterer or receiver asking the carrier to deliver without presentation.

Why this is dangerous

Delivery without presentation of an original bill is a breach of the contract of carriage and, if the goods go to the wrong party, a conversion. P&I clubs exclude liability arising from delivery of cargo without production of the original bill of lading from standard cover. The owner is then relying entirely on the creditworthiness of whoever signed the LOI — which is exactly the party most likely to have disappeared when a fraud unwinds.

Two distinct LOI situations — do not confuse them

An LOI offered to obtain a clean bill for known damaged cargo is a misrepresentation to the bank and the endorsee, and English courts have held such indemnities unenforceable as founded on fraud. An LOI offered for delivery without production of originals is not inherently fraudulent and is commercially common — but it still moves the risk outside P&I cover onto the indemnifier's balance sheet. The Chief Engineer's practical involvement is limited but real: the accuracy of your cargo-care records is what determines whether the underlying claim is defensible in the first place.

📖 Definition

A charterparty is a contract by which a shipowner places a vessel, or her cargo-carrying capacity, at the disposal of a charterer for a specific voyage or a defined period. Its function is to allocate cost, risk and control between the two: who buys the bunkers, who pays port dues, who employs and directs the crew, who carries the delay risk, and what happens to the money when the ship stops performing. The name comes from charta partita — the medieval practice of writing the agreement twice on one parchment and cutting it in a jagged line, each party keeping a half.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The charter type determines who the Chief Engineer's technical decisions cost money. Under a time charter the charterer buys the fuel, so every hour of poor combustion efficiency is a claim waiting to be raised against the owner under the speed and consumption warranty.
  • Off-hire is the single commercial term most directly driven by the engine room. A breakdown that stops the vessel suspends hire — the owner's revenue stops while costs continue. Planned maintenance scheduling is therefore a commercial decision as much as a technical one.
  • Under a time charter the charterer supplies the bunkers but the owner carries the machinery. Fuel-quality damage claims sit exactly on that boundary, and they are won or lost on the manifold sample and the bunker records the engine room controls.
  • Under bareboat the charterer takes over technical management including the survey cycle — which is why a bareboat handover triggers heightened PSC and Class scrutiny.

⏱ Laytime, Demurrage, Despatch and Laycan
  • Laytime — the time allowed by the charterparty for loading and discharging, without additional payment. It begins on tendering a valid Notice of Readiness and the expiry of any turn time.
  • Demurrage — liquidated damages payable by the charterer to the owner for each day (and pro rata) that laytime is exceeded. Once on demurrage, always on demurrage: exceptions that would have interrupted laytime generally do not interrupt demurrage unless expressly stated.
  • Despatch — a payment by the owner to the charterer when cargo work finishes before laytime expires, conventionally at half the demurrage rate.
  • Laycan (laydays / cancelling) — the window within which the vessel must arrive and tender readiness. Arrive early and the charterer need not accept before the first layday; arrive after the cancelling date and the charterer has an absolute right to cancel, whatever the reason for the delay.

The laycan point is the harsh one and examiners like it: heavy weather, a breakdown, or congestion at the previous port are all irrelevant to the charterer's cancelling right. This is why the passage plan, the achievable service speed and the Chief Engineer's honest assessment of what the machinery can sustain are commercial inputs, not just technical ones.

A Contract of Affreightment (COA) sits above individual voyages: an undertaking to carry a stated quantity of cargo over a period, across multiple shipments, with the specific vessel nominated later. It fixes freight, laycan windows, cargo specification, laytime terms, lien provisions and jurisdiction, and gives both sides volume certainty without tying up a named ship.

🛑 Off-Hire and the Performance Warranty

Off-hire suspends the charterer's obligation to pay hire for the period the vessel is prevented from giving full working service, for causes listed in the off-hire clause — typically breakdown of machinery, damage to hull, deficiency of crew, drydocking, or detention. The wording matters: a net loss of time clause deducts only time actually lost, while a period clause deducts the whole period from the event until full efficiency is restored.

  • Typical engine-room triggers: main engine breakdown at sea; turbocharger or auxiliary blower failure preventing full power; steering gear defect; cargo crane or hatch cover machinery failure during cargo work; generator shortage forcing reduced operations.
  • The owner keeps paying crew, insurance and stores throughout — off-hire is pure revenue loss, and it is directly attributable to the machinery. This is the commercial argument for spending on planned maintenance rather than deferring it.
  • Speed and consumption warranty: the owner warrants a service speed at a stated daily consumption, usually in good weather up to a defined Beaufort/sea-state limit. Underperformance gives the charterer a claim for the time lost and the excess fuel burned. Hull and propeller fouling, poor combustion, and turbocharger fouling are the usual technical causes.

Fuel supplied by the charterer. Under a time charter the charterer buys the bunkers and warrants that they meet the specification in the charterparty and the statutory requirements of MARPOL Annex VI. If off-spec fuel scores fuel pumps, blocks filters or damages the engine, the owner has a claim — but only if the evidence chain holds. The MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 18 retained sample drawn at the receiving ship's manifold, properly sealed, jointly signed and correctly stored, is the single most important piece of evidence in the case.

The answer he wants is a sequence, not an opinion: change over to a known-good tank, increase purifier throughput and lower throughput temperature margin, log all parameters, inform the Master, preserve the MARPOL manifold sample unopened, retain the BDN and the ship's own drip samples, and request a joint analysis. 🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Under a time charter the charterer supplies fuel that causes fuel pump scoring and filter clogging. What are your immediate actions and who is liable?
Immediate actions: change over to a tank of known-good fuel; isolate the suspect tank and stop further transfer from it; increase purification efficiency and adjust settling and purifier temperatures; monitor and log fuel pump, filter differential pressure, exhaust temperatures and any power limitation; inform the Master at once so a Letter of Protest can be issued and the charterer notified. Evidence: secure the MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 18 retained sample drawn at the receiving ship's manifold — unopened, sealed and jointly signed — together with the Bunker Delivery Note, the ship's own drip samples and the change-over record, and request a joint analysis at an accredited laboratory. On liability: under a time charter the charterer supplies and pays for bunkers and warrants that they meet the specification stated in the charterparty and the statutory MARPOL requirements. If off-spec fuel causes machinery damage or performance shortfall, the owner has a claim against the charterer — but that claim stands or falls on the sample chain of custody, which is my responsibility.
Q2. A vessel on a voyage charter arrives at the load port 12 hours after the cancelling date because of heavy weather. What is the position?
The charterer is entitled to reject the vessel and cancel the charterparty. The cancelling date in the laycan is an absolute contractual right; the reason for the delay is irrelevant, so heavy weather, machinery breakdown or congestion elsewhere provide no defence. In practice the charterer will often not cancel if the market has moved against them, and may instead seek a reduced rate, but the legal right exists. The operational consequence for us is that the achievable service speed must be assessed honestly and early — if it becomes clear the vessel cannot make the cancelling date, the Master and owner need to know while there is still time to negotiate an extension rather than after the window has closed.
Q3. Explain off-hire and give two engine-room events that would trigger it.
Off-hire is the mechanism in a time charter that suspends the charterer's obligation to pay hire for the period during which the vessel is prevented from providing full working service, for a cause listed in the off-hire clause. Two engine-room examples: a main engine breakdown requiring a stoppage or a significant power reduction at sea, and failure of cargo-handling machinery such as a crane or hatch cover system during cargo operations. Whether the whole period or only the net time lost is deducted depends on the clause wording — a net loss of time clause deducts only time actually lost, while a period clause runs from the event until full efficiency is restored, which can be far more expensive for the owner. Throughout the off-hire period the owner continues to pay crew, insurance and stores, so the loss is pure revenue.
Q4. What changes for you technically when a vessel goes on bareboat charter?
Under a bareboat charter the charterer takes possession and becomes responsible for crewing, technical management, insurance, maintenance and the statutory survey cycle, and normally becomes the "Company" for ISM purposes. Practically that means a new Document of Compliance and Safety Management Certificate, a new entry on the Continuous Synopsis Record, possibly a change of flag and of Recognised Organisation, and a new planned maintenance system and spares supply chain. The handover is a high-risk period: Class and PSC treat a change of manager as a targeting factor, so I would expect an expanded inspection at the next port and would make sure the maintenance records, oil record books, PMS history and certificates are complete and consistent with the new management entity before the handover is signed off.

Why most claims fail

Charterer-supplied fuel disputes are usually lost not on the science but on the evidence. The commonest failures are: the MARPOL retained sample was not drawn continuously at the receiving ship's manifold; the sample seals were broken or the joint signature is missing; the ship's own drip sample was taken from the barge side rather than the manifold; or the change-over record does not establish which fuel was actually in the engine when the damage occurred.

The chain that works

Draw the MARPOL Annex VI Regulation 18 sample continuously throughout delivery at the receiving ship's manifold, using a fitting dedicated to that purpose. Seal it with a unique identification number, have the barge representative and the ship's officer both sign the label, and record the number on the BDN. Retain it under the ship's control for the period required, in a safe place outside accommodation. Take the ship's own additional samples at the same point at the same time. Record the exact time of change-over to and from the disputed fuel against the running hours and the damage timeline.

Technical evidence to add

Filter differential pressure trend before and after change-over; purifier sludge discharge frequency and appearance; fuel pump plunger and barrel condition with photographs and part numbers; exhaust temperature deviation by unit; measured viscosity and settling tank temperature; any cat-fines analysis, since aluminium plus silicon above the fuel's stated limit is the classic mechanism for accelerated liner and pump wear. Present these as a timeline, not a list — the aim is to show the damage tracks the fuel.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

The practical discipline is that the sample locker and the BDN file are audited as carefully as the Oil Record Book. A missing joint signature on a seal label is a small administrative failure that can extinguish a six-figure claim, and it is entirely within the engine room's control to prevent.

📖 Definition

Maritime economics, in the ship-management sense, is the set of commercial frameworks that determine how shipping revenue is generated and how transport costs are allocated: how freight is built up and surcharged, where cost and risk pass between seller and buyer under standard trade terms, and how tanker freight is benchmarked. None of it is IMO law — Incoterms are published by the International Chamber of Commerce, and Worldscale is administered by the private Worldscale Association. They operate alongside statutory requirements, not under them.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Bunker consumption is the largest controllable variable cost in most trades. On a Worldscale-rated voyage the freight is fixed by the index and the market percentage; every tonne of fuel saved against the voyage estimate falls straight to the owner's margin.
  • The Bunker Adjustment Factor exists because fuel price volatility cannot be absorbed in a quoted rate. Understanding it explains why fuel-efficiency measures are visible at board level, not just in the engine room.
  • Incoterms decide who is exposed when cargo is damaged. The point at which risk passes governs whether the carrier's claim exposure runs to the seller or the buyer — and which party's insurer will be pursuing the ship.
  • Deadweight and draft verification is an engineering task with a direct commercial consequence: the tonnage actually loaded is what is billed, and a discrepancy between draft survey and manifest is a dispute.

💰 Building Up a Freight Rate

  • Terminal Handling Charge (THC) — terminal costs for lifting and positioning at each end.

  • CFR / CIF — seller pays freight (and, under CIF, marine insurance) to the destination port, but risk still passes on placement on board at the load port. The split between where cost ends and where risk ends is the single most examinable feature of these two terms.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. An LCL package measures 2.0 m × 1.5 m × 2.0 m and weighs 4.5 tonnes. How is the ocean freight assessed?
LCL shipments are charged on weight or measurement, whichever is greater. The volume is 2.0 × 1.5 × 2.0 = 6.0 cubic metres. On the ocean freight convention one cubic metre is charged as approximately one tonne, so 6.0 CBM is equivalent to 6.0 revenue tonnes. That exceeds the actual weight of 4.5 tonnes, so the shipment is billed on the volumetric basis of 6.0 CBM. The same package moving by air would be assessed very differently, because the IATA volumetric divisor of 6,000 cubic centimetres per kilogram values one cubic metre at only about 167 kg — around 6 cubic metres to the tonne — which is why bulky, light cargo is heavily penalised in air freight.
Q2. What is WS100, and what does a fixture at WS150 mean?
WS100 is the Worldscale flat rate for a specific route — the freight in US dollars per tonne calculated by the Worldscale Associations for a nominal standard vessel of 75,000 tonnes deadweight, 14.5 knots service speed, 55 tonnes per day bunker consumption, a fixed hire element of US$12,000 per day and four days total port time, on a round-voyage basis including the ballast leg. Flat rates are recalculated annually. A fixture at WS150 means the charterer pays 150% of that published flat rate for the route. The system exists so that owners and charterers can negotiate a single percentage figure rather than recalculating full voyage economics for every one of thousands of route and cargo combinations.
Q3. Under CIF terms, cargo is damaged during the ocean passage. Who bears the loss, and why does this surprise people?
The buyer bears the risk. Under CIF the seller pays the cost of carriage and marine insurance to the named destination port, but the risk of loss or damage passes to the buyer when the goods are placed on board the vessel at the port of shipment. Cost and risk therefore separate, which is what surprises people: the seller is still paying freight for a voyage during which the goods are already at the buyer's risk. The buyer's remedy is against the insurance the seller was obliged to arrange — and under Incoterms 2020 that obligation for CIF is still only minimum cover, Institute Cargo Clauses (C), whereas the multimodal equivalent CIP was upgraded to all-risks Clauses (A). It is also worth noting that the old "ship's rail" boundary was deleted in Incoterms 2010; the test is now whether the goods have been placed on board.
Q4. Which Incoterm places the greatest burden on the seller, and what does that mean operationally?
DDP, Delivered Duty Paid. The seller bears all costs and risks of bringing the goods to the named destination place, including main carriage, insurance arrangements, inland delivery, import customs clearance and payment of all import duties and taxes. Operationally it means the seller must be able to act as importer of record in the buyer's country, which is often impractical or legally impossible for a foreign seller — one reason DDP is used less than its apparent simplicity suggests. At the opposite end, EXW places the minimum obligation on the seller, who need only make the goods available at their own premises.

The structure of the exposure

On a voyage charter or a Worldscale-rated fixture, the freight is fixed at the moment of fixing. Every variable cost thereafter is the owner's. Bunkers are typically the largest single item, so the voyage result is decided by the difference between the consumption assumed in the voyage estimate and the consumption actually achieved.

Where the tonnes actually go

Hull roughness and biofouling: a fouled hull can add a double-digit percentage to propulsive power demand at constant speed, and the penalty compounds with time since the last cleaning. Propeller roughness and blade damage act similarly but are cheaper to correct. Air cooler fouling raises scavenge air temperature and reduces charge density, worsening combustion. Turbocharger fouling reduces available air, forcing later, hotter combustion. Poor injector condition raises specific fuel consumption directly. Excessive auxiliary load, particularly unnecessary running of pumps and compressors in parallel, is a quiet continuous drain.

Making it visible

Trend specific fuel oil consumption against corrected shaft power, not against speed over ground, so that current and weather are removed from the comparison. Log hull performance indicators consistently so that hull cleaning can be justified commercially rather than argued for on general principles. Where a CII rating clause exists in the charter, the same data set serves two purposes — commercial performance evidence and regulatory rating management.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

Container tonnage on liner service has an added dimension: schedule integrity means speed is often non-negotiable, so the savings must come from efficiency at the required speed rather than from slowing down. That focuses attention on hull and propeller condition, air path cleanliness and auxiliary load management, which are precisely the areas the Chief Engineer controls directly.

📖 Definition

An international carriage of goods regime is a body of rules fixing the minimum obligations, the immunities, and the maximum financial liability of a sea carrier under a bill of lading. Its purpose is a bargain: carriers accept a floor of non-excludable duties — principally due diligence to make the ship seaworthy and proper care of the cargo — and in exchange receive a defined list of defences and a ceiling on liability. Before these regimes, carriers used freedom of contract to exclude liability even for their own crews' negligence, and cargo interests and their financing banks had no reliable recourse.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The carrier's entire package of immunities and limits depends on proving due diligence to make the ship seaworthy before and at the beginning of the voyage. That proof is built almost entirely from engine room records — planned maintenance history, survey reports, test records and defect close-outs.
  • Unseaworthiness is not confined to the hull. Inoperative hold bilge systems, defective hatch cover seals, a failed hold ventilation fan or an undermanned engine room can each render a vessel unseaworthy for the intended cargo and voyage.
  • The distinction between a latent defect (a defence) and a failure to maintain (not a defence) is decided on documentation. A component that failed with no prior indication in the records is a latent defect; the same failure with two years of ignored condition-monitoring trends is negligence.
  • India moved to a Hague-Visby basis in 2025. For a Kochi MMD candidate this is the single most current and most examinable fact in this topic.

⚖ The Three Core Carrier Obligations
  • Seaworthiness. Due diligence to make the ship seaworthy — an obligation of due diligence, not an absolute warranty, but one that is non-delegable: the carrier remains answerable for the negligence of a repair yard or a servicing contractor.
  • Manning, equipping and supplying. Properly manned, equipped and supplied for the intended voyage — including sufficient bunkers, stores, spares and a competent, rested crew.
  • Cargoworthiness and cargo care. Holds, refrigerating and cool chambers fit and safe for the reception, carriage and preservation of the goods; and thereafter proper and careful loading, handling, stowage, carriage, custody, care and discharge.

The timing phrase "before and at the beginning of the voyage" is doing heavy lifting. The duty attaches to the period from the start of loading until the vessel breaks ground for the voyage. A defect arising genuinely at sea, which could not have been detected by due diligence beforehand, does not breach Article III rule 1 — which is precisely why the maintenance record for the period before departure decides most cases.

Ad valorem declaration. Under all these regimes the limit falls away if the shipper declares the nature and value of the goods before shipment and that declaration is inserted in the bill of lading. The carrier is then liable up to the declared value — and will charge a correspondingly higher freight. "Ad valorem" is the correct term; avoid informal abbreviations in an oral.

The container problem and its fix. Under the 1924 Rules, with only a package basis and no weight alternative, a carrier could argue that a container holding 500 cartons was a single "package", capping liability at one limit for the whole box. Article IV rule 5(c) of Hague-Visby resolves this: where the number of packages inside the container is enumerated in the bill of lading, each enumerated package counts separately. Where it is not enumerated, the container itself is the package. This is why the description on the face of the bill matters commercially and not merely administratively.

🇮🇳 The Indian Position after 2025
  • The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025 (Act No. 19 of 2025) received assent on 8 August 2025 and commenced on 10 September 2025, repealing the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925.
  • Its Schedule applies the Hague Rules as amended by the Brussels Protocol of 23 February 1968 and the Protocol of 21 December 1979 — that is, the Hague-Visby Rules with SDR limits.
  • The Act applies to carriage of goods by sea in ships carrying goods from any port in India to any other port, whether in or outside India — the outward-cargo scope inherited from the 1925 Act.
  • Every bill of lading or similar document of title issued in India that evidences a contract to which the Rules apply must carry an express statement that it has effect subject to the Rules.
  • The Central Government is empowered to amend the Schedule by notification, allowing India to adopt future international carriage conventions without fresh primary legislation.
  • It sits alongside the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 (assented 24 July 2025) and the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 in the same wave of maritime law reform.

For the oral, the sequence worth having ready is: 1925 Act was Hague-based → repealed → 2025 Act is Hague-Visby based with SDR limits → therefore the Indian package limitation is now 666.67 SDR per package or 2 SDR per kilogram, whichever is higher, not the old gold-value figure.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is meant by an ad valorem declaration and how does it affect the carrier's liability?
An ad valorem declaration is a statement by the shipper, made before shipment, of the nature and value of the goods, which is then inserted into the bill of lading. Its effect is to displace the package limitation: instead of being capped at 666.67 SDR per package or unit or 2 SDR per kilogram of gross weight, whichever is higher, the carrier becomes liable up to the declared value. The carrier will charge a higher freight to reflect the increased exposure. Under the original Hague Rules the same mechanism displaced the £100 gold-value limit. Where no declaration is made, the statutory limits apply regardless of the actual value of the goods, which is why high-value cargo either declares or is separately insured.
Q2. Distinguish a latent defect from a failure to exercise due diligence.
A latent defect is a hidden flaw in material or manufacture that could not have been discovered by reasonable inspection, testing or routine maintenance — for example a fatigue crack propagating from a subsurface inclusion in a forging, where the non-destructive testing was carried out at the correct interval and passed. Where such a defect causes cargo loss, the carrier has a defence under Article IV rule 2. A failure of due diligence is different in kind: the condition was known or reasonably discoverable and was not acted upon — an overdue planned maintenance job, a rising bearing temperature trend that was recorded but ignored, or a deferred defect carried without assessment. That invalidates the carrier's exemptions and exposes the owner to the full claim. The distinction is decided on records, which is why the engine room's documentation is the carrier's primary defence.
Q3. Why must due diligence be exercised "before and at the beginning of the voyage"?
Those words fix the temporal window in which the seaworthiness obligation applies. It runs from the commencement of loading until the vessel breaks ground for the voyage. Within that window the carrier must exercise due diligence to make the ship seaworthy, properly manned, equipped and supplied, and to make the holds and cool chambers fit for the cargo. The obligation is one of due diligence, not an absolute warranty of seaworthiness, but it is non-delegable, so the carrier answers for the negligence of a repair yard or servicing contractor. If a defect arises genuinely at sea and could not have been detected beforehand by due diligence, the carrier does not breach Article III rule 1 and can rely on the statutory exceptions. This is why maintenance and test records for the period immediately before departure are decisive in most cargo claims.
Q4. What is the current Indian statutory position on carriage of goods by sea?
The Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 has been repealed. The Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 2025, Act No. 19 of 2025, received Presidential assent on 8 August 2025 and commenced on 10 September 2025. Its Schedule applies the Hague Rules as amended by the Visby Protocol of 23 February 1968 and the Protocol of 21 December 1979, so India now operates on a Hague-Visby basis with SDR limits — 666.67 SDR per package or unit or 2 SDR per kilogram of gross weight, whichever is higher. The Act applies to ships carrying goods from any port in India to any other port, whether in or outside India, and requires bills of lading issued in India to state expressly that they take effect subject to the Rules. It also empowers the Central Government to amend the Schedule by notification, so India can adopt future conventions without new primary legislation. It was enacted alongside the Bills of Lading Act, 2025 and the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025.

The evidential burden

Once cargo damage is established, the carrier must show either that the loss falls within one of the Article IV rule 2 exceptions, or that due diligence was exercised to make the ship seaworthy before and at the beginning of the voyage. Where unseaworthiness is alleged as the cause, the burden of proving due diligence rests on the carrier — it is not for the cargo interest to prove negligence.

The document set that carries the burden

Planned maintenance system history for the systems implicated, showing jobs raised, done and closed with dates and running hours. Condition monitoring records — vibration, lube oil analysis, bearing temperatures, performance data — with evidence that adverse trends were investigated. Class and statutory survey status with no overdue or conditionally waived items relevant to the failure. Test records for hold bilge systems, bilge alarms, hold ventilation, hatch cover ultrasonic tightness testing and cargo hold fixed detection. Defect list with each open item risk-assessed and reported. Spares inventory demonstrating that critical spares were on board as required.

The three failure modes that lose cases

First, retrospective completion — PMS jobs signed off in a block after the incident, which is obvious from the system audit trail and destroys credibility. Second, trend data recorded but not acted on, which converts a latent-defect defence into proof of negligence. Third, an open defect carried without a documented risk assessment or notification to the office, which makes the unseaworthiness knowing rather than inadvertent.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

For container tonnage the reefer plant deserves specific attention, because reefer cargo claims are frequent, high-value and technically well-documented on both sides. Continuous logging of supply and return air temperatures, power interruption events with durations, defrost cycles and any alarm acknowledgements gives a defensible record. The absence of that record, on a cargo that is designed to be temperature-monitored, is itself read as a failure of care.
