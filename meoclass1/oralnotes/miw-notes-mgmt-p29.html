Lubricating-Oil Analysis and Condition Monitoring — Spectrometry, FTIR, Particle Counting, BN and AN, and Reading the Trend

📖 Definition

Used-oil analysis is the laboratory (or shipboard) testing of a sample of in-service lubricating oil to answer two questions at once: is the oil still fit for use, and — to a certain extent — what condition is the machinery it lubricates in. The tests fall into three families: fluid properties (viscosity, base number, acid number, oxidation), contamination (water, fuel, soot and insolubles, glycol, sea-water and fuel elements) and wear debris (wear metals and ferrous particles). The core methods are elemental spectrometric analysis (ICP or RDE atomic emission, reported in ppm), FTIR (infrared absorption by molecules), particle counting (ISO cleanliness code) and the BN/AN titrations. A single result rarely diagnoses anything: the value of oil analysis lies in the trend from the same sampling point, laboratory and method.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Simon asks what the report means, not what the laboratory does. The Chief Engineer must read a spectrometric, FTIR and BN result together and say what is happening in the engine — and what he will do next.
  • The limitations are where candidates fail. ICP does not see particles larger than about 5–7 µm, so iron can look steady while a bearing or liner is failing; a particle count on dark engine oil is unreliable; BN from two different titration methods cannot be compared.
  • System oil on a two-stroke is a shared fluid. It lubricates bearings, cools pistons and, on an ME engine, is the hydraulic medium for the HPS and HCUs — a stuffing-box leak, a cooler leak or a leaking fuel pump all show first in the oil report.
  • Oil analysis is condition-based maintenance evidence. It justifies opening up, or not opening up, a unit and supports the maintenance decision under the company's planned maintenance system.

🧪 Why Analyse Used Oil — Two Purposes, Three Families of Test

The source opens with the two purposes of analysis — the condition of the oil and the working condition of the machinery from which the sample was taken — and the three categories of test. CIMAC adds the honest qualifier: analysis shows oil condition and suitability for further use reliably, and machinery condition only to a certain extent, and only when the sample is representative.

Sampling decides everything. CIMAC's key points: sample with the machine running at normal temperature; from the main supply line at the engine inlet, in full flow (a side or top tapping on a horizontal pipe, not the bottom); always from the same point; flush about 0.5–1.0 litre through the valve first; fill a clean, dedicated bottle (about 250 ml, or about 100 ml for two-stroke scavenge drain oil) to about 90%; label it fully (engine, oil, sampling point, date, running hours) and dispatch it promptly. Sample monthly until a trend is established and then at intervals set by the OEM or oil supplier, normally not more than three months apart — and always at intervals shorter than the expected time to failure of the condition being watched.

⚛️ Elemental (Spectrometric) Analysis — ICP and RDE

Principle. In atomic emission spectroscopy the oil sample is exposed to a high-energy source — an electric arc/spark or a plasma. The atoms are excited and, as they relax, emit light at wavelengths characteristic of each element. The intensity at each element's wavelength, calibrated against oil-soluble metal standards, gives its concentration, reported in ppm (mg/kg). This is the most fundamental routine test, which is why the source also calls it wear-metal analysis — but it measures additive elements and contaminants as well as wear metals. CIMAC lists three spectroscopic routes: atomic emission (the usual one), atomic absorption and X-ray fluorescence.

What the elements point to (CIMAC Rec. 30): additive elements Ca, Mg, P, Zn (and Si) identify the oil and reveal mixing with another grade; wear elements Al, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mo, Pb, Sb, Sn indicate wear of bearings, rings, liners and other components; contaminants K, Mg, B, Cl and Na come from water, condensate, sea water or coolant, V and Ni from residual fuel, and Si from dust or sand as well as from anti-foam additive.

The limitation that matters. Because ICP misses particles above about 5–7 µm, wear-metal readings can flatten — or even fall — while a severe wear process accelerates and produces larger debris. CIMAC's remedy is non-routine analysis: the PQ index (a magnetometric measure of total ferrous debris, independent of particle size and most sensitive above about 20 µm), ferrography (ferrous particles aligned by size for expert examination of the wear mechanism — cutting, fatigue, adhesion), or ashing / XRF to capture all the material. CIMAC also warns that results depend on apparatus and preparation, so trends must use the same laboratory, apparatus and method, and that low values (below 5 ppm) should be read with caution.

🌈 FTIR — Infrared Absorption by Molecules

Principle. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy is a purely instrument-based test. Every chemical bond absorbs infrared energy at its own characteristic frequencies (the source's 'resonance frequencies'), expressed as wavenumbers in cm−1. A thin film of the used oil is placed in the infrared cell; the transmitted spectrum is compared with the incident beam and with a new-oil reference of the same product, and the absorbance at each characteristic wavenumber measures how much of that molecular species is present. Infrared is absorbed by molecules, not by particles — FTIR does not count or size particles.

CIMAC stresses that a fresh-oil baseline is needed and that only results from the same laboratory and method should be compared.

🔬 Particle Counting and the ISO Cleanliness Code

What it measures. Particle counting gives the number and size distribution of solid particles in the oil — a direct measure of cleanliness. Automatic optical counters use light obscuration or scattering (laser); the microscope method (ISO 4407) is older but still a current standard and a referee method for dark or heavily contaminated fluids, so 'old method' should not be read as 'obsolete'. Pore-blockage counters are a further type used for dark oils.

ISO 4406 cleanliness code (automatic counter calibrated to ISO 11171) three scale numbers = particles per mL ≥ 4 µm(c) / ≥ 6 µm(c) / ≥ 14 µm(c) e.g. 18/16/13 (illustrative) each step up the scale ≈ DOUBLES the count microscope count (ISO 4407) is reported as a two-number code at ≥ 5 µm and ≥ 15 µm alternative scales: NAS 1638, SAE AS4059

Where it works and where it does not. For hydraulic, gear and turbine oils particle counting is the proactive measure: a rising code warns of filter or purifier failure, ingress and early wear before damage. For used engine oils CIMAC is explicit: the ISO 4406/NAS optical methods were developed for hydraulic oils, are not very suitable for dark, sooty engine oils that must be diluted as much as 1:10, give significant scatter, and are therefore not routine. Wear in engine oils is tracked mainly by spectrometry, the PQ index and ferrography. The link to the engine room: on an ME engine the system oil is also the hydraulic oil for the hydraulic power supply and cylinder units, and the CIMAC two-stroke table lists a rising particle count among the risks to hydraulic system components (Topic 3).

⚖️ Base Number vs Acid Number

Definitions. Acid number (AN, formerly TAN) is the amount of acidic material in the oil; base number (BN, formerly TBN) is its alkaline reserve — the capacity left to neutralise acids from combustion. Both are expressed in mg KOH/g and found by potentiometric titration: AN by titration with potassium hydroxide (ASTM D664); BN by titration with an acid — perchloric acid (ASTM D2896, the usual method for new and marine engine oils, giving the higher value) or hydrochloric acid (ASTM D4739, often used for used oils). The two BN methods are not interchangeable, so a trend must stay on one method.

Low BN, correctly explained. Sulphur in the fuel burns to SO2 with a little SO3; with the water formed by combustion these condense as sulphuric acid, which the oil's alkaline additives neutralise. In a trunk-piston engine the acidic combustion products reach the crankcase oil with the blow-by gas. BN depletion is therefore driven by fuel sulphur, by oil consumption (fresh top-up restores alkalinity) and by operating conditions — a low BN is not by itself a measure of blow-by, and a wrong oil grade is always a possibility.

Method, not cost, is the limitation. BN and AN are routine, inexpensive titrations, and shipboard BN kits are standard for scrape-down and system-oil checks. The real caution is that the value depends on the test method — trend results from one laboratory and one method only.

📈 Reading the Report — Trend, Limits and Limitations
  • One analysis is not a diagnosis. CIMAC: a full and proper diagnosis cannot be achieved from the results of just one analysis. Plot each parameter against time or running hours and look for a change of trend — 'it is the trend that is most important'.
  • Limits are guidance. 'Caution' and 'action/urgent' limits come from the OEM and the oil supplier (OEM limits first, at least during warranty) and must be refined system by system as data builds up. Reliance on arbitrary limits gives false security.
  • Fast failures escape it. Oil analysis is less effective where the failure develops quickly — liner scuffing or a bearing fracture — because the sampling interval is longer than the time to failure.
  • Same point, same laboratory, same method. Spectrometry, insolubles, BN and FTIR are all method-dependent; a change of laboratory resets the baseline.
  • Context. The analyst needs engine running hours, oil top-up, fuel changes and any incident — the engine staff's information is part of the diagnosis.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is used lube-oil analysis, and what are its main categories?
Used-oil analysis is the testing of a representative sample of in-service oil to assess the condition of the oil and, to a certain extent, the condition of the machinery it lubricates. The tests fall into three families. Fluid properties tell me whether the oil is still fit: viscosity, base number, acid number and the FTIR degradation measures — oxidation, nitration and sulphation. Contamination tells me what has entered the oil: water, fuel shown by a falling flash point or viscosity, soot and insolubles, glycol, and contaminant elements such as sodium and magnesium from sea water or vanadium and nickel from residual fuel. Wear debris tells me which components are wearing: wear metals by spectrometry, supported by the PQ index or ferrography for larger ferrous particles. The sample must come from the same full-flow point at the engine inlet, with the engine running at temperature and the valve flushed, and the result is read as a trend, never as a single value.
Q2. Explain elemental spectrometric analysis and its main limitation.
Elemental analysis measures the concentration of metals in the oil in ppm, that is milligrams per kilogram. It is normally done by atomic emission spectroscopy: the sample is exposed to a high-energy source, an argon plasma in ICP or an arc between a rotating disc electrode and a counter electrode in RDE. The excited atoms emit light at wavelengths characteristic of each element, and the intensity at each wavelength, calibrated against standards, gives the concentration. Atomic absorption and X-ray fluorescence are also used. The results cover additive elements such as calcium and zinc, wear metals such as iron, chromium, copper, lead and tin, and contaminants such as sodium, vanadium and silicon. The key limitation is particle size: ICP does not detect particles larger than about 5 to 7 microns, so in severe wear the iron reading can stay flat while large debris is being produced. I would then ask for the PQ index, ferrography or an ashed sample. Results also depend on the laboratory and method, and values below about 5 ppm need caution.
Q3. How does FTIR work, and what does it detect?
FTIR is infrared spectroscopy of the oil film. Different chemical bonds absorb infrared at characteristic wavenumbers. The instrument passes infrared through a thin layer of the used oil, compares the transmitted spectrum with the incident beam and with a new-oil reference, and the absorbance at each wavenumber gives the amount of that molecular species. It therefore measures molecules, not particles. CIMAC uses about 1710 inverse centimetres for oxidation, 1620 for nitration and 1150 for sulphation; FTIR also shows soot, water, glycol and fuel as trend indicators, with Karl Fischer titration used to quantify water. It is a trend tool against a fresh-oil baseline, standardised as a condition-monitoring practice in ASTM E2412.
Q4. Distinguish base number and acid number. What does a low BN in a trunk-piston engine oil indicate?
Both are in milligrams of KOH per gram and found by potentiometric titration. Acid number measures the acidic content of the oil and is the main degradation test for turbine, hydraulic, gear and compressor oils, and is also relevant for low-BN and gas-engine oils; it rises mainly with oxidation. Base number measures the alkaline reserve left to neutralise acids and is the key test for engine oils. BN by perchloric acid and by hydrochloric acid are different methods and must not be mixed in a trend. Low BN in a trunk-piston engine means the alkaline reserve has been used up by sulphuric acid formed from fuel sulphur, which reaches the crankcase with the blow-by gas. Causes are high-sulphur fuel, low oil consumption so too little fresh oil is added, or the wrong grade. The risk is corrosive wear of liners, rings and bearings. Low BN is not in itself a measure of blow-by.
Q5. Your two-stroke main engine system-oil report shows BN and viscosity both rising over three samples. What is happening and what do you do?
Rising BN together with rising viscosity in two-stroke system oil is the classic signature of cylinder oil, which has a much higher BN, leaking down into the crankcase past the piston-rod stuffing boxes; a top-up with a different, higher-BN oil is the other possibility, so I first check the top-up record. Then I check the stuffing-box drain quantities cylinder by cylinder, compare them with the maker's guidance and look for the unit with abnormal leakage, plan inspection and overhaul of the stuffing-box rings, and review cylinder-oil feed rate and scavenge-space condition. Meanwhile I watch insolubles and viscosity, run the purifier correctly, and if the oil drifts towards the supplier's action limit I discuss partial renewal. I ask the laboratory for a follow-up sample after the repair to confirm the trend has turned.

Situation (illustrative)

A four-stroke auxiliary engine's monthly reports show iron steady at a familiar level for four samples. The engine is running hotter on one main bearing temperature, and the oil filter differential pressure rises faster than usual. The third engineer's summary reads 'oil analysis normal'.

What the Chief Engineer reads into it

The method. The shore laboratory uses ICP, which does not detect particles larger than about 5–7 µm. A developing bearing or journal failure produces larger debris, so the spectrometric iron can stay flat — or even fall — while the real wear rate rises. CIMAC describes exactly this pattern against a rising PQ index. The other evidence. A rising bearing temperature and a filter loading faster than normal are independent signals that larger particles are being generated. What to ask for. A PQ index and ferrography on the next sample, plus inspection of the filter element and its debris, and a check of the copper, lead and tin trends for bearing-metal involvement.

Action and lesson

The Chief Engineer does not wait for the next routine sample: the evidence justifies opening the suspect bearing under the planned maintenance system. The general lesson for the oral is that oil analysis is one instrument among several. A 'normal' spectrometric result does not over-rule temperatures, filter behaviour or noise, and the limitation of each method must be known before the result is trusted.

🤖 MASS — Definition and the Four Degrees of Autonomy

IMO uses MASS for 'a ship which, to a varying degree, can operate independent of human interaction'. Benefits and risks, balanced. Claimed benefits are lower crewing cost, a possible reduction in accidents caused by on-board human error and fatigue, efficiency gains from optimised route and speed, and operational flexibility. Human error is moved ashore, not removed — which is why the non-mandatory MASS Code is built on goal-based safety requirements rather than an assumption of 'no human error'.

🌐 Maritime Single Window — FAL Convention and India's SagarSetu

The source writes 'Maritime Safety Window' once; the instrument is the Maritime Single Window. The FAL Convention moved to digital clearance in two steps: electronic exchange of information became mandatory on 8 April 2019, and the 2022 FAL amendments — resolution FAL.14(46), adopted 13 May 2022 — made a single window mandatory from 1 January 2024. The obligation falls on the public authorities of Contracting Governments to the FAL Convention: they must establish, maintain and use a single window for the electronic exchange of the information required on the arrival, stay and departure of ships, persons and cargo, so that data is submitted once and routed to customs, immigration, health and port authorities. IMO's purpose is faster clearance and greater efficiency of shipping.

India. On 1 February 2024 the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways launched the MSW module, together with the MMD module, in SagarSetu (NLP-Marine), the National Logistics Portal (Marine) whose mobile app was launched in 2023. The MSW module enables electronic submission, processing and exchange of maritime information and documents between government agencies, port authorities, shipping agents and other trade stakeholders, along FAL Convention lines. 🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Which MARPOL record books may be kept electronically, and under what instrument?
MARPOL, as amended with effect from 1 October 2020, allows record books to be kept electronically in a method approved by the Administration, and IMO's guidelines for doing so are resolution MEPC.312(74), adopted 17 May 2019. Paragraph 2.1 lists seven records: the Oil Record Book Parts I and II, the Cargo Record Book, the Garbage Record Book Parts I and II, the Ozone-depleting Substances Record Book, the recording of the Tier and on/off status of marine diesel engines, the Record of Fuel Oil Changeover, and the Record Book of Engine Parameters under the NOx Technical Code. The guidelines do not cover the deck, engine or official logbooks; whether those may be electronic is a flag-State decision. On board, the ship carries a Declaration issued by or for the flag State confirming that the electronic record book meets the guidelines.
Q2. What features must an approved electronic record book have to make it trustworthy?
It must allow an entry to be saved for verification only when complete — IMO's example is an Annex V discharge that cannot be saved without the ship's position. Data should come in automatically wherever possible, with manual input on sensor failure logged as a change of data source, and the system must protect automatic data and record any attempt at manipulation. Each user needs a unique log-in and password under role-based access control, and entries carry the user's electronic signature, with an additional authentication factor for the master's verification. Every change keeps the original visible with date, time and signature, and an amended verified entry goes back to pending. Records must be kept for the MARPOL period, printable as true copies for the master to certify, backed up, and updated for MARPOL amendments before they enter into force. Receipts such as a sludge barge receipt can be attached.
Q3. Explain the four degrees of autonomy for MASS.
IMO's regulatory scoping exercise, reported in MSC.1/Circ.1638 of June 2021, described four degrees. Degree one is a ship with automated processes and decision support: seafarers are on board to operate and control the systems, some operations may be automated and at times unsupervised, but the crew is ready to take control. Degree two is a remotely controlled ship with seafarers on board who can take over. Degree three is a remotely controlled ship without seafarers on board. Degree four is a fully autonomous ship whose operating system makes decisions and determines actions by itself. The degrees were defined for the scoping exercise, they are not hierarchical, and a ship may operate at different degrees during one voyage, so they should not be presented as a mandatory classification.
Q4. What is the current status of the MASS Code?
The MASS Code is a Maritime Safety Committee instrument. A joint MSC-LEG-FAL working group deals only with the cross-cutting legal and facilitation issues such as the role of the master and the remote operator. MSC 109 in December 2024 agreed a revised road map, and MSC 111, held 13 to 22 May 2026, adopted the non-mandatory International Code of Safety for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships, which took effect on 1 July 2026 for cargo ships under SOLAS chapter I; Governments are invited to apply it as far as practicable to MASS below 500 GT. Next, MSC 112 in December 2026 is to develop the framework for an experience-building phase. Development of a mandatory code is to begin in 2028, with a new SOLAS chapter, and the mandatory code is expected to be adopted by 1 July 2030 and to enter into force on 1 January 2032. Those last dates are targets: no mandatory MASS Code exists today.
Q5. What is the Maritime Single Window, and how has India implemented it?
The Maritime Single Window is the single electronic portal through which a ship or its agent submits the information required for arrival, stay and departure of ship, persons and cargo once, with the data routed to customs, immigration, health and port authorities. Under the FAL Convention, electronic exchange of information became mandatory in April 2019, and the 2022 FAL amendments, resolution FAL.14(46), made the single window mandatory from 1 January 2024 for the public authorities of Contracting Governments. In India, the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways launched the MSW module, with the MMD module, on 1 February 2024 inside SagarSetu, the National Logistics Portal (Marine). It allows electronic submission, processing and exchange of maritime information and documents between government agencies, port authorities, agents and trade stakeholders.

Situation (illustrative)

The company announces that from the next port the ship will use an electronic record-book system for the Oil Record Book Part I, the Garbage Record Book and the fuel changeover record. The superintendent asks the Chief Engineer to 'stop the paper books once the software is installed'.

What the Chief Engineer checks first

Approval and Declaration. Is the system approved by or for the flag State, and is the Declaration on board? Without it the paper books remain the legal record. For an Indian-flag ship, the reference is DG Shipping (now DGMA) Merchant Shipping Notice No. 03 of 2024. Scope. Every officer has a personal log-in; nobody shares a password; the master's verification uses the extra authentication factor. Integrity. The electronic book changes the form of the record, not the Chief Engineer's responsibility for its truth. Changeover is controlled like any other change under the SMS: a dated cut-over entry, the closed paper books retained for the MARPOL period, and crew trained before the first entry.

📖 Definition

An ME engine (MAN B&W, now Everllence) is a two-stroke crosshead engine in which fuel injection, exhaust-valve actuation and starting air are controlled electronically instead of by a camshaft. Hydraulic oil at about 300 bar from a Hydraulic Power Supply drives, cylinder by cylinder, a fuel-oil pressure booster and an exhaust-valve actuator on each Hydraulic Cylinder Unit; a proportional valve (ELFI and ELVA, or the combined FIVA) commanded by the Cylinder Control Unit sets timing, quantity and rate. The same platform carries the dual-fuel engines: the ME-GI injects gas at high pressure (up to about 300 bar) into the flame of a pilot-oil injection and burns it on the Diesel principle; the ME-GA admits gas at low pressure into the cylinder during compression and ignites the premixed charge with pilot oil on the Otto principle. Their NOx and methane-slip behaviour, the MARPOL Annex VI NOx Tiers and NOx Emission Control Areas, the IGF and IGC Codes, and the newest exhaust-energy machines — power turbine generator, hybrid and electric-assist turbochargers — complete the picture.

🖥️ The ME Platform — What 'Electronically Controlled' Really Means

The MC-versus-ME comparison table — what the ME eliminates (camshaft, chain drive, fuel cams, roller guides, starting-air distributor, mechanical governor) and what replaces it — is already taught in Simon Notes Part 5 · Note 7. This topic goes deeper: how the hydraulics, valves and controllers fit together, and how the system behaves when part of it fails.

Not a common-rail engine. The source says the ME has 'no individual fuel pump for each unit but uses a common rail system'. That is wrong for the ME. Each cylinder has its own electronically controlled, hydraulically driven fuel-oil pressure booster on its HCU. What is common is the hydraulic oil supply at about 300 bar from the HPS; fuel reaches the boosters at low supply pressure. A fuel common rail — fuel stored at injection pressure and distributed to the injectors — is the WinGD RT-flex/X principle.

ME-C and ME-B. The large-bore ME-C (and the ME-GI, ME-GA and ME-LGI built on it) has no camshaft and no camshaft chain drive. The small-bore ME-B keeps a light camshaft, chain-driven, for the exhaust valves and controls only fuel injection electronically.

⚙️ ELFI, ELVA and FIVA — How the Hydraulic Booster Injects

The command chain. The tacho system gives the ECU the crank position; the ECU sends timing commands to the CCU; the CCU drives the actuator (the source's 'proportional coil') of a proportional multi-way valve; the valve admits ~300 bar hydraulic oil under the booster's hydraulic piston; the piston drives the fuel plunger and fuel is injected. The CCU positions the valve by an electrical command signal with position feedback.

An engine has either separate ELFI + ELVA valves or a single FIVA per cylinder. Quantity, timing and rate. The valve's opening (signal level) and opening duration set the booster plunger's stroke and speed, so the injected quantity follows the strength and duration of the command, not a fixed cam stroke and helix. Injection pressure is the hydraulic pressure multiplied by the booster's area ratio. The booster has a suction valve and no helix or spill port.

🛡️ Redundancy and Failure Behaviour — Going Deeper than the MC/ME Table

Everllence's ME Engine Control System is designed so that no single failure related to the system may cause the engine to stop. In most cases a single failure does not affect performance or power availability, or does so only partly by activating a slow-down (project guide 16.01, 'Units, layout and interfaces'):

  • ECU — two in parallel; if one fails the other takes over without interruption.
  • CCU — all identical; a CCU failure automatically cuts out only that cylinder.
  • ACU — auxiliary control divided among three units so that one failure leaves enough redundancy for continued operation; HPS pump control is split between them.
  • HPS — multiple engine-driven and electric pumps with stand-by; high-pressure pipes to the HCUs are double-walled, and emergency running is possible using the outer pipe as pressure containment.
  • Networks and panels — redundant control networks A and B; two MOPs; tacho sets A and B.
  • Repair on the run — any controller can be replaced without stopping the engine, which returns to normal operation once the defective unit is replaced.

The oil is a control fluid. On the ME engine the system oil is also the hydraulic medium for the HPS and HCUs, so cleanliness and water content in the system oil matter to the control valves as well as to the bearings — the oil-analysis link in Topic 1.

🔥 Why Gas and Alternative-Fuel Engines — and the ME Family

The source gives three reasons: efficiency, NOx and the IMO GHG Strategy. Corrected: two-stroke dual-fuel engines have thermal efficiency comparable to diesel operation, so efficiency is not the main driver. The real drivers are lower SOx and particulates and lower CO2 per kWh when burning methane (partly offset by methane slip), fuel cost and availability, and — on gas carriers — the use of cargo boil-off gas. On NOx, only the low-pressure Otto-cycle engine is Tier III in gas mode without after-treatment; the high-pressure Diesel-cycle engine is Tier II. On GHG, the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy is resolution MEPC.377(80) — not part of Annex VI. Its in-force short-term measures are EEXI, CII and SEEMP; the fuel-intensity rules of the Net-Zero Framework remain draft and not adopted (Part 22 · Topic 3). Methane, ethane and methanol all contain carbon; only ammonia (and hydrogen) are carbon-free at the funnel.

Knock versus pre-ignition. The source attributes ME-GA knock to 'pre-ignition'. They are different abnormal-combustion events. Knock is auto-ignition of the unburnt end-gas after the pilot has ignited the charge; pre-ignition is ignition before the pilot — from hot spots or lube-oil droplets. A premixed Otto engine is exposed to both; a Diesel-principle engine has no premixed charge and neither problem.

Operating modes on the ME-GA. Dual-fuel (gas) mode is started manually by the operator on the MOP. The control and safety system fails to safe: any failure detected during gas running stops the gas, changes over to fuel oil and the gas system is purged with inert gas.

🌡️ How NOx Forms — Temperature, Residence Time, Oxygen

Atmospheric nitrogen is normally inert, but at very high temperature it combines with oxygen — the thermal (Zeldovich) NOx mechanism. Engine-out NOx is mostly NO, oxidising to NO2 later; the limits are expressed as NO2. Fuel-bound nitrogen adds a minor 'fuel NOx' share with residual fuels. Three factors govern thermal NOx:

FactorCorrected teachingSource's version

2. Residence timeHow long the burned gas stays above that temperature'How long the temp is maintained' — correct
3. Oxygen availabilityLocal oxygen concentration (air-fuel ratio) in the hot zone. More fuel (load) raises NOx only indirectly, by raising temperature and pressure'Amount of fuel injected' — replaced

Why the ME-GI stays at Tier II. In the ME-GI the gas is injected at high pressure into hot compressed air after the pilot oil has ignited, and it burns as it enters — a diffusion flame, exactly as with fuel oil. The flame zones burn close to stoichiometric, so local temperatures are very high and oxygen surrounds them: ideal conditions for thermal NOx. Engine-out NOx is therefore at Tier II level in both gas and fuel-oil modes, and Tier III needs EGR (less oxygen, more heat capacity in the charge) or SCR. Combustion lasts only tens of crank-angle degrees — milliseconds — so 'a very long time at high temperature' is the wrong picture.

Why the ME-GA is low-NOx in gas mode. The ME-GA burns a lean, premixed gas-air charge. Because the excess air is spread evenly through the charge, there are no hot near-stoichiometric flame zones and the peak flame temperature stays well below that of diffusion combustion. With a lower peak temperature, little time is spent above the temperature at which NOx forms quickly. Premixed flame propagation is not 'gradual' — the difference is temperature, not speed. The NOx countermeasures in general (EGR, SCR, Miller timing, HAM) are taught in Part 4 · Topic 19.

💨 Methane Slip

Definition. Methane slip is methane that is not burnt in the combustion chamber and leaves with the exhaust gas. It is highest in low-pressure Otto-cycle engines, where gas is mixed with air before ignition: premixed charge trapped in crevice volumes — the piston top land and ring pack — is not reached by the flame; in four-stroke engines gas can also short-circuit to the exhaust during valve overlap; and combustion is less complete at low load. A high-pressure Diesel-cycle engine has no gas in the cylinder during compression and scavenging, so its slip is very low.

Why it matters. Methane is a powerful greenhouse gas. IMO's 2024 LCA Guidelines (MEPC.391(81), non-mandatory) count methane with a 100-year global warming potential of 28 (IPCC AR5). Under EU law, FuelEU Maritime (from 1 January 2025) counts methane slip on a well-to-wake basis using default slip factors by engine type, unless verified actual values are used, and the EU ETS covers methane from 1 January 2026. The IMO LCA default factors by engine class are tabulated in Part 25 · Topic 1, and methane's place among the greenhouse gases in Part 22 · Topic 2; the IMO Net-Zero Framework remains a draft, not adopted (Part 22 · Topic 3).

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Is the ME engine a common-rail engine? Describe its fuel-injection system.
No. In the MAN B&W, now Everllence, ME engine each cylinder has its own fuel-oil pressure booster on its Hydraulic Cylinder Unit. The common element is the hydraulic oil supply at about 300 bar from the Hydraulic Power Supply, which is engine-driven as standard, with electric start-up pumps, and is a separate unit from the HCUs. Fuel is supplied to the boosters at low pressure. The Engine Control Unit, using crank position from the duplicated tacho system, sends timing commands to the Cylinder Control Unit, which positions a proportional valve — ELFI for fuel and ELVA for the exhaust valve, or a single FIVA for both. The valve admits hydraulic oil under the booster piston and the plunger injects fuel. Injection pressure is the hydraulic pressure times the booster area ratio, and quantity and rate follow the valve command, because there is no helix or spill port. A true fuel common rail, with fuel stored at injection pressure, is the WinGD RT-flex and X design.
Q2. What happens on an ME engine if one control unit fails?
The Engine Control System is designed so that no single failure related to the system may cause the engine to stop. There are two ECUs in parallel, one in hot stand-by, which takes over without interruption. There is one CCU per cylinder, all identical; if one fails, only that cylinder is cut out. Auxiliary control is divided among three ACUs, which also share control of the HPS pumps, so one failure leaves enough redundancy. The HPS has stand-by pumps, and the high-pressure pipes to the HCUs are double-walled so that emergency running is possible using the outer pipe. Control networks A and B, two MOPs and tacho sets A and B are duplicated. Any controller can be replaced without stopping the engine. In most cases a single failure has no effect on power, or only causes a slow-down.
Q3. Compare the ME-GI and ME-GA dual-fuel engines.
The ME-GI works on the Diesel principle: pilot oil is injected first and ignites, and gas is then injected at high pressure, up to 300 bar at the engine inlet, and burns as it enters. There is no premixed charge, so there is no knock and methane slip is very low, but engine-out NOx is at Tier II in both modes and Tier III needs EGR or SCR. The ME-GA works on the Otto principle in gas mode: gas is admitted at low pressure, which Everllence gives as 5.5 to 12 barg at the engine inlet depending on load and rating, through safe gas admission valves in the cylinder liner during the compression stroke. The lean premixed charge is ignited by about 0.5% pilot oil from a micro booster valve. That gives low NOx, but the engine is susceptible to knock and pre-ignition and has higher methane slip. Its design specifies EGR as standard (Everllence project guide), which ensures Tier III in dual-fuel and fuel-oil-only modes and cuts slip by up to half, but Tier III is not guaranteed in specified dual-fuel mode. The ME-GA's low-pressure gas plant is cheaper; the ME-GI needs a high-pressure compressor or pump.
Q4. State the NOx Tier limits and explain where Tier III applies.
Under MARPOL Annex VI regulation 13, for engines over 130 kW, with n the rated speed: Tier I is 17.0 g/kWh below 130 rpm, 45 times n to the power minus 0.2 from 130 to below 2000 rpm, and 9.8 at 2000 rpm and above, for ships constructed from 1 January 2000 to before 1 January 2011. Tier II is 14.4, 44 times n to the minus 0.23, and 7.7, for ships constructed on or after 1 January 2011. Tier III is 3.4, 9 times n to the minus 0.2, and 2.0 — about 80% below Tier I. Tier III applies only inside a NOx Emission Control Area, and each ECA has its own construction date: 1 January 2016 for the North American and US Caribbean ECAs; 1 January 2021 for the Baltic and North Sea; 1 January 2025 for the Canadian Arctic; and 1 March 2026 for the Norwegian Sea, by building contract, keel or delivery date. Both entered into force on 1 March 2026. The North-East Atlantic ECA was adopted in May 2026 and enters into force on 1 September 2027. The Mediterranean ECA is for sulphur and particulates only. Outside ECAs, a Tier III ship meets Tier II.
Q5. How is NOx formed in a diesel engine, and why is the ME-GI not Tier III without after-treatment?
NOx is mainly thermal NOx: nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine at very high temperature. Its formation depends on peak local temperature — it becomes significant above roughly 1,800 to 2,000 kelvin in the flame zones — on the residence time at that temperature, and on oxygen availability. More fuel raises NOx only indirectly, by raising temperature. In the ME-GI, gas is injected into hot air after the pilot ignites and burns as a diffusion flame. The flame zones are near stoichiometric and very hot, with oxygen around them, just as with fuel oil, so engine-out NOx is at Tier II. To reach Tier III it needs EGR, which lowers the oxygen and raises the heat capacity of the charge, or SCR, which removes NOx from the exhaust. The ME-GA in gas mode burns a lean premixed charge with no hot stoichiometric zones, so its peak temperature, and its NOx, are much lower.
Q6. Distinguish a power turbine generator, a hybrid turbocharger and an electric-assist turbocharger.
A power turbine generator is not a turbocharger. It is a separate exhaust-gas turbine in a bypass of the main-engine exhaust that drives a generator through a gearbox — turbo-compounding. It recovers surplus exhaust energy as electricity when the turbochargers are efficient enough to spare some exhaust flow, and it is shut down at low load. A hybrid turbocharger, such as Mitsubishi's MET-MAG, has a high-speed generator/motor coupled to the turbocharger rotor. It generates electricity from surplus exhaust energy, and in motoring mode it drives the rotor at low load in place of the auxiliary blowers. An electric-assist turbocharger has a motor on the compressor shaft that supplies energy to accelerate the rotor at low load for better scavenging. The first two take energy out of the exhaust; electric assist puts energy into the rotor.

📖 Definition

A two-stroke crosshead engine excites the ship in four ways (MAN B&W): external unbalanced moments from the inertia of the moving masses (first order, vertical and horizontal; second order, vertical only); guide-force moments, the lateral moments from the crosshead guide forces that rock (H-type) or twist (X-type) the engine top; axial vibration of the crankshaft, excited by gas forces deflecting the crank throws and passed to the hull through the thrust bearing; and torsional vibration of the shaft system, excited by the varying torque from gas pressure and the crank mechanism. Each becomes a problem at resonance, when the engine speed multiplied by the order of excitation equals a natural frequency of the engine, shaft line or hull. The countermeasures are an axial vibration damper, torsional design (shaft diameter, tuning wheel, dampers, barred speed range), lateral top bracing and moment compensators.

  • The barred speed range is the Chief Engineer's daily limit. It comes from torsional resonance and class stress limits, and the engine must pass through it quickly — not run in it (Part 24 · Topic 2).
  • Vibration complaints arrive after delivery. Heavy vibration in the accommodation in ballast or at one speed is often a hull resonance with an engine moment; the remedy — compensator, top bracing or a damper — depends on the order and the node position.
  • The hardware needs looking after. Axial and torsional dampers, top-bracing units and compensator drives are machinery items on the planned maintenance system, and a failed damper can turn a harmless critical into a cracked crankshaft.

📐 Vibration Basics — Excitation, Order, Natural Frequency and Nodes

Every elastic system — crankshaft, shaft line, engine frame, hull girder — has natural frequencies, each with its own mode shape. A hull girder mode is named by its nodes, the points that do not move: the source's sketch shows a 2-node and a 3-node vertical mode. An engine produces forces and moments at whole multiples of its rotational speed — the orders. Resonance, and large vibration, occur where engine speed × order = a natural frequency; the deflection then depends on the excitation, the damping, and how close the frequencies are. A compensating force placed at or near a node has no effect, because it produces no moment about that node.

↔️ Axial Vibration and the Axial Vibration Damper

Mechanism. When the gas force loads a crank throw through the connecting rod, the crank arms deflect in the axial direction of the crankshaft. The resulting axial vibration can be passed through the thrust bearing to the hull. Its dominant order equals the number of cylinders on engines with fewer than seven cylinders, and half the number on engines with more than six.

Why it is fitted to every engine. At the introduction of the MC series the damper was standard only on engines with six or more cylinders, where resonance would otherwise over-stress the crank throws. Then a 5L50MC in an LPG tanker showed heavy axial vibration on trial: the crankshaft was not in resonance, but its vibration coupled with the engine frame and double bottom, and the whole engine and the superstructure vibrated longitudinally. A retrofitted axial damper alone cured it. That case and others led MAN to fit axial vibration dampers as standard on all its engines.

Construction. A damping flange (collar) on the crankshaft, at the fore end beside main bearing No. 1, runs inside a cylindrical casing. Both sides of the flange are filled with oil from the main lubricating (system) oil supply, and the two chambers are connected through a throttle — the 'adjusting throttle valve' in the printed Fig. U11. As the crankshaft vibrates axially, oil is forced from one side to the other through the throttle, and this throttling dissipates the vibration energy.

Monitoring. Damper oil pressure and axial vibration are monitored according to the engine's own alarm list, which differs between engine types; the damper works only while its oil supply is maintained.

🔄 Torsional Vibration — Criticals, Barred Speed Range, Viscous Damper, Tuning Wheel

Cause. The gas pressure in each cylinder varies through the cycle and, through the crank and connecting-rod mechanism, produces a varying torque on the crankshaft — the source's 'pressure pulses from the different firing units'. Harmonic analysis splits that torque into orders. Stresses peak at resonance, where speed × order equals the shaft system's torsional natural frequency.

Class limits and the barred speed range. Classification societies require the torsional characteristics to be calculated and set two stress limits: a lower limit that may be exceeded only for a short time — so the speed range where it is exceeded becomes a barred speed range, passed through quickly — and an upper limit that may not be exceeded at all. On the load diagram the barred range is a layout limit (Part 24 · Topic 2).

Two ways to move a critical. A thinner shaft or a heavier tuning wheel lowers the natural frequency (over-critical: the resonance sits below the running range). A much stiffer, larger-diameter intermediate shaft raises it above MCR (under-critical). Under-critical running avoids a barred range but leaves a high varying torque in the shaft, which produces a varying propeller thrust. In one MAN case, 5L80MCE tankers built with a large shaft and no tuning wheel had a torsional-vibration-induced thrust variation of about 30% of the mean thrust, and heavy longitudinal vibration of the engine frame and superstructure on trial — cured only after longitudinal top bracing and strengthening of the deck steelwork.

The viscous damper. Where a damper is needed, the most common type is the viscous damper. A closed casing is bolted to the crankshaft; inside it an inertia ring floats freely in a thin film of high-viscosity silicone fluid. The ring tends to keep turning at the mean speed while the casing twists back and forth with the crankshaft's torsional vibration. The relative circumferential slip shears the silicone film, and the shear turns vibration energy into heat. The ring does not move radially. Spring-type dampers are an alternative.

🏗️ Guide-Force Moments and Top Bracing — Lateral, not 'Longitudinal'

When the piston is not exactly at top or bottom dead centre, the force transmitted through the connecting rod has a component perpendicular to the cylinder axis. It acts at the crosshead guide shoe and is reacted at the main bearing; together these form a guide-force moment. In a multi-cylinder engine the gas and inertia forces combine into moments of all orders, of two kinds:

Top bracing is a detuner. MAN recommends a set of lateral top bracings between the engine's upper gallery and the hull structure (casing side), either mechanical, with a frictional connection, or hydraulically adjustable. The bracings stiffen the double-bottom and main-engine system so that its natural frequency rises above the engine's running range; the guide-force moments then cannot excite resonance and are harmless. Measurements in service show that, with adequate bracing, resonance occurs above the normal running range. The bracing is not there to 'transmit vibration to the hull'.

Longitudinal top bracing is a different device for a different problem — fore-and-aft vibration of engine and superstructure, coupled to axial vibration or thrust variation — as in the 5L50MC and 5L80MCE cases above.

🧭 Recognising and Responding on Board

The following are illustrative shipboard practice, built on the MAN mechanisms above, not regulatory requirements:

  • Pattern first. Note whether the vibration is tied to an engine speed (resonance), to the loading condition (hull natural frequencies change with draught), and to direction — vertical, transverse at the engine top, or fore-and-aft in the superstructure.
  • Match the order. Twice engine speed, vertical, on a 4–6-cylinder engine points to the 2nd-order moment; cylinder-number frequency, transverse at the engine top, points to guide-force moments and the top bracing; fore-and-aft superstructure vibration points to axial vibration or thrust variation.
  • Respect the barred speed range. Pass through it quickly; never run continuously in it. Any change to the engine rating, shafting or propeller is referred to the maker and class, because the torsional calculation behind the barred range depends on them.
  • Keep the hardware working. Axial damper oil supply, viscous damper condition as the damper maker specifies, top-bracing friction or hydraulic setting, and compensator chain drives belong in the PMS.
  • Measure before modifying. Vibration surveys against ISO recommended levels, with the node positions, decide whether a compensator or bracing change will help.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Name the main sources of vibration in a large two-stroke engine and the countermeasure for each.
MAN B&W classifies four excitation sources. First, external unbalanced moments from the inertia of rotating and reciprocating masses: first order, acting vertically and horizontally, and second order, acting vertically only. They are handled by adjustable counterweights and moment compensators. Second, guide-force moments, the lateral moments from the crosshead guide forces, of H-type and X-type. They are handled by lateral top bracing. Third, axial vibration of the crankshaft from the gas force deflecting the crank throws, transmitted through the thrust bearing. It is handled by the axial vibration damper. Fourth, torsional vibration from the varying gas and inertia torque. It is handled by shaft-line design, a tuning wheel or a damper, and where necessary a barred speed range. All of them matter only near resonance, where engine speed times order equals a natural frequency.
Q2. Explain the barred speed range.
Torsional vibration stresses peak at resonance, where the engine speed multiplied by the excitation order equals the torsional natural frequency of the shaft system. Classification societies require the torsional characteristics to be calculated and set two stress limits: a lower limit that may be exceeded only for a short time, and an upper limit that may never be exceeded. The speed range in which the lower limit is exceeded is barred: the engine may pass through it but not run continuously in it,. On five-cylinder engines the main fifth-order critical lies close below normal speed, so MAN fits a tuning wheel and uses a thinner, higher-strength intermediate shaft to put the critical well below the running range — over-critical running. Six-cylinder engines with class-diameter shafting normally have a barred range; engines with seven or more cylinders normally do not.
Q3. Describe the construction and working of a viscous torsional vibration damper.
It is a closed casing bolted to the crankshaft, containing a heavy inertia ring that floats freely in a thin film of high-viscosity silicone fluid. When the crankshaft vibrates torsionally the casing twists back and forth with it, but the inertia ring, being free, tends to keep rotating steadily at the mean speed. The relative circumferential slip between ring and casing shears the silicone film, and the viscous shear converts vibration energy into heat, damping the vibration. The motion is rotational, not radial.
Q4. What are guide-force moments and what is the purpose of top bracing?
When the piston is away from the dead centres, the connecting-rod force has a component perpendicular to the cylinder, which acts at the crosshead guide shoe and is reacted at the main bearing, forming a guide-force moment. There are two kinds. The H-moment rocks the engine top transversely, with a main order equal to the number of cylinders, and dominates on engines with fewer than seven cylinders. The X-moment twists the engine in an X shape, with a main order of half the number of cylinders, and dominates above six cylinders. The resulting vibration is lateral, not longitudinal. Lateral top bracing between the upper gallery and the hull structure, mechanical with a friction connection or hydraulically adjustable, stiffens the engine and double-bottom system so that its natural frequency rises above the running speed range. It acts as a detuner, so the moments cannot cause resonance; its purpose is not to pass vibration into the hull.
Q5. Explain first- and second-order external moments and how they are compensated.
First-order moments come from the rotating and reciprocating masses, act at engine speed and act both vertically and horizontally. They rarely cause trouble, except possibly on four-cylinder engines with 2- or 3-node hull modes. Adjustable counterweights can make the vertical moment insignificant at the cost of a larger horizontal one, and a compensator with two counter-rotating masses at crankshaft speed in the chain-tightener wheel can neutralise the horizontal moment. Second-order moments come only from the reciprocating masses, act vertically at twice engine speed and matter on four-, five- and six-cylinder engines, where they can excite 4- or 5-node vertical hull modes. They are compensated by counterweights rotating at twice engine speed, chain-driven at the aft or fore end, at both ends, or by an electrically driven compensator in the steering-gear room. Because the compensating moment is force times distance to the node, a single compensator near a node is ineffective.

Situation (based on an example in the MAN B&W vibration paper)

A 40,000 dwt general cargo ship was to have a 6-cylinder L67GFCA engine. A rough calculation showed only a small risk of excitation from the 2nd-order external moment of 760 kNm, but with some uncertainty, so owner, yard and engine builder agreed to deliver the engine prepared for later 2nd-order compensators. On trials the vibration was satisfactory loaded, but in the specified ballast condition it measured 11 mm/s against the 9 mm/s the ISO recommendation gave as acceptable.

Diagnosis

Which excitation. Vertical vibration at twice engine speed on a 6-cylinder engine — the 2nd-order moment, which originates only in the reciprocating masses. Why only in ballast. Hull natural frequencies change with loading, so resonance with a vertical hull mode can appear in one condition only. Where to compensate. Measurements showed the node of the 4-node hull girder vibration lay very close to the aft end of the engine, nearly independent of loading. A compensator at the aft end would act almost at the node and produce almost no compensating moment.

Remedy and lesson

Only the forward compensator was fitted. The lesson for the oral: identify the order and direction first, then find the node — the moment from a compensator is its force times its distance from the node. Decide at the project stage whether the engine is prepared for compensators, because late remedies are costly.
