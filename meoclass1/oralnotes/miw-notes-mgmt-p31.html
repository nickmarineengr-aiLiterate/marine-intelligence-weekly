📖 Definition

Artificial intelligence (AI): a machine-based system that, for given objectives, infers from its input how to generate outputs — predictions, recommendations or decisions — that influence a physical or virtual environment (OECD, 2023). Machine learning is the main AI technique today: models learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules. The Internet of Things (IoT) connects sensors and devices so that data flows automatically to processing and users (ITU-T Y.2060). Big Data is data whose volume, velocity and variety need scalable methods to store and analyse (NIST SP 1500-1). Augmented reality (AR) overlays computer-generated information on the user's view of the real world in real time; virtual reality replaces that view entirely. Together they make the data-driven ship: IoT collects the data, Big Data stores it, AI finds patterns in it, and AR puts the result in front of the engineer.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Simon asks for definitions that hold up. 'What is AI?' answered with 'computers thinking like humans' invites the follow-up 'So is your governor AI?' Know that AI ≠ automation ≠ machine learning ≠ Big Data.
  • The engine room is where most of this lands. Condition monitoring, maker anomaly-detection services, reefer monitoring and AR remote assistance are already in service — and every one of them is a connection into OT (Part 30).
  • Deployed or demonstrated? An examiner who reads trade press will know that a famous autonomous voyage was a demonstration and that terminal automation is not AI. Present examples honestly and dated.
  • The CE stays accountable. AI flags and AR guidance assist; they never remove a statutory survey, override watchkeeping judgement or replace the SMS.

🧠 AI — Definition, and What It Is Not

Uday's opening line is popular-press wording. The definition to give is the OECD's (2023), mirrored in ISO/IEC 22989: an AI system is a machine-based system that, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs — predictions, content, recommendations or decisions — that can influence physical or virtual environments.

TermWhat it isEngine-room example
AutomationFixed logic written by an engineer: same input, same outputPID governor, auxiliary-engine auto-start, alarm and safety shutdowns
Machine learning (ML)A subset of AI: a model learns patterns from data; Uday's 'labelled training data' describes supervised MLAnomaly detection trained on months of normal engine data
AIThe wider field — ML plus rule-based reasoning, optimisation and language modelsA decision-support tool that ranks probable causes of a high exhaust temperature

Learning, reasoning, self-correction. Uday's 'three cognitive skills' is a teaching description, not a standard. In practice, 'self-correction' means a model is retrained or updated on new data — on board that is a managed software change, not autonomous self-improvement. And in ML the algorithm that trains the model is written by people, but the model's rules are learned.

'Three types of AI'. Every AI system in use today, maritime or otherwise, is narrow AI, built for one task. General AI (human-level across all tasks) and super-intelligence are hypothetical, and super-intelligence is not 'machine consciousness'. Uday's Cortana example is dated: Microsoft retired the Windows app in 2023.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define artificial intelligence. How does it differ from automation and from machine learning?
An AI system is a machine-based system that, for given objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations or decisions that influence a physical or virtual environment — that is the OECD definition of 2023, close to ISO/IEC 22989. Automation is fixed logic designed by an engineer: a governor, an auto-start sequence or a safety shutdown gives the same output for the same input and learns nothing. Machine learning is the main branch of AI in use today: instead of hand-written rules, a model learns patterns from data — supervised learning from labelled examples, unsupervised learning from unlabelled data. Big data is different again: it is the raw material that machine learning works on. All AI in use today is narrow AI, built for a specific task; general AI and super-intelligence are hypothetical.
Q2. Give examples of how AI is being used in shipping, distinguishing deployed systems from demonstrations.
Deployed: port-call optimisation that predicts arrival and berth times so that ships can arrive just in time; weather routing and performance models refined by machine learning for fuel optimisation; maker anomaly-detection services that learn an engine's normal behaviour and flag deviations, such as Wärtsilä's Expert Insight; and computer-vision collision-avoidance aids used as decision support on the bridge. Demonstrated: autonomous ocean voyages such as HD Hyundai's trans-Pacific HiNAS voyage of the Prism Courage in 2022 and Japan's MEGURI 2040 trials. Automated container terminals, such as those in Rotterdam since 1993, are mainly fixed-logic automation, not AI. Autonomous ships are regulated only by IMO's non-mandatory MASS Code, effective 1 July 2026.
Q3. What is the Internet of Things? Give its main parts and its advantages and disadvantages on board.
The Internet of Things is a network of physical devices — sensors and actuators with embedded electronics — connected so that their data flows automatically, without human data entry, to processing and to users. Its main parts are the sensors or devices, the connectivity (ship network and satellite link), the data processing, on board or in the cloud, and the user interface such as a dashboard or alarm. Advantages: efficiency and cost reduction, real-time information, better decisions from analytics, predictive maintenance and fuel saving; examples include hull and machinery condition monitoring and reefer monitoring. Disadvantages: cost, technical complexity, dependence on technology, connectivity and power, integration with existing systems — and above all security risk, because every IoT link is a path into the ship's operational technology, so it must be segmented and controlled.
Q4. What is Big Data? Explain its characteristics and its uses in ship management.
Big data is data whose volume, velocity and variety are too great for conventional tools, so that scalable methods are needed to store and analyse it. The five Vs used in teaching are volume, the quantity of data; velocity, the speed at which it is generated and must be processed; variety, the different types such as sensor values, reports and images; veracity, its trustworthiness; and value, the usefulness of the insight relative to the cost. The original three Vs are Doug Laney's from 2001. Uses include chartering — matching the right ship to the cargo at the right price; operations — finding the optimum economic speed; vetting — charterers selecting ships on inspection and performance data; and ship design, using operating data from existing ships. Regulation is a driver: shipping produced about 2.9 percent of global greenhouse-gas emissions in 2018, and IMO's 2023 Strategy targets net-zero GHG by or around 2050, which needs verified fuel and performance data.
Q5. Differentiate augmented, virtual and mixed reality, and explain how AR helps the engine room.
Augmented reality overlays computer-generated information on the user's view of the real world in real time; Azuma's three characteristics are that it combines real and virtual, is interactive in real time and is registered in three dimensions. Virtual reality replaces the view of the real world with a fully synthetic environment. Mixed reality blends real and virtual objects so that they interact. In the engine room, AR lets a shore technician see what the engineer sees through smart glasses and guide him step by step — Wärtsilä introduced this for stern-tube seal work in 2018 — which reduces downtime and service visits; it also supports remote class surveys and maintenance instructions overlaid on the machine. VR is mainly used for training and drills ashore. The challenges are cost, latency, information overload, the need for intrinsically safe devices in hazardous areas, and cyber security, because the remote-assistance link is a form of remote vendor access.

Situation (illustrative)

The maker's remote-monitoring service e-mails an alert: 'Unit 4 — abnormal exhaust temperature trend; recommend inspection within 48 hours.' All readings on the ship's alarm system are inside limits.

How the Chief Engineer handles it

Verify, don't obey. The alert is decision support from a model that learned the engine's normal pattern; cross-check it against your own instruments, the indicator diagrams or engine-diagnostic data, recent fuel analysis and the scavenge-port inspection. Check the data first. A drifting thermocouple is the commonest cause of a 'trend' — veracity before intelligence. Decide and record. If the evidence supports it, plan the inspection under the PMS; if not, record why the alert was not acted on. Feedback. Tell the maker the outcome, so that the model improves. Boundaries. The alert cannot defer a class survey or replace the planned maintenance a condition-monitoring notation still requires; the remote link to the maker must sit inside the ship's cyber procedures (Part 30).

Lesson

AI moves the engineer from collecting data to judging it. The value lies in earlier warning; the risk lies in trusting it blindly — or in ignoring it because nobody understood why it flagged.

📖 Definition

A blockchain is a distributed digital ledger in which transactions are grouped into blocks, each carrying a cryptographic hash of the previous block and signed by its originator, and copies of the ledger held by many nodes are kept in step by a consensus mechanism, usually without a central authority. The result is tamper-evident: changing a past record breaks the chain of hashes and is visible to the other nodes. Distributed ledger technology (DLT) is the wider family — every blockchain is a DLT, but not every DLT uses chained blocks. In shipping, DLT is used mainly on permissioned platforms for electronic trade documents, above all the electronic bill of lading (eBL).

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Nair asks about the law, not the code. 'Is an electronic B/L valid?' is answered with MLETR, national statutes and the P&I-approved platform rulebook — not with 'it's on blockchain'.
  • Hype is the trap. 'Immutable', 'hack-proof' and 'private' are the three words examiners use to catch candidates who learnt blockchain from marketing.
  • Real projects have failed. TradeLens, the flagship shipping blockchain, closed in 2023. Knowing what is current and what is historical shows judgement.
  • The CE meets it at the bunker manifold. Digital bunker documentation (e-BDN) is now mandatory in Singapore — and it is not blockchain; the MARPOL sample still proves the fuel.

⛓️ What a Blockchain Actually Is
[Block n-1] [Block n] [Block n+1] data + signatures ┌─> hash(n-1) + data ┌─> hash(n) + data hash(n-2) ─────────┘ + signatures ──────┘ + signatures Alter block n-1 → its hash changes → block n no longer matches → TAMPER EVIDENT Copies on many nodes, kept in step by CONSENSUS (PoW / PoS / permissioned voting)

Uday's definition — a digital ledger distributed among the nodes of a peer-to-peer network rather than held in one central database — is the right starting point; his sketch of chained boxes shows the idea. What makes it a blockchain is the rest: transactions grouped into blocks, each block carrying the hash of the one before, digital signatures proving who submitted each record, and a consensus rule deciding which new block all nodes accept. NIST calls blockchains 'tamper evident and tamper resistant digital ledgers implemented in a distributed fashion'.

DLT and blockchain. Uday's 'tree and branch' is correct: every blockchain is a DLT, but some DLTs do not chain blocks. The pasted CFTE diagram on PDF 748 contrasts a centralised ledger, with one bank holding the record, against a decentralised ledger, where every party holds a synchronised copy.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is blockchain? How does it differ from distributed ledger technology in general?
A blockchain is a distributed digital ledger in which transactions are grouped into blocks; each block contains a cryptographic hash of the previous block and digitally signed records, and many nodes hold copies that are kept identical by a consensus mechanism, usually without a central authority. Because each block depends on the hash of the one before, altering an old record breaks the chain and is evident to the other nodes, so the ledger is tamper-evident and very hard to alter undetected rather than absolutely immutable. Distributed ledger technology is the wider family of synchronised, consensus-based ledgers held across several sites; every blockchain is a DLT, but not every DLT chains its records in blocks. Ledgers are either permissionless and open to anyone, like Bitcoin, or permissioned, where only vetted members participate — the usual model in shipping.
Q2. Is blockchain secure and private? Comment on the common claims made for it.
It is secure against one specific threat — undetected alteration of the shared record — because of hash-linking, signatures and consensus, and it removes a single central database as a point of failure. It is not hack-proof: attacks on the consensus, faulty smart-contract code, stolen private keys and compromised user devices all occur, and if false data is entered at the start, the ledger faithfully preserves it. It is not inherently private: hashing is not encryption, and a public blockchain is pseudonymous, with every transaction visible to everyone, which is why commercial data is usually kept off-chain or on permissioned ledgers with only hashes recorded. It is not 'free of fraud' or impossible to close down: the flagship shipping platform TradeLens was shut down by its two owners in 2023.
Q3. What is an electronic bill of lading, and what gives it legal validity?
An electronic bill of lading is a digital record that performs the functions of a paper bill — receipt for the goods, evidence of the contract of carriage and document of title — with 'possession' transferred by passing exclusive control of the record on a platform. Its legal validity does not come from the technology but from the law or from contract. Where a country has adopted the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Transferable Records, as the UK did in its Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 and Singapore in 2021, an eBL can have the same effect as paper. Elsewhere it depends on a contractual rulebook binding all users of the platform. The International Group of P&I Clubs covers liabilities arising from eBLs only on systems it has approved. India has not enacted the Model Law, and the Bills of Lading Act 2025 does not mention electronic bills, so an Indian eBL rests on contract. Some approved platforms use blockchain and others do not.
Q4. What is a smart contract? Give a shipping example and its limitations.
A smart contract is code recorded on a blockchain that executes automatically when defined conditions are met. A shipping example is releasing a freight payment, or starting demurrage, when a verified arrival event is recorded. Its limitations are that it can only act on data it is given, so it needs a trusted source of real-world events — the oracle problem: if the arrival time fed in is wrong, the contract executes wrongly; the code can contain errors that are hard to correct once deployed; and its legal enforceability depends on an underlying legal contract, not on the code itself. In shipping, smart contracts remain at pilot stage.
Q5. What are the challenges in implementing blockchain in shipping?
Cost and initial investment; the need for every trading partner to join, since a ledger with one participant is worthless — the network effect that led to TradeLens closing in 2023; immaturity, scalability and lack of interoperability between platforms; governance — who runs the network and decides the rules; the oracle problem of feeding trustworthy data; legal recognition of electronic records, which varies by country; private-key management; data privacy, because public ledgers are transparent; and training. Energy consumption is a problem mainly for proof-of-work chains; proof-of-stake and permissioned ledgers use little energy.

Situation (illustrative)

A container ship arrives at an Asian port. The receiver presents no paper bill but asks the agent to release the cargo against an 'electronic B/L on the blockchain platform'. The master is unsure and asks the Chief Engineer, the senior officer on board familiar with the company's digital systems, to help check.

What has to be established

Was the B/L issued electronically by the carrier? The eBL must be on the platform named in the bill and the charter party. Is the platform approved? 📖 Definition

Additive manufacturing (AM) — commonly called 3D printing — is 'the process of joining materials to make parts from 3D model data, usually layer upon layer, as opposed to subtractive and formative manufacturing methodologies' (ISO/ASTM 52900:2021). A printed part is only as good as its qualified process: the feedstock, the machine and its parameters, the post-processing and the inspection. A printed part used where a classed or safety-critical component is required must therefore come from a class-qualified manufacturer with a part certificate, exactly as a cast or forged part would — a digital file alone is not a certified spare.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Spares at sea are the CE's problem. AM promises faster spares for obsolete or remote-port items. The CE must know which parts that promise applies to.
  • Paul asks about certification. 'Can you print a spare on board?' tests whether the candidate knows class approval, material certificates and the critical/non-critical distinction.
  • The risk is invisible. A printed part can look perfect and hide porosity, lack of fusion or residual stress. Only a qualified process and inspection prove it.
  • OEM rights are real. Printing a maker's part without a licence raises intellectual-property, warranty and liability questions.

🖨️ How Additive Manufacturing Works — the Full Workflow

Uday's four steps — create a 3D model, slice it into thin layers, send the file to the printer, print layer by layer — are correct for a hobby polymer part. For an engineering part, especially metal, the steps that decide whether the part is safe come after the printing:

CAD MODEL → BUILD PREP → PRINT → POST-PROCESS → INSPECT & TEST → CERTIFY (licensed orientation, qualified supports off, dimensions, NDT class part design) supports, machine + heat treat / (CT scan), test certificate slicing controlled HIP, machining coupons if required feedstock

Materials. 'Plastic, metal or any material' is overstated. Each process accepts particular feedstocks — polymers, metals, ceramics or composites — and metal powders and wires must themselves be controlled and certified.

The seven ISO/ASTM 52900 process categories: binder jetting, directed energy deposition (DED, including wire-arc additive manufacturing, WAAM), material extrusion, material jetting, powder bed fusion (PBF), sheet lamination and vat photopolymerisation. For marine metal parts the relevant routes are powder bed fusion for small, complex parts and DED/WAAM for large parts such as propellers; IACS Rec. 186 also covers binder jetting.

🏛️ Class Qualification — Why a File Is Not a Spare

There is no IACS Unified Requirement on AM yet. Class societies apply their own rules, harmonised by IACS Recommendation 186 (March 2025, non-mandatory), which sets three AM levels of testing according to the criticality of the part. DNV, for example, grades AM parts AMC 1 to AMC 3 by consequence of failure; its certificates state that categories 2 and 3 need part qualification on top of build-process qualification. 🚢 Onboard Printing vs Shore Hubs — Practice in 2026

Merchant practice is shore-based. Verified commercial examples are parts printed at qualified facilities ashore and delivered to the ship — e.g. Wilhelmsen's printed scupper plugs for Berge Bulk (February 2020), later through a joint venture with thyssenkrupp — the 'digital warehouse' model, in which OEM-licensed build files are held digitally and printed at a qualified hub near the port.

Onboard metal printing is not merchant practice. It needs controlled powder handling, inert gas, heat treatment, inspection and trained operators, and ship motion and vibration affect the build — the US Navy itself cites motion and vibration limits for the metal printer it installed on USS Bataan in November 2022. A small polymer printer on board can make jigs, gauges and non-critical items, under the SMS. A 2017 industry proposal for onboard polymer printers on merchant ships is not known to have been taken further.

Intellectual property. Most machinery parts are the maker's design. Printing one without a licence can infringe design rights, void the warranty and leave the owner carrying the liability if it fails. OEM-licensed files through an approved hub avoid this.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is additive manufacturing, and how does it differ from conventional manufacturing?
Additive manufacturing, commonly called 3D printing, is defined in ISO/ASTM 52900 as the process of joining materials to make parts from 3D model data, usually layer upon layer. Conventional manufacturing is either subtractive — removing material from a solid, as when a bore is machined out of a billet — or formative — shaping material by casting or forging. Additive manufacturing uses little more material than the part itself and can make complex internal shapes that cannot be machined, but the properties of the part depend heavily on the process: a printed metal part can be anisotropic, porous or carry residual stress unless the process is qualified and the part is heat-treated and inspected, so it is not automatically cheaper or stronger.
Q2. Describe the steps in producing an engineering part by additive manufacturing.
First, a 3D CAD model of the part is obtained, under licence if the design belongs to the maker. Second, the build is prepared: the part is oriented, support structures added and the model sliced into thin layers by software. Third, it is printed layer by layer on a qualified machine with controlled feedstock — polymer filament, metal powder or wire, depending on the process. Fourth, it is post-processed: supports removed, heat-treated or hot isostatically pressed to relieve stress and close porosity, and machined to final dimensions. Fifth, it is inspected and tested — dimensional checks, non-destructive testing such as CT scanning, and mechanical tests on coupons. Finally, where the part is classed or critical, it is certified by the classification society.
Q3. Can a Chief Engineer print a spare part on board? Discuss.
For a classed or safety-critical component, no. Such a part must be made by a manufacturer approved by the classification society, with qualified feedstock and a qualified build process, post-processing, non-destructive testing and a part certificate, to the same standard as a cast or forged replacement. IACS Recommendation 186 of March 2025 sets the level of testing by the part's criticality, and DNV, ABS, Lloyd's Register, Bureau Veritas and ClassNK have their own rules. The design usually belongs to the maker, so a licence is needed, and printing it without one raises intellectual-property, warranty and liability issues. Metal printing on board is impractical: powder handling, inert gas, heat treatment, inspection and ship motion all work against it. A small polymer printer on board can produce non-critical items such as covers, knobs or tool holders under the SMS. The practical route for critical spares is a shore hub printing an OEM-licensed file under class approval.
Q4. What do classification societies qualify before accepting an additively manufactured part?
They qualify the manufacturer or AM facility, through an approval of manufacturer covering its quality system, machines and operators; the feedstock, its chemistry, particle size, reuse and traceability; the build process, meaning the machine, parameters, orientation and post-processing, because the same file on different settings gives a different part; and the part itself, through dimensional checks, non-destructive testing and mechanical testing of coupons. They also expect control of the digital file. The depth of qualification depends on criticality: IACS Recommendation 186 uses three levels, and DNV grades parts AMC 1 to AMC 3 by the consequence of failure.
Q5. Give examples of additive manufacturing in the marine industry.
Demonstrations and first approvals include the RAMLAB and Damen 'WAAMpeller', a wire-arc printed ship propeller tested with Bureau Veritas witnessing in November 2017, and DNV's first approval of an additive-manufacturing manufacturer, thyssenkrupp, in August 2019. In commercial use, Wilhelmsen delivered printed scupper plugs — non-critical parts — to a Berge Bulk carrier in February 2020, and ABS approved printed parts after a six-month in-service trial on the tanker Polar Endeavour in November 2021. In naval use, the US Navy installed a metal printer on USS Bataan in 2022. IACS published Recommendation 186 on additively manufactured metallic parts in March 2025. These are dated examples; the principle to remember is qualification and certification by criticality.

Situation (illustrative)

A small cast-steel part inside a main-engine fuel pump has cracked. The maker has discontinued it, and the lead time for a cast replacement is ten weeks. A supplier offers to scan the broken part and 3D-print a metal replacement within two days.

What the Chief Engineer does

Classify the part. It is in a fuel-injection pump, so it is safety-critical and within class scope. Design right. Ask the maker for a licensed build file, or for its own AM or alternative supply route; a scan of a worn, cracked part is not a valid design. Class route. Any printed replacement must come from a class-approved AM facility, with qualified material and process, heat treatment, NDT and a part certificate accepted by the ship's class society. Interim. Meanwhile, run on the remaining pumps if the engine design and class allow, inform the superintendent and class, and record the deviation under the SMS. Reject the two-day offer if it cannot show facility approval and part certification.

Lesson

Additive manufacturing can shorten the lead time for a critical spare — but only through the same qualification and certification chain as any other manufacturing route. Speed never replaces the certificate.
