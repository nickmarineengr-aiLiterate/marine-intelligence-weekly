📖 Definition

Alternative marine fuels are fuels other than conventional fuel oil that a ship can use to reduce its greenhouse-gas intensity: in this Part LNG, methanol, ammonia and hydrogen. Two frameworks govern how they are judged and approved. The lifecycle framework of the 2024 IMO LCA Guidelines (MEPC.391(81)) measures a fuel's GHG intensity in grams of CO2-equivalent per megajoule on a well-to-wake basis, the sum of its well-to-tank (upstream) and tank-to-wake (shipboard) emissions of CO2, CH4 and N2O. The safety framework is the IGF Code, mandatory under SOLAS chapter II-1 Part G since 1 January 2017, which is prescriptive for natural gas and admits any other low-flashpoint fuel through its alternative-design route (section 2.3, equivalence under SOLAS II-1/55), guided by a family of IMO interim guidelines. Each fuel then presents its own engineering problem: LNG is cryogenic with methane slip; methanol is a low-flashpoint toxic liquid; ammonia is toxic and corrosive but easily liquefied; hydrogen is non-toxic but extremely flammable and hard to store.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Simon's decarbonisation questions have moved from 'what is CII' to 'which fuel, and why'. The examiner wants the lifecycle logic, the regulatory route and a fuel-by-fuel comparison stated in one connected answer.
  • A Chief Engineer on a dual-fuel ship owns the fuel-gas supply system, the gas detection, the ventilation and the bunkering: every one of them is an IGF Code or interim-guideline requirement.
  • The safety hazards differ in kind, not degree: toxicity for ammonia and methanol, invisible flame and wide flammability for hydrogen, cryogenic temperature and methane slip for LNG. Confusing them in an oral is dangerous as well as wrong.
  • Well-to-wake accounting decides which fuels will actually count under FuelEU Maritime and the draft IMO fuel standard. A fuel that is 'clean at the funnel' can be worse than fuel oil overall.

  • Lean-burn Otto engines achieve the NOx reduction of up to about 80% that the source quotes; Diesel-cycle high-pressure engines keep the thermal efficiency and almost eliminate slip but need after-treatment for Tier III.
  • 'Gas ready' (DNV GAS READY, ABS LNG Fuel Ready) is a design-compliance notation: the design and a convertible main engine are approved in advance. The conversion itself — tanks, fuel-gas supply system, double-walled piping, ventilation, detection, class approval — is a major project, not an 'easy retrofit'.
  • Redundancy. IGF Code 9.3 requires redundancy within the gas fuel system (at least two tanks and segregated supply) for single-fuel ships; it does not require a diesel fallback. Most LNG-fuelled ships are dual-fuel by commercial choice.
  • Bunkering is a mature, standardised operation (IGF chapter 8: bunkering station on open deck; ISO 20519; EMSA guidance). The source's 'new technology' and 'bunker station close to the tank to save insulation' statements are out of date and unfounded respectively.
  • Price. LNG is not structurally cheaper than fuel oil; in 2022 LNG bunkers were far dearer than VLSFO. Do not teach a price ranking as fact.

Operating example — Stena Germanica. The Stena Line ro-pax was converted at Remontowa, Gdansk, in January–March 2015: four Wärtsilä-Sulzer 8ZA40S engines were converted to dual-fuel methanol operation with diesel pilot injection — the first methanol-fuelled ro-pax conversion. Zero tank-to-wake CO2 is not zero well-to-wake GHG. Hydrogen from unabated natural-gas reforming carries the reforming CO2 upstream; only renewable-electrolytic ('green') or effectively abated ('blue') hydrogen gives a low well-to-wake figure. The production chain for grey hydrogen is desulfurisation → steam reforming → shift conversion (CO + H2O → CO2 + H2) → CO2 removal → pressure-swing purification.

Context box — production shares (volatile; IEA Global Hydrogen Review 2024, 2023 data). World production about 97 Mt a year: roughly two-thirds from unabated natural gas, about one-fifth from coal, most of the rest by-product hydrogen; all low-emissions hydrogen under 1% and electrolytic about 0.1%. IEA avoids colour labels and speaks of 'low-emissions hydrogen'. These shares move every year; they are context, not memorisation content, and older percentage splits found in study notes should not be quoted. IEA 2024 cost ranges: unabated natural-gas hydrogen about USD 0.8–5.7/kg; renewable-electrolytic hydrogen carries a premium of roughly USD 1.5–8/kg today.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Explain well-to-tank, tank-to-wake and well-to-wake, and state the IMO basis.
Under the 2024 IMO LCA Guidelines, resolution MEPC.391(81), well-to-tank covers the upstream emissions of producing the fuel — feedstock extraction or cultivation, conversion, transport and bunkering — per unit energy delivered to the ship. Tank-to-wake covers everything from the bunker manifold to the energy converter: combustion CO2 plus methane or ammonia slip, nitrous oxide and fugitive losses. Well-to-wake is their sum, expressed as a GHG intensity in grams of CO2-equivalent per megajoule, counting CO2, methane and nitrous oxide with 100-year global warming potentials from IPCC AR5 of 1, 28 and 265. The CII is tank-to-wake with fixed carbon factors; the draft IMO GHG fuel intensity and FuelEU Maritime are well-to-wake.
Q2. What is the regulatory status of ammonia, hydrogen and methanol as ship fuels today?
The IGF Code, resolution MSC.391(95), is mandatory under SOLAS chapter II-1 Part G and has been in force since 1 January 2017, but its prescriptive Part A-1 covers natural gas only. Other low-flashpoint fuels are approved under section 2.3 alternative design, demonstrating an equivalent level of safety under SOLAS regulation II-1/55, supported by IMO interim guidelines: MSC.1/Circ.1621 for methyl and ethyl alcohol, MSC.1/Circ.1647 for fuel cells, MSC.1/Circ.1666 and 1679 for LPG, MSC.1/Circ.1687 for ammonia, and MSC.1/Circ.1701 for hydrogen and 1702 for ammonia cargo as fuel, both approved at MSC 111 in May 2026. All are interim and recommendatory; no ammonia, hydrogen or methanol chapter has yet been adopted into the IGF Code, although a revised methanol guideline goes to MSC 113 in 2027 with a mandatory chapter as the stated aim.
Q3. Describe the hazards of ammonia as a fuel and how the ship is protected.
Toxicity is the dominant hazard: the NIOSH exposure limits are 25 ppm as an eight-hour average and 35 ppm short-term, with 300 ppm immediately dangerous to life, and ammonia is corrosive to skin and eyes. Odour is not a reliable warning because people detect it at roughly 5 ppm but the reported range runs from about 0.04 to 53 ppm, overlapping the limits, so MSC.1/Circ.1687 requires fixed detection with indication at 25 ppm, alarm at 110 ppm and safety actions at 220 ppm, plus toxic-area control, double-walled piping, ventilation, release mitigation and personal protection. The flammable range is about 15 to 28 per cent in air with an auto-ignition temperature around 630 to 651 degrees, so fire risk is lower than for other fuels. Materials exclude copper, zinc, aluminium and their alloys; steels follow IGC Code chapter 17.12 with dissolved oxygen limited below 2.5 ppm and a small water content to prevent stress corrosion cracking.
Q4. How is ammonia stored on board and how does it compare with hydrogen and LNG?
Ammonia liquefies at about minus 33 degrees at atmospheric pressure, or at ambient temperature under its own vapour pressure of about 8.6 bar absolute at 20 degrees; MSC.1/Circ.1687 sets a piping design pressure of 18 bar, the vapour pressure at 45 degrees. All IGF tank types are admissible — independent Types A, B and C and membrane — and Type C pressurised or semi-refrigerated tanks are the usual fuel-tank choice, with fully refrigerated Type A or B for large volumes. Its volumetric energy density of about 11 to 13 megajoules per litre means roughly 2.8 to 3.1 times the fuel volume of marine gas oil, compared with about 1.8 times for LNG at minus 162 degrees and about 4.2 times for liquid hydrogen at minus 253 degrees before insulation. Boil-off handling is much less demanding than for LNG or hydrogen.
Q5. What are the emissions concerns with ammonia and LNG engines?
For LNG the concern is methane slip, unburnt methane escaping mainly from Otto-cycle engines; the 2024 LCA Guidelines assign default slip of 3.5 per cent for medium-speed and 1.7 per cent for slow-speed low-pressure dual-fuel engines, 2.6 per cent for lean-burn spark-ignited engines and 0.15 per cent for high-pressure Diesel-cycle engines, with methane weighted at a GWP100 of 28. For ammonia the concerns are nitrous oxide, with a GWP100 of 265 in the Guidelines and 273 in AR6, and ammonia slip, unburnt ammonia in the exhaust; current two-stroke designs report N2O below a few ppm with tuning and slip below 10 ppm after SCR, with NOx lower than on fuel oil. Both engines burn about 5 per cent pilot fuel at full load, more at part load.
Q6. Compare methanol and hydrogen as marine fuels.
Methanol is an ambient liquid with a flash point of about 11 to 12 degrees, so it is a low-flashpoint fuel under the IGF alternative-design route and MSC.1/Circ.1621; it is toxic, burns with a near-invisible flame, needs about 2.3 times the volume of gas oil, and its tank-to-wake CO2 is the same whatever its origin, so its climate value depends entirely on whether it is fossil, bio- or e-methanol on a well-to-wake basis. Hydrogen is a non-toxic asphyxiant but extremely flammable across 4 to 75 per cent in air with a very low ignition energy; it is stored at 350 to 700 bar or as a liquid at minus 253 degrees, needing four to thirteen times the volume of gas oil; it gives zero tank-to-wake CO2 in fuel cells under MSC.1/Circ.1647 or in engines under MSC.1/Circ.1701, but its well-to-wake footprint depends on the production pathway, and most hydrogen today is still made from unabated natural gas.

📖 Definition

Design for energy efficiency is the choice of a ship's size and capacity, its service speed, its principal dimensions (length, beam and draught) and its hull form so that the transport work it performs costs the least fuel. The physics is simple to state: resistance is mostly friction on the wetted surface plus wave-making, propulsion power rises roughly with the cube of speed, and a larger ship carries more cargo per unit of resistance. The design problem is the trade-off: a longer, finer hull resists less but carries less and may be less stable; a fuller, wider hull carries more but makes more waves. Hull-form optimisation — the fore-body (bulbous bow, forward shoulder, waterline entrance), the aft-body (stern waves, flow separation, wake to the propeller, stern flaps and stern form) and, on full ships, a twin-skeg arrangement — is the naval architect's lever once the main dimensions are fixed, and is now worked with potential-flow and RANS computational fluid dynamics confirmed by model tests.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The EEDI (Part 23 Topic 1) is a design index: reference lines fall with deadweight and the attained value is driven by installed power at the reference speed. Size, speed and hull form are the three levers that set it.
  • A Chief Engineer who understands why the ship was shaped as it was can explain in-service behaviour — why she is sensitive to trim, why slow steaming helps less than the cube law suggests at low speed, why the bulb hurts in ballast.
  • Retrofit decisions — re-bulbing for a new service speed, fitting a stern flap, choosing a slow-steaming profile — are made with the same physics and the same CFD tools, and the engine department supplies the performance data.
  • Simon uses the topic to test whether a candidate can connect regulation (EEDI, SEEMP, CII) to naval-architecture cause and effect rather than reciting measures.

📦 Ship Size and Capacity — The Scale Effect
  • A larger ship has more wetted surface and more total resistance, so it burns more fuel in absolute terms, but it carries proportionally more cargo, so fuel per tonne-mile falls. Volume grows with the cube of dimensions while wetted surface grows with the square.
  • The IMO codified this in the EEDI: the reference line is a × DWT−c, so the permitted gCO2/(t·nm) falls as deadweight rises (tankers c ≈ 0.49, bulk carriers ≈ 0.48, container ships ≈ 0.20). A small ship is allowed to be less efficient because physics makes it so.
  • The trade-off is commercial as much as hydrodynamic: a larger ship needs the cargo to fill it, port depth and crane reach to serve it, and an owner who can accept fewer, bigger parcels. Container-ship upsizing and the tanker fleet's reliance on speed rather than size are the two familiar illustrations (qualitative; no statistics quoted).
Resistance components (full-form ships, MAN): friction ~70-90 % wave-making + residual ~10-30 % Wetted surface ∝ L^2 Displacement / cargo ∝ L^3 ⇒ resistance per tonne of cargo falls as the ship grows (the 'scale effect') EEDI reference line: Required EEDI = (1 - X/100) · a · DWT^(-c) — lower allowance for bigger ships
⏱️ Service Speed — The Cube Law and Speed Optimisation
Propulsion power P ∝ V^3 (propeller law; valid at low Froude number — exponent 4 or more for fast ships) Fuel per voyage ∝ P × time ∝ V^3 × (D / V) = V^2 · D ⇒ 10 % less speed ≈ 27 % less power, ≈ 19 % less fuel on the voyage (before the hotel load and the engine's part-load efficiency)
  • Speed optimisation (SEEMP Guidelines) means the speed that gives the minimum fuel per tonne-mile for the voyage, not the minimum speed: auxiliary and hotel consumption, charter-party speed, cargo-care and schedule all enter. Slow steaming's engine-side effects (heavy running, de-rating, light running margin) are in Part 24 Topic 2.
  • Just-in-time arrival removes the waste of steaming fast and then waiting at anchor: the ship adjusts speed so as to arrive when the berth, fairway or pilot is actually available. The IMO/GIA Just In Time Arrival Guide (2020) sets out the barriers — chiefly the charter-party 'due despatch' obligation and port data-sharing — and it is a company-specific SEEMP measure (Part 24 Topic 1).
  • Fuel is a major component of voyage operating cost, which is why speed is the first operational lever (qualitative statement; shares vary with fuel price and ship type).

🌊 Fore-Body Optimisation and the Bulbous Bow
  • The fore-body design variables are the bulbous bow, the forward shoulder and the waterline entrance. Potential-flow (panel) calculations are routinely used here because wave-making dominates at the bow; viscous RANS CFD is added for the shoulder and for separation.
  • How the bulb works. The bulb generates its own wave system ahead of the stem. Designed correctly, its wave trough coincides with the crest of the natural bow wave, so the two partly cancel, the resultant bow wave is smaller and the wave-making resistance falls; hull (propulsive) efficiency rises.
  • Kracht's parameters (SNAME 1978) define a bulb by its volume, its section area at the forward perpendicular, its protruding length and height, and its section type; the optimum depends on block coefficient, L/B, B/T and, above all, on speed and draught.
  • The bulb is tuned to one condition. A bulb designed for the contract speed at design draught can add resistance at a slower speed or in ballast, where it may surface. That is why slow steaming drove a wave of re-bulbing retrofits, with reported bow-related savings of up to about 10% in class case studies, and why Part 23 Topic 2 lists bulbous-bow modification among EEXI measures.
  • Bulb section shape (Kracht's section types) is a design choice made for the ship's own speed, draught and seakeeping requirements; there is no one-to-one rule linking a particular section shape to a particular benefit.

🔚 Aft-Body Optimisation, Stern Flaps and Stern Forms
  • The aft-body designer wants to mitigate the stern wave, avoid eddies (flow separation and eddy-making resistance over too-steep buttocks or a bluff stern) and deliver a uniform wake to the propeller. Resistance falls and propulsive efficiency rises when the flow around the stern is improved; wake conditioning and propeller devices themselves are Part 26.
  • Stern flap. A small extension of the hull bottom surface aft of the transom (Cusanelli and Hundley, US Navy, 1999). On transom-stern ships at moderate and higher speeds it modifies the transom flow and pressure field and reduces powering; it is one of the cheapest hydrodynamic retrofits where the hull is suitable.
  • Stern forms — transom, cruiser and counter (elliptical) sterns — each have advantages and penalties in resistance, deck area, slamming and wake quality. The choice for a given hull is made by computational fluid dynamics and confirmed by model tests; there is no universal best stern.
🦶 Twin-Skeg Design

A skeg is a deep, vertical, fin-like projection on the bottom of the hull near the stern. It supports the lower edge of the rudder and, on a single-screw ship, carries the propeller shaft and stern tube, and it provides a bearing line when the ship sits on dry-dock blocks (Wärtsilä Encyclopedia of Marine Technology). A twin-skeg hull has two such skegs, each carrying a shaft and propeller.

Do not quote percentage savings you cannot source, and call it Computational Fluid Dynamics.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Explain how ship size affects energy efficiency and how the EEDI reflects it.
A larger ship has more wetted surface and more total resistance, so it uses more fuel in absolute terms, but because displacement and cargo grow with the cube of the dimensions while wetted surface grows only with the square, the fuel per tonne-mile falls as the ship grows. The EEDI reference line is a times deadweight to the power minus c, so the permitted grams of CO2 per tonne-mile fall with size; a smaller ship is allowed a higher index because the physics makes it less efficient. The limits are commercial and physical: cargo availability, port depth and crane reach, and the hull resistance of a very full form.
Q2. Describe the relationship between service speed and fuel consumption and explain speed optimisation and just-in-time arrival.
For a displacement ship at low Froude number the propeller law gives propulsion power proportional to the cube of speed, so fuel per voyage, power times voyage time, is proportional to the square of speed; a ten per cent speed reduction saves roughly a quarter of the power and nearly a fifth of the voyage fuel, less once hotel load and part-load engine efficiency are counted, and more for fast ships where the exponent is higher. Speed optimisation under the SEEMP Guidelines means the speed that minimises fuel per tonne-mile for the voyage, not the minimum speed. Just-in-time arrival adjusts speed so the ship arrives when the berth, fairway or pilot is actually available instead of steaming fast and waiting at anchor; the IMO and Global Industry Alliance guide identifies the charter-party due-despatch clause and port data-sharing as the main barriers.
Q3. Discuss the trade-offs in choosing length, beam and draught for energy efficiency.
A longer, more slender hull with a lower block coefficient runs at a lower Froude number for the same speed and makes smaller waves, but it needs more steel, has more wetted surface per tonne and may exceed berth lengths. A wider beam increases initial stability and cargo volume but increases wave-making and form resistance, and a very stiff ship rolls uncomfortably. A deeper draught improves propeller immersion and reduces wetted surface per tonne but is limited by ports and canals. A fuller hull carries more on the same dimensions at the cost of resistance and a less uniform wake. The designer holds capacity and stability, respects the port and dock limits, and then minimises resistance at the design speed with the Froude number, the form coefficients and the position of the centre of buoyancy, today with a parametric model and computational fluid dynamics.
Q4. What are the three routes to a hull form, and how does a bulbous bow reduce resistance?
An owner can accept the yard's proven standard hull; modify a parent hull by a Lackenby-type systematic distortion of the lines to a new block coefficient, prismatic coefficient or centre of buoyancy; or commission a new design developed by hydrostatic, hydrodynamic and structural analysis with potential-flow and RANS computational fluid dynamics confirmed by model tests. A bulbous bow creates its own wave system ahead of the stem; when its trough coincides with the crest of the natural bow wave the two partly cancel, the resultant wave is smaller and the wave-making resistance falls. Kracht's parameters — bulb volume, section area at the forward perpendicular, protruding length and height — are chosen for the block coefficient, length-to-beam and beam-to-draught ratios, and above all for the design speed and draught, which is why a bulb can hurt at a slower speed or in ballast and why ships were re-bulbed for slow steaming.
Q5. Explain aft-body optimisation, the stern flap and the twin-skeg arrangement.
The aft body is shaped to reduce the stern wave, to avoid flow separation and eddy-making over the buttocks, and to deliver a uniform wake to the propeller; improving the stern flow lowers resistance and raises propulsive efficiency. A stern flap is a small extension of the hull bottom aft of the transom which alters the transom flow and pressure field and reduces powering on suitable transom-stern ships. The stern form — transom, cruiser or counter — is selected by computational fluid dynamics and model tests because each has pros and cons. A skeg is a deep vertical fin at the stern that supports the rudder heel, carries the shaft on a single-screw ship and bears on the dock blocks; a twin-skeg hull carries two shafts and propellers and is beneficial on full-form ships with restricted draught or a high beam-to-draught ratio, and on tankers requiring redundant propulsion such as shuttle tankers. The designer varies the distance between the skegs, the buttock slope between them, their vertical and longitudinal inclination and the skeg form, and proves the result by CFD and model test.

Situation (illustrative — assumed values)

A 2010-built container ship designed for 25 knots now trades at 18 knots under an EEXI power limitation and a CII target. Her bulb was optimised for 25 knots at design draught; at 18 knots and the lighter operating draught the bow wave is visibly larger than it should be. The technical department asks for a hull-form review.

Analysis

The naval architect starts from the existing lines, varies the bulb with a parent-hull distortion of the fore-body (Kracht parameters: smaller protruding length and section area at the forward perpendicular, lower height) and runs potential-flow calculations at 18 knots across the operating draught range, followed by RANS CFD and a model test of the preferred candidate. A stern flap is screened in the same CFD model because the transom is partly immersed at the operating draught. The CFD and model test show a bow-related resistance reduction at the operating condition, a smaller gain from the flap, and a warning: the new bulb is slightly worse at 25 knots, which the ship no longer uses.

Decision and the engine-room role

The re-bulb proceeds at the next dry-docking; the flap is deferred pending a longer sea-trial data set. The Chief Engineer's part is to provide the baseline: shaft power, rpm, fuel and speed through the water at constant draught and trim before the docking, recorded through the SEEMP monitoring step (Part 24 Topic 1), and the same measurements after, so that the saving is proven rather than assumed and the EEXI/CII technical file can be updated. The percentage saving is left to the measurements; the examiner wants the method, not a quoted figure.
