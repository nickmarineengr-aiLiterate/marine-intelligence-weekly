Part 21 · Topic 1

The HSSC Certification Framework — Certificate Typologies, Validity Windows, Extensions and Electronic Certificates

Certificate Typology · Anniversary Date · SOLAS I/14 Extensions · e-Certificates Uday pp. 501–508 (approx.) Examiner: Paul ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Four corrections. (1) Superseded resolution. The draft anchors the survey windows to A.1156(32) (2021). That has been revoked twice over. The current instrument is resolution A.1207(34), Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2025, adopted at the 34th Assembly in December 2025, revoking A.1186(33), which had revoked A.1156(32). (2) A.883(21) mischaracterised. The draft calls it "the foundational enabling instrument establishing the harmonized system". It is not. The harmonized system was introduced by the 1988 Protocols to SOLAS 74 and the Load Line Convention 1966 and by corresponding MARPOL amendments (resolutions MEPC.39(29) and, for Annex VI, MEPC.132(53)), entering into force 3 February 2000. Resolution A.883(21) is guidance on the global and uniform implementation of that system — important, but not the enabling instrument. Corrected. (3) Wrong issuing regulations for certificates. The draft attributes the Cargo Ship Safety Equipment Certificate to "SOLAS Chapter III" and the Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate to "Chapter II-1". Both are issued under SOLAS Chapter I, Regulation 12; the chapters cited describe the subject matter the certificates attest to, not the issuing provision. (4) Renewal and extension rules incomplete. The draft gives only "renewal window = expiry − 3 months". SOLAS I/14 in fact provides three distinct dating rules and three separate extension mechanisms, all examinable; the complete position is set out below. (5) Cross-reference — Parts 1–18. Part 11 Topic 48 (Statutory Survey Engineering — Load Line Framework, HSSC Protocols & Certificate Extensions) already sets out the same core SOLAS I/14 survey-window and extension mechanics — the five-year validity, the ±3-month annual/intermediate window, and the three extension provisions — anchored to the Load Line Certificate specifically. This topic generalises that mechanics across all statutory certificate types and adds the three-category classification (statutory / commercially mandatory / classification) and the electronic-certificate treatment under FAL.5/Circ.39/Rev.2, neither of which appears in Part 11 Topic 48 or elsewhere in Parts 1–18. Study the two together rather than assuming either is a full substitute for the other.
📖 Definition

The HSSC certification framework is the administrative half of the harmonized system. Part 20 Topic 3 dealt with the surveys themselves — what is inspected and when. This topic deals with the paper that results: which certificates exist, what legal category each falls into, how validity is calculated from the anniversary date, what happens when a survey cannot be completed on time, and how an electronic certificate is verified.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • A certificate that has ceased to be valid stops the ship as effectively as a seized main engine, and unlike machinery it cannot be repaired at sea.
  • The dating rules under SOLAS I/14 are counter-intuitive: completing a renewal survey early can cost the owner validity if it falls outside the three-month window. Knowing the rule is worth real money.
  • Extensions exist and are legitimate, but each has a different trigger, a different maximum, and a different effect on the next certificate's expiry. Confusing them in an oral is an easy fail.
  • Electronic certificates are now routine, and PSC officers verify them online. If the ship cannot demonstrate the verification path, the certificate is treated as unverified.

📅 Anniversary Dates and Validity Rules

The anniversary date is the day and month of each year corresponding to the expiry date of the certificate. Every window is measured from it. Maximum validity is five years for cargo ship certificates and twelve months for the Passenger Ship Safety Certificate.

SurveyWindow
AnnualAnniversary date ±3 months
IntermediateAnniversary date ±3 months, at the 2nd or 3rd anniversary, replacing that annual survey
Periodical (radio)Anniversary date ±3 months
RenewalMay commence at the 4th annual survey and be progressed; must be completed before the certificate expires

The three renewal dating rules (SOLAS I/14) — this is where candidates lose marks:

  • Completed within 3 months before expiry: the new certificate runs from completion of the renewal survey to a date not exceeding five years from the expiry date of the existing certificate. No validity is lost.
  • Completed after the expiry date: same rule — new certificate valid to a date not exceeding five years from the existing expiry date. The lost period is not recovered but neither is it added.
  • Completed more than 3 months before expiry: the new certificate runs from completion to a date not exceeding five years from the date of completion. Surveying too early therefore shortens the effective cycle — this is the counter-intuitive one.

The three extension mechanisms — different triggers, different maxima:

SituationProvisionMaximumEffect on the next certificate
Renewal survey completed but the new certificate cannot be issued or placed on board before expirySOLAS I/14(d)Existing certificate endorsed as valid for a further 5 monthsNew certificate dated from the original expiry as normal
Ship is not in a port in which it is to be surveyed when the certificate expiresSOLAS I/14(e)Extension of up to 3 months, granted by the AdministrationExtension is only to allow the ship to complete its voyage to the survey port; the new certificate is dated from the original, un-extended expiry date
Ship engaged on short voyages, not previously extendedSOLAS I/14(f)Grace period of up to 1 monthNew certificate dated from the original expiry date

The critical point common to all three: an extension does not move the five-year cycle. The new certificate's expiry is calculated from the original expiry date, so the extension is repaid out of the following cycle rather than added to it.

💻 Electronic Certificates

Under FAL.5/Circ.39/Rev.2, an electronic certificate has the same legal force as a paper certificate provided it satisfies four conditions:

  • Format. The presentation must correspond to the model form required by the relevant convention.
  • Integrity protection. Technical measures preventing unauthorised alteration — secure file formats, controlled access, and verification against the issuer's own system.
  • Unique Tracking Number. A dedicated alpha-numeric identifier allowing the specific document to be located on the issuing authority's system.
  • Visible verification feature. A printable, visible symbol — typically a QR code or hyperlink — resolving to the flag State or RO verification portal so an inspector can confirm authenticity independently.

Two practical consequences. First, the ship must be able to demonstrate the verification path on demand — a PSC officer who cannot resolve the UTN will treat the certificate as unverified, so connectivity and a printed fallback matter. Second, the electronic certificate remains the flag State's document: it can be revoked or amended centrally without anything changing on board, which is why the onboard file must be refreshed after any survey, endorsement or company change.

🎓 CE Oral Tip (Paul): Paul's high-yield question in this area is the dating trap: "you complete the renewal survey five months before expiry — when does the new certificate run out?" The answer is five years from the date of completion, not from the old expiry, because it was completed more than three months before expiry. The ship loses two months of cycle. Only a renewal completed within the three-month window (or after expiry) is dated from the original expiry date. Have the three extension provisions ready with their maxima — 5 months, 3 months, 1 month — and be able to say which one applies to which situation. And quote the current guidelines: A.1207(34), 2025 edition.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define the anniversary date and state the survey windows under the HSSC.
The anniversary date is the day and month of each year corresponding to the expiry date of the certificate. Annual, intermediate and periodical surveys must be carried out within a window of three months before to three months after the anniversary date — a six-month window in total. The intermediate survey is carried out within that window at either the second or the third anniversary and replaces the annual survey for that year rather than being additional to it. The renewal survey may commence at the fourth annual survey and be progressed through the following year, but must be completed before the certificate expires. If a required survey is not completed within its window, the certificate ceases to be valid until the survey is completed. The current guidance is resolution A.1207(34), the Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2025 edition, which revoked A.1186(33); the guidelines are revised every two years, so the edition should always be confirmed.
Q2. A renewal survey is completed two months before the certificate expires. When does the new certificate expire, and is any validity lost?
Because the survey was completed within the three months preceding the expiry date, the new certificate is dated from completion of the survey but is valid to a date not exceeding five years from the expiry date of the existing certificate. No validity is lost — the five-year cycle continues unbroken from the original expiry date. The rule differs if the survey is completed more than three months before expiry: in that case the new certificate is valid only to a date not exceeding five years from the date of completion, so surveying too early actually shortens the effective cycle and costs the owner the difference. If the survey is completed after the certificate has already expired, the new certificate is again dated from the original expiry date, so the lapsed period is neither recovered nor added.
Q3. The certificate is about to expire and the ship is at a port with no survey facilities. What can be done?
The company applies to the flag Administration under SOLAS Chapter I Regulation 14(e) for an extension. Where a ship at the time a certificate expires is not in a port in which it is to be surveyed, the Administration may extend the validity of the certificate, but the extension is granted only for the purpose of allowing the ship to complete its voyage to the port in which it is to be surveyed, and only where it appears proper and reasonable to do so. The maximum extension is three months, and no ship may leave the survey port under that extension without a new certificate. Critically, the new certificate is then dated from the original, un-extended expiry date, so the extension is repaid out of the next cycle rather than added to it. In support of the application I would compile the planned maintenance system records, recent self-assessment checklists and machinery condition data to demonstrate the ship is being properly maintained. Two other provisions exist and should not be confused with this one: Regulation 14(d) allows the existing certificate to be endorsed as valid for up to five further months where the renewal survey has been completed but the new certificate cannot yet be issued or placed on board, and Regulation 14(f) allows a grace period of up to one month for ships engaged on short voyages.
Q4. What makes an electronic certificate valid, and how does a PSC officer verify it?
Under IMO circular FAL.5/Circ.39/Rev.2, an electronic certificate has the same status as a paper certificate provided four conditions are met. It must follow the format and layout of the model form required by the relevant convention. It must have technical measures protecting it against unauthorised alteration, such as a secure file format, controlled access and verification against the issuer's system. It must carry a Unique Tracking Number that identifies that specific document on the issuing authority's system. And it must include a printable, visible verification feature — normally a QR code or hyperlink — that resolves to the flag State or Recognised Organisation verification portal. The officer verifies it by following that link and confirming the tracking number returns a current, valid document issued to that ship. The practical implication on board is that connectivity must be available during the inspection and a printed copy should be to hand as a fallback; a certificate whose tracking number cannot be resolved is treated as unverified, which is a deficiency in itself.

Why the distinction matters

Ship safety oversight runs on two parallel methodologies that are frequently confused in orals. A technical survey examines the physical condition of an asset. An administrative audit examines whether a management system is functioning. They answer different questions, use different methods, and produce different remedies.

Focus and direction of travel

A survey looks forward: it asks whether this hull, this machinery, this equipment can be expected to remain fit until the next scheduled inspection. An audit looks backward: it samples historical records — training, drills, maintenance, non-conformity handling — to judge whether the system has been working, and infers from that whether it will continue to.

Method

A survey is a complete physical examination of the defined scope: every hatch cover, every specified thickness measurement point. An audit is deliberately a sampling exercise: the auditor cannot read every record and does not try, but selects a sample sufficient to test whether the system is systematically applied. This is why an audit finding on one record is treated as evidence about the whole system, not as an isolated defect.

Remedy

A survey deficiency is closed by rectification — repair the defect, re-present the item. An audit non-conformity is closed by root cause analysis and corrective action — fixing the individual instance is not enough, because the finding is evidence of a systemic gap. This is the distinction that catches candidates out: replacing the missing entry does not close an ISM non-conformity; explaining why the entry was missing, and changing the process so it cannot recur, does.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

The practical test of whether the two are properly integrated is what happens between surveys. The SMS is what keeps the ship in survey condition — the maintenance schedules, the defect reporting loop, the drills. If the engine department only reaches statutory condition in the fortnight before a surveyor is due, the survey may still pass while the audit correctly fails, and the audit is the one that reflects reality.

Enhanced Structural Inspection Regimes — ESP, CAS and CAP, Substantial Corrosion and Suspect Areas

ESP Code · CAS · CAP Ratings · Substantial Corrosion Uday pp. 508–517 (approx.) Examiner: Paul ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Three corrections. (1) "ESP Code 2017" is not a recognised designation. The instrument is the International Code on the Enhanced Programme of Inspections during Surveys of Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers, 2011 (ESP Code), adopted by resolution A.1049(27) and subsequently amended by MSC resolutions, made mandatory by SOLAS Chapter XI-1, Regulation 2. References to a "2017 Code" have been replaced with the correct citation and a note that amendments are issued periodically, so the applicable amendment set should be confirmed against the ship's class. (2) CAS is now historical for operational purposes. The draft presents the single-hull phase-out as live regulation. Every date in the scheme has passed: Category 1 tankers were prohibited from 2005, and the Category 2 and 3 extensions expired in 2015 or at 25 years of age. CAS is retained here because it remains examinable and because the Category 1/2/3 definitions still appear in oral questions, but it is now presented as the historical mechanism it is, not as a current compliance obligation. (3) Surveyor attendance. The draft's statement about dual surveyor attendance has been retained with its own hedge intact, because the tonnage and age thresholds are genuinely RO-specific and no single figure could be verified as universal.
📖 Definition

The Enhanced Survey Programme (ESP) is the mandatory, prescriptive framework of close-up surveys, thickness measurements and tank testing applied to bulk carriers and oil tankers, whose structural failure modes proved that a general visual survey was not enough. CAS was the statutory verification scheme that policed the single-hull tanker phase-out. CAP is neither statutory nor mandatory: it is a commercially driven condition rating bought by owners to satisfy charterers that an older ship is better than its age suggests.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • ESP is why the Chief Engineer's docking and survey preparation includes safe tank entry — gas freeing, ventilation, lighting, staging and access for the thickness measurement team. Poor preparation does not just delay the survey; it can force close-up items to be deferred, which becomes a class condition.
  • The 75% substantial corrosion threshold is a decision point with commercial consequences: accept it and live with annual close-up tracking of those points forever, or crop and renew now and clear the designation.
  • Corrosion control is engineering work the department owns — anode condition and renewal, coating breakdown assessment in ballast tanks, and keeping bilge and drainage arrangements clear so water does not stand against steel.
  • CAP ratings determine whether an older ship can be fixed at all with the major charterers. A CAP-1 or CAP-2 rating is a commercial asset; CAP-4 effectively ends the ship's trading life with those charterers.

📘 The ESP Code Structure and the ESP File

ESP requirements are not a separate survey: they are integrated into the existing annual, intermediate and renewal surveys, with the scope escalating sharply with the age of the ship. What changes with age is the number of tanks subject to close-up survey, the number of thickness measurement locations, and the extent of tank pressure testing.

The ESP File must be maintained on board throughout the ship's life and made available to the surveyor. It contains, as a minimum:

  • Survey reports — the record of every hull structure inspection, including close-up survey findings.
  • Condition Evaluation Report — the surveyor's summary identifying problem areas, wear trends, suspect areas and the action taken.
  • Thickness measurement reports — the complete ultrasonic gauging dataset, by location, with the readings referenced to the structural drawings.
  • Supporting material: survey planning document, structural drawings, previous repair history, coating condition and anode records, and the ship's own inspection reports.

A missing or disordered ESP File is not a paperwork problem. Without the previous thickness measurement dataset the surveyor cannot establish a corrosion rate, only a present value, and will default to the conservative outcome — more gauging, more close-up survey, or a class condition.

📉 Substantial Corrosion and Suspect Areas

Substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that the assessed wastage is 75% or more of the allowable margin set by the classification society, but still within acceptable limits. The plate is not yet below the minimum permitted thickness — it is on the trajectory towards it.

Wmeasured ≥ 0.75 × Wmax allowable  →  substantial corrosion

Original as-built thickness ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ │ └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘ ◄───────────── maximum allowable reduction (100%) ─────────► ┌────────────────────────────────────┬─────────────────────────┐ │ acceptable wastage (0 – 75%) │ SUBSTANTIAL CORROSION │ │ │ (75 – 100%) │ └────────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────┘ ▲ 75% threshold — becomes a SUSPECT AREA
  • Identification. The thickness measurement team maps the structure by ultrasonic gauging; readings are plotted against the as-built scantlings and the class allowable reduction.
  • Designation. The surveyor records the coordinates as suspect areas in the Condition Evaluation Report.
  • Consequence. Suspect areas are subject to close-up survey and thickness gauging at every subsequent annual survey, so the surveyor can measure the rate of decay rather than a single value. That is a permanent recurring burden on the ship until it is cleared.
  • Options. Either accept the annual tracking, or crop and renew the affected plating to original specification. Renewal removes the suspect designation on re-gauging and the item is cleared from the report.
  • Mitigation. Whichever route is taken, the underlying cause must be addressed — coating breakdown, anode depletion, standing water, or a drainage path that keeps the area wet. Renewing steel into an unchanged corrosive environment simply resets the clock.

The engineering judgement: annual close-up tracking of a bulkhead in a ballast tank means gas freeing, ventilating, staging and entering that tank every year for the rest of the ship's life. The cost of that, compounded, very often exceeds the cost of cropping and renewing at the next docking — which is the argument the Chief Engineer needs to be able to make to the superintendent with the numbers attached.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define substantial corrosion and explain what follows once an area is so designated.
Substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that the assessed wastage has reached 75% or more of the allowable margin established by the classification society, while still remaining within acceptable limits — so the plating is not yet below its minimum permitted thickness, but it is well down the path towards it. It is identified by ultrasonic thickness measurement plotted against the as-built scantlings and the class allowable reduction. Once identified, the surveyor records the coordinates in the Condition Evaluation Report as a suspect area. The consequence is that those locations must then be subjected to close-up survey and thickness gauging at every subsequent annual survey, so that the rate of decay can be tracked rather than a single value. That is a permanent recurring burden — for a ballast tank it means gas freeing, ventilating, staging and entering that tank annually for the rest of the ship's life. The alternative is to crop and renew the affected plating to original specification, after which re-gauging clears the suspect designation. Either way the underlying cause must be dealt with: coating breakdown, anode depletion or standing water, because renewing steel into an unchanged environment only resets the clock.
Q2. What is the ESP File and why does the surveyor care about its continuity?
The ESP File is the dedicated documentation file that a bulk carrier or oil tanker subject to the Enhanced Survey Programme must maintain on board throughout its life. It contains the hull structure survey reports, the Condition Evaluation Report identifying problem areas and suspect areas with the actions taken, and the complete thickness measurement reports referenced to the structural drawings, together with the survey planning document, repair history and coating and anode records. Its continuity matters because a single set of thickness readings tells the surveyor only a present value. It is the comparison against the previous datasets that establishes a corrosion rate, and it is the rate that determines whether the structure will remain above its minimum thickness until the next survey. If the historical data is missing or cannot be correlated to specific locations, the surveyor has no basis for that projection and will take the conservative route — more gauging, wider close-up survey, or a class condition. So a well-kept ESP File directly reduces survey scope and cost.
Q3. Distinguish ESP, CAS and CAP.
ESP is statutory and mandatory. It is the Enhanced Survey Programme required by SOLAS Chapter XI-1 Regulation 2 and given effect by the ESP Code, resolution A.1049(27) as amended, applying to bulk carriers and oil tankers from delivery, with the scope of close-up survey and thickness measurement escalating as the ship ages. CAS, the Condition Assessment Scheme under MARPOL Annex I and resolution MEPC.94(46), was also statutory but served a single narrow purpose: verifying the structural condition of Category 2 and 3 single-hull tankers over 5,000 deadweight and over 15 years of age so that they could continue trading under the phase-out extensions. Every date in that scheme has now passed — Category 1 tankers were prohibited from 2005 and the extensions expired in 2015 or at 25 years of age — so CAS is examinable history rather than live compliance. CAP, the Condition Assessment Programme, is neither statutory nor mandatory. It is a commercial scheme bought by owners at the request of charterers and underwriters, typically for ships past about fifteen years, involving deep gauging, structural strength and fatigue analysis and detailed machinery assessment, and producing a graded rating from CAP-1 very good down to CAP-4 poor, split between CAP-Hull and CAP-Machinery and cargo systems. The key contrast is that CAS answered a yes or no statutory question while CAP answers a graded commercial one.
Q4. What is your role as Chief Engineer in preparing the vessel for an ESP renewal survey?
My role is to make the structure accessible and the data available. On access: coordinating tank cleaning, gas freeing, ventilation, lighting and staging so the close-up survey positions can actually be reached safely, and managing the enclosed space entry permits and standby arrangements for the thickness measurement team and the surveyor. On the plant side: ensuring the ballast system, tank level gauging and bilge arrangements are fully operational so tanks can be emptied, checked and re-ballasted on the survey programme without holding up the yard. On condition: assessing and reporting coating breakdown in ballast tanks and anode wastage as a percentage, so corrosion control decisions are based on measurement rather than impression. On documentation: keeping the ESP File complete and in order, with the previous thickness measurement reports correlated to the drawings, the repair history and the previous Condition Evaluation Report, so the surveyor can establish trends. And on planning: making sure known suspect areas are on the docking specification in advance, so a decision to crop and renew is taken before the ship is in the dock rather than as an expensive variation once it is.

🧠 Memory Box
  • Substantial corrosion = 75% or more of the allowable margin, still within limits. Result: suspect area → close-up survey and gauging at EVERY annual thereafter.
  • ESP = statutory (SOLAS XI-1/2, ESP Code A.1049(27)). CAS = statutory but spent. CAP = commercial and graded.
  • ESP Annex A = bulk carriers (single/double side skin); Annex B = oil tankers (double hull / other).
  • ESP File trio: survey reports · Condition Evaluation Report · thickness measurement reports.
  • CAP-1 very good · CAP-2 good · CAP-3 satisfactory · CAP-4 poor. Split CAP-Hull and CAP-Machinery.
  • Single-hull categories: Cat 1 pre-MARPOL no SBT (out 2005); Cat 2 MARPOL with SBT; Cat 3 5,000 DWT up to the Cat 1/2 thresholds.

Where the steel actually goes

Ballast tanks are the dominant loss mechanism: alternating wet and dry cycles, warm cargo adjacent, oxygen-rich seawater and coating breakdown at edges, welds and stiffener toes. Cargo hold tank tops and lower hopper plating on bulk carriers lose steel to mechanical damage from grabs and bulldozers, which removes coating and exposes bare steel to residual moisture and cargo acids. Under-deck and upper wing structures suffer from condensation. In each case the corrosion follows the coating failure, which follows the mechanical damage or the surface preparation defect.

What the department controls

Anodes: condition assessed and recorded as a percentage consumed at each tank entry, not a subjective note — a consistent percentage record allows the renewal interval to be set from data. Coatings: breakdown assessed against a defined scale by area and location, photographed and logged; touch-up carried out where surface preparation can actually be achieved rather than painting over rust. Drainage: bilge suctions, strums and drain paths kept clear so water does not stand — the single commonest local cause of accelerated wastage. Ballast management: minimising unnecessary partial filling that leaves a waterline oscillating in the same band of plating.

Making the renewal case

The argument to the superintendent should be built as a cost comparison over the remaining survey cycles, not as an appeal to good practice. Annual close-up tracking of a suspect area in a ballast tank carries a recurring cost: cleaning, gas freeing, ventilation, staging, enclosed-space entry manning, surveyor attendance and the operational time. Multiply that by the number of annual surveys remaining before the next planned docking window in which renewal could be done anyway, and compare it with the cropping and renewal cost at the next docking. In most cases where the ship has meaningful life left, renewal wins on cost alone.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

Container tonnage is not ESP tonnage, but the same logic governs ballast tank and void space condition, and the same corrosion-rate discipline applies to the class hull survey. The habit worth carrying across is recording anode and coating condition quantitatively at every tank entry, so that when the docking specification is written the decisions are made from a trend rather than from the last person's recollection.

📖 Definition

This topic covers the ship's administrative compliance layer: the documents that establish who the ship is and who is responsible for it, and the audits that verify the management system behind them. The Continuous Synopsis Record (CSR) is the ship's unbroken identity history. The Document of Compliance and Safety Management Certificate are the ISM Code's evidence that a functioning safety management system exists ashore and on board. Together they answer the question a surveyor cannot answer by looking at machinery: who is accountable for this ship, and is their system working?

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • A break in the CSR chain is a detainable finding that no amount of engine room condition can offset. It is also the classic marker of a ship whose ownership history someone has tried to obscure.
  • A major non-conformity raised against the SMS can suspend the Safety Management Certificate. That stops the ship as surely as a main engine failure, and the trigger is very often an engine department item — an untracked defect, a bypassed alarm, an unrecorded maintenance interval.
  • Non-conformities are closed by root cause analysis, not by repair. An engineer who fixes the item and reports it fixed has not closed the finding, and the follow-up audit will say so.
  • Under a bareboat charter or a management change, the ISM "Company" changes — new DOC, new SMC, new CSR entry. Getting this sequence wrong at handover is a common and entirely avoidable detention.

📜 The Continuous Synopsis Record

The CSR is a continuous, chronological record of a ship's history, issued by the flag Administration and kept on board for the life of the ship. Its purpose is to make ownership, management and flag history traceable and un-erasable, so that substandard ownership cannot be laundered through a change of name and register. When a ship changes flag, the outgoing Administration transmits the complete CSR file to the incoming one; the chain is never restarted.

Contents required by SOLAS XI-1/5:

  • Every document in the sequence stays on board — the current CSR, all superseded CSRs, the index of amendments, and the amendment forms. The file is the evidence; a gap in the numbering is a gap in the chain.
  • The date on which the ship ceased to be registered under a previous flag must appear, so there is no unaccounted period.
  • The CSR is verified at statutory surveys and is a standing PSC target. A missing intermediate document or an unindexed history page following an ownership or flag transition is a detainable finding.

Related and often examined alongside it: SOLAS XI-1/3 requires the ship identification number to be permanently marked in a visible place on the stern or on the ship's sides, and in an easily accessible place inside the ship — on a transverse bulkhead of a machinery space, or on a hatchway, or on the inside of a ro-ro space. A mismatch between the marked number and the number on the certificates is treated as a serious documentary discrepancy.

⚠ Non-Conformities — the Distinction That Matters
  • Observation. A statement of fact made during an audit and substantiated by objective evidence. It is not a finding of non-compliance; it flags something that could develop into one.
  • Non-conformity. An observed situation where objective evidence indicates the non-fulfilment of a specified requirement. Closed by corrective action within an agreed timeframe.
  • Major non-conformity. An identifiable deviation posing a serious threat to personnel, ship safety or the environment, requiring immediate corrective action — or the absence of effective and systematic implementation of a requirement of the ISM Code.

The critical procedural point. A major non-conformity must be downgraded to a non-conformity before the audit team leaves the ship, by taking immediate corrective action that removes the serious threat. If it cannot be downgraded, the SMC — or, if the finding is against the shore organisation, the DOC — is withdrawn or its validity suspended. Downgrading is not the same as closing out: the underlying non-conformity remains open and must still be closed by root cause analysis and corrective action within the agreed period.

[Major non-conformity raised] │ Can immediate action remove the serious threat before the team departs? ├── YES ──> DOWNGRADED to a non-conformity │ └─> still open: root cause analysis + corrective action + verification └── NO ──> SMC (or DOC) WITHDRAWN / SUSPENDED └─> ship cannot trade until re-verified

Closing out is not repairing. The remedy for a non-conformity is: correction (fix the instance), root cause analysis (establish why the system permitted it), corrective action (change the process so it cannot recur), and verification of effectiveness at the next audit. An engineer who replaces the missing entry and reports the finding closed has completed step one of four. This is the single most common weakness in engine department audit responses.

Invalidation triggers for DOC and SMC: periodical verifications not carried out within their windows; major non-conformities left unrectified; and unauthorised or unnotified changes to the management system or the Company. Loss of the DOC withdraws the SMC of every ship it covers.

🏷 The Commercial Vetting Boundary

Vetting inspections are commercial, not statutory. SIRE, introduced by OCIMF in 1993, and the CDI scheme for chemical and gas carriers, are risk-assessment tools operated by cargo interests, charterers and underwriters. No international convention requires them. They are nonetheless a practical prerequisite to trading with the oil and chemical majors, and a poor report can end a ship's employment prospects faster than a PSC detention.

The Chief Engineer's standing obligations in this area are the same regardless of scheme: maintenance records that match the actual condition of the equipment; a defect list that is live rather than decorative; clean bilges and a demonstrably functioning oily water separator with an intact 15 ppm alarm and no bypass arrangements; steering gear and emergency systems that work on demand; and factual, unembellished clarification of any disputed observation at the closing meeting.

The full treatment of SIRE 2.0, CDI and TMSA — architecture, question library, risk-based inspection targeting and the human-factors dimension — is Part 22, Topic 1.

The answer that scores is procedural, not remedial: it must be downgraded to a non-conformity before the audit team leaves the ship by immediate corrective action removing the serious threat, failing which the SMC is withdrawn or suspended — and downgrading is not closing out, because the non-conformity remains open pending root cause analysis and corrective action. If he then asks how you close it, do not say "we repaired it". Say correction, root cause, corrective action, verification of effectiveness. That four-step answer separates candidates who have been audited from candidates who have read about auditing.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the Continuous Synopsis Record and why is a break in it treated so seriously?
The Continuous Synopsis Record is required by SOLAS Chapter XI-1 Regulation 5. It is a continuous, chronological record of the ship's history, issued by the flag Administration and kept on board for the life of the ship, recording the flag State and date of registration, the IMO number, the ship's name and port of registry, the registered owner and owner identification number, any bareboat charterer, the ISM Company and Company identification number, the classification society, and the bodies that issued the Document of Compliance, the Safety Management Certificate and the International Ship Security Certificate. Every change to those particulars produces an amendment and a new numbered document, and all superseded documents, the amendment forms and the index remain on board. When the ship changes flag, the outgoing Administration transmits the complete file to the incoming one, so the chain is never restarted. A break in the chain is serious because the entire purpose of the instrument is to make substandard ownership traceable — a missing intermediate document or an unindexed transition is exactly what would be produced by an attempt to obscure a period of the ship's history, and Port State Control treats it as a detainable finding for that reason.
Q2. An ISM auditor raises a major non-conformity. What happens next?
A major non-conformity is an identifiable deviation posing a serious threat to personnel, the ship or the environment and requiring immediate corrective action, or the absence of effective and systematic implementation of a requirement of the ISM Code. The immediate requirement is that it must be downgraded to a non-conformity before the audit team leaves the ship, by taking corrective action that removes the serious threat. If it cannot be downgraded, the Safety Management Certificate is withdrawn or its validity suspended — and if the finding is against the shore organisation, the Document of Compliance is withdrawn, which withdraws the SMC of every ship that DOC covers. It is important not to confuse downgrading with closing out. Downgrading only removes the immediate danger; the non-conformity remains open and must be closed within the agreed timeframe by correction of the instance, root cause analysis establishing why the system permitted it, corrective action changing the process so it cannot recur, and verification of effectiveness at the following audit.
Q3. State the validity periods and verification requirements for the DOC and the SMC.
The Document of Compliance is issued to the Company, for each type of ship it operates, and is valid for five years, subject to annual verification within three months before or after each anniversary date. A copy must be carried on board. The Safety Management Certificate is issued to the ship, is valid for five years, and requires at least one intermediate verification carried out between the second and third anniversary dates. An SMC can only be issued to a ship whose Company holds a valid DOC covering that ship type. There are also interim certificates: an interim DOC valid for twelve months, issued to a new Company or where an existing Company takes on a new ship type, and an interim SMC valid for six months, issued for a new ship, a change of flag or a change of Company, which the Administration may extend for a further six months. The certificates are invalidated if the periodical verifications are not carried out within their windows, if major non-conformities are left unrectified, or if unauthorised changes are made to the management system or the Company.
Q4. A vessel is transferring to a new manager under a bareboat charter. What documentation must change and in what order?
The ISM Company changes, so the certification chain has to be rebuilt rather than amended. The incoming manager must hold a Document of Compliance covering that ship type — if they do not, an interim DOC valid for twelve months is issued after an initial audit of the shore organisation. The existing Safety Management Certificate becomes invalid on the change of Company, so an interim SMC valid for six months is issued to the ship following an initial verification, extendable by the Administration for a further six months, during which the full SMC audit is carried out. The Continuous Synopsis Record must be amended under SOLAS XI-1 Regulation 5 to record the new bareboat charterer, the new ISM Company and Company identification number, and the issuing body of the new DOC and SMC, with the amendment form completed and a new numbered CSR document issued, all previous documents retained on board. If flag or class changes as well, those entries and the corresponding statutory and class certificates change too, and the previous Administration must transmit the CSR file to the new one. The practical risk is sequencing: if the ship sails after the change of Company but before the interim certification is in place, it is trading without valid ISM certification, which is a detention at the next port.

The pattern

Major non-conformities raised against the engine department rarely concern a broken component. They concern evidence that a system for controlling risk was not operating: a critical equipment defect that was never entered on the defect list, a safety device found isolated with no risk assessment or authorisation, a maintenance interval that has silently lapsed across several cycles, or an Oil Record Book entry that cannot be reconciled with the tank soundings and the transfer log.

Why these are treated as major

Each of them satisfies the second limb of the definition — the absence of effective and systematic implementation of an ISM requirement — rather than the first limb about immediate physical danger. An isolated bilge alarm is a component fault; an isolated bilge alarm with no entry anywhere in the system is evidence that the ship's defect control process is not functioning, and the auditor is entitled to conclude that other defects are being handled the same way. That inference is what escalates the finding.

The three habits that prevent it

First, everything isolated gets a record: a defect entry, a risk assessment, an authorisation, and a notification to the office if it affects critical equipment. An authorised, documented isolation is a managed risk; an undocumented one is a system failure. Second, overdue maintenance is declared rather than absorbed — an overdue job with a reason, a risk assessment and a plan is a finding at worst, while an overdue job hidden by a back-dated sign-off is a major non-conformity and a credibility problem for every other record on board. Third, the Oil Record Book is reconciled against soundings and the transfer log before it is signed, not after a question is asked.

On my vessel (Maersk A/S container tonnage)

The most useful discipline is treating the defect list as the audit's primary exhibit rather than an internal working document. If every isolation, deferral and overdue item on board appears on it, with a date, a reason, a risk assessment and an owner, then the auditor's sampling will keep confirming that the system works. The engine room is then being judged on how it manages problems, which is what the ISM Code actually asks, rather than on whether it has any — which no ship can claim.
