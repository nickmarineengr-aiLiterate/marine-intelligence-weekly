EEDI — Required versus Attained Values, the Reference Line, Verification and the IEE Certificate

Reg. 22 / Reg. 24 · Reference Line · Verification · IEE Certificate Uday pp. 551–555 Examiner: Simon ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Hand-off from Part 22 Topic 3. Part 22 Topic 3 gives the IMO GHG Strategy context, the one-line EEDI definition and the phase ladder as an overview. This topic takes the chapter from there and does not repeat the Strategy narrative; read the two together. Four material corrections to the source notes. (1) Regulation numbers. The notes cite ‘regulation 21’ for the required EEDI. That was the numbering before the 2021 revision of MARPOL Annex VI, in force 1 November 2022. The current numbers are regulation 22 (attained EEDI) and regulation 24 (required EEDI); the reference-line parameters are in regulation 24, Table 2, and the reduction factors in regulation 24, Table 1. Minimum propulsion power is regulation 24.5. (2) Phases are not a uniform five-year 0/10/20/30 ladder. That ladder is correct for bulk carriers and tankers of 20,000 DWT and above, but MEPC.324(75) brought Phase 3 forward to 1 April 2022 for containerships, general cargo ships, gas carriers of 15,000 DWT and above, LNG carriers and cruise ships with non-conventional propulsion, and raised the containership Phase 3 factor to 30–50% by size. (3) Auxiliary engine power is not ‘5% of main engine power’. The EEDI Calculation Guidelines (MEPC.364(79)) set PAE = 0.025 × MCR + 250 kW where the total propulsion power is 10,000 kW or more, and 0.05 × MCR below that. (4) In the reference-line formula a × b−c, b is the ship’s capacity (deadweight, or GT for cruise ships), not a constant. A fuel-density temperature-correction formula that appeared in the source has been removed: it is a bunker-survey practice, not part of the EEDI calculation. The source’s ‘five factors determining the EEDI’ is kept in the Memory Box as a memory aid only; it is not IMO text. Cross-reference: Part 4 Topic 19 (NOx Technical Code) covers the NOx Technical File itself; it is cited here only as the source of the certified SFC values. Currency check, MEPC 84 (27 April – 1 May 2026): the governing survey guideline is now the 2026 EEDI Survey and Certification Guidelines, resolution MEPC.411(84), which supersede MEPC.365(79) and its amendments MEPC.374(80) and MEPC.403(83); the two-stage process, document list, verifier duties and certificate issue are unchanged, the sea-trial standards are now ITTC 7.5-04-01-01.1 (2024) or ISO 15016:2025, and the dual-fuel treatment is clarified. The EEDI Calculation Guidelines remain MEPC.364(79), now as amended by MEPC.410(84) (two footnotes on dual-fuel engines; nothing changes for a conventional ship). Every citation in this topic uses the 2026 position.
📖 Definition

The attained EEDI (regulation 22) is the ship-specific value, in grams of CO2 per tonne-nautical mile, calculated at the design stage and confirmed at sea trial, that expresses how much CO2 the ship emits for a unit of transport work at a defined reference condition. The required EEDI (regulation 24) is the maximum attained value permitted for that ship type and size in the phase in which the ship was contracted, laid down or delivered: required EEDI = (1 − X/100) × reference line value, where X is the reduction factor in regulation 24, Table 1, and the reference line value is a × b−c with a and c from regulation 24, Table 2 and b the ship’s capacity. Compliance means attained EEDI ≤ required EEDI, verified in two stages and recorded by the issue of the International Energy Efficiency Certificate.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The attained EEDI is fixed at design and construction. Once the IEE Certificate is issued, nothing the Chief Engineer does changes it — but any later change to engines, power limitation, propeller or hull appendages that departs from the EEDI Technical File can invalidate the certificate and must be verified.
  • The EEDI Technical File on board is the authority for the ship’s reference speed, installed power, SFC values and the electric power table. Surveyors, vetting inspectors and charterers read it; the Chief Engineer must know where it is and what it commits the ship to.
  • Minimum propulsion power (regulation 24.5) is the safety counterweight to EEDI: a ship cannot be made ‘efficient’ by under-powering it to the point where it cannot hold course in adverse weather. This is the engineering tension examiners like to explore.
  • Everything in EEXI (Topic 2) is built on EEDI machinery — the same reference line, the same PME/PAE rules, the same Technical File logic. Understanding EEDI properly makes EEXI a short step.

The reference line is a regression curve through the attained values of a historical fleet: for most types a × DWT−c (cruise ships with non-conventional propulsion use GT). The parameters a and c are fixed in regulation 24 Table 2 — for example bulk carriers a = 961.79, c = 0.477; tankers a = 1,218.80, c = 0.488; containerships a = 174.22, c = 0.201 — and were derived under MEPC.231(65) from the IHS Fairplay database (now S&P Global Sea-web) of ships of 400 GT and above delivered between 1 January 1999 and 1 January 2009, using each ship’s capacity, service speed and total installed main power.

Because the exponent c is less than one, the curve falls with size: a 200,000 DWT bulk carrier is allowed far fewer grams per tonne-mile than a 30,000 DWT one. That is the regulatory expression of economies of scale.

🧮 The Attained-EEDI Formula and Its Principal Terms

EEDI ≈ [ (PME × SFCME × CF) + (PAE × SFCAE × CF) ] ÷ (Capacity × Vref) g CO2/t·nm

This is the teaching form. The full expression in MEPC.364(79) adds shaft-motor and shaft-generator terms, innovative-technology deductions and the correction factors fj, fw, fi, fc, fl and fm; for a conventional single-screw diesel ship without them the teaching form is numerically the same.

🔍 Verification — Design Stage and Sea Trial

The 2026 EEDI Survey and Certification Guidelines, MEPC.411(84), require survey and certification of the EEDI in two stages: preliminary verification at the design stage and final verification at the sea trial. The verifier is the Administration or its recognized organization.

Why the sea trial matters: the only term that cannot be certified on paper is Vref. If the measured speed at 75% MCR falls below the design estimate, the attained EEDI rises. The guidelines therefore prescribe how speed is measured (at more than two power points spanning PME), how it is corrected for wind, waves, current, shallow water, displacement and water temperature, and how the design power curve is adjusted — with a warning against deliberately conservative design curves intended to buy an upward speed adjustment.

Minimum propulsion power. Regulation 24.5 requires that installed propulsion power is not less than the power needed to maintain manoeuvrability in adverse conditions, assessed under MEPC.1/Circ.850/Rev.3. It is checked at the same verification and is the reason an owner cannot chase a low EEDI by simply fitting a smaller engine.

📄 The IEE Certificate
  • Issued by the Administration or its recognized organization after the survey under regulation 5.4, to any ship of 400 GT and above (regulation 6.4), in the form of Appendix VIII.
  • Supplement records the attained and required EEDI (and, for existing ships, EEXI — Topic 2) and confirms the SEEMP is on board.
  • Validity: the IEE Certificate has no periodic expiry date and no annual, intermediate or renewal survey cycle; under regulation 9.10 it remains valid throughout the life of the ship, subject to the cessation-of-validity conditions in regulation 9.11. Do not tell an examiner it ‘never expires’ — say it has no expiry date but ceases to be valid in the regulation 9.11 cases.
  • Regulation 9.11 — it ceases to be valid: (1) if the ship is withdrawn from service, or if a new certificate is issued following a major conversion of the ship; (2) upon transfer of the ship to the flag of another State — a new certificate is issued only when the new Administration is satisfied the ship complies with Chapter 4, and the former flag transmits copies of the certificate and survey reports on request within three months; or (3) if the ship’s equipment, systems, fittings, arrangements or material covered by the survey are changed without the express approval of the Administration (regulation 5.5), unless regulation 3 (exceptions and exemptions) applies.
  • The EEDI Technical File is its supporting document and must be kept on board.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Distinguish attained EEDI from required EEDI and state the governing regulations.
The attained EEDI is the ship’s own calculated index, in grams of carbon dioxide per tonne-nautical mile, produced from the design parameters and confirmed at sea trial; it is governed by regulation 22 of MARPOL Annex VI under the 2021 revised numbering. The required EEDI is the maximum attained value allowed for that ship type, size and phase, governed by regulation 24, and equals one minus the reduction factor X over one hundred, multiplied by the reference line value. Compliance is attained less than or equal to required, verified by the Administration or recognized organization and recorded on the International Energy Efficiency Certificate.
Q2. What is the EEDI reference line and how was it derived?
The reference line is a curve representing the average attained index of a defined group of ships, expressed as a times capacity to the power minus c, where capacity is deadweight for most types and gross tonnage for cruise ships with non-conventional propulsion. The parameters a and c are fixed per ship type in regulation 24 Table 2. They were derived under resolution MEPC.231(65) by regression on data from the IHS Fairplay database, now S&P Global Sea-web, for ships of 400 gross tonnage and above delivered between 1 January 1999 and 1 January 2009, using each ship’s capacity, service speed and total installed main engine power.
Q3. Explain how auxiliary engine power is taken into the attained EEDI.
Under the 2022 EEDI Calculation Guidelines, MEPC.364(79) as amended by MEPC.410(84), the auxiliary power term PAE is the power required to supply the normal maximum sea load for propulsion machinery and accommodation. For ships with total propulsion power of 10,000 kilowatts or more it is taken as 0.025 times the total main engine MCR plus 250 kilowatts; below 10,000 kilowatts it is 0.05 times the MCR. Where that rule would misrepresent the real sea load, for example on passenger ships, a validated electric power table is used instead. It is multiplied by the auxiliary engine SFC at 50 per cent MCR from the NOx Technical File and by the carbon factor of the fuel.
Q4. Describe the two stages of EEDI verification and the role of the sea trial.
Under the 2026 EEDI Survey and Certification Guidelines, resolution MEPC.411(84), which superseded the 2022 guidelines MEPC.365(79) in May 2026, verification is in two stages. At the design stage the shipbuilder submits the EEDI Technical File with the ship’s particulars, installed power, estimated power curve, reference speed, SFC values from the NOx Technical File and the electric power table where required; the verifier reviews it, may witness the model tank test and confirm the qualification of the test facility, and issues a report of pre-verification. At the sea trial the verifier attends and confirms the propulsion arrangement, draught and trim, sea conditions, ship speed and shaft power. The submitter develops the power curves from the measured points, corrects them for environmental effects, and recalculates the attained EEDI using the measured speed at 75 per cent MCR and the final deadweight; the revised Technical File is then verified and the International Energy Efficiency Certificate is issued.
Q5. Can a shipowner reduce the attained EEDI simply by installing a smaller main engine?
Only within limits. Regulation 24.5 requires that the installed propulsion power is not less than the power needed to maintain the manoeuvrability of the ship in adverse conditions, assessed under the interim guidelines in MEPC.1/Circ.850/Rev.3, and this minimum-power check is part of the EEDI verification. So the reduction in power and speed that lowers the index is bounded by a safety floor, and in practice designers look to hull form, propeller efficiency, waste heat recovery and shaft generators rather than simple under-powering.

Situation

A 63,000 DWT Ultramax bulk carrier contracted in Phase 3 is designed to a reference speed of 14.2 knots at 75% MCR, giving a preliminary attained EEDI comfortably inside the required value. At the sea trial, run in Beaufort 5 with a following current, the raw measured speed at the EEDI power point is 13.6 knots.

What the verifier does

The verifier does not take the raw figure. Under MEPC.411(84) paragraphs 4.3.5 to 4.3.9 the submitter corrects the measured points for wind, waves, current, shallow water, displacement, water temperature and density using ITTC 7.5-04-01-01.1 (2024) or ISO 15016:2025, develops the trial power curve, and compares it with the design curve. Where the trial curve sits to the right of the design curve the attained EEDI is recalculated at the corrected trial speed; where the ship was tried at a draught other than the EEDI draught, the speed is adjusted through the tank-test relationship between the two conditions.

Outcome

After correction the speed at PME is 13.9 knots. The attained EEDI rises by about two per cent but remains below the required value, the revised Technical File is verified and the IEE Certificate is issued. Had it crossed the line, the yard’s options would have been a verified power limitation with a recalculated Vref, or hull and propeller changes and a re-trial — not a quiet edit of the Technical File. The lesson for the Chief Engineer who later joins the ship: the Vref on the certificate is a corrected, verified figure, and every machinery change that could move it is a certificate matter.

EEXI — Existing-Ship Technical Efficiency, Power Limitation and Certification

Reg. 23 / Reg. 25 · One-off · EPL / SHaPoLi · EEXI Technical File Uday pp. 556–561 Examiner: Simon ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Five material corrections to the source notes, each of which an examiner would penalise. (1) Applicability. EEXI applies to ships of 400 GT and above of the listed types (regulations 19, 23 and 25), not ‘above 5,000 GT’; 5,000 GT is the threshold for the Data Collection System and CII (Topics 3 and 4). (2) Dates. The amendments (MEPC.328(76), adopted June 2021) entered into force on 1 November 2022, not 2021; the attained EEXI is verified at the first annual, intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2023. (3) No phases. The reduction factor Y is a single fixed value per ship type and size in regulation 25 Table 3; EEXI is a one-off requirement and there is no separate ‘EEXI reference line’ — the required EEXI is taken from the EEDI reference line. (4) No sea trial is required. The reference speed is taken from the approved EEDI speed-power curve, an estimated curve, an existing sea-trial report, or the statistical approximation Vref,app in MEPC.350(78); a ship that fits power limitation recalculates Vref through the power curve, it does not re-trial. (5) The ‘Case I / II / III’ construction-date scheme in the source is a teaching device, not an IMO classification. What the regulation actually says is in regulation 23.3: a ship whose verified attained EEDI is at or below its required EEXI may use that EEDI as its attained EEXI and is verified on the EEDI Technical File; every other ship calculates an attained EEXI. The source’s numerical worked example has not been reproduced: its auxiliary SFC of 30 g/kWh is physically impossible and its carbon factor was rounded. A rebuilt example using the verified method and realistic inputs is given below. Currency check, MEPC 84 (May 2026): no EEXI calculation or survey guideline was amended; MEPC.350(78) and MEPC.351(78) remain current, including the rule that with overridable power limitation PME is 83% of the limited power or 75% of the original MCR, whichever is lower. The 2022 EEDI Calculation Guidelines that EEXI borrows from are now MEPC.364(79) as amended by MEPC.410(84); the amendment adds only dual-fuel footnotes. MEPC.407(84), adopted at MEPC 84 and entering into force on 1 September 2027, replaces the fixed review clause in regulation 25.3 with a standing review; it changes nothing an existing ship must do now.
📖 Definition

The Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index extends the EEDI logic to ships that were never subject to it. Every ship of 400 GT and above of the listed types must have an attained EEXI (regulation 23), calculated once with the EEDI formula and the EEXI Calculation Guidelines and recorded in an EEXI Technical File, that does not exceed the required EEXI (regulation 25) = (1 − Y/100) × EEDI reference line value, where Y is the fixed reduction factor for the ship type and size. It is a technical, design-side measure verified at the first survey on or after 1 January 2023, after which the International Energy Efficiency Certificate is reissued; it is not recalculated annually.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • For most existing tonnage EEXI compliance was achieved by limiting power — an overridable engine power limitation (EPL) or shaft power limitation (SHaPoLi). The Chief Engineer owns the sealed setting, the power reserve, the Onboard Management Manual and the record of every un-limiting event. Examiners test exactly that.
  • Un-limiting is permitted only to secure the safety of the ship or save life at sea (regulation 3.1 scenario); every use must be recorded and reported. A casual override to make a tide is a non-compliance with a certificate attached to it.
  • EEXI fixes the technical baseline; CII (Topic 3) then judges how the ship is actually operated inside that baseline. A ship that only just passes EEXI by limitation has less speed margin to trade against CII.
  • The EEXI Technical File and the IEE Certificate supplement are PSC-inspectable documents since 2023.

🧮 Calculating the Attained EEXI — What Changes from EEDI

MEPC.350(78) uses the EEDI formula and parameters, reading ‘EEDI’ as ‘EEXI’, with these differences:

  • PME with overridable power limitation fitted: 83% of the limited power (MCRlim) or 75% of the original MCR, whichever is lower, for each main engine.
  • PAE: the EEDI rule (0.025 × MCR + 250 kW above 10,000 kW; 0.05 × MCR below). Where that differs significantly from the real sea load, the electric power table or onboard-monitored annual average at sea may be used.
  • Vref: for an EEDI ship, from the approved speed-power curve; for a non-EEDI ship, from an estimated speed-power curve under MEPC.351(78), or from an existing sea-trial report if run under EEDI draught and sea conditions; or, where neither exists, the statistical approximation Vref,app = (Vref,avg − mV) × (ΣPME / (0.75 × MCRavg))1/3. A new sea trial is not required.
  • SFC: from the NOx Technical File at 75% MCR (main) and 50% MCR (auxiliary); where the file lacks the point, manufacturer data or an approved default is used.
  • Boilers are not a term in the formula; the index covers main engines, auxiliary engines and shaft motors/generators only.

The cube-root relationship between power and speed is the key engineering idea: reducing PME by 40% reduces the numerator by about 40% but reduces Vref by only about 16%, so the index falls. That is why power limitation is so effective, and why it costs speed.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is EEXI, to which ships does it apply and when did it take effect?
The Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index is a one-off technical efficiency requirement for existing ships introduced by the 2021 revision of MARPOL Annex VI, resolution MEPC.328(76), which entered into force on 1 November 2022. Under regulations 23 and 25 every ship of 400 gross tonnage and above of the listed types must have an attained EEXI, calculated with the EEDI method and recorded in an EEXI Technical File, that does not exceed the required EEXI. The attained value is verified at the first annual, intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2023, after which the International Energy Efficiency Certificate is reissued.
Q2. How is the required EEXI determined?
Required EEXI equals one minus Y over one hundred, multiplied by the EEDI reference line value for the ship type and capacity. Y is a single fixed reduction factor per ship type and size in regulation 25 Table 3 — for example 20 per cent for bulk carriers and tankers between 20,000 and 200,000 deadweight, 15 per cent above 200,000, 30 per cent for gas carriers of 15,000 deadweight and above and 50 per cent for containerships of 200,000 deadweight and above. There is no separate EEXI reference line and there are no phases.
Q3. A ship built in 2016 already holds a verified attained EEDI. Must it calculate an EEXI?
Not necessarily. Regulation 23.3 provides that where the attained EEDI verified by the Administration is at or below the required EEXI, that EEDI value may be taken as the attained EEXI and verification is carried out on the EEDI Technical File. If the EEDI is above the required EEXI, the ship must achieve compliance, typically by an overridable engine or shaft power limitation, and produce an EEXI Technical File with the limited power and the recalculated reference speed.
Q4. Explain how reference speed is obtained for an existing ship that has no EEDI and no usable sea-trial data.
Under MEPC.350(78) the reference speed for a ship outside the EEDI scope is taken from an estimated speed-power curve developed under the EEXI survey and certification guidelines, or from an existing sea-trial report if the trial was run at the EEDI draught and sea conditions. Where neither is available the guidelines permit a statistical approximation, Vref,app, derived from the fleet-average speed and power for the ship type and corrected by the cube root of the ratio of the ship’s PME to 75 per cent of the average MCR. No new sea trial is required.
Q5. Describe the Chief Engineer’s responsibilities for a ship that complies with EEXI by engine power limitation.
The limitation must be fitted and sealed as described in the Onboard Management Manual under MEPC.335(76) as amended, and the EEXI Technical File and the IEE Certificate supplement must be on board. The Chief Engineer ensures the limited power setting is not exceeded in normal operation, that the power reserve above the limit is used only to secure the safety of the ship or save life at sea, that every un-limiting is recorded with time, reason and duration and reported to the Administration or recognized organization as the manual requires, and that the limitation is reinstated and re-verified afterwards. Any engine modification that changes the limited power or the reference speed is a certificate matter.

Situation

A 2009-built Aframax complies with EEXI by an electronic engine power limitation sealed at 62% of MCR. Crossing the Bay of Biscay in winter the master orders full power to maintain steerage into a Force 10 head sea. The Chief Engineer releases the limitation under the procedure in the Onboard Management Manual.

What must happen

The release itself is lawful: MEPC.335(76) permits use of the power reserve to secure the safety of the ship. What converts it into a compliance problem is the record. The OMM requires the time of un-limiting, the reason, the duration and the time of reinstatement to be logged; the SEEMP and the Administration or recognized organization must be informed as the manual specifies; and the limitation must be reinstated and, where the system requires it, re-sealed and verified before the next survey. MEPC.390(81) tightened the reactivation and reporting expectations after Administrations reported limitations left released for convenience.

Lesson

The Chief Engineer should treat an EPL release exactly like an oily-water-separator bypass for safety: permissible in the defined emergency, indefensible without the entry. On the next PSC inspection the engine log, the OMM record and the EEXI Technical File will be read together.

📖 Definition

The Carbon Intensity Indicator (regulation 28) is the operational counterpart of EEDI/EEXI: an annual measure of how much CO2 a ship of 5,000 GT and above actually emitted per unit of transport work, using the fuel-consumption data collected under regulation 27. The attained annual operational CII is total CO2 divided by capacity × distance travelled (the Annual Efficiency Ratio, AER, in g CO2/dwt·nm). The required annual operational CII is (1 − Z/100) × CIIref, where CIIref = a × Capacity−c is the 2019 fleet reference line and Z the annual reduction factor. The ratio of attained to required places the ship in rating band A to E; a D for three consecutive years or a single E obliges a corrective action plan in SEEMP Part III. The rating is recorded on the annual Statement of Compliance.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • CII is the only Chapter 4 measure that recurs every year and is driven by operation — speed, trim, hull and propeller condition, auxiliary load, waiting time at anchor all feed the AER. It is the Chief Engineer’s regulation.
  • The required line falls every year: a ship that sits in C today drifts into D by 2028 if nothing changes, because Z rises from 11% to 21.5% between 2026 and 2030.
  • A D×3 or E rating forces a verified corrective action plan into SEEMP Part III and delays the Statement of Compliance until it is accepted — a document the charterer and PSC will ask for.
  • Distance-based AER rewards full-speed laden passages and punishes waiting and ballast legs equally; the Chief Engineer must understand that the metric, not just the fuel bill, shapes the voyage orders.

  • SEEMP Part III itself (required since 1 January 2023 with a Confirmation of Compliance) contains the required CII for the next three years, the implementation plan for achieving it and the self-evaluation procedure.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define the attained annual operational CII and state how it is calculated.
The attained annual operational carbon intensity indicator under regulation 28 is the ship’s actual carbon intensity over a calendar year, expressed for most ship types as the Annual Efficiency Ratio: the total mass of CO2 emitted, obtained by multiplying the mass of each fuel consumed under the Data Collection System by its carbon factor, divided by the ship’s deadweight multiplied by the distance travelled over ground in nautical miles, giving grams of CO2 per deadweight-tonne-nautical mile. For cruise, ro-ro and ro-pax ships gross tonnage replaces deadweight and the indicator is called cgDIST. It is a tank-to-wake metric using the fixed carbon factors of the guidelines, with correction factors and voyage adjustments permitted under the G5 guidelines.
Q2. How is the required annual operational CII determined and what are the current reduction factors?
The required CII is one minus Z over one hundred multiplied by the reference CII, where the reference is a times capacity to the power minus c with parameters fitted to the 2019 Data Collection System results for each ship type under the G2 guidelines. Z is the annual reduction factor relative to 2019: five per cent for 2023, seven for 2024, nine for 2025 and eleven for 2026 under MEPC.338(76), and, following resolution MEPC.400(83) adopted at MEPC 83 in April 2025, 13.625 per cent for 2027, 16.25 for 2028, 18.875 for 2029 and 21.5 for 2030.
Q3. Explain the A to E rating and what follows a poor rating.
The ratio of attained to required CII is compared with four boundaries — superior, lower, upper and inferior — obtained by multiplying the required CII by exponential factors derived from the dd vectors in the G4 guidelines. Below the superior boundary is A, major superior; between superior and lower is B, minor superior; between lower and upper, with the required value at its centre, is C, moderate; between upper and inferior is D, minor inferior; above the inferior boundary is E, inferior. A ship rated D for three consecutive years or E for one year must develop a plan of corrective actions to achieve the required CII, incorporate it in a revised SEEMP Part III, submit the revised SEEMP for verification no later than one month after reporting the attained CII, and then carry the actions out. The Statement of Compliance is issued after the revised plan has been verified.
Q4. Describe the annual CII reporting and certification cycle.
The ship collects fuel consumption, distance and hours-under-way data throughout the calendar year according to its SEEMP Part II. Within three months of the year end it reports the aggregated data and the attained annual operational CII to its Administration or recognized organization. The Administration or recognized organization verifies the data and the CII, determines the rating and issues the Statement of Compliance for fuel oil consumption reporting and operational carbon intensity rating no later than five months from the beginning of the year; the Statement is valid for that year and the first five months of the next. Not later than one month after issuing the Statements the Administration transfers the data to the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database. Ships triggered into corrective action submit the revised SEEMP within one month of reporting.
Q5. Is carbon sequestration part of the CII mechanism?
No. Carbon sequestration — the capture and long-term storage of atmospheric CO2 in geological formations, soils or the ocean, or by direct air capture — is a climate-mitigation concept outside the ship. The CII counts only the CO2 produced by burning fuel on board, through fixed carbon factors, divided by transport work; nothing captured ashore or by the ocean enters the formula or the rating. The question is asked to see whether a candidate can separate the regulatory metric from the wider decarbonisation vocabulary.

Why this block exists

The source notes place a one-page oral-question summary of carbon sequestration immediately after CII. It is retained here because examiners do ask it, but it must be understood as decarbonisation vocabulary, not shipboard regulation: nothing in this block changes a CII value, a rating or a Statement of Compliance.

Definition and recognised routes

Carbon sequestration is the capture and long-term storage of atmospheric or point-source CO2. The routes recognised in authoritative climate-science taxonomies are: geological sequestration — injection into depleted oil and gas reservoirs, unmineable coal seams or deep saline aquifers; soil sequestration — storage as soil organic carbon through land-management practice; ocean sequestration — the ocean already absorbs roughly a quarter of the CO2 released by fossil-fuel burning and other human activities, and deliberate enhancement is under study; and direct air capture — chemical capture from ambient air followed by storage. Biofuel use and conversion of CO2 to graphite or graphene, which the source lists alongside these, are carbon-utilisation or emissions-avoidance routes rather than sequestration, and are not presented here as equivalent methods.

Maritime relevance

For shipping the live engineering link is onboard carbon capture and storage — capturing CO2 from the exhaust for later discharge ashore to a geological store. Under the current CII guidelines captured CO2 is not deducted from the attained CII; any future credit would require amendment of regulation 28 and the G1 guidelines. A Chief Engineer should be able to say that clearly.

📖 Definition

The IMO Data Collection System (regulation 27, originally regulation 22A under MEPC.278(70)) requires every ship of 5,000 GT and above engaged in international voyages to collect, for each calendar year from 2019, the data in Appendix IX — fuel consumption by fuel type and the method used (since the 1 August 2025 enhanced-granularity amendment, also per consumer type and while not under way), total distance travelled over ground, hours under way, onshore power, and for CII ships the transport work and CII values, together with the ship’s technical particulars — according to the methodology in SEEMP Part II, and to report the aggregated data to its Administration within three months of the year end. The Administration verifies the report, issues the Statement of Compliance (Appendix X) no later than five months into the year, and transfers the data to the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database within a month. That database is a module of GISIS, the Global Integrated Shipping Information System through which Administrations supply, and the public can access, data required under IMO instruments.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • The DCS is the measurement layer under CII: the same fuel, distance and hours figures produce the attained CII (Topic 3). An error in a bunker delivery note or flow-meter log is an error in the rating.
  • The Chief Engineer is the custodian of two of the three fuel-measurement methods (flow meters and tank monitoring) and of the BDN file, and must apply the method that SEEMP Part II says the ship uses — not the convenient one.
  • The Statement of Compliance is a PSC document from June each year; a missing or expired Statement is a detainable deficiency under the Annex VI survey and certification regime.
  • GISIS is where a Chief Engineer can check, for example, port reception facility details, the status of a convention for a flag, or an Administration’s contact points — a practical tool, not only a reporting destination.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the IMO Data Collection System and to which ships does it apply?
The Data Collection System under regulation 27 of MARPOL Annex VI, introduced by resolution MEPC.278(70) as regulation 22A and in force since 1 March 2018, requires each ship of 5,000 gross tonnage and above engaged in international voyages to collect, for every calendar year from 2019, the data in Appendix IX — fuel oil consumption by fuel type with the measurement method and, since the enhanced-granularity amendment in force on 1 August 2025, per consumer type and while not under way; total distance travelled over ground; hours under way; onshore power; the transport work and CII values for CII ships; and the ship’s technical characteristics — according to the methodology described in Part II of its SEEMP, and to report the aggregated data to its Administration or recognized organization within three months of the end of the year.
Q2. What are the three fuel-measurement methods and who decides which the ship uses?
The 2024 SEEMP guidelines, MEPC.395(82) as amended, recognise three methods: bunker delivery notes combined with tank soundings; flow meters on the fuel systems; and bunker fuel tank monitoring on board. The method, the equipment, its calibration and the quality-control procedure are written into SEEMP Part II, which the Administration or recognized organization verifies with a Confirmation of Compliance. The ship must use the method in its plan; a change of method is a change to the plan and must be recorded and re-verified.
Q3. Describe the Statement of Compliance — who issues it, when, and for how long is it valid?
On receipt of the ship’s annual report the Administration or recognized organization determines whether the data was collected and reported in accordance with regulation 27 and the SEEMP and, since reporting year 2023, verifies the attained CII and determines the rating. It then issues the Statement of Compliance for fuel oil consumption reporting and operational carbon intensity rating, in the form of Appendix X, no later than five months from the beginning of the calendar year. Under regulation 9.12 the Statement is valid for the calendar year in which it is issued and for the first five months of the following year, and must be kept on board for at least five years.
Q4. What happens to the data after the Statement is issued?
Not later than one month after issuing the Statements of Compliance the Administration transfers the Appendix IX data for its ships to the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database, a module of GISIS, electronically in the standard format. The Secretary-General maintains the database in anonymised form so that no individual ship can be identified, gives Parties access to the anonymised data for analysis, and produces an annual summary report for the Marine Environment Protection Committee. The 2019 data set from this process was used to build the CII reference lines. Since 1 August 2025 a company may ask for its own ships’ reports to be published in non-anonymised form (regulation 27.15, MEPC.385(81)). From 1 September 2027, under MEPC.407(84) adopted at MEPC 84, Administrations will also see their own ships’ full history and Parties will have a non-anonymised database for analysis; until then the anonymised public database stands.
Q5. What is GISIS and how is it accessed?
GISIS, the Global Integrated Shipping Information System, is the web-based platform developed and maintained by the IMO Secretariat for the collection, processing and sharing of data that Member States and others supply under IMO instruments. It is organised in modules such as maritime security, marine casualties, port reception facilities, the global SAR plan, ship fuel oil consumption, MARPOL Annex VI, survey and certification and cargoes. It has three access levels: a public area available after registering an IMO Web Account, an IMO Members area for Administrations with upload and compliance functions, and a Secretariat area for system administration.

Situation

A 5,500 TEU container ship uses Method 2, mass flow meters on the main and auxiliary engine fuel lines, as written in SEEMP Part II. In December the Chief Engineer reconciles twelve months of flow-meter totals against bunker delivery notes and tank soundings and finds the meters have under-read by about 2.4 per cent since a sensor replacement in May.

Why it matters

The DCS report due by 31 March is built on the flow-meter figures; the attained CII for the year, and therefore the rating on the Statement of Compliance, moves with them. Reporting the raw figure would understate CO2 by 2.4 per cent — enough to shift a ship near a band boundary — and the verifier’s cross-check against BDNs under MEPC.292(71) would expose the gap.

What the Chief Engineer does

Quantifies the drift period and magnitude from the May calibration record; applies the correction described in the SEEMP Part II quality-control procedure, or reverts to Method 1 for the affected months if the plan provides for it; documents the correction with the supporting soundings and BDNs; informs the company so the report submitted to the Administration carries the corrected totals and an explanatory note; and schedules recalibration. The point for the examiner is that data integrity under the DCS is a Chief Engineer responsibility, and the SEEMP Part II procedure, not improvisation, is the authority for how a discrepancy is handled.

EU MRV — Scope, Gases, Verification and the Comparison with IMO DCS

Reg. (EU) 2015/757 · CO2+CH4+N2O · Accredited Verifier · 31 March Uday pp. 574–575 Examiner: Nair ⭐ High Frequency
🔎 Verification note: Three material corrections and one currency update. (1) Scope has widened. The source says ‘5,000 GT and above’. Regulation (EU) 2015/757 originally applied to ships above 5,000 GT; since 1 January 2025 it also applies to offshore ships and general cargo ships of 400 GT and above but below 5,000 GT (Regulation (EU) 2023/957). (2) Gases. The source lists CO2, CH4 and N2O without a date; MRV covered CO2 only for reporting years 2018–2023 and added methane and nitrous oxide from 1 January 2024. (3) Deadlines. The source’s ‘31 April 2019’ does not exist; the first emissions report was due by 30 April 2019, and from reporting year 2024 the deadline is 31 March. The IMO DCS deadline in the comparison table (31 March 2020) was the first cycle and recurs every year. Currency: since 2024 the MRV data is also the basis for the inclusion of maritime transport in the EU Emissions Trading System and for FuelEU Maritime compliance; those regimes are noted for context only and are not covered here. Do not conflate: EU MRV and IMO DCS are both tank-to-wake fuel-based reporting systems; FuelEU Maritime and the draft IMO GHG fuel intensity standard are well-to-wake and belong to Part 2 Topic 9 and Part 22 Topic 3.
📖 Definition

The EU Monitoring, Reporting and Verification Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2015/757 as amended by Regulation (EU) 2023/957, is European Union law requiring companies operating ships above 5,000 GT — and, from 2025, offshore and general cargo ships of 400 GT and above — on voyages to, from or between ports under the jurisdiction of an EU/EEA Member State to monitor, on a per-voyage and annual basis, fuel consumption, CO2 and (from 2024) CH4 and N2O emissions, distance, time at sea and cargo carried; to have the monitoring plan assessed and the annual emissions report verified by an accredited verifier; to submit the verified report by 31 March; and to carry a document of compliance. The Commission publishes the per-ship data. It is a regional legal regime that runs in parallel with, and is distinct from, the IMO Data Collection System.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • A ship trading to Europe reports the same fuel twice under two laws — DCS to the flag, MRV to the EU — with different scopes, different capacity measures and different verifiers. The Chief Engineer’s fuel records must satisfy both.
  • MRV is per-voyage and includes cargo carried; the auditor can trace a single voyage’s fuel, distance and cargo. Data discipline that is adequate for the DCS annual aggregate may not survive an MRV verification.
  • Since 2024 the MRV figures drive EU ETS allowance surrender and FuelEU Maritime compliance balances — the emissions report has a direct cost attached.
  • Examiners use the DCS-versus-MRV comparison to test whether a candidate can keep two similar regimes separate under questioning.

🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. What is the EU MRV Regulation and to which ships and voyages does it apply?
Regulation (EU) 2015/757, in force since 1 July 2015 and amended by Regulation (EU) 2023/957, requires companies to monitor, report and have verified the greenhouse gas emissions of ships on voyages to, from or between ports under the jurisdiction of an EU or EEA Member State, including emissions in those ports. It applied from 1 January 2018 to ships above 5,000 gross tonnage and, from 1 January 2025, also to offshore ships and general cargo ships of 400 gross tonnage and above but below 5,000. Warships, naval auxiliaries, fish-catching and fish-processing ships, wooden ships of primitive build, ships not propelled by mechanical means and government ships on non-commercial service are excluded.
Q2. Which gases and parameters are monitored, and how did this change in 2024?
From 2018 to 2023 the regulation covered carbon dioxide only. Regulation (EU) 2023/957 added methane and nitrous oxide from 1 January 2024, with monitoring plans updated by 1 April 2024. For each voyage and for the year the company monitors fuel consumed by type, emissions of each gas using emission factors, distance travelled, time at sea, cargo carried and transport work, and derives energy-efficiency indicators such as fuel consumption and emissions per distance and per transport work.
Q3. Explain the verification arrangements and the document of compliance.
The monitoring plan is assessed, and the annual emissions report verified, by an independent verifier accredited for maritime MRV by a national accreditation body under Regulation (EC) 765/2008; classification societies may act only if so accredited. The verified report is submitted through THETIS-MRV by 31 March following the reporting year, previously 30 April. Where the report is satisfactory the verifier issues a document of compliance for the ship, valid for eighteen months after the end of the reporting period, which must be carried on board and may be checked by Member State authorities.
Q4. Compare the IMO Data Collection System with EU MRV.
Both are tank-to-wake reporting systems built on the ship’s fuel records, but they differ in five respects. Legal basis: the DCS is regulation 27 of MARPOL Annex VI enforced by the flag State; MRV is an EU regulation enforced by Member States and the Commission. Scope: the DCS covers all voyages of ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above; MRV covers only EU and EEA-related voyages but now extends to 400 gross tonnage for offshore and general cargo ships. Granularity: the DCS reports annual totals with deadweight as the capacity; MRV reports per voyage with actual cargo carried and transport work. Gases: the DCS feeds a CO2 metric through fixed carbon factors; MRV covers carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide since 2024. Verification and output: the DCS is verified by the Administration or recognized organization and produces a Statement of Compliance and an anonymised IMO database; MRV is verified by an accredited verifier and produces a verified emissions report, a document of compliance and per-ship publication, and since 2024 the MRV figure is the basis for EU ETS allowance surrender.
Q5. What are the deadlines in each system?
Under the DCS the ship reports within three months of the calendar year end, in practice by 31 March, and the Administration issues the Statement of Compliance no later than five months from the start of the year, by 31 May, transferring the data to the IMO database within a further month. Under MRV the verified emissions report is due by 31 March from reporting year 2024, having been 30 April for the first reports in 2019, and the Commission publishes the data by 30 June.

Situation

A 115,000 DWT Aframax trades Primorsk–Rotterdam–Houston–Fos. Her flag RO verifies the DCS; a Dutch accredited verifier handles MRV. In the ETS phase-in the owner is surrendering allowances on the MRV figure and the charterer is disputing the voyage split.

Where the regimes diverge

The DCS wants one annual total per fuel type for every voyage, with deadweight as capacity. MRV wants each EU-related leg separately — Primorsk to Rotterdam counts, Rotterdam to Houston counts as a departure from an EEA port, Houston to Fos counts as an arrival — with the cargo carried on each, and from 2024 with methane and nitrous oxide emission factors applied. Port stays in Rotterdam and Fos are MRV-reportable; the Houston stay is not.

What the Chief Engineer sets up

One measurement method, fixed in both SEEMP Part II and the MRV monitoring plan, so the verifiers see the same meter and the same BDNs; a voyage log that records fuel ROB and consumption at every arrival and departure, not only at bunkering; the fuel type and sulphur grade per tank at each boundary, because the carbon and methane factors differ; and a reconciliation at month end between flow meters and soundings. With that in place the annual DCS aggregate is a sum of the voyage records, the MRV report is a filter of them, and the ETS and charter-party disputes are settled from the same evidence.
