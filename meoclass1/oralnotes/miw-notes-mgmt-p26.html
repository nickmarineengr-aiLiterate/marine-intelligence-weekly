📖 Definition

In propulsion, the wake is the water around the after body that the hull drags along with it, so that it reaches the propeller slower than the ship moves. Its size is measured by the Taylor wake fraction w = (V − VA)/V, where V is ship speed and VA the speed of advance — the mean speed of water arriving at the propeller disc. The propeller blades work as lifting sections: the face (aft, pressure side) carries higher pressure than the back (forward, suction side), and that pressure difference is the thrust. Lift also sheds energy the propeller cannot use: a tip vortex where water leaks round each blade tip, a hub vortex where root vorticity gathers behind the boss, and swirl — rotation left in the slipstream by the shaft torque. Energy-saving devices (ESDs) are fixed or free-running appendages that recover part of these losses: wake-equalising devices ahead of the propeller make the inflow more uniform (Schneekluth WED); pre-swirl devices give the inflow a counter-rotation (pre-swirl stator, Becker Mewis Duct); hub-vortex devices on the boss cap weaken the hub vortex (PBCF, ECO-Cap); and post-propeller devices on or behind the rudder recover slipstream swirl and hub-vortex energy (rudder thrust fins, twisted leading-edge rudders, rudder bulbs, the Grim vane wheel).

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Simon's energy-efficiency questions name the devices. The insert at the end of this source lists 'wake equalizing devices, pre-swirl devices, post-swirl devices' and, in three separate papers, 'rudder thrust fins, asymmetric rudders, rudder bulbs, Grim vane wheels'. The candidate must explain each mechanism and sort them correctly.
  • A Chief Engineer is asked by the owner whether a retrofit duct or boss-cap fin will 'save 8%'. The honest answer depends on the hull, the wake, the propeller loading and the maker's evidence, and on the fact that savings of different devices do not add up.
  • Welded ESDs are structural additions to the stern. Cracked or lost fins can strike the propeller, so the Chief Engineer owns their inspection at every dry-docking and must not accept that 'class does not survey them'.
  • Wake fraction, hull efficiency and wake non-uniformity explain cavitation, vibration and propeller design choices that the engine room lives with every day.

🌊 The Propulsion Wake — What the Propeller Actually Sees

Two meanings of 'wake'. The visible trail behind a ship — a turbulent track plus the Kelvin wave pattern, whose classical half-angle in deep water is about 19.5° — is also called a wake, and the source lists it ('turbulent wake, Kelvin wake'). It is not the wake used in propulsion. The propulsion wake is the slowed water around the after body that the propeller works in.

Wake componentCauseNote
Potential (streamline) wakeStreamlines close in round the stern and the flow slowsPresent even in an ideal fluid
Frictional (boundary-layer) wakeWater dragged along by hull skin frictionUsually the largest component on full single-screw ships
Wave wakeWhether a crest or a trough of the stern wave system sits over the propellerSmall; can be positive or negative

Hull form governs all three — it is a cause, not a component. On a single-screw ship the upper blades work closest to the hull, so the inflow velocity is lowest near the 12 o'clock position and higher toward the bottom of the disc; each blade meets a changing inflow as it turns. This non-uniformity drives cavitation, propeller-excited pressure pulses and vibration.

  • 'Less w is better' is wrong as a rule. A high wake raises hull efficiency ηH (about 1.1–1.4 for single-screw ships, highest for full forms, per MAN) but lowers the propeller's open-water efficiency, so overall propulsive efficiency is usually unchanged or slightly worse.

  • CB and wake. 🌀 Blade Sections, Lift and the Vortices That Waste Energy

How a blade section makes lift. A blade section set at an angle of attack, usually with camber, turns the water passing it. This sets up a circulation: flow speeds up over the back and slows on the face. By Bernoulli the back (suction side) is at low pressure and the face (pressure side) at higher pressure; the integrated pressure difference is the lift, whose forward component is the thrust. The popular explanation that 'the path over the top is longer, so the water must go faster' is a myth — the two flows do not have to meet at the trailing edge, and flat or symmetric sections at an angle of attack also lift.

SurfaceFacesPressure (ahead running)
FaceAftPressure side — high (discharge)
BackForwardSuction side — low

Thrust acts from face toward back, i.e. forward. Astern, the pressure roles reverse but the names face and back stay with the geometry.

  • Tip vortex. Because the face is at higher pressure than the back, water leaks round each blade tip from face to back and rolls up into a trailing tip vortex.
  • Hub vortex. Vorticity shed at the blade roots gathers behind the boss into a single rotating hub vortex. Its low-pressure core sucks on the boss cap (boss drag, reducing net thrust), carries away rotational energy and can cavitate, eroding downstream parts.
  • Swirl (rotational loss). The torque that turns the propeller also sets the slipstream rotating. That rotational energy does no propulsive work. Propeller losses are usually grouped as axial (normally the largest), rotational and frictional; no fixed percentage split is taught.
  • Propeller and ESD developments aim to reduce these losses so that more of the delivered power becomes useful thrust — no propeller converts all of it.
🎛️ Wake Conditioning — The Idea Behind the Devices

Wake (flow) conditioning means modifying the flow ahead of the propeller to suit it: making the wake more uniform, accelerating the slow inflow (usually the upper part of the disc), and often adding a counter-swirl opposite to the propeller's rotation. The best route is at design stage, through the stern form (Part 25 Topic 2); appendages are the retrofit route.

Who benefits. Full-form, high-wake ships — tankers and bulk carriers with heavily loaded propellers — benefit most, because their inflow is slow and uneven. The devices do not work 'by lowering w': pre-propeller devices equalise and pre-swirl the inflow; post-propeller devices recover swirl and hub-vortex energy left in the slipstream.

🔧 Wake-Equalising Duct (Schneekluth WED)

A wake-equalising duct is a fixed hull appendage ahead of the propeller that redirects and accelerates the slow wake flow so that the propeller sees a more uniform, more axial inflow. It is a corrective device: most useful where the stern produces a poor wake, of little value on a hull already designed for good flow.

  • Arrangement. Two half-ring ducts of aerofoil section, one on each side of the stern, ahead of the upper half of the propeller disc (above the shaft line).
  • Function. The ducts gather and accelerate flow into the upper half of the disc, where the wake is heaviest, making the wake field more uniform and cutting propeller-excited pressure fluctuations.
  • Counter-swirl. The half-ducts are set at a slight asymmetric angle, giving the inflow a small rotation opposite to the propeller's — but the WED is primarily a wake-equalising device, not a pre-swirl device.
  • Attributed figures. Schneekluth reports fuel savings of up to 12%; its own typical range for full-form ships (CB above 0.7) is about 3–8%, and ABS gives 0–5% as an advisory range for this class of device. Schneekluth also claims up to 50% vibration reduction (a maker figure, sometimes stated for duct and spoilers together). None of these is a universal saving.
🔩 Becker Mewis Duct — Pre-Duct plus Pre-Swirl Fins

The Becker Mewis Duct is not simply a WED variant. It combines two separate principles in one fixed structure ahead of the propeller: a pre-duct, mounted off-centre with its axis above the shaft axis, which straightens and accelerates the hull wake into the propeller; and an integrated pre-swirl fin system inside the duct, which gives the inflow a counter-rotation so less rotational energy is left in the slipstream. Becker's figure labels — upper bracket, duct, customised fins, propeller shaft, hull — match the source's pasted diagram. Since 2018 Becker has offered a semi-ring rather than a full ring to reduce drag.

  • Application. Slow, full-form ships with heavily loaded propellers — tankers and bulk carriers below about 20 knots (designers' guidance: thrust-loading coefficient CTh above about 1).
  • Attributed figures. Becker quotes up to 8–10% depending on hull–propeller interaction; the designers reported a mean of about 6.5% over 35 model tests and 6.5% at full scale on one 57,000 dwt Supramax bulker; an independent in-service study of one oil tanker found about 3.5–5% against a sister ship.
  • Structure. A welded structural addition, installed at dry-docking; Becker states the design meets classification-society strength and stiffness requirements.
⚙️ Pre-Swirl Stator (PSS)

A pre-swirl stator is a set of fixed fins on the stern boss just ahead of the propeller, often fitted asymmetrically (more fins on one side). The fins give the inflow a swirl opposite to the propeller's rotation; the propeller removes that swirl, so the slipstream leaving it has less rotation and lower rotational energy loss, and the blade angles of attack improve. The fins themselves have drag and save nothing alone — the saving is a stator–propeller interaction, and the propeller is normally designed to work behind the stator. A PSS does not make the inflow 'uniform' in the WED sense.

🧢 Hub-Vortex Devices — PBCF and ECO-Cap

Propeller Boss Cap Fins (PBCF) replace the plain boss cap with a cap carrying small fins — one per blade in the original design — that rotate with the propeller. The fins straighten the swirling root flow so the hub vortex is weakened or broken up; part of its rotational energy is recovered as a little extra thrust and reduced shaft torque, and the low-pressure suction on the cap (boss drag) falls. Model tests showed PBCF removing hub-vortex cavitation and reducing propeller noise by about 3–6 dB in some frequency bands. The fins do not 'obstruct' the flow.

ECO-Cap is Nakashima Propeller's own hub-cap-with-fins device. It works on the same broad principle as PBCF — suppressing the hub vortex — but it is a different design, not the same product. Nakashima and NMRI model tests report up to about 4.7% propulsion-efficiency gain, depending on how strong the hub vortex was on the original propeller (maker and model data).

🚢 Post-Propeller Devices on and Behind the Rudder

The source heads these 'Rudder Improvements', and the exam questions say 'rudder-efficiency improvement devices'. They are really post-propeller energy-saving devices: they recover energy lost in the propeller slipstream (swirl and hub vortex) and so improve propulsive efficiency, not rudder steering. 🎓 CE Oral Tip (Simon): Simon asks 'What is a wake equalising duct, and what else would you fit?' Answer in four moves. One: the wake — water dragged by the hull, slowest at the top of the disc on a single-screw ship; w = (V − VA)/V with VA the speed of advance, and the problem is non-uniformity, not the number. Two: the losses — tip vortex, hub vortex and slipstream swirl. Three: sort the devices by mechanism — wake-equalising (Schneekluth WED), pre-swirl (stator, Mewis Duct), hub-vortex (PBCF, ECO-Cap), post-propeller (rudder fins, twisted rudder, rudder bulb, Grim wheel) — and say a CRP is a propeller system, not a post-swirl device. Four: give any saving only with its owner — 'Schneekluth reports up to 12%, typically 3–8% on full forms; ABS gives 2–6% for pre-swirl devices' — and say savings do not add. Three answers fail candidates: that VA is propeller speed, that a WED or a stator needs no class attention, and that 'a duct saves 12%'.
🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. Define the wake fraction and explain its effect on propulsive efficiency.
The Taylor wake fraction is w equals V minus V A, divided by V, where V is ship speed and V A is the speed of advance, the mean speed of water arriving at the propeller disc, so V A equals V times one minus w. Nominal wake is measured without the propeller; effective wake is inferred with the propeller working and is the value used for powering. For single-screw ships w is typically about 0.20 to 0.45, rising with block coefficient; twin-screw ships with open sterns are much lower. A higher wake raises hull efficiency, one minus t over one minus w, but lowers the propeller's open-water efficiency, so propulsive efficiency is not generally improved. The practical problem of high-wake full-form ships is a non-uniform wake field, which causes cavitation, pressure pulses and vibration.
Q2. Explain the tip vortex and the hub vortex and the energy they waste.
A propeller blade works as a lifting section: at an angle of attack it turns the flow and sets up circulation, so the face is at higher pressure than the back. At the tip, water leaks round from face to back and rolls up into a tip vortex. At the roots, the shed vorticity of all the blades gathers behind the boss into a single hub vortex, whose low-pressure core sucks on the boss cap, carries away rotational energy and can cavitate. In addition the shaft torque leaves the whole slipstream rotating. This rotational energy does no propulsive work; pre-swirl devices, boss-cap fins, post-propeller devices and contra-rotating propellers all aim to recover part of it.
Q3. Describe a wake equalising duct and a Becker Mewis Duct and state how they differ.
A Schneekluth wake equalising duct is two half-ring ducts of aerofoil section fitted on each side of the stern ahead of the upper half of the propeller disc. It accelerates the slow wake flow into the upper disc, making the wake more uniform and reducing pressure fluctuations; a slight asymmetric setting adds a small counter-swirl. The Becker Mewis Duct combines a pre-duct, mounted off-centre above the shaft axis to straighten and accelerate the wake, with pre-swirl fins inside the duct that counter-rotate the inflow; it is a pre-swirl device, not just a WED, and is aimed at slow full-form ships with heavily loaded propellers. Savings are maker- and ship-specific: Schneekluth reports up to 12 per cent but typically 3 to 8 per cent on full forms; Becker quotes up to 8 to 10 per cent, and an independent tanker study found about 3.5 to 5 per cent. Both are welded structural additions reviewed and surveyed by class as a structural alteration.
Q4. Explain how a pre-swirl stator works and what the Chief Engineer must watch.
A pre-swirl stator is a set of fixed fins on the stern boss just ahead of the propeller, often more on one side than the other. The fins give the inflow a swirl opposite to the propeller's rotation; the propeller takes that swirl out, so the slipstream has less rotation and less rotational energy loss, and the blade angles of attack improve. The fins themselves add drag; the gain comes only from the stator and propeller working together, and the propeller is designed to suit. ABS gives 2 to 6 per cent as an advisory range for pre-swirl devices, and DSME reported about 4 per cent on its first VLCC sea trial. The Chief Engineer must not treat it as maintenance-free: the fins and welds are fatigue-loaded, cracked fins have failed in service and a lost fin can strike the propeller, so they are inspected at every dry-docking.
Q5. Explain the working of propeller boss cap fins and compare ECO-Cap.
The hub vortex forms when root vorticity from the blades gathers behind the boss; its low-pressure core causes boss drag, carries away rotational energy and can cavitate. Propeller Boss Cap Fins replace the plain cap with a cap carrying small fins, one per blade, rotating with the propeller. They straighten the swirling root flow so the hub vortex is weakened, recovering a little thrust and reducing shaft torque; model tests showed hub-vortex cavitation removed and noise reduced by about 3 to 6 decibels in some bands. Retrofit is a cap exchange with no hull modification. MOL quotes 3 to 5 per cent fuel saving and one independent Aframax trial reported about 4 per cent. ECO-Cap is Nakashima Propeller's own hub-cap-with-fins device on the same broad principle but a different design; model tests show up to about 4.7 per cent depending on the original hub vortex strength.
Q6. Discuss rudder thrust fins, asymmetric rudders, rudder bulbs and Grim vane wheels.
All four are post-propeller devices that recover energy left in the propeller slipstream. Rudder thrust fins are aerofoil fins on the rudder at about hub height, set to the swirling slipstream so part of its rotation becomes forward thrust; Kawasaki claims about 2 to 7 per cent for its RBS-F, which is a rudder bulb with fins. An asymmetric or twisted leading-edge rudder has its leading edge offset in opposite directions above and below the propeller axis to meet the rotating flow, reducing drag and leading-edge cavitation; Damen Marine Components markets one version as ART. A rudder bulb sits in line behind the propeller hub, weakening the hub vortex and preventing hub-vortex erosion of the rudder; Becker claims up to about 1.5 per cent for the bulb alone. The Grim vane wheel is a free-running wheel behind and larger than the propeller, whose inner part acts as a turbine and outer part as a propeller, adding thrust for no extra shaft power; it was patented in 1966 but in-service damage limited its use. Their savings must not be added together.

🧠 Memory Box
  • w = (V − VA)/V · VA = SPEED OF ADVANCE (not propeller speed) · nominal = no propeller, effective = propeller working.
  • ηH = (1 − t)/(1 − w) rises with w — high wake is not 'bad'; NON-UNIFORMITY is the problem (cavitation, pulses, vibration).
  • Lift = angle of attack + circulation → low pressure on BACK (suction), high on FACE (pressure). 'Longer path' is a myth.
  • Losses: TIP vortex (face→back leakage) · HUB vortex (root vorticity behind boss) · SWIRL (slipstream rotation).
  • Sort by mechanism: WAKE-EQUALISING = Schneekluth WED · PRE-SWIRL = stator, Mewis Duct · HUB-VORTEX = PBCF, ECO-Cap · POST-PROPELLER = rudder fins, twisted rudder, rudder bulb, Grim wheel · CRP = propeller system (T2).

  • Savings belong to their owner and never add up — quote the maker or class source, never a bare percentage.

Situation (illustrative)

A superintendent forwards two vendor proposals for a single-screw bulk carrier going into dry-dock: a pre-duct with pre-swirl fins, and boss-cap fins on the existing propeller. The cover note says 'about 8% plus 5%, so 13% fuel saving' and asks the Chief Engineer for comments before the yard order.

What the Chief Engineer's reply covers

Suitability. The ship is slow, full-form and heavily loaded — the class of hull where wake non-uniformity is greatest and where duct-type pre-swirl devices are aimed. The hub-vortex device addresses a different loss, at the boss. Expectations. Each vendor figure is a maker figure for other ships; the evidence to ask for is model-test or CFD work on this hull and propeller, and any independent in-service data. The two savings must not be added: both devices act partly on the same rotational energy, and Everllence (formerly MAN Energy Solutions) cautions that combined ESD savings above about 10% are unlikely. Class and EEDI/EEXI. The duct is a welded structural addition: drawings, strength and fatigue calculations and the installation go to the classification society as a structural alteration; the boss-cap fins come within the propeller approval. Any claimed EEXI benefit is evidenced to the verifier through the speed–power curve. Through-life care. Fins and welds are fatigue-loaded: inspection at every dry-docking goes into the planned-maintenance system, with propeller polishing and boss-cap fin inspection together. Measurement. Agree a before-and-after method — same draught, speed and weather filter — so the saving can be verified rather than assumed.

The lesson for the examiner

The candidate who says 'the duct saves 8%' fails; the candidate who explains which loss each device attacks, who owns the figure, why savings do not add, and what class and the planned-maintenance system must cover, passes.

📖 Definition

High-efficiency propellers are propeller arrangements that reduce the propeller's own losses — axial, rotational and tip-vortex — compared with a conventional open propeller. Four are named in the source's exam question: the ducted (Kort) propeller, where a fixed duct of hydrofoil section around the propeller adds thrust and cuts tip leakage on heavily loaded propellers; the contra-rotating propeller (CRP), where a second, smaller propeller turning the opposite way recovers the swirl of the first and shares its load; tip-modified propellers such as the Kappel (blade tip curved smoothly toward the suction side) and the CLT (Contracted and Loaded Tip, with pressure-side end plates); and azimuth propulsion, where the propeller unit turns about a vertical axis so that thrust can be directed in any horizontal direction — mechanically through L- or Z-drive gearing, or electrically with the motor inside an underwater pod, as in ABB's Azipod.

⚓ Why It Matters (CE Perspective)
  • Simon asks this directly. The insert at the end of the source prints, from three papers, 'Discuss any three of the following high-efficiency propellers with its merits and demerits: ducted propellers, Kappel propellers, contra-rotating propellers, azimuth propulsion.'
  • Each arrangement changes what the engine room maintains: duct clearance and erosion, coaxial shafts and seals, end plates and tips, pod bearings, seals and high-voltage converters.
  • Maker claims for these propulsors are large and easily repeated; the examiner listens for whether the candidate knows the mechanism, the baseline and the ship type behind each figure.
  • Diesel-electric podded ships are now common in cruise, ferry, icebreaking and Arctic LNG trades — a Class 1 engineer will meet them.

🌀 Ducted (Kort) Propeller

A Kort nozzle is a fixed, non-rotating annular duct of hydrofoil section around the propeller. The blade tips run with a small clearance at the duct's narrowest section, the throat, which is at the propeller; the inlet is much wider and the duct opens out again aft of the propeller.

MechanismExplanation
Duct thrustThe propeller's suction, helped by the circulation round the duct's foil section, draws more water through the throat at higher speed. That circulation leaves low pressure on the duct's inner forward surface, so the duct itself is pushed forward and produces part of the total thrust
Momentum efficiencyA larger mass of water is accelerated by a smaller velocity increment — more efficient when the propeller is heavily loaded
Tip-loss reductionThe close-fitting duct restricts leakage round the blade tips from face to back, reducing tip-vortex loss — only if the tip clearance is kept small
What is wrong in the source'Q = A × V, area reduces so velocity increases' gets cause and effect backwards: continuity only relates area and velocity once the flow is fixed, and an unpowered duct towed through water only adds drag
  • Where it pays: heavily loaded propellers at low speed — tugs at bollard pull, trawlers towing, push boats, slow full-form ships, and cases where propeller diameter is restricted.
  • Where it does not: as ship speed rises, duct drag grows and the advantage disappears, so fast ships do not use accelerating ducts.
  • Attributed figure: Wärtsilä states its high-efficiency nozzle can raise bollard pull by up to about 30% at low vessel speeds (maker claim).
  • Practical point: the benefit depends on keeping the tip clearance small, so blade-tip and duct-interior condition belong in the dry-dock checks (Part 20 Topic 3).
🔄 Contra-Rotating Propellers (CRP)

A contra-rotating propeller has two propellers on the same axis turning in opposite directions. A single propeller leaves swirl — rotational energy — in its slipstream. In a CRP the aft propeller, turning the other way, recovers most of the forward propeller's swirl as extra thrust, and splitting the load between two propellers lowers the blade loading of each. The hull wake is not changed by this: the source's 'wake is zero' is wrong.

Design pointReason
Aft propeller smallerKeeps its disc inside the contracted slipstream of the forward propeller, clear of its tip vortices (ABB guidance: about 85–92% of the forward diameter at an equal power split)
Different blade numbersBlades do not all cross at the same instant, so interaction pulses spread over several frequencies — lower peak shaft vibration, hull pressure pulses and noise
Lower blade loadingDelays cavitation; smaller diameters give more hull clearance
MeritsDemerits
Higher propulsive efficiency through swirl recovery and lower blade loadingCoaxial inner and outer shafts, contra-rotating gearbox, inter-shaft bearings and more complex stern-tube seals
More power absorbed within a limited diameterHigher first cost (stated by the maker itself)

Attributed figures — no universal value. Japanese full-scale results reported up to about 15% (IHI bulk carrier Juno, 1991; MHI VLCC, 1994 — maker data); makers today typically claim about 10%; and ABB notes that for a moderately loaded single-screw ship with an already optimised propeller the gain may be small, even marginal. A hybrid layout — a shaft propeller ahead of a contra-rotating pod (ABB CRP Azipod) — avoids the coaxial shafting.

🪽 Tip-Modified Propellers — Kappel and CLT

Both designs attack the tip-vortex loss (Topic 1) so that the blade can carry load nearer the tip. They are the two main commercial designs, not the only ones: TVF (CLT's predecessor), various end-plate, winglet and tip-rake propellers also exist.

Why the CLT plate is described as 'not loaded'. On a conventional blade, water leaks round the tip from the high-pressure face to the low-pressure back, so the load must fall to zero at the tip and the leakage feeds the tip vortex. The CLT end plate acts as a fence that greatly reduces this leakage, which is what lets the tip section carry load. The plate itself is aligned with the slipstream to keep its drag low and carries only small incidental forces. No independent full-scale efficiency figure was found for either Kappel or CLT; all figures above are maker or designer figures.

🧭 Azimuth Propulsion — Mechanical L- and Z-Drives

An azimuth thruster is a propeller unit on a steerable housing below the hull that can be turned about a vertical axis to direct its thrust in any horizontal direction. Where azimuth units are the main propulsion, they steer the ship by vectoring thrust, most can turn through 360°, and a conventional rudder is normally not fitted; ships with azimuth units only as auxiliary or dynamic-positioning thrusters keep their rudder. Full thrust can be pointed in any direction even at low or zero speed, when a rudder is ineffective — hence the manoeuvrability.

Mechanical typePower pathTypical prime mover
L-driveVertical input shaft down to the propeller through one right-angle bevel-gear setVertically mounted electric motor above the unit
Z-driveHorizontal input shaft, then down, through two right-angle bevel-gear sets (upper and lower)Diesel engine (with clutch) or electric motor

In both, the gears and shafts carry power to the propeller; a separate slewing (steering) drive, usually hydraulic or electric motors on a slewing ring, turns the underwater unit. The source's 'the outboard unit is rotated using gears and shafts' mixes the two functions.

⚡ Electric Podded Propulsion — Azipod

In a podded propulsor an AC electric motor is housed in the underwater pod and drives a fixed-pitch propeller directly on its shaft, with no gearing. Power is fed from the ship's switchboard through a frequency converter (variable-frequency drive) and a slip-ring unit. Because the propeller is fixed-pitch, thrust is controlled by motor speed: the converter varies the frequency and voltage supplied to the motor, giving stepless speed and full torque in either direction. The source's 'frequency modulation' is a signal-encoding term, not the drive. Azipod is ABB's registered trademark for its podded propulsion; the generic term is podded propulsor, and other makers build them. An electrically driven L-drive (motor in the hull, geared) is electric but not 'motor-in-pod'.

Where pods are used. 🎯 Oral Q&A
Q1. How does a Kort nozzle improve propulsion, and when is it not worth fitting?
A Kort nozzle is a fixed duct of hydrofoil section around the propeller, with the blade tips running close to its narrowest section, the throat, at the propeller. The propeller's suction, helped by the circulation round the duct's foil section, draws more water through the throat; that circulation leaves low pressure on the duct's inner forward surface, so the duct produces forward thrust of its own. A larger mass of water is accelerated by a smaller velocity increase, which is more efficient at heavy loading, and the close-fitting duct reduces tip leakage. It pays on heavily loaded propellers at low speed — tugs, trawlers, push boats and some slow full-form ships; Wärtsilä states bollard pull can rise by up to about 30 per cent. At higher speed the duct's drag outweighs its thrust, so fast ships do not use it.
Q2. Explain the principle of contra-rotating propellers and their merits and demerits.
Two propellers on one axis turn in opposite directions. The forward propeller leaves swirl in its slipstream; the aft propeller, turning the other way, recovers most of that rotational energy as thrust, and the load is shared so each propeller has lower blade loading. The hull wake is not affected. The aft propeller is smaller, about 85 to 92 per cent of the forward diameter in ABB's guidance, to stay inside the contracted slipstream and clear of the forward tip vortices, and the blade numbers differ so that interaction pulses do not coincide. Merits are higher propulsive efficiency, more power within a limited diameter and lower pressure pulses; demerits are coaxial shafts, a contra-rotating gearbox, inter-shaft bearings and seals, higher first cost and extra mechanical losses. Japanese sea trials reported up to about 15 per cent and makers claim about 10 per cent, but on a moderately loaded, optimised single screw the gain can be marginal.
Q3. Compare the Kappel and CLT propellers.
Both reduce tip-vortex loss so the blade can carry load nearer the tip. The Kappel propeller, from Everllence, formerly MAN Energy Solutions, has the outer blade curved smoothly toward the suction side as an integral winglet-like tip with no separate plate; it is offered on fixed- and controllable-pitch propellers, and the maker quotes about 3 to 6 per cent lower required power. The CLT, Contracted and Loaded Tip, propeller from Sistemar has a finite tip chord with end plates projecting toward the pressure side, pitch increasing from root to tip and moderate skew. Its blade tip is loaded, while the end plates are passive fences that stop face-to-back leakage and are not designed to carry lift; Sistemar claims 4 to 8 per cent. These are the two main commercial tip-modified designs but not the only ones, and no independent full-scale figure was found for either.
Q4. Describe the types of azimuth thrusters.
An azimuth thruster is a propeller unit on a steerable housing below the hull that turns about a vertical axis to direct thrust in any horizontal direction; as main propulsion it steers the ship, so a conventional rudder is normally not fitted. Mechanical units keep the prime mover inside the hull: an L-drive has a vertical input shaft, normally from an electric motor mounted above, with one bevel-gear set; a Z-drive has a horizontal input shaft from a diesel engine or electric motor with two bevel-gear sets. The gears carry power to the propeller, and a separate slewing drive turns the unit. In electric podded units an AC motor inside the underwater pod drives a fixed-pitch propeller directly, fed through a frequency converter and slip rings.
Q5. Discuss the merits, demerits and applications of podded propulsion such as Azipod.
Azipod is ABB's trademark for its podded propulsor, with the motor in the pod driving a fixed-pitch propeller and speed set by a frequency converter. Merits are excellent manoeuvrability with 360-degree thrust, no shaftline, stern tube or rudder so machinery can be arranged flexibly, and usually lower vibration and noise; fuel and emissions can be lower, and ABB has claimed up to 20 per cent for Azipod XL against a modern shaftline, but that is a maker's best case. Demerits are high initial cost, a diesel-electric plant with conversion losses and high-voltage equipment, pod bearings and seals that have failed in service and may need dry-docking to repair, and specialised training for engineers and bridge teams. Pods are used on cruise ships, ferries, icebreakers, Arctic tankers and icebreaking LNG carriers; on ordinary deep-sea cargo ships a direct-drive two-stroke shaftline is usually more economical.

  • Kappel = tip curved to SUCTION side, no plate · CLT = LOADED tip + PASSIVE pressure-side end plates.
  • Azimuth: L-drive 1 bevel set (vertical motor) · Z-drive 2 bevel sets (horizontal input) · separate slewing drive steers.
  • Pod: motor in pod, fixed pitch, FREQUENCY CONVERTER (VFD) · diesel-electric · bearings and seals critical.
  • Pods: cruise, ferries, icebreakers, Arctic tankers, Arc7 LNG carriers — NOT 'cruise only'.
  • Every percentage has an owner — Everllence, Sistemar, IHI/MHI, Wärtsilä, ABB — and a baseline.

Situation (illustrative)

An owner's technical team is comparing two concepts for an ice-class shuttle tanker that must also hold position at an offshore loading terminal: a conventional two-stroke shaftline with a controllable-pitch propeller and rudder plus tunnel thrusters, or diesel-electric propulsion with azimuthing pods. The Chief Engineer-designate is asked for the engineering view.

What the Chief Engineer's note covers

Operating profile. Variable power demand — open-water transit, ice operation, slow approach and position-keeping — favours a diesel-electric plant where the number of running generator sets follows the load, and pods that can vector full thrust at zero speed. A double-acting ship can run stern-first through heavy ice with the pods pulling. Efficiency claims. The pod maker's 'up to 20%' is a best case against a shaftline on other ship types; the shaftline concept has no electrical conversion losses and is usually more economical in steady open-water service. The comparison has to be made on this ship's profile, including converter and motor losses. Maintenance. Pods move critical bearings and seals underwater and add a high-voltage converter plant; the note asks for the maker's bearing-monitoring and seal-replacement procedure, the spares strategy and whether major repair needs dry-docking. Crew. Engineers need HV and converter training; the bridge needs pod-handling training. Alternatives considered. A CRP on the shaftline would recover swirl but adds coaxial shafting; a ducted propeller would help only in the heavily loaded ice and low-speed conditions; tip-modified blades are a lower-cost efficiency option for the shaftline concept.

The lesson for the examiner

Podded propulsion is chosen for the operating profile — manoeuvrability, ice and variable load — not for a headline percentage. The candidate who can say why pods suit an Arctic tanker but a conventional bulk carrier keeps its two-stroke shaftline has understood the topic.
