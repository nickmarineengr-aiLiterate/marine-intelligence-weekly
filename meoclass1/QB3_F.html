Define Special Area and PSSA under MARPOL/IMO, with examples.
MARPOL|PSSA
15-Second AnswerA Special Area is a MARPOL-designated zone with mandatory strict discharge limits (Annexes I, II, IV, V); a PSSA is a separate IMO Resolution A.982(24), as amended by MEPC.267(68) designation for ecologically/socially significant areas needing protective traffic measures.
60-Second AnswerSpecial Areas sit inside MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V and impose stricter discharge criteria due to oceanographic and traffic characteristics. PSSAs are an IMO Assembly designation under Resolution A.982(24), as amended by MEPC.267(68), independent of any single Annex, requiring Associated Protective Measures like routing schemes or mandatory pilotage. Some zones, like the Baltic and North Sea, are designated as both simultaneously.

Regulatory Context

A Special Area (SA) is defined within MARPOL Annexes I, II, IV and V as a sea area requiring mandatory stricter discharge methods due to oceanographic/ecological condition and traffic density. A Particularly Sensitive Sea Area (PSSA) is a separate IMO designation under Resolution A.982(24), as amended by MEPC.267(68) for areas needing protection for ecological, socio-economic or scientific significance vulnerable to shipping.

Key Distinctions

  • SA — standardised strict discharge limits set by the relevant Annex (e.g. Mediterranean, Baltic).
  • PSSA — custom Associated Protective Measures (APMs) such as traffic separation schemes, mandatory pilotage or ship reporting (e.g. Great Barrier Reef, first PSSA in 1990; the Torres Strait was added as a separate extension in 2005 via MEPC.133(53), not designated simultaneously with the Reef).
  • An area can be both (e.g. Baltic Sea, North Sea).

PSSA Designation Criteria

At least one of: ecological (uniqueness, critical habitat), socio-economic (fisheries, tourism dependency), or scientific/educational significance, combined with demonstrated vulnerability to international shipping.

Key Numbers

PSSA governing instrument: Resolution A.982(24), as amended by MEPC.267(68). First PSSA: Great Barrier Reef, 1990 — Torres Strait added as a separate extension in 2005 (MEPC.133(53)). Do not state these as one combined 1990 event.

What statutory certificates are required under MARPOL Annex VI?
MARPOL VI|CII
15-Second AnswerMARPOL Annex VI requires the IAPP and IEE Certificates plus annual DCS and CII Statements of Compliance for ships ≥400 GT (DCS/CII from ≥5,000 GT), supported by EIAPP certificates, BDNs and an ODS Record Book where applicable.
60-Second AnswerFor ships 400 GT and above, the IAPP Certificate (SOx/NOx/PM/ODS, 5-year validity) and IEE Certificate (EEDI/EEXI, life-of-ship validity, tied to SEEMP) are mandatory. Ships also carry annual Statements of Compliance for IMO DCS fuel reporting (SEEMP Part II) and, above 5,000 GT, for CII operational rating (SEEMP Part III). Supporting records include per-engine EIAPP certificates, Bunker Delivery Notes kept 3 years, and fuel samples kept 1 year.

Regulatory Context

For ships of 400 GT and above on international voyages, MARPOL Annex VI requires a defined set of statutory certificates verifying air-pollution and energy-efficiency compliance.

Primary Certificates

  • IAPP Certificate — SOx/NOx/PM/ODS compliance, with a Record of Construction and Equipment supplement; valid max 5 years.
  • IEE Certificate — EEDI/EEXI attainment per Reg. 5.4, requiring an onboard SEEMP; valid for ship's life absent major conversion.
  • Statement of Compliance — IMO DCS — annual fuel-consumption data verification (SEEMP Part II).
  • Statement of Compliance — CII rating — for ships ≥5,000 GT, annual carbon-intensity rating A-E (SEEMP Part III).

Supporting Records

  • EIAPP Certificate per engine >130 kW, with Engine Technical File and Parameter Record Book.
  • Bunker Delivery Notes (retained ≥3 years) and MARPOL fuel samples (retained ≥1 year or until substantially consumed).
  • ODS Record Book where ODS-containing equipment is fitted.

Key Numbers

400 GT IAPP/IEE threshold; 5,000 GT DCS/CII threshold; BDN retention 3 years; fuel sample retention 1 year.

What is the discharge criteria for oil tankers under MARPOL Annex I?
MARPOL I|ODME
15-Second AnswerMARPOL Annex I Reg. 34 permits oil-tanker cargo-space discharge only outside Special Areas, en route, beyond 50 NM, at ≤30 L/NM and ≤1/30,000 of cargo total, with an operating ODME — distinct from Reg. 15's 15 ppm machinery-space limit.
60-Second AnswerRegulation 34 sets cumulative conditions for tanker cargo-space discharge: outside a Special Area, en route, more than 50 NM from land, instantaneous rate not exceeding 30 L/NM, total quantity not exceeding 1/30,000 of the cargo, with an operational ODME enforcing automatic shutoff. This is separate from Regulation 15's machinery-space rule of under 15 ppm oil content for all ships.

Regulatory Context

MARPOL Annex I, Regulation 34 governs discharge of oily mixtures from cargo tank spaces of oil tankers, distinct from Regulation 15 which covers machinery-space bilge discharge on all ships.

Regulation 34 — Cargo Space Discharge

All of the following must apply simultaneously:

  • Outside a designated Special Area
  • En route
  • More than 50 nautical miles from nearest land
  • Instantaneous rate ≤ 30 litres per nautical mile
  • Total quantity ≤ 1/30,000 of the total cargo quantity from which the residue formed
  • Operational Oil Discharge Monitoring & Control system (ODME) in use, with automatic shutoff on breach

Regulation 15 — Machinery Space (all ships ≥400 GT)

Oil content must be below 15 ppm, en route, with an approved 15 ppm separator and alarm (automatic stopping device required inside Special Areas).

Formula

Instantaneous Rate (L/NM) = [Oil content (ppm) × Discharge flow (m³/hr)] ÷ [1000 × Ship speed (knots)]

Key Numbers

50 NM from land; 30 L/NM instantaneous rate; 1/30,000 total quantity fraction; machinery bilge 15 ppm.

Describe MARPOL Annex V record books and the procedure followed onboard.
MARPOL V|Garbage
15-Second AnswerMARPOL Annex V requires ships ≥100 GT (or ≥15 persons certified, or any fixed/floating platform) to keep a two-part Garbage Record Book — Part I for all operational waste, Part II for cargo residues on bulk carriers only — supported by a Garbage Management Plan with strict segregation and logging procedures.
60-Second AnswerThe Garbage Record Book has Part I, applicable to every ship above the threshold, logging discharge or incineration of plastics, food waste, domestic waste, ash, non-HME cargo residues and fishing gear; and Part II for solid-bulk-cargo residue-generating vessels — bulk carriers, not tankers, whose cargo tank washings fall under Annex I/II instead. Onboard, waste is segregated at source per the Garbage Management Plan, processed via incinerator/compactor or shore reception, and every event is logged with position, quantity and dual sign-off.
Verify before quoting: Threshold was lowered from 400 GT to 100 GT and above (or ≥15 persons, or any fixed/floating platform) under MEPC.360(79), in force 1 May 2024. This is a live "recent amendment" trap — 400 GT is the outdated figure.

Regulatory Context

MARPOL Annex V requires ships of 100 GT and above (raised from the earlier 400 GT threshold by MEPC.360(79), effective 1 May 2024), or certified to carry 15 or more persons, or any fixed/floating platform, to maintain a two-part Garbage Record Book (GRB) supported by a Garbage Management Plan (GMP).

GRB Structure

  • Part I (all ships): logs discharges/incineration of plastics, food waste, domestic waste, cooking oil, incinerator ash, non-HME cargo residues, operational waste, animal carcasses and fishing gear.
  • Part II (bulk carriers only): cargo residues and associated tank washings from solid bulk cargoes. Tankers are not covered here — tanker cargo tank washings fall under MARPOL Annex I/II, not Annex V Part II.

Onboard Procedure

  • Segregation at source into colour-coded bins per the GMP.
  • Processing via approved equipment — incinerator, compactor, or shore reception.
  • Every discharge, incineration or accidental loss logged with date, position (GPS), category and quantity, signed by the officer in charge and countersigned by the Master.

Key Numbers

Threshold: 100 GT (MEPC.360(79), eff. 1 May 2024) or ≥15 persons certified capacity, or any platform. Part II applies to bulk carriers only (solid bulk cargo), not tankers. GRB retention: 2 years after last entry.

Discuss the Hong Kong Convention and India's perspective on ship recycling ratification and implementation.
Hong Kong Convention|Ship Recycling
15-Second AnswerThe Hong Kong Convention applies to ships of 500 GT and above on international voyages, and entered into force globally on 26 June 2025; India implemented it via the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019, upgrading Alang yards and requiring an Inventory of Hazardous Materials and Recycling Plan for every vessel scrapped.
60-Second AnswerThe Hong Kong Convention, adopted in 2009, applies to ships of 500 GT and above on international voyages, and entered into force on 26 June 2025 once tonnage and recycling-capacity thresholds were met. India ratified early and enacted the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019, upgrading Alang-Sosiya yards to compliant status ahead of global entry into force, making India the leading HKC-compliant recycling nation. Every vessel now carries a maintained IHM and, at end of life, a yard-approved Recycling Plan before a Ready for Recycling Certificate is issued.

Regulatory Context

The Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships, 2009 applies to ships of 500 GT and above on international voyages, and entered into force globally on 26 June 2025, having met the tonnage/capacity ratification thresholds.

India's Implementation

  • India ratified the Convention and enacted the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019, giving it domestic legal force ahead of global entry into force.
  • Alang-Sosiya (Gujarat) yards were upgraded to HKC-compliant status, driving India's position as the world's largest ship-recycling hub by volume.
  • Requirements include an Inventory of Hazardous Materials (IHM) onboard, a ship-specific Recycling Plan approved before dismantling, and a Ready for Recycling Certificate.

Operational Impact

Vessels destined for recycling must maintain and update the IHM through their life, culminating in a final IHM survey before delivery to a compliant yard.

Key Numbers

Applicability: 500 GT and above, international voyages. HKC entry into force: 26 June 2025. India: Recycling of Ships Act, 2019.

What is a ship-breaking credit note and how does it work?
Ship Recycling|Commercial
15-Second AnswerA Shipbreaking Credit Note (SCN) is a Government of India financial instrument under the Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme (SBFAS 2.0, notified 24 Sep 2025): a shipowner who recycles a vessel at an HKC-compliant Indian yard receives a tradeable credit worth 40% of the ship's fair scrap value, redeemable against up to 5% of a new vessel's contract price at an Indian shipyard, valid 3 years.
60-Second AnswerThe Shipbreaking Credit Note is not a private commercial settlement instrument — it is a Government of India subsidy mechanism under SBFAS 2.0, administered by the DGMA. When an eligible vessel is recycled at an Indian ship-recycling facility certified Hong Kong Convention-compliant, the owner is issued an SCN worth 40% of the vessel's fair scrap value. The application is made online within three months of the Certificate of Recycling/Demolition Completion. The SCN is transferable/tradeable on the open market, valid for three years, and can be redeemed against up to 5% of the contract price of a new vessel built at an Indian shipyard — the DGMA pays the redeemed value directly to the shipyard, not the owner. It is entirely separate from the BIMCO RECYCLECON, which is the private commercial sale-and-purchase contract for the recycling transaction itself.

Regulatory/Policy Context

The Shipbreaking Credit Note (SCN), also referred to as the Ship Recycling Credit Note (SbCN), is a Government of India financial incentive under the Shipbuilding Financial Assistance Scheme (SBFAS 2.0), notified by the Ministry of Ports, Shipping & Waterways with guidelines effective 24 September 2025. It is not a commercial price-adjustment instrument between shipowner and recycling yard.

How It Works

  • Eligibility: the vessel must be recycled at an Indian ship-recycling facility certified Hong Kong Convention (HKC)-compliant.
  • Value: owner receives a credit equal to 40% of the vessel's fair scrap value.
  • Application: submitted online to the Competent Authority (DGMA) within 3 months of the Certificate of Recycling/Demolition Completion issued by the State Maritime Board/State Government.
  • Redemption: usable against up to 5% of the fair price of a new vessel built at an Indian shipyard; DGMA pays the redeemed value directly to the shipyard, not the owner.
  • Transferability/validity: the SCN is tradeable/transferable on the open market and valid for 3 years from issue, after which it lapses.

Distinguishing from Commercial Sale Documents

The SCN must not be confused with the BIMCO RECYCLECON, which is the private commercial sale-and-purchase contract governing the recycling transaction (price, delivery, condition) between owner/cash buyer and yard. The SCN is a government subsidy layered on top of, not part of, that commercial sale.

Key Numbers

Credit value: 40% of fair scrap value. Redemption cap: 5% of new-vessel fair price. Validity: 3 years. Application window: 3 months from Recycling/Demolition Completion Certificate. Scheme effective: 24 September 2025 (SBFAS 2.0).

How will you order bunkers for your vessel for both quantity and quality, how do you deal with a bunker dispute, and how many samples are drawn during bunkering — their purpose and retention?
BunkeringMARPOL VICommercial
15-Second AnswerThe purchaser specifies the fuel; the supplier must deliver to that specification and the statutory limits. Quantity is proved by joint gauging before and after, cross-checked against my own tanks. The MARPOL sample is the one statutory sample, kept 12 months; the Bunker Delivery Note 3 years. Protest in writing before the barge disconnects.
60-Second AnswerOrdering is a specification exercise: IMO guidance is explicit that the purchaser is responsible for correctly specifying the fuel and the supplier for delivering to that specification and the statutory limits. So the order names the ISO 8217 grade, the sulphur limit for the trading pattern, flashpoint above 60 °C, and the quantity in metric tonnes in vacuum. On delivery, quantity comes from joint gauging with temperature and density, cross-checked against my own soundings — that is where aerated bunkers and short delivery are caught. Samples are drawn continuously at the receiving ship's manifold: one MARPOL delivered sample, sealed and signed and retained under the ship's control for not less than twelve months, plus the commercial samples. For a dispute I protest before the barge sails, isolate the tank, and never break the MARPOL seal.

1. Ordering — Who Is Responsible for What

The IMO best-practice guidance for fuel oil purchasers and users settles the responsibility split in one sentence: fuel oil purchasers are responsible for correctly specifying the fuel oil which is to be supplied; it is the responsibility of the supplier to deliver fuel oil which is compliant with the agreed specification and statutory limits. An imprecise order is therefore the owner's problem, not the supplier's. My requisition must state:

  • Grade and specification — the ISO 8217 table and grade (for example RMG 380 or DMA), which fixes viscosity, density, CCAI, water, ash, aluminium plus silicon, sodium, pour point and stability.
  • Maximum sulphur content for the intended trading: 0.50% m/m globally since 1 January 2020, or 0.10% m/m where the ship will operate inside a SOx emission control area — unless the ship uses an approved equivalent such as an exhaust gas cleaning system.
  • Flashpoint not less than 60 °C, as required by SOLAS chapter II-2.
  • Quantity in metric tonnes in vacuum, with the density at 15 °C and the expected delivery temperature stated.
  • Compatibility and stability with the fuel already on board — segregation is planned before the order, not after the delivery.
  • The sample set and sampling method to be provided, and the Safety Data Sheet.

2. Quantity — How It Is Actually Proved

  1. Before delivery: attend the barge with the supplier's cargo officer, take ullages or soundings of every tank against stamped calibration tables, record temperatures and density, and verify there are no unlisted tanks or pipelines that can be drained or filled during the transfer.
  2. Take the ship's own tank soundings before and after — the independent check that does not depend on the barge's figures.
  3. After delivery: re-gauge jointly, convert volume to metric tonnes in vacuum using the density at 15 °C with the temperature correction, and compare with the Bunker Delivery Note.
  4. Watch for aerated fuel — the "cappuccino" effect, where air is entrained into the delivery so the volume reads high and the mass is short. Foam on the ullage, an abnormally high delivery temperature and an unstable sounding are the indicators.

3. The Sample Set — What Each One Is For

Samples are drawn continuously throughout the bunkering period at the receiving ship's manifold, using a manual valve-setting continuous drip sampler or an automatic sampler, and then split and sealed in the presence of both parties. The set normally comprises:

  • The MARPOL delivered sample — the one statutory sample. It accompanies the Bunker Delivery Note, is sealed and signed by the supplier's representative and the master or officer in charge, and is retained under the ship's control until the fuel is substantially consumed, but in any case for not less than 12 months from the date of delivery. Its purpose is verification of MARPOL compliance — in practice, sulphur content — by the flag State or Port State Control. It need not physically remain on board, but must be readily accessible if required by the authorities.
  • The ship's retained sample — commercial, kept on board as the owner's evidence of what was delivered.
  • The supplier's or barge's retained sample — commercial, held by the supplier as their evidence.
  • The test sample — commercial, landed immediately to an accredited fuel-testing laboratory for a full ISO 8217 analysis. This is the sample that actually decides a quality dispute.

Separately, and often confused with the above, are the in-use sample and the onboard sample. Since 1 April 2022, ships must have sampling point(s) fitted or designated to allow a representative sample of the fuel oil being used on board to be drawn — the in-use sample, taken downstream of the service tank close to the combustion machinery. Those are compliance-verification samples for the fuel in service, not delivery samples.

4. The Bunker Delivery Note

The BDN records the delivery details specified in Appendix V of MARPOL Annex VI — including the ship's particulars, the port and date of delivery, the supplier's name and address, the product, the quantity in metric tonnes, the density at 15 °C, the sulphur content, and the supplier's declaration of conformity. It must be kept on board readily available for inspection at all reasonable times and retained for three years after the fuel oil is delivered.

5. Handling a Dispute — Quantity First, Then Quality

Quantity dispute. Raise it while the barge is still alongside. Call for joint re-gauging, and if the difference is not resolved, issue a letter of protest to the barge master before disconnection, have it signed or note the refusal to sign, record the figures and photographs, make the Oil Record Book entry for the quantity actually received, and inform the company and the charterer immediately. A protest raised after the barge has sailed is worth very little.

Quality dispute. This runs on a different track and a different sample:

  1. Isolate the tank and do not commingle the new fuel with fuel already on board.
  2. Do not burn it until the laboratory result is available, unless there is no alternative and the risk is assessed.
  3. Land the commercial test sample to an accredited laboratory for full ISO 8217 analysis.
  4. Issue a letter of protest to the supplier within the time limit set by the sale contract, and notify the owner, the charterer and the P&I club.
  5. If the fuel is confirmed off-specification and unsafe or unusable, debunkering may be required — normally at the supplier's or the charterer's cost, depending on the contract.
  6. Never break the seal on the MARPOL delivered sample to settle a commercial argument. It is statutory evidence held for the authorities; destroying it leaves you unable to demonstrate compliance and does not help the claim.

Under a time charter the charterer supplies and pays for the bunkers, so the commercial claim generally runs from the charterer to the supplier. The Chief Engineer's job in that chain is not to argue the contract — it is to produce unimpeachable evidence: gauging records, timings, temperatures, seal numbers, the signed sample receipt, the protest and the log entries.

6. When Compliant Fuel Simply Is Not Available

If compliant fuel cannot be obtained, the owner must make best efforts to obtain it, and the non-availability is reported to the flag State and the port State through a fuel oil non-availability report, with the evidence of the attempts made. Non-availability is a reporting route, not a self-declared exemption.

The oily water separator fails two days before arrival port. What is your single first action?
MARPOL Annex IBilgeISM
15-Second Answer

Stop the overboard discharge and retain the bilge water on board. Shut and secure the overboard valve, line the bilge system up to the bilge holding tank, and log it. That is the one action, and it is the one the examiner is listening for — because MARPOL Annex I Reg 15 permits a machinery-space discharge only through working 15 ppm equipment. With the separator defective there is no lawful overboard route, so the discharge has to stop before anything else is decided.

60-Second Answer

Sir, the single first action is to stop discharging overboard and start retaining. The reasoning is one line of MARPOL: Annex I Reg 15 makes a machinery-space bilge discharge lawful only when it passes through approved 15 ppm oil filtering equipment with its alarm operating, the ship is en route, and the effluent is below 15 ppm. If the separator is defective, that condition cannot be met, so there is no lawful discharge to make and the only correct state of the system is closed and secured. Two days from arrival is not a technical problem — almost any ship can hold two days of bilge generation — it is a pressure problem, and the examiner is testing whether the deadline changes my answer. It does not. Everything else follows in order: raise it as a defect and, because the separator is critical equipment under ISM 10.3, as a non-conformity under ISM 9; inform the Master and the company; arrange spares or a service attendance for the arrival port; make the Oil Record Book entries; and reduce bilge generation at source by finding and stopping the leaks that are filling the well in the first place.

1. Why the answer is “stop” and not “repair”

Repair is the obvious instinct and it is the wrong first action, because it does not change the ship's legal state. While the separator is down the ship is capable of an illegal discharge at any moment, by nothing more than an open valve and a running bilge pump. Closing that route is the action that removes the risk; repairing is the action that restores the capability. Give them in that order and the answer is complete in one sentence.

2. The legal basis, in one line

MARPOL Annex I Reg 15 controls the discharge of oil from machinery spaces. For a ship of 400 GT and above, a discharge is permitted only where the ship is proceeding en route, the oily mixture is processed through the oil filtering equipment required by Reg 14, the effluent is below 15 ppm, and the mixture does not originate from cargo pump-room bilges or contain cargo oil residues. Reg 14 is what puts the equipment on board: oil filtering equipment of an approved type for ships of 400 GT and above, and, for ships of 10,000 GT and above, arrangements that raise an alarm and automatically stop the discharge when 15 ppm is exceeded. Two qualifications are worth having ready. In practice the 15 ppm alarm is universal, because every 15 ppm bilge separator type-approved under MEPC.107(49) includes a bilge alarm whatever the tonnage — it is the automatic stopping device that the 10,000 GT threshold governs. And inside a special area the standard rises: a machinery-space discharge from a ship of 400 GT and above must be through equipment meeting the Reg 14.7 arrangements, so alarm-and-automatic-stopping reaches far below 10,000 GT there.

Read those two together and the answer writes itself. Reg 15 does not say a discharge below 15 ppm is permitted — it says a discharge processed through the Reg 14 equipment and below 15 ppm is permitted. A defective separator removes the first limb, so no discharge is lawful regardless of what any figure appears to show.

3. What “retain on board” actually means

  • Shut and secure the overboard valve. On most ships this means shut, lashed or padlocked, and the key under the Chief Engineer's control — the same discipline as the standard discharge connection.
  • Line the bilge wells to the bilge holding tank, not to the separator suction.
  • Check the capacity you actually have against the daily bilge generation rate. Two days at a normal rate is comfortable; two days at a leaking-stern-tube rate may not be. If the holding tank will not hold it, nominate a suitable clean tank, sound and record it, and tell the company before the tank is used, not after — with two exclusions. Not a segregated ballast tank, because Annex I keeps oil and water ballast apart. And not any tank whose use would put the ship at odds with the arrangement shown in the IOPP Certificate Form A/B supplement, which is the document a PSC officer compares your soundings against.
  • Attack the source. Every litre you do not generate is a litre you do not have to store. Leaking fuel and lube connections, gland packing, air-conditioning and refrigeration drains and a weeping stern tube are the usual contributors.
  • Do not transfer bilge water into the sludge tank as a matter of routine to make space. Sludge and bilge water are different streams with different ORB entries, and mixing them to solve a capacity problem is how record-keeping starts to drift.

4. The chain after the single answer

The examiner asked for one action, so give one. But he will almost always ask “and then?”, and the chain should come out in this order:

  • Defect report and non-conformity. The 15 ppm equipment is critical equipment, so ISM 10.3 requires measures to promote reliability including regular testing of standby arrangements, and its failure is reported under ISM 9.1. Inform the Master and raise it in the company system the same day.
  • Diagnose. The usual causes are a coalescer element blinded by detergent or emulsified oil, a fouled or air-locked 15 ppm monitor cell, a stuck three-way valve, a failed solenoid or a lost sample line — and a monitor fault is far more common than a separator fault. Establish which of the two has failed, because it changes both the spare and the report.
  • Spares and attendance. Order to the arrival port through the agent; two days is usually enough lead time if the order goes out now, and not enough if it goes out on arrival.
  • Class and flag. If the defect affects a condition of the IOPP Certificate or its Form A/B supplement, report it to class and the flag Administration. A defect properly reported and being repaired is a manageable conversation; the same defect found by a surveyor is not.
  • Oil Record Book. Reg 17 entries continue as normal — bilge water transferred to the holding tank is a recordable operation, and the absence of any entries for two days is itself a signal.
  • Plan the discharge. On arrival, either repair and test before use, or land the bilge water ashore to a port reception facility and take the receipt.

5. The line that must not be crossed

There is no version of this answer that involves discharging anyway. Not a bypass, not a “temporary” line, not running it through the sludge pump, not a discharge in the belief that the water looks clean, and not an Oil Record Book entry describing an operation that did not happen. A false ORB entry is a separate and, in enforcement practice, frequently more serious matter than the discharge itself, because the discharge may be an accident and the entry cannot be. If an examiner offers you the shortcut — and this examiner asked the question two days before arrival for a reason — the answer is that the ship retains and arrives with full tanks and an honest book.

6. Arriving with it still defective

This is a normal, defensible position, provided it is documented. Have the defect report, the ISM non-conformity, the spare order and the ORB in order, declare it, and land the bilge water to a reception facility if repair will take time. Port State Control will look at the separator, the monitor, the overboard valve and the ORB together, and what they are really assessing is whether the three tell the same story.

Form A and Form B — what are they, and which one does your ship carry?
MARPOL Annex IIOPPCertificates
15-Second Answer

They are the two versions of the Supplement to the IOPP Certificate — the Record of Construction and Equipment under MARPOL Annex I, Appendix II. Form B is for oil tankers; Form A is for everything else. The Supplement is permanently attached to the IOPP Certificate, so a certificate without its correct Form is incomplete.

60-Second Answer

Sir, the IOPP Certificate itself says very little — the detail is in its Supplement, the Record of Construction and Equipment, which lists what pollution-prevention equipment the ship actually has: the 15 ppm filtering equipment, the alarm and automatic stopping device, sludge and bilge holding tanks, the standard discharge connection, and for a tanker the cargo, ballast, COW and ODME arrangements. Annex I gives it in two forms, and the dividing line is the three ship types the IOPP Certificate itself categorises. Form B is used for the first two — “oil tankers” and “ships other than oil tankers with cargo tanks coming under regulation 2.2 of Annex I”. Form A is used for the third — “ships other than any of the above”. So on my container ship it is Form A. Both forms carry the same instruction: “This Record shall be permanently attached to the IOPP Certificate”, and the Certificate “shall be available on board the ship at all times”.

What the Supplement is

  • The Record of Construction and Equipment required with the International Oil Pollution Prevention (IOPP) Certificate, in the form set out in MARPOL Annex I, Appendix II.
  • The Certificate proves the ship has been surveyed and complies; the Supplement records what she is actually fitted with. It is the document a PSC officer opens to see whether the equipment in front of him is the equipment the ship is certified for.
  • It is permanently attached to the Certificate and is not valid separately. Losing or replacing equipment without amending the Supplement is a certificate defect, not a paperwork detail.

Which form — the rule as Annex I actually writes it

The commonly taught shorthand is “Form A for non-tankers, Form B for tankers”. That is right in nine cases out of ten and wrong in the case an examiner will pick. The Annex keys the choice to the three ship types the IOPP Certificate categorises:

  • Form B — for the first two types: “oil tankers” and “ships other than oil tankers with cargo tanks coming under regulation 2.2 of Annex I”.
  • Form A — for the third type: “ships other than any of the above”.

So a ship that is not an oil tanker can still carry Form B, if she has cargo tanks falling under regulation 2.2 — the provision that applies the tanker requirements to “cargo spaces which are constructed and utilized to carry oil in bulk of an aggregate capacity of 200 cubic metres or more” — and that 200 m³ figure is exactly what “which non-tankers, then?” is asking for. Say “Form B for tankers and for non-tankers with regulation 2.2 cargo tanks” and the follow-up disappears.

What each form records

  • Both forms — particulars of ship; equipment for the control of oil discharge from machinery-space bilges and oil fuel tanks: the oil filtering (15 ppm) equipment, the 15 ppm alarm and the automatic stopping device where required, sludge and oily bilge water tank capacities, and the standard discharge connection; plus exemptions and equivalents granted.
  • Form B only — the tanker sections: construction, segregated ballast tanks and their distribution, crude oil washing where fitted, the Oil Discharge Monitoring and Control System, slop tank arrangements, and the oil/water interface detector.

Currentness — the amendment chain, in order

The allocation rule above has never changed. The content of the forms has, three times, and “when was your Supplement last amended?” is a fair follow-up:

  • MEPC.187(59) — adopted 17 July 2009, deemed accepted 1 July 2010, in force 1 January 2011. Amends regulations 1, 12, 13, 17 and 38, the Supplement, and the Oil Record Book Parts I and II. Its Annex 2 replaces Section 3 of BOTH Form A and Form B with “Means for retention and disposal of oil residues (sludge) (regulation 12) and oily bilge water holding tank(s)”. This is the amendment that put the sludge and oily-bilge-water tank entries into your Form A, so it is the one that matters on a non-tanker.
  • MEPC.276(70) — adopted 28 October 2016, in force 1 March 2018. Form B only: Section 1 paragraphs 1.11.8 and 1.11.9 deleted; Section 5 rewritten around regulation 18.9 segregated-ballast qualification.
  • MEPC.359(79) — adopted 16 December 2022, in force 1 May 2024. Best known for Arctic reception facilities, but it also amends Appendix II, Form B: “The title of section 5 is replaced by the following: ‘5 – Construction (regulations 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 26, 27, 28 and 33)’”. This is the most recent amendment to the Supplement.

So the honest answer to “when was it last amended?” is 2024 for the Supplement as a whole, and 2011 for Form A specifically — because the two 2016 and 2022 amendments both reach Form B alone.

How it is checked, and what goes wrong

  • At an IOPP survey the surveyor works down the Supplement item by item against the plant. A tick against equipment that is not there, or is there but unserviceable, is the finding.
  • After a retrofit or a conversion — a new OWS, a changed sludge tank, an added incinerator — the Supplement must be amended. This is the commonest real-world defect: the equipment changed and the Record did not.
  • A flag or ship-type change can change which form applies. A ship converted to carry oil in bulk in cargo spaces moves from Form A to Form B.

The Oil Record Book in detail — and what are the codes?
MARPOL Annex IORBRecord Books
15-Second Answer

The statutory record of oil operations, in the form set by Appendix III to MARPOL Annex I. Part I, machinery spaces — tankers ≥150 GT, other ships ≥400 GT. Part II, cargo and ballast — tankers only. Entries go under lettered codes, and a certified copy is evidence in court.

60-Second Answer

Sir, the Oil Record Book is required by regulation 17 for Part I and regulation 36 for Part II, and its form is fixed by Appendix III — the ship does not design it. Part I covers machinery-space operations: ballasting or cleaning of oil fuel tanks, discharge of that dirty ballast or cleaning water, collection and disposal of oil residue (sludge), discharge or disposal of machinery-space bilge water, and bunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oil. Part II covers cargo and ballast operations on oil tankers. Entries go under lettered section codes — A to I in Part I, A to R in Part II — recorded without delay. The officer in charge signs each completed operation; the master signs each completed page. It must be readily available for inspection, kept on board, and preserved three years after the last entry. Since 1 October 2020 it may be an electronic record book, but only one approved by the Administration. And under reg. 17.7 a master-certified copy is admissible in judicial proceedings — which is why the entries must be true.

What it is, and who must carry which part

  • Part I — Machinery space operations. Regulation 17.1: every oil tanker of 150 gross tonnage and above, and every ship of 400 gross tonnage and above other than an oil tanker. Not “all ships” — the two thresholds are different and the examiner is listening for both. And beware the book in your own hands: the Appendix III form is headed “PART I – Machinery space operations (All Ships)”. That heading is a form title, not the applicability rule; regulation 17.1 governs, and quoting the header is how the “all ships” answer gets into circulation.
  • Part II — Cargo/ballast operations. Regulation 36.1: every oil tanker of 150 gross tonnage and above. A tanker therefore carries both parts; your container ship carries Part I only.
  • Either part may be part of the ship’s official log-book or a separate book, but in both cases it shall be in the form specified in Appendix III.

Part I — the five operations that must be recorded (reg. 17.2)

Recorded on each occasion, on a tank-to-tank basis where appropriate:

  • .1 ballasting or cleaning of oil fuel tanks;
  • .2 discharge of dirty ballast or cleaning water from oil fuel tanks;
  • .3 collection and disposal of oil residue (sludge);
  • .4 discharge overboard or disposal otherwise of bilge water which has accumulated in machinery spaces;
  • .5 bunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oil.

Plus, under reg. 17.3, a statement of the circumstances and reasons for any discharge under regulation 4 or any accidental or other exceptional discharge; and under reg. 17.5, any failure of the oil filtering equipment.

A precision point worth a mark: reg. 17.2.3 once read “oil residues (sludge and other oil residues)”. MEPC.187(59) deleted “and other oil residues” and replaced “sludge” with the defined term “oil residue (sludge)” throughout regs 12.2, 13, 17.2.3, 38.2 and 38.7. Using the current defined term shows you have read the Annex and not a summary.

The codes — Part I, sections (A) to (I)

The “codes” the examiner means are the lettered sections of the Appendix III form. Each operation is entered under its letter with numbered sub-items. Sections (A) to (H) were replaced in their entirety by MEPC.187(59), in force 1 January 2011 — the letters did not change, the wording did — and (I) was not replaced:

  • (A) Ballasting or cleaning of oil fuel tanks
  • (B) Discharge of dirty ballast or cleaning water from oil fuel tanks referred to under Section (A)
  • (C) Collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge) — note transfer, added in 2011; the quantity retained on board is recorded weekly, and item 11.4 records the quantity collected by manual operation
  • (D) Non-automatic starting of discharge overboard, transfer or disposal otherwise of bilge water accumulated in machinery spaces
  • (E) Automatic starting of discharge overboard, transfer or disposal otherwise of bilge water accumulated in machinery spaces
  • (F) Condition of the oil filtering equipment — time of failure, time made operational, reasons
  • (G) Accidental or other exceptional discharges of oil
  • (H) Bunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oil
  • (I) Additional operational procedures and general remarks

The 2011 rewrite is the answer to “in detail”. (C) became collection, transfer and disposal; (D) and (E) changed from “non-automatic/automatic discharge” to “non-automatic/automatic starting of discharge, transfer or disposal”. The common thread is internal transfer: moving oily water or sludge between tanks is now expressly recordable, because unrecorded internal transfers were how quantities were made to disappear.

The codes — Part II, sections (A) to (R)

Cargo and ballast operations on a tanker: loading, internal transfer and unloading of oil cargo; crude oil washing; ballasting of cargo tanks and of dedicated clean ballast tanks; tank cleaning; discharge of dirty ballast; discharge of water from slop tanks; disposal of residues; discharge of clean ballast and of ballast from CBT; condition of the oil discharge monitoring and control system; accidental discharges; general remarks; and the ballast-water items. Part II carries its own copies of the Part I housekeeping rules, and the follow-up “who signs Part II, and for how long?” is answered by reg. 36.5 (same signature split), reg. 36.6 (failures of the oil discharge monitoring and control system are recorded) and reg. 36.7 (same three-year retention); reg. 36.9 provides that for tankers below 150 GT operating under reg. 34.6 the Administration develops an appropriate Oil Record Book. Section (J) was replaced by MEPC.187(59) and now reads “Collection, transfer and disposal of residues and oily mixtures not otherwise dealt with”, expressly covering transfer from the machinery-space sludge and oily bilge water tanks — the point where Part II meets your Part I.

Entries, signatures and language (reg. 17.4)

  • Each operation is fully recorded without delay, so that all entries appropriate to it are completed.
  • Each completed operation shall be signed by the officer or officers in charge of that operation.
  • Each completed page shall be signed by the master. Since October 2020 the wording is “each completed page or group of electronic entries”.
  • Entries, for ships holding an IOPP Certificate, shall be at least in English, French or Spanish. Where the flag State’s official language is also used, that version prevails in a dispute or discrepancy.

Availability and retention (reg. 17.6)

Kept in such a place as to be readily available for inspection at all reasonable times; kept on board, except for unmanned ships under tow; and preserved for three years after the last entry. The three years runs from the last entry, not from the ship’s year-end or the certificate date.

Electronic Oil Record Book — and the qualification that matters

MEPC.314(74), in force 1 October 2020, permits it — conditionally. It added regulation 1.39: “Electronic Record Book means a device or system, approved by the Administration, used to electronically record the required entries for discharges, transfers and other operations as required under this Annex in lieu of a hard copy record book”; and amended regs 17.1 and 36.1 so the book may be kept “as an electronic record book which shall be approved by the Administration taking into account the Guidelines developed by the Organization”. An electronic log is not an electronic ORB unless the flag State has approved it. Do not say “we keep it on the computer” — say whether the system is flag-approved, and expect to be asked.

Port State Control, and why the entries are legally serious

Regulation 17.7 is the sentence to know: the competent authority may inspect the ORB in port, make a copy of any entry, and require the master to certify it a true copy — and “any copy so made which has been certified by the master…shall be made admissible in any judicial proceedings as evidence of the facts stated in the entry”. That is what separates the ORB from every other book onboard. A PSC officer reconciles entries against tank soundings, the sludge and bilge tank capacities declared in the IOPP Supplement (see Q9), bunker delivery notes and reception-facility receipts. Unexplained quantity discontinuities are the finding, not untidy handwriting.

On correcting an entry: strike through once so the original remains legible, write the correction, initial and date it. Never erase, never overwrite, never leave a blank line to be filled later, and never make an entry for an operation that did not happen — a false entry is treated far more seriously than the operational failure it was written to conceal.

CE Relevance, Traps, Failures & Exam Strategy
CE Relevance

This is the Chief Engineer’s book in a way no other statutory record is. Every one of the five Part I operations is machinery-space work you or your engineers perform, and the entries you sign are the evidence that the 15 ppm equipment, the sludge system and the bilge system were used as certified. The discipline is arithmetic before it is legal: the book must reconcile with the tanks. Daily soundings of the sludge and bilge holding tanks, quantities in cubic metres that match what was actually moved, weekly sludge retention recorded under (C), bunker figures under (H) that agree with the bunker delivery note, and reception-facility receipts filed against the disposal entries. Where a discontinuity exists, explain it in the book at the time under section (I) — an explained anomaly is a record; an unexplained one is a case.

Trap Questions

“Which ships carry ORB Part I?” — Oil tankers 150 GT and above AND other ships 400 GT and above; not “all ships”, and the two thresholds are different. “Who signs?” — the officer in charge signs each completed operation, the master signs each completed page. Company practice may add a CE countersignature; MARPOL does not require one, so do not present it as the regulation. “How long is it kept?” — three years from the last entry. “Can you keep it electronically?” — Only as an Electronic Record Book approved by the Administration under reg. 1.39; an unapproved software log is not an ORB. “Where is the form from?” — Appendix III; Appendix II is the IOPP Certificate and its Supplement. “What is code C?” — Collection, transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge). “You made a wrong entry — what now?” — Single strike-through leaving the original legible, correction, initial and date.

Common CE Failures

Saying “all ships” for Part I. Giving the master’s and officer’s signature duties the wrong way round, or asserting a CE countersignature as a MARPOL requirement. Quoting a retention period other than three years, or running it from the wrong date. Teaching the pre-2011 wording of sections (C), (D) and (E) — the letters are unchanged, which is exactly why a stale answer sounds right. Confusing Appendix II with Appendix III. Claiming any electronic log satisfies the ORB. Treating it as an engine-room diary rather than a document whose certified copy is admissible in court. And the most damaging: leaving blank lines, or writing an entry to make the arithmetic work.

Casualty / Enforcement Link

The enforcement hook is written into the regulation rather than into any one case, and quoting the regulation is stronger than naming a prosecution. Regulation 17.7 lets a port State copy an entry, require the master to certify the copy, and have that copy “made admissible in any judicial proceedings as evidence of the facts stated in the entry”. That is why a false ORB entry is commonly treated as a distinct and graver matter than the discharge it concealed: the discharge may be an accident or a defect, while the entry is a deliberate act, and it is the entry that the State can prove from the ship’s own signed document. The same logic runs through Q8 — when the separator fails, the entries continue, and two days of silence in the book is itself a signal.

On a large container vessel, as Second Engineer, the ORB Part I work is routine and daily. Sludge and bilge holding tanks are sounded and logged every day so the book can be reconciled against the tanks by someone else without my help; sludge retained on board is entered weekly under (C); every transfer between the bilge holding tank, the sludge tank and the incinerator service tank is entered when it is done, not at the end of the week; incineration is entered under (C) with the total time of operation; disposals ashore are entered against the reception-facility receipt with the port identified; and bunkering is entered under (H) with the quantities checked against the bunker delivery note before signing.
