Effects of CO₂ and methane on the atmosphere.
MARPOLGHG

15-Second Answer

Both are greenhouse gases: they absorb outgoing long-wave (infrared) radiation from the Earth's surface and re-radiate it, warming the lower atmosphere. CO₂ is the largest anthropogenic contributor and persists for centuries; methane (CH₄) is far more potent per molecule — roughly 28–30 times CO₂ over 100 years and around 80 times over 20 years (IPCC AR6 values) — but is shorter-lived (~12 years). Shipping's response sits in MARPOL Annex VI and the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy.

60-Second Answer

Solar radiation reaching the Earth is re-emitted as long-wave infrared; greenhouse gases absorb this outgoing radiation and re-emit it in all directions, trapping heat — the greenhouse effect. CO₂ from fossil-fuel combustion is the dominant driver of anthropogenic warming because of the sheer quantity emitted and its atmospheric lifetime of centuries; a portion also dissolves into seawater, driving ocean acidification. Methane is a much stronger infrared absorber per molecule — a Global Warming Potential of roughly 28–30 (100-yr) and about 80 (20-yr) per IPCC AR6 — but oxidises in the atmosphere within about 12 years, so its warming effect is intense but shorter-lived. For a marine engineer the methane point has a hard practical edge: methane slip — unburned CH₄ escaping from LNG-fuelled engines, particularly low-pressure Otto-cycle types — can erode much of LNG's CO₂ advantage on a CO₂-equivalent basis. The regulatory response is the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy (net-zero "by or around 2050") implemented through MARPOL Annex VI (EEXI, CII, SEEMP), with a well-to-wake fuel-intensity framework (GFI) still in draft.

Governing instrument

IPCC assessment framework (source of GWP values and lifetimes); MARPOL Annex VI and the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy (Res. MEPC.377(80)) — the shipping regulatory response resting on this science.

The Mechanism

  • Greenhouse effect: gases with three or more atoms (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O, water vapour) have vibrational modes that absorb infrared; N₂ and O₂ do not — that molecular difference is the whole story.
  • CO₂: long-lived (a large fraction remains for 100+ years, some for millennia); cumulative — total emitted mass is what fixes peak warming; also acidifies the ocean (carbonic acid), affecting marine ecosystems.
  • Methane: lifetime ~12 years; oxidises to CO₂ and water vapour; per-kg far more potent while it lasts — cutting methane gives fast near-term climate benefit.

Why This Matters in Shipping

  • Fuel choice: LNG cuts CO₂ at the funnel (~20–25% lower carbon per unit energy) but methane slip can offset the gain when counted as CO₂-equivalent — this is exactly why newer frameworks move to well-to-wake CO₂-equivalent accounting (all GHGs), while the current CII remains tank-to-wake CO₂-only with fixed Cf factors.
  • Regulation: MARPOL Annex VI Chapter 4 (EEXI/CII/SEEMP) addresses CO₂; the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy targets net-zero GHG (all gases) by or around 2050, with checkpoints at 2030 and 2040.

Trap point

Quoting a GWP without its time horizon is the classic fail — methane is ~80× CO₂ over 20 years but ~28–30× over 100 years; and the persistence pair matters: CO₂ endures for centuries while methane's atmospheric lifetime is ~12 years.

Q: "Does CII capture methane slip?" A: No — CII is tank-to-wake CO₂-only with fixed Cf factors; methane is addressed in the GHG Strategy and the draft well-to-wake GFI framework, a separate track.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Not a casualty in the classic sense — the relevant "incident" is the industry-wide methane-slip measurement campaigns (e.g. EU-funded on-water studies from 2023 onward) showing real-world slip from low-pressure dual-fuel engines exceeding some certification values, which pushed regulators toward well-to-wake CO₂e accounting.

What is carbon footprint, and how do you check the carbon footprint of your ship?
MARPOLGHG

15-Second Answer

A carbon footprint is the total CO₂ (or CO₂-equivalent) emitted by an activity. For a ship I check it from measured fuel consumption × the fixed carbon conversion factor Cf for each fuel — e.g. 3.114 t CO₂ per tonne of HFO — reported annually through the IMO DCS (MARPOL Annex VI Reg.27), and expressed per transport work as the attained CII (Reg.28), verified against the ship's rating boundaries.

60-Second Answer

Carbon footprint is the total mass of CO₂ (or CO₂e) attributable to the ship's operation over a period. Onboard, the primary measurement is fuel consumption — from mass flowmeters, bunker delivery notes and tank soundings — because CO₂ emitted is simply fuel burned multiplied by a fuel-specific carbon factor Cf (HFO 3.114, MDO/MGO 3.206, LNG 2.750 t CO₂/t fuel). Verification runs through three regimes: (1) IMO DCS — annual fuel-consumption data under MARPOL Annex VI Reg.27, verified by the Administration/RO, leading to a Statement of Compliance; (2) CII — Reg.28 converts that data into attained CII = CO₂ emitted ÷ (capacity × distance sailed), in gCO₂/DWT·nm for my ship type, rated A–E annually against a reducing required CII, with a corrective action plan mandatory for a D three years running or a single E; and (3) EU MRV / EU ETS for voyages touching EU ports — per-voyage monitoring and, since 2024, surrender of emission allowances. The SEEMP (Part III) is the ship-specific plan for keeping the footprint inside its rating.

Governing instrument

MARPOL Annex VI Regs 26–28 (SEEMP, IMO DCS, CII) with the fixed Cf conversion factors of the IMO guidelines — a Tank-to-Wake track; EU MRV and FuelEU Maritime run a separate Well-to-Wake track.

How It Is Checked in Practice

  1. Measure: daily fuel oil consumption per fuel type (flowmeters/soundings), distance sailed, hours underway — logged and reported in noon data.
  2. Convert: CO₂ = Σ (fuel consumed × Cf) per fuel grade.
  3. Report: annual aggregated data to the DCS verifier by 31 March; SoC issued and retained onboard (and EU MRV emissions report for EU-scope voyages).
  4. Rate: attained CII computed and compared with required CII → rating A–E; documented in SEEMP Part III with verified corrective actions where required.

Attained CII = M / (C × Dₓ) — M = total CO₂ (g), C = capacity (DWT for container ships), Dₓ = distance sailed (nm)

The Boundary Trap — Which Footprint?

  • CII (MARPOL Annex VI Reg.28): tank-to-wake, CO₂ only, fixed Cf factors.
  • FuelEU Maritime / draft IMO GFI: well-to-wake CO₂e (includes upstream emissions and CH₄/N₂O) — separate regulatory tracks; do not mix their baselines or scopes in one sentence.

The Other Boundary — Scope 1, 2 and 3

If the examiner says "carbon footprint" rather than "CII", he may be after the corporate boundary rather than the regulatory one. That is the GHG Protocol split, and on a ship it maps cleanly:

  • Scope 1 — direct: fuel actually burned on board — main engine, auxiliaries, boilers, incinerator. This is the fuel figure the DCS report and the attained CII are built from.
  • Scope 2 — purchased energy: electricity taken from ashore, i.e. shore power / cold ironing in port. It leaves someone else’s stack, but it is your consumption.
  • Scope 3 — the value chain: all other indirect emissions, and for a ship the dominant one is upstream fuel production and delivery — the well-to-tank portion, which the Protocol places in Scope 3 Category 3 (fuel- and energy-related activities). Newbuilding, drydocking and chartered-in tonnage also sit here.

Do not merge the two boundary systems. Scope 1/2/3 is corporate carbon accounting — it answers “what does this company emit”. Tank-to-wake versus well-to-wake is regulatory carbon accounting — it answers “what does this instrument make the ship account for”. They overlap without being the same cut: Scope 1 is broadly the tank-to-wake ship, while Scope 1 plus the fuel part of Scope 3 is broadly well-to-wake.

Trap point

Track confusion: CII is Tank-to-Wake only (fixed Cf factors, MARPOL Annex VI Reg 28), while FuelEU Maritime and the IMO GFI work Well-to-Wake — separate regulatory tracks; merging their baselines or scopes in one sentence is the trap.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE owns data integrity at the source: flowmeter calibration and drift, correct segregation of consumption by fuel grade, honest logging of at-anchor vs underway consumption, and reconciliation of BDNs against soundings. Bad primary data becomes a wrong CII rating that the office cannot fix downstream.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is CII well-to-wake?" A: No — tank-to-wake, CO₂ only, fixed Cf factors; well-to-wake CO₂e belongs to FuelEU Maritime and the draft IMO GFI framework — separate tracks. Q: "Who verifies the footprint?" A: The DCS data is verified by the Administration or its RO before the Statement of Compliance is issued; EU MRV uses an accredited independent verifier. Q: "Can you improve CII without cutting fuel?" A: Yes — CII is intensity per capacity-mile, so more distance at efficient speed improves it; but slow-steaming with long idle periods can worsen it — a known perversity of the metric.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Confusing CII (TtW, CO₂-only) with FuelEU/GFI (WtW, CO₂e); mixing the baselines of different regimes; not knowing the Cf values; saying "EEDI" when the question is operational footprint (EEDI/EEXI are design indices); forgetting the DCS 31 March reporting deadline and the SoC.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Cf: HFO 3.114, MDO/MGO 3.206, LNG 2.750; DCS report by 31 March; CII rating bands A–E; corrective plan trigger: 3×D or 1×E; CII units for container ships: gCO₂/DWT·nm (AER).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

No physical casualty — the relevant enforcement risk is commercial/regulatory: ships with E ratings or unverified corrective plans face charterer exclusion clauses and PSC scrutiny of the SEEMP/SoC; EU ETS non-surrender carries financial penalties and ultimately expulsion orders from EU ports.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: Define carbon footprint → how measured onboard → Cf factors → DCS process and deadline → CII formula and rating → what if D/E → difference from EU MRV/ETS → (advanced) TtW vs WtW boundary.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessels report through the fleet performance system: automated flowmeter data feeds daily consumption per grade; the office compiles the DCS submission; attained CII is tracked live against the year's required CII so speed/routing decisions can protect the rating before year-end — the CE verifies the primary data behind all of it.

Statutory certificates under SOLAS and MARPOL — give a comprehensive list, beyond just Annex VI.
MARPOLSOLASSURVEYS

15-Second Answer

Under SOLAS: Cargo Ship Safety Construction, Safety Equipment and Safety Radio Certificates (or the combined Cargo Ship Safety Certificate), plus ISM DOC/SMC, ISPS ISSC, Minimum Safe Manning Document, and trade-specific documents (Polar Ship Certificate, IGC Certificate of Fitness, Document of Compliance for Dangerous Goods). Under MARPOL: IOPP (Annex I), NLS/CoF (Annex II), ISPP (Annex IV), and IAPP + IEE Certificate + SEEMP SoC (Annex VI) — Annexes III and V carry no certificate, only documented compliance.

60-Second Answer

Statutory certificates are issued by or on behalf of the flag State under the HSSC survey system (Resolution A.1207(34) survey guidelines, 2025 — supersedes A.1186(33)/2023 and A.1140(31)/2019). SOLAS certificates for a cargo ship: Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate (hull/machinery, Ch. II-1), Cargo Ship Safety Equipment Certificate (LSA/FFA/nav, Ch. II-1/II-2/III/V), Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificate (Ch. IV) — or one combined Cargo Ship Safety Certificate; the Safety Management Certificate (ship) with the company's Document of Compliance (ISM Code, Ch. IX); the International Ship Security Certificate (ISPS, Ch. XI-2); the Minimum Safe Manning Document (Reg. V/14); and where applicable the Polar Ship Certificate (Ch. XIV), Document of Compliance for Carriage of Dangerous Goods (Reg. II-2/19), and for a gas carrier the Certificate of Fitness under the IGC Code (Ch. VII). MARPOL: Annex I — IOPP; Annex II — Certificate of Fitness/NLS Certificate; Annex III — no certificate (packaged goods, IMDG compliance); Annex IV — ISPP; Annex V — no certificate (Garbage Management Plan + Record Book + placards); Annex VI — IAPP, IEE Certificate (EEDI/EEXI), EIAPP per engine (NOx), and the DCS Statement of Compliance. Related statutory but non-SOLAS/MARPOL: Load Line, Tonnage, AFS, BWM, MLC, CLC/Bunker CLC insurance certificates, and the HKC IHM/Inventory certification now that Hong Kong Convention is in force (26 June 2025).

Governing instrument

SOLAS Ch. I Reg 12 certificate suite, with Ch. IX (ISM DOC/SMC) and Ch. XI-2 (ISSC); MARPOL certification per annex — Annex I Reg 7 (IOPP), Annex IV Reg 5 (ISPP), Annex VI Regs 6–8 (IAPP/EIAPP).

Structured List — SOLAS

  • Safety Construction Cert. — hull, machinery, electrical (5-yr, annual/intermediate under HSSC).
  • Safety Equipment Cert. — LSA, fire protection, navigation equipment (with Record of Equipment Form E).
  • Safety Radio Cert. — GMDSS installation (Form R).
  • SMC + DOC — ISM Code; SMC for the ship, DOC for the company, verified by audit not survey.
  • ISSC — ISPS Part A; verification audits.
  • Minimum Safe Manning Document — SOLAS V/14.
  • Trade-specific: Polar Ship Cert. (Ch. XIV); DoC Dangerous Goods (II-2/19); IGC/IBC Certificates of Fitness (Ch. VII); Grain loading authorisation (Ch. VI).

Structured List — MARPOL

  • Annex I: IOPP Certificate + Supplement (Form B for ships ≥400 GT).
  • Annex II: International Certificate of Fitness (IBC ships) / NLS Certificate.
  • Annex III: no certificate — IMDG Code compliance for packaged harmful substances.
  • Annex IV: ISPP Certificate (sewage).
  • Annex V: no certificate — Garbage Management Plan, Garbage Record Book, placards.
  • Annex VI: IAPP (ship), EIAPP (each engine >130 kW, NOx Technical Code), IEE Certificate, SEEMP onboard, DCS Statement of Compliance.

Trap point

Mixing class documents into the statutory list — a class certificate or Condition of Class is not a statutory certificate; statutory means issued under a convention by or on behalf of the flag.

What are the tiers under OPRC?
MARPOLOPRC

15-Second Answer

The tiered response concept classifies oil-spill preparedness by scale: Tier 1 — small, local spill handled by the ship/terminal's own resources; Tier 2 — larger spill needing regional/national resources beyond the facility; Tier 3 — major spill requiring national and international assistance. The international framework is the OPRC Convention 1990; the tier structure itself is applied through national contingency plans (in India, the NOS-DCP) and IPIECA/IMO industry guidance.

60-Second Answer

The OPRC Convention 1990 (in force 13 May 1995) obliges parties to establish national systems for oil-pollution preparedness and response — a national contingency plan, designated authorities, and ships carrying an approved SOPEP (linked to MARPOL Annex I Reg.37). The convention establishes the preparedness-and-cooperation framework; the familiar three-tier classification is how that framework is operationalised in national plans and IMO/IPIECA guidance: Tier 1 — operational spills at or near a facility/ship, contained with local on-site resources (ship's SOPEP equipment, terminal booms); Tier 2 — spills exceeding local capability, escalated to regional/mutual-aid and national resources; Tier 3 — major incidents overwhelming national capacity, drawing international assistance (regional agreements, industry stockpiles such as Oil Spill Response Ltd., and OPRC's mutual-assistance provisions). Escalation is by response capability required, not a fixed tonnage alone — national plans define their own working thresholds. In India the tiers are implemented through the NOS-DCP, coordinated by the Indian Coast Guard.

Governing instrument

OPRC Convention 1990 (Art. 3 shipboard plans, Art. 6 national response systems); the tiered-preparedness concept per IPIECA/industry good practice, adopted into national plans including India's NOS-DCP.

The Tier Structure

  • Tier 1 — Local: ship and terminal resources; SOPEP actions — stop transfer, contain on deck, plug scuppers, notify; typical of bunkering/transfer spills.
  • Tier 2 — Regional: mutual-aid arrangements, port/state equipment stockpiles, national agencies mobilised; the ship's role becomes reporting, cooperation and evidence preservation.
  • Tier 3 — National/International: full national plan activation plus international assistance under OPRC Articles on co-operation; industry Tier-3 centres (e.g. OSRL) provide global response capability.

Honest Boundary

The tier concept originates in industry/IMO guidance and national plans rather than in the OPRC treaty text itself — the convention mandates the national system; the tiers are the standard structure that national plans (like India's NOS-DCP) use to build it. Say this distinction and you separate yourself from candidates reciting "OPRC has three tiers" as if it were an article of the convention.

Trap point

The tiers grade escalation of response capability, not fixed spill-size tonnage bands — quoting hard tonnage thresholds as if they were in OPRC is the trap.

What is NOS-DCP?
MARPOLINDIA

15-Second Answer

NOS-DCP is the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan — India's national plan for responding to marine oil spills, implementing the OPRC Convention 1990, with the Director General, Indian Coast Guard as the Central Coordinating Authority, structured around a Tier I / II / III response escalation.

60-Second Answer

The National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan is India's apex framework for oil-spill preparedness and response in Indian waters. The Indian Coast Guard has been the central coordinating agency for marine oil-pollution response since 1986, and the DG Coast Guard chairs the plan as Central Coordinating Authority; the plan is reviewed periodically at national NOS-DCP preparedness meetings and exercised through pollution-response drills. It defines the responsibilities of resource agencies — ports, oil companies, state governments, Navy, shipping — the reporting and alerting chain, response operations, and the escalation structure: Tier I (local spill, port/facility resources), Tier II (regional response, Coast Guard pollution-response assets), Tier III (national mobilisation with international assistance). It is India's implementation of its obligations as a party to OPRC 1990, and the shore-side counterpart of the ship's own SOPEP: if my vessel spills oil in Indian waters, my report to the coastal State (MARPOL Protocol I) lands in the NOS-DCP machinery, with the Coast Guard's Marine Rescue/Pollution Response centres as the operational contact.

Governing instrument

OPRC 1990 as implemented nationally by India's NOS-DCP, with the Director General, Indian Coast Guard as Central Coordinating Authority; the shipboard interface is the SOPEP under MARPOL Annex I Reg 37.

Key Elements

  • Central Coordinating Authority: Director General, Indian Coast Guard (Ministry of Defence) — coordination role dating from March 1986.
  • Scope: reporting/alerting, spill assessment, response operations, dispersant policy, waste management, cost recovery and claims interface, exercises and training.
  • Tiered response: Tier I/II/III by scale and capability. Exact working volume thresholds are defined in the current NOS-DCP text — verify against the latest ICG edition before quoting figures in the oral.
  • Resource agencies: major ports (Tier-1 equipment holdings), oil-handling agencies, coastal states, ICG pollution-response vessels and Dornier aircraft.

A Nuance Worth Knowing

Legal commentary has noted that the NOS-DCP operates as an executive/administrative plan rather than under a dedicated statute — a point about India's oil-spill legal architecture that a well-read candidate can raise carefully ("commentators have observed…") rather than as a settled fact, since the position may evolve under the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 framework.

Trap point

NOS-DCP is India's national response plan (ICG-coordinated) — it is not a shipboard document; the ship-level counterpart the examiner may be fishing for is the SOPEP.

The Indian Statutory Frame — and Who You Actually Call

Two Indian limbs finish this answer, and both are commonly missed.

The statute. Pollution from ships sits in Part VII of the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 (Act 24 of 2025) — prevention and containment of pollution from vessels, and response. The Central Government's 2026 pollution rules — oil, air, sewage, garbage and noxious liquid substances — are each made under the powers in sections 133 to 143 of that Part, and that is the block to name when asked where the Indian obligation comes from. The 2025 Act is a consolidating statute of sixteen Parts: name the Part and that section block, because there is no single "spill reporting" section to quote.

The report. On an incident involving a discharge or probable discharge of oil in Indian waters, the master reports without delay and to the fullest extent possible to the nearest Indian Coast Guard Maritime Rescue Coordination Centre (MRCC), using the Coast Guard's Spill Notification Pro forma for Oil and HNS. The same duty applies to an event merely observed at sea. There is no minimum quantity — the duty bites on a discharge of any quantity, and assuming a reportable threshold is the single most common error on this question.

What is new in ballast water treatment technology, particularly UV? How do you know onboard that the discharge meets the D-2 standard, and does a UV system satisfy US requirements?
BWMEnvironmentMachinery

15-Second Answer

Three limbs. New technology: the movement is towards UV-LED reactors replacing mercury lamps, better dose control driven by measured UV transmittance, lower power draw and smaller footprint. Onboard D-2 verification: you do not count organisms — you demonstrate compliance by operating the system inside its approved System Design Limitations, with a healthy self-monitoring record, calibrated sensors and complete Ballast Water Record Book entries; sampling and analysis is a port State and laboratory exercise, not a routine ship one. US requirements: yes, UV systems do hold USCG type approval — but the USCG judges living/dead by the FDA/CMFDA stain method, not IMO's viable/non-viable MPN method, so a UV system needs a substantially higher dose to pass. That difference is the whole point of the question.

The distinction that must be right before anything else

The BWM Convention is the legal framework and D-2 is the discharge performance standard; the BWMS Code is how equipment is type-approved; G8 was the approval guideline the BWMS Code superseded; G9 is the extra IMO approval needed only by systems using an Active Substance. A UV system uses no Active Substance, so it does not go through G9 at all. For the Convention's status, structure and the G7/G8/G9 family, see QB1_A Q31; this card is the onboard and technology answer.

Limb 1 — what is actually new

  • UV-LED reactors. Mercury-free, instant on/off with no lamp warm-up, long life, dimmable so dose can track demand, and unaffected by the pressure to remove mercury from shipboard equipment. The trade-off is presently cost and available output per unit, so LED units appear first at lower flow rates.
  • Dose control by measured UV transmittance (UVT). Rather than running lamps flat out, the control system measures UVT and flow and modulates lamp power to hold the required dose — cutting power consumption sharply, which matters because BWTS power draw at high ballasting rates is a real generator-loading problem.
  • Reactor and filter improvements. Better hydraulics so every element of flow gets the design dose, automatic wiper systems to keep quartz sleeves clean, and self-cleaning backwash filters with lower differential pressure.
  • On the chemical side, electrochlorination and side-stream designs continue to develop, and both routes now come with tighter residual-oxidant control — the direction the regime itself is moving in, since MEPC 84 introduced mandatory Active Substance discharge limits and annual residual sampling.
  • Compliance-monitoring tools — indicative self-monitoring instruments the crew can actually use — are the other growth area, and they lead straight into limb 2.

Limb 2 — how you know onboard that D-2 is being met

Answer honestly first: the ship cannot verify D-2 directly. D-2 is expressed in viable organisms per cubic metre and per millilitre and in cfu per 100 mL; establishing those figures requires accredited laboratory analysis. What the CE demonstrates instead is that the approved system was operated correctly within the conditions it was approved for. That is what a PSC officer will actually examine.

  1. Operate inside the System Design Limitations. Every type approval states the conditions the system was proved under — flow rate, UV transmittance, salinity, temperature, turbidity or total suspended solids, holding time. Ballasting in turbid coastal or river water can push UVT below the approved floor, and the system is then outside its SDL and cannot be assumed to deliver D-2. Knowing your ship's SDL figures is the single strongest thing to say here.
  2. Read the Control and Monitoring System. A type-approved system continuously self-monitors and records the parameters that determine treatment — UV intensity or dose, flow, filter differential pressure, alarms and bypasses. Those logs are the primary evidence, and an unexplained bypass or a suppressed alarm destroys the case.
  3. Keep the sensors honest. Calibration and maintenance of the UV intensity sensor, flow meter and UVT monitor to the manufacturer's schedule, with records. An out-of-calibration intensity sensor produces a compliant-looking log from a non-compliant discharge.
  4. Records. The approved Ballast Water Management Plan, the Ballast Water Record Book with every operation entered, the International Ballast Water Management Certificate, type approval certificate, and the manufacturer's operation and maintenance manual.
  5. Indicative analysis as a self-check. Portable indicative tools give a rapid pass/doubtful signal. Treat them as an operational check that tells you to investigate — not as proof of compliance and not as a substitute for the laboratory.
  6. Detailed analysis to the G2 sampling guidelines (Res. MEPC.173(58)) is the definitive route, and it is a port State or accredited-laboratory activity.
  7. Contingency. If the system fails, the answer is the plan, not improvisation: record it, notify the port State and the Company, and follow the contingency measures in the BWMP — which may include retention on board, discharge to a reception facility where available, or an alternative agreed with the port State.

Limb 3 — does a UV system meet US requirements?

Yes — UV systems hold USCG type approval. Filtration-plus-UV systems have been US type-approved since December 2016, when Optimarin's UV system and Alfa Laval's PureBallast 3 were both approved. So the honest answer to "does UV type meet US requirements" is that it does, provided the specific system on your ship carries a USCG Type Approval Certificate.

But the test is different, and that is what the examiner is digging for:

And the old Alternate Management System route, which allowed a foreign-approved system to be used temporarily, was a time-limited bridge and is no longer the answer to give. Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Three things are the CE's alone here. Power — a UV BWTS at full ballasting rate is a significant generator load, so the ballasting plan and the generator configuration have to be thought about together, and this is where UVT-modulated dose control earns its keep. Water quality awareness — knowing before you ballast that a turbid river berth may put the system outside its UVT limitation, and planning to ballast elsewhere or at reduced rate rather than discovering it in the log afterwards. Record integrity — the BWRB is treated with the same seriousness as the Oil Record Book; a bypass that is not explained in the record is the ballast-water equivalent of a magic pipe entry, and it is prosecuted in the same spirit.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "How do you check onboard that your discharge meets D-2?"
A: I cannot measure D-2 onboard, Sir. I demonstrate that the type-approved system was operated within its System Design Limitations, with the control and monitoring record intact, sensors calibrated, and the Ballast Water Record Book complete. Indicative tools give me an operational check; only accredited detailed analysis under G2 establishes the standard itself.

Q: "Does your UV system need G9 approval?"
A: No — UV is a physical process using no Active Substance, so G9 does not apply. G9 catches electrochlorination, ozone, chlorine dioxide and biocide-dosing systems. Every system, including UV, needs BWMS Code type approval.

Q: "Your ship trades to the US with an IMO-approved UV system. Any problem?"
A: Yes, unless it also holds USCG type approval. The USCG counts living organisms by the FDA/CMFDA method rather than viable organisms by MPN, so the two approvals are not interchangeable and the US test demands a higher UV dose.

Q: "Why is UV specifically affected by the US method?"
A: Because UV works by preventing reproduction rather than by killing. Under MPN those organisms count as non-viable and the system passes; under the living/dead stain they are still alive and counted, so roughly two to three times the dose is needed.

Q: "The BWTS fails at sea and you must deballast to berth. What do you do?"
A: Follow the contingency measures in the approved BWMP, record the failure and the actions in the Ballast Water Record Book, and notify the port State and the Company in advance to agree the way forward. I do not simply discharge untreated and write it up later.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Claiming D-2 can be verified onboard with a test kit; not knowing what a System Design Limitation is, or not knowing the ship's own UVT limit; confusing the BWMS Code with G8 or thinking G8 is still the live approval standard; putting UV through G9; treating IMO and USCG approval as one thing; answering the US limb with a flat "no, UV is not accepted in the US", which was never quite true and is certainly not true now; forgetting the Ballast Water Record Book entirely; and describing D-1 exchange as still being an available compliance choice, when the phase-in ended on 8 September 2024.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

On my Maersk container vessel the BWTS is a filtration-plus-UV unit, which suits container trading because there is no residual chemical to manage and no Active Substance approval chain. The two practical issues are the ones worth describing: generator loading when ballasting at full rate, and UV transmittance at turbid berths, where the treatment rate is reduced to stay inside the approved limitation rather than pushing flow and leaving the system's design envelope.
