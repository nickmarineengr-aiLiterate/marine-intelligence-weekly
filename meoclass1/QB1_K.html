What qualifications are needed to become a Class auditor?
SURVEYSIACS

15-Second Answer

The framework is the RO Code (Res. MSC.349(92)/MEPC.237(65)): a classification society acting as Recognized Organization must have documented qualification, training and authorization systems for its surveyors and auditors. In practice: a marine engineering / naval architecture degree or a management-level CoC (Chief Engineer / Master) with sea service, followed by the society's structured surveyor/auditor training, a formal lead-auditor course (ISO 19011 methodology, ISM/ISPS/MLC modules), supervised audits, and individual authorization by the RO and the flag for the audit types performed.

60-Second Answer

"Class auditor" in the oral usually means the RO auditor performing statutory management audits — ISM (DOC/SMC), ISPS (ISSC), MLC inspection — on the flag's behalf, distinct from a hull/machinery surveyor. The governing standard is Part 2 of the RO Code, which requires the RO to define competency criteria, deliver documented training, examine, and formally authorize each auditor for each scheme, with continued-competence monitoring. The typical pathway: (1) base qualification — degree in naval architecture/marine/mechanical/electrical engineering, or a management-level certificate of competency with sea experience (a sailing CE or Master converting ashore is a classic entry); (2) society induction — class rules, statutory conventions, survey procedures; (3) auditor formation — accredited lead-auditor training built on ISO 19011 auditing methodology and, for ISM, the Code plus the flag's implementation guidelines (in India, DGS/DGMA requirements for authorized ISM auditors); (4) supervised audits as trainee/observer with a qualified lead auditor, then formal authorization in the individual's name for defined audit types; (5) maintenance — minimum audit activity, refresher training and periodic re-approval, all subject to the RO's ISO 9001-family quality system and IACS/flag vertical audits. For internal SMS audits onboard, the ISM Code (Sec. 12) requires the company to use trained internal auditors independent of the area audited — a lighter version of the same competency logic.

Governing instrument

RO Code (Res. MSC.349(92)/MEPC.237(65)) Part 2 — qualification, training and authorisation of RO survey/audit personnel; society ISO 9001-based competence systems; ISM auditor criteria applied under Res. A.1118(30) guidance.

Structure the Answer

  • Base: engineering degree or management-level CoC + sea service.
  • Formation: RO training scheme + ISO 19011-based lead-auditor course (ISM/ISPS/MLC endorsements).
  • Field: supervised audits → individual authorization by RO/flag.
  • Governance: RO Code Part 2 competency system; flag oversight; IACS QSCS.

Trap point

Auditor and surveyor are different competence tracks — the ISM auditor examines the management system, the surveyor the physical ship; blending the two qualification routes is the fail.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Simon likes the career-path framing — answer as the route you could take: management CoC + sea time → society training → ISO 19011 lead-auditor course → supervised audits → named authorization. Anchor it to the RO Code Part 2 so it isn't just anecdote. The weak answer is "an ISM course" with no base qualification, no supervised-audit stage, and no authorization concept.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE meets auditors from the other side of the table — knowing their competency framework changes how you handle an audit: you may verify the auditor's authorization scope, and you understand why findings must trace to objective evidence (their own quality system is audited on exactly that).

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is a class surveyor automatically an ISM auditor?" A: No — hull/machinery survey authorization and management-audit authorization are separate competency streams under the RO Code; each requires its own training and named authorization. Q: "Who audits the auditors?" A: The RO's internal quality system, IACS QSCS assessments, and the flag State's oversight of its ROs (RO Code Part 3). Q: "Can you, as CE, do the internal SMS audit of the engine department?" A: Only if trained as internal auditor and independent of the area audited — ISM Sec.12 independence rule, unless company size makes it impracticable and the SMS says how objectivity is preserved.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Confusing surveyor with auditor; no mention of the RO Code as the governing standard; forgetting ISO 19011 as the audit methodology; not knowing internal auditors also need training and independence.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

RO Code mandatory since 1 Jan 2015 (SOLAS XI-1/1, MARPOL, LL amendments); ISO 19011; ISM internal audits at intervals not exceeding 12 months (per A.1118(30) practice); ISM Sections 12 (verification) and 13 (certification).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Herald of Free Enterprise (1987) — the management failures behind the capsize produced the ISM Code itself, and with it the profession of the marine management-system auditor whose qualifications this question probes.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: Auditor qualifications → surveyor vs auditor → who authorizes them → internal vs external audits → your own path to becoming one after Class 1 → what an auditor looks for in the engine room.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

On a Maersk vessel the annual internal ISM audit is done by trained company auditors from the fleet group; external DOC/SMC audits by the RO's authorized auditors — the CE hosts the engine-room portion, presenting PMS, critical-equipment and non-conformity records as objective evidence.

Condition of Class — who issues it, and when?
SURVEYSIACS

15-Second Answer

A Condition of Class (some societies say "Recommendation") is imposed by the classification society, through its attending surveyor, whenever a defect or outstanding item affecting class is found — at any survey, or after damage/breakdown — requiring specific measures, repairs or surveys to be completed within a stated time limit in order to retain class (the IACS harmonized definition). Miss the deadline without an agreed extension and class is suspended, which collapses the statutory certificates issued on class's behalf and the ship's insurance basis.

60-Second Answer

Who: only the classification society — in practice the attending surveyor, ratified by the society's technical office. Neither the owner nor the flag issues it; the flag's parallel instrument for statutory defects is a deficiency/dispensation. When: (1) at periodical surveys (annual/intermediate/special) when an item cannot be fully rectified or credited at the time; (2) after damage, grounding, collision, fire or machinery breakdown affecting class, where permanent repair is deferred — e.g. a temporary doubler accepted with a CoC to effect permanent repair at the next docking; (3) when a survey item is postponed by agreement (e.g. tailshaft survey extension conditions); (4) on transfer-of-class, where outstanding CoCs follow the ship under the IACS transfer-of-class procedures (TOCA) — the gaining society must deal with them. Character: each CoC states the requirement and a due date; it appears in the ship's class status (visible to charterers, insurers, PSC via Equasis-linked records). Consequences of default: class suspension — automatic on an overdue CoC unless postponed — which voids the basis of statutory certificates issued by that society as RO and typically breaches H&M/P&I cover terms. The CE's duties: track CoCs like survey due-dates in the PMS, plan repairs/spares to close them, and never let one lapse silently.

Governing instrument

Classification society rules (class maintenance and survey parts, IACS-aligned); the statutory interface where the society acts as RO and an outstanding CoC can affect statutory certification.

Say It in the Examiner's Frame

  • Definition (IACS harmonized): requirement for specific measures/repairs/surveys within a time limit to retain class.
  • Issuer: class surveyor / society — not flag, not owner.
  • Triggers: survey findings · damage/breakdown · postponed items · transfer of class.
  • Default: suspension of class → statutory/insurance cascade.

Trap point

A Condition of Class is a class instrument with a due date — not a PSC detention and not itself a statutory deficiency, though ignoring it can cascade into statutory and insurance consequences.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Lead with the IACS harmonized definition — "specific measures within a specific time limit to retain class" — then the issuer (class surveyor, never flag/owner) and the four trigger scenarios. Simon's follow-up is predictable: "what if it's not complied with?" — answer suspension of class and the statutory/insurance cascade without hesitation. The failing answer treats a CoC as a casual "note" rather than a time-limited retention condition.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE owns machinery-side CoCs end to end: understand exactly what the surveyor wrote, agree a realistic due date before he leaves, enter it in the PMS with lead-time alarms, arrange spares/workshop, and present the closure survey with evidence — an overdue machinery CoC discovered by a charterer's vetting is a commercial incident as much as a technical one.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Difference between a Condition of Class and a Memorandum?" A: A memo/note records information (e.g. acceptable wastage being monitored) with no compliance time limit; a CoC is a time-limited requirement to retain class. Q: "Can the Master/CE get a CoC extended?" A: Only the society can extend, on application with justification — usually with an attending survey. Q: "Ship sails with an overdue CoC — status?" A: Class is suspended; statutory certificates issued by that RO lose their basis; insurers must be notified — the ship is effectively untradeable until reinstated.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Saying the flag or PSC issues it; no time-limit concept; not knowing suspension is the sanction; confusing CoC with a PSC deficiency or an SMS non-conformity — three different regimes that can all arise from one defect.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

No universal CoC duration — the surveyor sets the due date case-by-case (say this explicitly); IACS transfer-of-class: outstanding items handled under TOCA; suspension is per society rules — typically automatic when a CoC falls overdue.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Prestige (2002) — scrutiny of the ship's survey and repair history, and of how class handled known structural conditions, drove tighter IACS harmonization of survey/condition regimes and EU oversight of ROs.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: What is a CoC → who issues → when → what if overdue → CoC vs memo vs PSC deficiency → how you as CE manage one on the main engine → transfer of class with open CoCs.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk practice: class status reports reviewed monthly by ship and office; any machinery CoC gets a repair plan with owner's superintendent within days; closure surveys are combined with scheduled port calls to avoid off-hire — the CE drafts the technical completion evidence.

Difference between GT and DWT.
TONNAGELOAD LINE

15-Second Answer

Gross Tonnage is a dimensionless index of the ship's total enclosed volume, computed under the Tonnage Convention 1969 as GT = K₁·V (V = total enclosed volume in m³) — it measures size, not weight, and drives regulatory thresholds and fees. Deadweight is a mass in tonnes: the carrying capacity — displacement at the summer load draught minus lightship — i.e. cargo + fuel + fresh water + ballast + stores + crew.

60-Second Answer

GT (ITC 1969, in force 18 July 1982): a logarithmic function of the total volume of all enclosed spaces, GT = K₁V with K₁ = 0.2 + 0.02·log₁₀V. It has no units — "GT 95,000" is an index, not tonnes. It is the trigger for most statutory applicability (SOLAS ≥500 GT cargo ship regime, MARPOL thresholds like 400 GT / oil tanker 150 GT, manning, STCW, port and canal dues, registration fees). Its sibling NT indexes the cargo-earning volume. DWT: a physical mass — displacement (Archimedean mass of water displaced) at the summer load line minus the lightship mass. It is what the ship can lift: cargo, bunkers, FW, ballast, stores, crew and effects. DWT is the commercial capacity number for bulk trades and the capacity term C in the container-ship CII (gCO₂/DWT·nm). The two do not convert into each other: a volume-carrier like a car carrier or container ship has huge GT relative to DWT; a dense-cargo bulker the reverse. Complete the triangle for the examiner: Displacement = Lightship + Deadweight, all in tonnes; GT/NT stand apart as volume indices under a different convention.

Governing instrument

International Convention on Tonnage Measurement of Ships 1969 (GT/NT formulae); deadweight follows from the Load Line Convention 1966/88 assignment and the ship's approved stability and loading documents.

Side-by-Side

  • Nature: GT — volume index, unitless · DWT — mass, tonnes.
  • Source: GT — Tonnage Convention 1969 (International Tonnage Certificate) · DWT — hydrostatics/Load Line assignment (stability booklet, Load Line Certificate context).
  • Use: GT — regulatory applicability, fees, manning · DWT — cargo capacity, chartering, CII capacity for most ship types.
  • Formula anchors: GT = K₁V · DWT = Δ(summer) − Lightship.

Trap point

Saying 'GT in tonnes' is the instant fail — GT is a dimensionless volume-based index under the 1969 Convention; deadweight is a mass in tonnes at the assigned load line.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): The one sentence that scores immediately: "GT is a dimensionless volume index under the 1969 Tonnage Convention; DWT is a mass — displacement minus lightship." Add the formula GT = K₁V and the triangle Δ = LS + DWT. Simon's favourite follow-up on a container/DP session: "which one does CII use?" — DWT for most ship types, GT for cruise ships and vehicle carriers. The classic fail is "gross tonnes" — saying GT is a weight.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE quotes GT when establishing which statutory regime applies (EIAPP >130 kW engines on ships ≥400 GT, ISPP ≥400 GT, etc.) and DWT when working CII, bunker planning against deadweight limits, and load-line compliance during heavy bunkering — mixing them up in paperwork creates real compliance errors.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is GT measured in tonnes?" A: No — dimensionless index of volume (the 1969 Convention deliberately dropped 'tons'). Q: "What is NT?" A: Net tonnage — the index of cargo-earning volume, GT-derived with deductions, floor at 0.30 GT. Q: "Lightship includes what?" A: Hull, machinery, outfit and liquids in machinery at working level — no cargo, no bunkers, no stores, no crew.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Calling GT a weight; equating GT with displacement; not knowing DWT = Δ − lightship; unable to name the certificate (International Tonnage Certificate 1969); missing the CII capacity nuance.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

GT = K₁V, K₁ = 0.2 + 0.02 log₁₀V; ITC 1969 in force 18 Jul 1982; NT floor 0.30 GT; Δ = Lightship + DWT; CII capacity: DWT (containers/bulkers/tankers), GT (cruise, vehicle carriers).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Not a casualty topic — the historical driver was tonnage-measurement gaming under pre-1969 national rules (open shelter-deckers) distorting safety applicability, which the universal ITC 1969 volume basis closed.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: GT vs DWT → NT → displacement/lightship triangle → which certificates state them → CII capacity → why a container ship is "big in GT, modest in DWT".

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

A large Maersk container vessel illustrates the split perfectly: GT well above 200,000 on the ULCV classes while DWT sits near 220–240k tonnes but is rarely the operating constraint — slot/stack limits and draught govern before deadweight does.

What is the recent trend in large container ship size, and why?
CONTAINERGHG

15-Second Answer

The ULCV generation has plateaued around 24,000+ TEU (~400 m LOA × 61 m beam, 24 bays / 24-wide) — driven there by economies of scale: cost, fuel and CO₂ per TEU-mile fall as size rises, which also improves EEDI/CII. The newer trend is a shift from ever-bigger to mid-size (8,000–17,000 TEU) dual-fuel newbuildings, because port/canal/crane limits, cargo-concentration risk and network flexibility now outweigh further scale gains.

60-Second Answer

Size growth ran from Panamax (~4,500 TEU) through post-Panamax and the 18,000 TEU Triple-E generation (2013) to today's 24,000+ TEU ULCVs capped near 400 m × 61 m. Why bigger: slot cost per TEU falls (hull steel, crew, machinery scale sub-linearly with capacity); propulsion power per TEU falls (wetted surface and wave resistance grow slower than volume), so fuel and CO₂ per TEU-mile drop — directly improving EEDI at design and CII in operation, and cutting unit cost on the deep-sea Asia–Europe trades where the giants deploy. Why the plateau and the pivot: diminishing returns meet hard constraints — berth depth, crane outreach and yard capacity at only a handful of ports; Suez/Malacca dimensional practicalities; longer port stays eroding the sea-leg savings; insurance and salvage exposure of concentrating >$1bn of cargo in one hull (the industry read Ever Given, 2021, as the case study); and schedule-resilience lessons from the pandemic squeeze. So the current ordering trend is fleet renewal at mid sizes with alternative-fuel propulsion — methanol and LNG dual-fuel — where decarbonisation compliance, port flexibility and cascading economics beat raw scale; Maersk's methanol-fuelled series is the flagship example.

Governing instrument

MARPOL Annex VI (EEDI/EEXI/CII) as the efficiency driver of the trend; classification rules for ULCV structural design (whipping/springing, torsional strength notations) — a market-plus-regulation question, not a single statute.

The Engineering Logic — Say the Physics

  • Cube-square scaling: capacity ~ volume; resistance ~ wetted area → power per TEU falls with size.
  • Regulatory coupling: lower fuel per TEU-mile = better EEDI/CII — scale was the cheapest early decarbonisation lever.
  • Limits: port infrastructure, canal dimensions, cargo-risk concentration, network flexibility — the reasons scale stopped paying.

Trap point

Assuming bigger means faster — the driver is cost, fuel and CO₂ per TEU-mile (economies of scale), with the current trend shifting from ever-larger hulls toward fuel/fleet flexibility.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): Nair asked this on a container session — he wants the why in engineering-economic terms, not a list of ship names. Give the cube-square argument ("power per TEU falls with size"), tie it to EEDI/CII, then show currency with the pivot: mid-size methanol/LNG dual-fuel ordering — and name your own company's methanol series as the live example. The weak answer is "ships are getting bigger" with no mechanism and no awareness that the trend has actually turned.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Scale changes the CE's plant: single large-bore 2-stroke at very low RPM with de-rated layouts, shaft generators/PTO for reefer loads, EGCS or 0.50% fuels, and on the new tonnage dual-fuel systems (fuel-supply rooms, gas/methanol detection, ESD philosophy) — the size question is really a propulsion-technology question one layer down.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "If bigger is more efficient, why not 30,000 TEU?" A: Port/canal limits, cargo-risk concentration, port-stay share of round-voyage time, and network inflexibility — the marginal saving no longer covers the marginal constraint. Q: "Does a bigger ship automatically get a better CII?" A: Generally yes per TEU-capacity — but CII uses DWT-based AER for container ships, and idle/waiting time can still wreck the rating. Q: "Why did Maersk choose methanol over LNG?" A: Simpler ambient-temperature storage, no methane slip, and green-methanol offtake strategy — accepting lower energy density.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

No mechanism (economies of scale asserted, not explained); not knowing the plateau/pivot; quoting TEU numbers wildly wrong; missing the EEDI/CII coupling that makes this a regulatory question, not just commercial.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

ULCV plateau ~24,000+ TEU, ~400 m × 61 m; Triple-E 18,270 TEU (2013); pivot band ~8,000–17,000 TEU dual-fuel; Ever Given grounding 2021.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Ever Given, Suez (2021) — six days blocking the canal quantified the systemic risk of extreme scale; with ULCV container-fire losses (e.g. Maersk Honam, 2018) it reframed "bigger" as a risk-concentration question, feeding the mid-size pivot.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: Size trend and why → economies of scale mechanism → EEDI/CII link → why the plateau → dual-fuel pivot and fuel choice → what changes for the CE on such tonnage.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk operates both ends of the story: Triple-E/E-class ULCVs on mainhaul loops, and the methanol dual-fuel series leading the renewal — a CE moving between them sees the size-vs-fuel-technology trade-off first-hand in bunkering, machinery arrangement and CII management.

Difference between DP-1 and DP-2.
MACHINERYDP

15-Second Answer

IMO DP equipment classes come from MSC.1/Circ.1580 (2017 guidelines, applying to vessels built on/after 9 June 2017; MSC/Circ.645 for earlier builds). Class 1: loss of position may occur after a single fault — no redundancy required. Class 2: loss of position must not occur from a single fault in any active component or system — generators, thrusters, switchboards, control computers, position references — so full redundancy of active elements, verified by FMEA and proving trials, with the class recorded in the vessel's DPVAD. (Class 3 additionally survives fire/flooding of any one compartment — A-60-separated backup systems.)

60-Second Answer

The equipment class expresses the worst-case single failure the DP system must ride through while keeping position and heading. DP-1 (Class 1): a single DP control system, non-redundant power and thrusters permitted; a single fault — one generator trip, one thruster loss, one control computer failure — may lose position. Used where drift-off consequences are low. DP-2 (Class 2): the design criterion is no loss of position from any single failure of an active component/system; static components (cables, pipes, manual valves) may be excluded if adequately protected. That drives the architecture: split main switchboards (bus-tie philosophy defined in the FMEA), redundant generator groups, thrusters in independent groups sized so the remaining group holds position after worst-case failure, at least two DP control computers with automatic transfer, redundant networks, an automatic power management system with blackout prevention, and three position-reference systems (of at least two different principles) for class 2/3 operations. The whole redundancy claim is proven by FMEA + annual trials, and the Administration states the class in the DP Verification Acceptance Document. Class notations map across societies (ABS DPS-1/2/3, DNV DYNPOS-AUT/AUTR family) aligned to the IMO classes. The one-line discriminator for the examiner: DP-1 tolerates no fault; DP-2 tolerates any single active-component fault; DP-3 tolerates loss of a whole compartment to fire or flood.

Governing instrument

MSC.1/Circ.1580 (16 Jun 2017 DP guidelines, vessels built on/after 9 Jun 2017; MSC/Circ.645 for earlier builds) — equipment classes 1–3, implemented through class DP notations.

What the Single-Failure Criterion Buys — Architecture Deltas DP-1 → DP-2

  • Power: single plant → redundant generator/switchboard groups + auto PMS with blackout prevention.
  • Thrust: any layout → independent thruster groups, each able to hold position alone in the design environment.
  • Control: one DP computer → minimum two, auto changeover; redundant networks and UPSs.
  • References: minimum fit → three PRS of ≥2 different principles online for the operation.
  • Verification: — → FMEA, proving trials, annual trials; DPVAD states the class.

Trap point

Mixing DP-2 and DP-3 — DP-2 survives a single failure of any active component; DP-3 additionally covers static components and loss of a whole compartment to fire or flooding.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): The source session was a DP-2 vessel — answer as its engineer. Lead with the criterion, not the kit: "DP-2 = no loss of position from any single active-component failure, proven by FMEA and trials." Then give the four architecture deltas (split power + PMS, thruster groups, dual DP computers, three PRS) and name the paper: MSC.1/Circ.1580, DPVAD. The classic fail is defining the classes by equipment counts ("DP-2 has two computers") without the single-failure design criterion they exist to satisfy.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

On a DP-2 ship the CE lives inside the FMEA: bus-tie open/closed philosophy for the operation, generator standby/spinning-reserve settings against blackout prevention, maintenance isolations that would breach redundancy (a "DP-degraded" notification item), and the annual DP trials where the engine department proves the worst-case failure ride-through.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Is a closed bus-tie allowed in DP-2?" A: Only if the FMEA proves a single fault (e.g. a bus short) cannot propagate across — otherwise operate split; the FMEA/ASOG governs, not habit. Q: "Are cables and pipes included in the single-failure criterion for DP-2?" A: Static components may be excepted where adequately protected — but they ARE included at DP-3, along with fire/flood of a compartment. Q: "Who states the equipment class?" A: The Administration, in the DPVAD, based on the guidelines' criteria.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Defining classes by hardware count instead of the failure criterion; not knowing MSC.1/Circ.1580 vs 645 applicability; forgetting FMEA/annual trials as the verification backbone; confusing class notations (DPS-2, DYNPOS-AUTR) with the IMO class they implement.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

MSC.1/Circ.1580 — 16 Jun 2017, builds on/after 9 Jun 2017; classes 1/2/3; DP-2/3 operations: 3 position references, ≥2 principles; DP-3 separation: A-60 + watertight compartmentation; document: DPVAD.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Offshore drive-off/drift-off incidents catalogued by IMCA — typically single hidden failures defeating assumed redundancy — are the reason 1580 added hidden-failure monitoring and hardened FMEA expectations over the 1994 guidelines.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: DP-1 vs DP-2 → what single failure means → bus-tie philosophy → position references → FMEA and annual trials → DP-3 delta → who certifies (DPVAD/class notation).

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessels are not DP-classed, but Maersk Supply Service's fleet runs DP-2 AHTS/SSVs — and the transferable engineering (PMS blackout prevention, split-board operation, UPS integrity testing) mirrors what a container-ship CE already manages during manoeuvring redundancy.

List the plans onboard related to ship structure.
SURVEYSSTRUCTURE

15-Second Answer

Core structural plan set: General Arrangement, Capacity Plan (with deadweight scale), Midship Section & scantling drawings, Shell Expansion, Docking Plan, construction profiles/decks, Loading Manual & Stability Booklet, Damage Control Plan & Booklet, Fire Control Plan (structural fire divisions), plus the modern statutory additions: Coating Technical File (PSPC), thickness-measurement/survey reports and, where applicable, the ESP file (tankers/bulkers) and the GBS Ship Construction File.

60-Second Answer

Group them so the list has architecture. (1) Geometry and arrangement: General Arrangement plan; Capacity Plan with deadweight scale; Lines/hydrostatics reference data. (2) Structure proper: Midship section with scantlings; construction profile and deck plans; Shell Expansion plan (plate-by-plate — the working drawing for locating wastage/damage and for thickness-measurement reporting); Docking Plan (block positions, plugs, appendages) for dry-docking. (3) Strength and loading: class-approved Loading Manual (permissible still-water bending moments/shear forces, loading conditions) and the loading instrument with its approved test conditions; Stability Booklet (Trim & Stability) approved under the IS Code/Load Line. (4) Damage/emergency structure: Damage Control Plan and Booklet (SOLAS II-1/19 — watertight subdivision, closures, cross-flooding); Fire Control Plan (SOLAS II-2/15) showing A/B-class divisions. (5) Survey-support records: Coating Technical File (SOLAS II-1/3-2, PSPC for ballast tanks); thickness measurement reports and the survey report file; the ESP file for tankers and bulk carriers (SOLAS XI-1/2, 2011 ESP Code); and for CSR tankers/bulkers delivered under Goal-Based Standards, the Ship Construction File (SOLAS II-1/3-10) kept onboard and ashore. On a container vessel add the Container Securing Manual (lashing forces into structure) as the cargo-interface plan.

Governing instrument

Class rules' onboard documentation requirements plus SOLAS anchors — Damage Control Plan (II-1/19), stability information (II-1/5-1); Loading Manual per class and the Load Line assignment.

Why Each Exists — One-Liners the Examiner Rewards

  • Shell expansion: the plate map — every gauging point and doubler in survey reports is referenced to it.
  • Loading manual: the legal envelope for SWBM/SF — exceeding it is a structural, class and insurance event.
  • Docking plan: wrong block loading has broken ships in dock — the yard works from this plan.
  • CTF/ESP/SCF: the paper trail that lets a surveyor judge structural condition across the ship's life.

Trap point

Confusing the docking plan with the shell expansion — one locates blocks/plugs for dry-docking, the other maps every shell plate and weld seam for survey and repair reference.

The CE is custodian of the machinery-space structural interfaces: engine seating and tank-top drawings for chocking/alignment work, shell expansion for any hull penetration or doubler decision, docking plan liaison before every dry-dock, and the loading instrument's approved condition file at annual survey.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

A: It is approved stability information — grouped here because loading/strength limits (SWBM/SF) live alongside it; strictly the loading manual is the strength document. What documents must be maintained for lifting appliances — and what does the CE check?
SURVEYSSOLASCRANES

15-Second Answer

Since 1 January 2026, lifting appliances and anchor-handling winches are directly under SOLAS — new Regulation II-1/3-13 (MSC.532(107)): design/construction per Administration-accepted standards, and through life operational testing, thorough examinations, inspection and maintenance documented in a register of lifting appliances and loose gear kept onboard, per the associated IMO guidelines (approved at MSC 107, 2023). The working paper set: the register, test/load certificates per appliance, annual thorough-examination records, 5-yearly load-test certificates, loose-gear certificates (wires, shackles, blocks), and maker-based maintenance in the PMS — the same family as the long-standing ILO C.152 cargo-gear regime.

60-Second Answer

Regulatory frame: SOLAS II-1/3-13 (in force 1 Jan 2026) closed the gap that shipboard cranes were previously governed only by class rules, ILO Convention 152 (dock-work cargo gear) and flag practice. It requires: design/construction/installation to standards acceptable to the Administration; testing after installation and after repairs/alterations; periodic operational testing and thorough examination; documented inspection/maintenance per the manufacturer; and records maintained onboard — implemented through the IMO guidelines for lifting appliances and for anchor-handling winches approved alongside the amendment, which recommend a register of lifting appliances with proof tests and annual thorough examination, including loose gear. The document set the CE maintains: (1) the Register (ILO/flag format — Form of Register with certificates attached); (2) initial and 5-yearly proof-load test certificates per appliance (test load per the standard — e.g. 25% overload up to 20 t SWL band under the classic cargo-gear regime — quote the certificate/standard, not one universal number); (3) annual thorough examination entries by a competent person; (4) loose gear certificates — wire ropes (with renewal criteria), shackles, hooks, blocks — each traceable by ID; (5) maker's manual-based PMS records: brake tests, limit-switch and safety-device (overload cut-out) function tests, NDT of critical welds where specified; (6) repair/alteration records with re-test. What the CE physically checks before use/survey: SWL markings legible and matching certificates; brake holding (noting winch/windlass brake-test values are ship-specific per the class-approved procedure — name the document, don't invent numbers); limit switches and emergency stops; wire condition against discard criteria; slew/luffing systems; hydraulic/electrical safety devices; and register entries current with no overdue examination.

Governing instrument

SOLAS Reg II-1/3-13 (Res. MSC.532(107), in force 1 Jan 2026) with MSC.1/Circ.1663 guidelines; the ILO Convention 152 regime for cargo-gear registers and test certificates.

Structure the Answer — Documents vs Checks

  • Documents: Register · test certificates (initial/5-yearly/after repair) · annual thorough-exam records · loose-gear certificates · maker-based PMS records.
  • CE checks: SWL vs certificate · brakes & safety devices per approved procedure · wire discard criteria · limits/E-stops · register currency.

Trap point

Quoting universal proof-load percentages is the trap — test values follow the applicable standard/flag table and the appliance's register documents; and from 1 Jan 2026 the governing anchor is SOLAS II-1/3-13.

Simon asked "What is CSR?" — what does he mean, and what is its scope?
IACSSURVEYS

15-Second Answer

On a structures chain, CSR means the IACS Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers (CSR BC & OT) — a single harmonised set of minimum hull structural requirements. Scope: bulk carriers of 90 m in length and above (single or double side skin) and double-hull oil tankers of 150 m in length and above, in each case contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2015. Always give the contract date with the length — length alone is an incomplete answer.

60-Second Answer

CSR are structural design rules published by IACS and adopted into the rules of each member society, so that a bulk carrier or double-hull oil tanker receives materially the same scantlings whichever IACS society classes it. They were first adopted by the IACS Council in December 2005 as two separate sets — CSR for Double Hull Oil Tankers (CSR-OT) and CSR for Bulk Carriers (CSR-BC) — applying to ships contracted for construction on or after 1 April 2006. Those two sets were later harmonised into a single set, CSR BC & OT, which entered into force on 1 July 2015 and applies to bulk carriers of 90 m and above and double-hull oil tankers of 150 m and above whose construction contracts are dated on or after 1 July 2015. The Rules are in two parts — Part One common to both ship types, Part Two additional specialised requirements for one type or the other. The design philosophy is a net scantling approach with corrosion additions applied separately, direct strength (finite-element) analysis, and fatigue assessed over a design fatigue life of 25 years in a North Atlantic environment. Separately, IMO sets Goal-Based Standards under SOLAS II-1 Reg. 3-10. Do not treat the two scopes as identical: GBS applies at 150 m and above for both ship types, so a 120 m bulk carrier can be a CSR ship while sitting outside Reg. 3-10.

Governing instrument

IACS Common Structural Rules for Bulk Carriers and Oil Tankers (CSR BC & OT), adopted into each member society's rules; SOLAS II-1 Reg. 3-10 (Res. MSC.290(87)) and the International Goal-Based Ship Construction Standards (Res. MSC.287(87)) for the IMO layer.

Scope — state it in full

  • Bulk carriers of unrestricted service, single or double side skin, 90 m and above.
  • Double-hull oil tankers of unrestricted service, 150 m and above.
  • Contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2015 (harmonised CSR BC & OT); 1 April 2006 for the earlier separate CSR-BC / CSR-OT.
  • The threshold is a defined rule length in the Rules, not LOA. The 2006 CSR-BC applied to bulk carriers "having length L of 90 m or above"; the harmonised Rules use freeboard length LLL for certain requirements, such as the 150 m threshold for the BC-A / BC-B / BC-C additional service features. Say "rule length" if pressed, not "overall length".
  • The CSR bulk-carrier definition is restrictive — generally single deck, double bottom, hopper side tanks and topside tanks, excluding ore carriers and combination carriers.
  • For the meaning of "contracted for construction", the Rules point to IACS Procedural Requirement (PR) No. 29.

What is IMO GBS?

GBS is a framework of "rules for rules". IMO sets the safety goals and functional requirements; the detailed scantlings come from class or Administration rules, which are then verified for conformity. Classification societies acting as recognized organizations and/or national Administrations develop the rules, and those detailed requirements become part of the GBS framework once verified as conforming to the GBS.

The framework has five tiers:

  1. Tier I — Goals: high-level objectives to be met.
  2. Tier II — Functional requirements: criteria to be satisfied in order to conform to the goals.
  3. Tier III — Verification of conformity: procedures for verifying that the rules and regulations conform to the goals and functional requirements.
  4. Tier IV — Rules and regulations for ship design and construction: detailed requirements developed by IMO, national Administrations and/or recognized organizations.
  5. Tier V — Industry practices and standards.

One Tier II number worth carrying: under Res. MSC.287(87), the specified design life shall not be less than 25 years. SOLAS II-1 Reg. 3-10 was adopted by Res. MSC.290(87) and entered into force on 1 January 2012.

Why do a private body's rules bind a ship?

  1. CSR are classification rules, applicable to ships classed with IACS member societies. Their practical force comes through class — and through the flag State / Recognized Organization framework, statutory certification, and the insurance and commercial requirements that depend on class being in good standing.
  2. For ships within SOLAS II-1 Reg. 3-10, the applicable structural rules must additionally conform to the IMO Goal-Based Ship Construction Standards. The regulation operates at the level of the structural requirements of a Recognized Organization or the Administration — SOLAS does not prescribe scantlings itself and does not name CSR.
  3. IACS CSR have been submitted to and verified through the IMO GBS verification process. At MSC 96 the Committee confirmed that the rules of the 12 IACS Members conform to the GBS Standards, and that ships contracted under those verified rules are deemed to meet the Standards.
  4. IACS states that CSR are the only Rules complying with the IMO Goal-Based Standards. Attribute that to IACS — SOLAS does not mandate CSR specifically.

Ship Construction File (SCF)

Required by SOLAS II-1 Reg. 3-10.4 and detailed in MSC.1/Circ.1343 (approved at MSC 87, 2 June 2010). Provided on delivery of new bulk carriers and oil tankers, kept on board the ship and/or ashore, and updated throughout the ship's life at any major event including substantial repair, conversion or any modification to the ship structure. It must remain with the ship and be available to its classification society and flag State throughout the ship's life. Purpose: to facilitate safe operation, maintenance, survey, repair and emergency measures.

Contents are organised against the GBS Tier II functional requirements, in four groups:

  • Design — design life; assumed environmental conditions; structural strength (general design, deformation and failure modes, ultimate strength, safety margins); fatigue life; residual strength; protection against corrosion (coating life and corrosion addition); structural redundancy; watertight and weathertight integrity; human element considerations; design transparency.
  • Construction — construction quality procedures; survey during construction, including the NDT regime and certificates of forgings and castings welded into the hull.
  • In-service considerations — survey and maintenance; structural accessibility.
  • Recycling considerations — identification of materials used in construction that may need special handling for environmental and safety reasons.

Typical documents named in the Circular: key construction plans (midship section, main O.T. and W.T. transverse bulkheads, construction profiles, shell expansions, forward and aft sections in the cargo region, engine-room, forward and stern construction drawings), general arrangement, capacity plan, loading manual, trim and stability booklet, docking plan, Ship Structure Access Manual, the Coating Technical File required by PSPC, plans showing areas prone to yielding, buckling, fatigue or excessive corrosion, and gross and net scantlings. Bulky items such as full strength and fatigue calculations, yard plans and the lines plan normally sit in the shore archive, with onboard procedures specifying how to access them.

Trap point

CSR scope and GBS scope are two separate tests with different thresholds, different carve-outs and different trigger dates. Apply both, in that order, and never quote a length without its contract date.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE meets CSR through its in-service consequences rather than its scantling formulae: the net scantling approach means the corrosion addition is a known, finite margin, so wastage found at thickness measurement is measured against a design assumption, not a guess. The SCF is where the design assumptions live — hull girder section modulus to be maintained, areas prone to yielding, buckling, fatigue and excessive corrosion, docking details, Ship Structure Access Manual. On an ageing ship those plans drive where you look during ESP close-up survey and what you tell the surveyor.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "200 m bulk carrier built 2003 — CSR?" A: No — it predates the original CSR contract applicability of 1 April 2006. Q: "120 m bulk carrier contracted in 2018 — CSR?" A: Within CSR ship-size applicability for bulk carriers (90 m and above) and after the 1 July 2015 contract date — but outside SOLAS II-1/3-10, which requires 150 m and above. Two separate tests. Q: "Single-hull oil tanker of 180 m?" A: Not CSR BC & OT — the Rules cover double-hull oil tankers. Q: "Does CSR apply to container ships?" A: No — CSR BC & OT covers bulk carriers and oil tankers only. Q: "An ore carrier of 250 m?" A: Excluded from the CSR bulk-carrier definition, and expressly excluded from Reg. 3-10. Q: "CSR means Continuous Synopsis Record?" A: It can — SOLAS XI-1 Reg. 5, on a documentation chain. Ask which the examiner means. Q: "IACS is private — how can CSR be mandatory?" A: Class → flag/RO and statutory/commercial framework → Reg. 3-10 conformity requirement → GBS verification of the RO's rules.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Quoting 90 m / 150 m without the contract date; saying every bulk carrier over 90 m is a CSR ship regardless of build date; treating CSR and GBS applicability as identical (the 90 m versus 150 m bulk-carrier gap is the examiner's opening); forgetting the Reg. 3-10 ship-type carve-out for ore and combination carriers; saying IACS is part of IMO — it is not, it holds consultative status; saying SOLAS specifies scantlings — it sets goals and functional requirements; confusing Common Structural Rules with Continuous Synopsis Record, or with CSM (Continuous Survey Machinery).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

The bulk carrier losses of the 1980s–90s — Derbyshire (1980) above all, and the run of side-shell and hold-flooding losses that followed — drove the structural agenda that produced the Enhanced Survey Programme, SOLAS Chapter XII, PSPC and ultimately CSR. The GBS philosophy that a ship should stay safe across a specified design life if properly operated and maintained is the direct regulatory answer to hulls that failed well inside their intended lives.
