1
What are the common problems associated with container vessels?
CONTAINERSTABILITY

15-Second Answer

Cluster them: seakeeping/stability — parametric rolling and synchronous rolling (fine bow/wide stern hull forms), large GM variation with stack weight, whipping/springing of the long flexible hull; cargo — lashing failures and container loss, mis-declared weights (SOLAS VI/2 VGM) and mis-declared dangerous goods leading to deck/hold fires; fire-fighting limitation — inaccessible seat of fire inside boxes; machinery/operation — high-load 2-stroke plants on tight schedules, EGCS/fuel-switch demands in ECAs, and bridge-visibility/air-draught constraints from high deck stacks.

60-Second Answer

Parametric rolling is the signature hazard: with a fine bow, flared sections and a broad transom, the waterplane — and hence GM — varies strongly as waves pass; when the encounter period is near half the natural roll period (head/following seas), roll can build to 30°+ in a few cycles, generating the lashing-failure and stack-collapse casualties (APL China 1998; more recently ONE Apus 2020, ~1,800 boxes lost). Cargo integrity: mis-declared weight was answered by the SOLAS VI/2 verified gross mass (VGM) requirement (in force 1 July 2016 — no VGM, no load); mis-declared dangerous goods (calcium hypochlorite, charcoal, lithium batteries) drives the major fires (MSC Flaminia 2012, Maersk Honam 2018, X-Press Pearl 2021), and IMDG segregation only works if the declaration is honest. Fire-fighting: a fire inside a stack is shielded from CO₂/water; boundary cooling and early external help are the realistic responses, which is why SOLAS II-2/10.7.3 added water-mist lances and mobile monitors for ships designed to carry containers on deck (builds on/after 1 Jan 2016). Add hull-girder whipping and springing fatigue on ultra-large ships (MSC Napoli 2007 and the MOL Comfort 2013 hull-girder failure are the case studies), bridge visibility limits under SOLAS V/22 constraining stack heights, wind heel and increased windage in port manoeuvring, and the commercial reality — minimal port stays pressing maintenance windows. A CE frames each problem with its mitigation: route/heading change and roll-damping awareness, VGM and lashing plan compliance (Cargo Securing Manual, SOLAS VI/5.6), DG manifest verification, and drilled boundary-cooling strategies.

Governing instrument

SOLAS VI/2 (VGM, 1 Jul 2016), SOLAS II-2/10.7 (container-ship fire provisions, builds ≥2016), MSC.1/Circ.1228 (avoiding dangerous situations in adverse weather); CSS Code and the ship's Cargo Securing Manual.

Key Numbers

  • VGM: SOLAS VI/2, in force 1 Jul 2016.
  • Parametric rolling trigger: encounter period ≈ ½ natural roll period.
  • II-2/10.7.3: water-mist lance + mobile monitors, container ships built ≥ 1 Jan 2016.

Trap point

Parametric is not synchronous rolling — GM fluctuation building at about half the roll period in head/following seas versus classic beam-sea resonance; naming the wrong mechanism fails the question.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Structure wins — "seakeeping, cargo, fire, machinery" — then go one level deep on parametric rolling (why GM varies, the half-period resonance) because that is the follow-up. Tie each problem to a named casualty and a named regulation; that converts a list into a management-level answer.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Parametric rolling puts sudden load swings on the main engine (racing, torque fluctuation) and demands rapid manual intervention with governor/load programmes; reefer loads size the auxiliary plant; ECA fuel changeovers and EGCS operation sit with the CE on the schedule the deck cannot slip.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Parametric vs synchronous rolling?" A: Synchronous — encounter period equals natural roll period, typically beam seas, classic resonance; parametric — restoring moment itself varies (GM fluctuation) at about twice roll frequency, head/following seas. Q: "What did SOLAS do about mis-declared weight?" A: VGM, VI/2, 1 Jul 2016 — no verified mass, container shall not be loaded. Q: "Can you flood a hold for a container fire?" A: Consider stability/hull stress first — free surface and weight; it is a masters'-level decision with the CE calculating.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Unstructured lists; explaining parametric rolling as ordinary resonance without the GM-variation mechanism; no casualty names; no regulatory hooks (VGM, II-2/10.7, CSM); ignoring the machinery consequences.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

VGM 1 Jul 2016; II-2/10.7.3 builds ≥ 1 Jan 2016; parametric threshold Tₑ ≈ Tᵣ/2; ONE Apus ~1,800 containers lost (Nov 2020).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

APL China (1998) — parametric rolling benchmark; MSC Flaminia (2012) & Maersk Honam (2018) — DG fires with fatalities; MOL Comfort (2013) — hull-girder failure of a 8,000+ TEU ship; X-Press Pearl (2021) — DG fire and environmental disaster; ONE Apus (2020) — weather-routeing/stack-collapse.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: weather-routeing with parametric-roll polar warnings, lashing software linked to the approved CSM, reefer-load management, and drilled deck-fire response with mist lances and mobile monitors per II-2/10.7 — the daily face of every cluster above.

Difference between Type 1 and Type 2 chemical tankers.
CHEMICALIBC

15-Second Answer

IBC Code Ch. 2 ship types graded by cargo hazard and survival capability. Type 1: products with very severe environmental and safety hazards — maximum preventive measures: cargo tanks outside B/5 (max 11.5 m) from the side and B/15 (max 6 m) from the bottom, survives the most onerous damage assumptions, tank size limited to 1,250 m³. Type 2: appreciably severe hazards — significant measures: tanks at least 760 mm from the shell, B/15 from bottom, tank size limited to 3,000 m³. (Type 3 completes the scale — no special tank-location requirement.) The ship type each product needs is column (e) of IBC Ch. 17.

60-Second Answer

The logic is containment survivability proportional to consequence: the worse the product, the further its tanks sit from plausible collision/grounding damage and the smaller any single release can be. Type 1 ships must survive damage anywhere in their length; the tank-location minima (B/5 side, B/15 bottom, with the metre caps) keep cargo out of the statistical damage-penetration zone, and the 1,250 m³ per-tank cap limits the worst single-tank release. Type 2 relaxes location to 760 mm from the shell plating and caps tanks at 3,000 m³; damage-stability assumptions also grade by ship length. In practice very few full Type-1 products remain in Ch. 17; most modern parcel tankers are certified Type 2/Type 3 with stainless or coated tanks, and one ship commonly holds multiple type entries on its Certificate of Fitness — the certificate plus the approved P&A Manual and the ship's Ch. 17 product list define exactly what may be carried in which tank. The examiner's follow-up is always an example: Type 1 — e.g. certain phosphorus/propylene oxide-class entries; Type 2 — the bulk of common solvents/acids (always answer "per column (e) of Chapter 17 for the specific product" rather than guessing).

Governing instrument

IBC Code Ch. 2 (ship types and survivability) with Ch. 17 column (e) assignment — mandatory via SOLAS VII and MARPOL Annex II; current text per MEPC.318(74)/MSC.460(101), in force 1 Jan 2021.

Key Numbers

  • Type 1: B/5 (≤11.5 m) side, B/15 (≤6 m) bottom, tank ≤ 1,250 m³.
  • Type 2: ≥760 mm from shell, B/15 bottom, tank ≤ 3,000 m³.
  • Assignment: IBC Ch. 17, column (e); certification: Certificate of Fitness (IBC 1.5).

Trap point

Ship type (IBC safety/survivability) is not Category X/Y/Z (MARPOL pollution) — a product carries both labels answering different questions; and a higher type may carry lower-type cargo, never the reverse.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): Give the principle first — "containment survivability graded to cargo hazard" — then the two number sets. When Nair asks "which chemicals need Type 1?", the safe management answer is the method: check column (e) of Ch. 17 and the ship's Certificate of Fitness; quoting a memorised product wrongly is worse than quoting the lookup.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Ship type drives the machinery the CE runs: tank-material limits and heating/cooling ranges per the P&A Manual, pump-room ventilation and gas detection, and damage-stability documentation kept with the loading computer — the CE co-owns the certificate conditions with the master.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Where is a product's ship type found?" A: IBC Ch. 17 column (e). Q: "Can a Type 2 ship carry a Type 3 cargo?" A: Yes — higher type covers lower; never the reverse. Q: "What limits a Type 1 tank?" A: 1,250 m³ and the B/5 / B/15 location minima. Q: "Who issues the Certificate of Fitness?" A: Flag or RO under IBC 1.5.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Swapping the 1,250/3,000 m³ caps; forgetting the 760 mm Type-2 figure; claiming ship type is about tank material rather than location/survivability; unable to name where product-to-type assignment lives.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

B/5 ≤11.5 m; B/15 ≤6 m; 760 mm; 1,250 m³; 3,000 m³; IBC Chs. 2 (types), 17 (table), 1.5 (certification).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

The type system embodies pre-IBC chemical-spill experience: collision releases of highly toxic parcels drove the location-plus-size philosophy — small maximum releases, tanks clear of the damage zone — formalised when SOLAS/MARPOL made the Code mandatory (1986 builds onward).

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: Type 1 vs 2 → the numbers → where assignment is found → Certificate of Fitness → can Type 2 carry Type 1 cargo (no) → P&A Manual role.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Container-vessel candidates: answer from the Code, then bridge honestly — "my DG exposure is packaged IMDG, not bulk IBC" — and show the parallel: segregation and quantity limits serve the same release-limitation philosophy.

3
What different chemicals are carried, and where do you refer if in doubt about a chemical?
CHEMICALIBC

15-Second Answer

Families: petrochemicals (aromatics — benzene, toluene, xylene; olefins), alcohols & glycols (methanol, ethanol, MEG), acids & caustics (sulphuric, phosphoric, caustic soda), vegetable/animal oils and fats, lube-oil additives and esters, molten products (sulphur, asphalt). If in doubt: IBC Code Ch. 17/18 (carriage requirements per product), the ship's Certificate of Fitness product list, the approved P&A Manual, the shipper's MSDS/SDS, and for products not yet in the Code, the latest MEPC.2/Circular provisional/tripartite assessments — backed by the ICS Tanker Safety Guide (Chemicals) and company/charterer vetting desks.

60-Second Answer

Structure the cargo universe by hazard driver: flammability (aromatics, alcohols), toxicity (benzene, acrylonitrile, phenol), corrosivity (acids, caustics), reactivity/self-reaction (styrene monomer and other inhibited cargoes, propylene oxide), pollution category X/Y/Z under MARPOL Annex II, and carriage-condition demands (heated molten sulphur/asphalt, high-melting-point veg oils). The reference chain in doubt is hierarchical and the examiner wants it in order. (1) Is the product listed — IBC Ch. 17 (dangerous/noxious, full requirement line: ship type, tank type, venting, gauging, fire protection, special requirements in Ch. 15) or Ch. 18 (non-dangerous NLS); if unlisted, it may not be carried until assessed — the MEPC.2/Circ annual circular publishes provisional classifications and tripartite agreements between flag, port and shipping states. (2) May THIS ship carry it — Certificate of Fitness attachment list and the P&A Manual (tank coatings/materials compatibility, heating limits, cleaning and prewash requirements). (3) How to handle it — shipper's SDS (mandatory before loading), compatibility chart for adjacent-tank stowage (e.g. USCG-style compatibility grouping), inhibitor certificate where required (Ch. 15.13), and the ICS Tanker Safety Guide (Chemicals). Never load on verbal assurance: no listing + no assessment = no carriage.

Governing instrument

IBC Code Chs. 17/18, MARPOL Annex II Reg 6 (Categories X/Y/Z) and Reg 14 (P&A Manual); the annual MEPC.2/Circular for products not yet in the Code.

Key Forms

  • Certificate of Fitness + product attachment list (IBC 1.5).
  • P&A Manual (MARPOL Annex II Reg 14) — approved, ship-specific.
  • SDS per cargo; inhibitor certificate for inhibited cargoes; MEPC.2/Circ for unlisted products.

Trap point

Assuming the SDS authorises carriage is the fail — a product absent from Chs. 17/18 with no tripartite/MEPC.2 assessment simply may not be carried.

⚖ Regulatory References
IBC Code Ch. 17 & 18Summary of minimum requirements; list of products to which the Code does not apply
IBC Code Ch. 15Special requirements — inhibited cargoes, propylene oxide, acids, etc.
MARPOL Annex II Regs 6 & 14Categorization of NLS (X/Y/Z); Procedures & Arrangements Manual
MEPC.2/Circ (annual)Provisional categorization and tripartite assessment of products not in the Code
SOLAS VII / MARPOL Annex IIMandatory status of the IBC Code
CE Oral Tip (Nair): The question is really "what is your lookup discipline?" Give families in one breath, then the ordered chain: Ch. 17 line → Certificate of Fitness → P&A Manual → SDS → MEPC.2/Circ if unlisted. Saying "if it is not listed and not assessed, it does not load" is the sentence Nair is waiting for.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Every Ch. 17 line lands on CE systems: tank heating coils to SDS limits, nitrogen padding, pump-room gas detection matched to the product, cargo-pump seals and materials per the P&A Manual, and prewash/slop handling under Annex II — cargo chemistry read as machinery constraints.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Cargo not in Ch. 17 — can you carry it?" A: Only after assessment — check the current MEPC.2/Circ tripartite/provisional entry naming the flag; otherwise no. Q: "What are categories X, Y, Z?" A: MARPOL Annex II pollution categories (X worst — prewash mandatory) — distinct from ship types 1/2/3. Q: "Where are heating and tank-material limits?" A: P&A Manual and the certificate documentation, product by product.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Random chemical name-dropping with no families; jumping straight to "MSDS" without the Code chain; confusing pollution categories with ship types; not knowing MEPC.2/Circ exists for unlisted products.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

IBC Ch. 17 (listed) / 18 (non-dangerous NLS) / 15 (special requirements); Annex II Reg 6 (X/Y/Z), Reg 13 (discharge/prewash), Reg 14 (P&A Manual); MEPC.2/Circ issued annually (December).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Stolt Groenland (Ulsan, 2019) — styrene monomer runaway polymerisation and explosion in port: inhibited-cargo management, temperature monitoring and the Ch. 15/SDS discipline this question tests, in one casualty.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: What chemicals → reference in doubt → unlisted product → X/Y/Z vs ship type → inhibited cargo controls → prewash rules → who approves the P&A Manual.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

From a container ship, translate: the IMDG declaration + segregation table is my Ch. 17; the DG manifest is my certificate attachment; the discipline — no verified paperwork, no loading — is identical.

After ship delivery, how do you verify tank cleaning is proper (first-time tank cleaning machine operation)?
CHEMICALTANK CLEANING

15-Second Answer

Three layers of verification. Coverage: confirm the fixed machines actually wash the whole tank — run against the maker's/yard's shadow diagram for each machine position, checking drop lengths, nozzle rotation cycle and wash pressure against the P&A Manual/maker data. Process evidence: monitor wash-water supply pressure/temperature and machine rotation (audible cycle/portable check), stripping performance and tank-atmosphere readings. Result evidence: physical inspection (dry, odour-free, no residues/scale in way of shadows, sumps and under heating coils) and, for high-purity first cargoes, a surveyor's wall-wash test — methanol wipe samples analysed for chlorides, hydrocarbons, permanganate time and appearance against the charterer's specification.

60-Second Answer

A newly delivered ship has never proven its cleaning system, so treat the first operation as commissioning, not routine. Before washing: study the tank drawings and the cleaning-machine shadow diagrams — every fixed machine has zones blocked by structure (stringers, heating coils, pump stack); the diagram tells you where portable machines or hand work must supplement. Verify machine drive type and cycle time, programmed drop steps, design throughput and pressure from maker data, and line up per the P&A Manual. During washing: confirm each machine is actually rotating/indexing (listen at the deck flange or use the indicator), hold wash pressure and temperature at the specified values, strip continuously so pooling does not mask coverage, and log the sequence — cold/hot seawater, chemical additive stage if used, fresh-water rinse, then steaming/drying as the next cargo demands. After washing: enclosed-space entry under permit, physical inspection of shadow areas, sumps, behind coils and the aft bulkhead; check for loose mill-scale/coating defects typical of a new ship. For a first high-spec cargo (e.g. methanol-class purity), an independent cargo surveyor performs the wall-wash: solvent wiped over defined plate areas, lab-tested — chlorides (ppm), hydrocarbons, permanganate time (min), colour/appearance — against the charterer specification; failures map back to specific machines/shadows, closing the loop on the coverage verification. Acceptance values are cargo- and charterer-specific — quote the method, not universal numbers.

Governing instrument

MARPOL Annex II Regs 13/14 and the ship's approved P&A Manual (cleaning/prewash procedures); wall-wash acceptance figures are charterer/commercial criteria, not statutory values.

Trap Warning

  • Wall-wash acceptance limits are commercial/charterer figures, not statutory — never quote them as regulation.
  • Shadow diagram first — a passed wall-wash on accessible plating proves nothing about un-washed shadow zones.
  • Tank entry only under the enclosed-space permit system (SOLAS III/19 drills context; company procedure).

Trap point

Quoting wall-wash limits as regulation is the trap — chlorides/hydrocarbons/permanganate-time acceptance figures are charterer commercial criteria, never statutory values.

MARPOL Annex II Reg 14 (P&A Manual)Approved procedures for washing, prewash and residue handling — the governing ship document
MARPOL Annex II Reg 13 & Appendix VIDischarge control and prewash procedures where applicable
Maker manuals / yard shadow diagramsMachine coverage, cycle, pressure/throughput — ship-specific controlling data
ICS Tanker Safety Guide (Chemicals)Industry-standard cleaning and inspection practice
Company SMS / enclosed-space proceduresPermit-to-work regime for post-wash inspection entries
CE Oral Tip (Nair): The word the examiner wants is shadow diagram — open with it. Then the three-layer frame (coverage / process / result) and the wall-wash parameters by name (chlorides, hydrocarbons, permanganate time). Close with "acceptance values are charterer-specific; my job is proving coverage and process per the P&A Manual".
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE owns the hardware proof: tank-cleaning pump/heater performance to spec, machine drive condition, stripping/eductor performance, and the steam/FW consumption plan — on a new ship, first-operation data becomes the baseline the PMS trends against for the ship's life.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "What is permanganate time?" A: A wall-wash purity test — time for KMnO₄ colour to fade in the sample; shorter time indicates oxidisable contamination; limit per charterer spec. Q: "Machine not rotating — how would you know?" A: Cycle sound/vibration at the deck stand, indexing indicator where fitted, and ultimately an uncleaned sector at inspection matching that machine's arc. Q: "Who accepts the tank?" A: Commercially, the cargo surveyor against the charterer spec; the P&A Manual governs the statutory side.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

No mention of shadow diagrams; quoting wall-wash limits as statutory; skipping the during-wash process checks; forgetting enclosed-space permits for inspection; treating a new ship like a proven one.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Method names, not figures: chlorides · hydrocarbons · permanganate time · appearance; all acceptance values [ship-/charterer-specific] per the cargo specification and P&A Manual.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Cargo-contamination claims are the chemical trade's silent casualties — multi-million-dollar off-spec cargoes traced to shadow-zone residues or wet tanks; and tank-entry fatalities during post-wash inspection keep this question welded to the enclosed-space regime.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: First-time verification → shadow diagram → during-wash checks → wall-wash parameters → who sets limits → what if one sector fails → enclosed-space entry controls.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Container-vessel bridge to the concept: commissioning verification of any new system — first ballast-tank coating survey, first EGCS washwater run — follows the same coverage/process/result discipline with maker data as the yardstick.

How do you carry out an onboard test of cargo under IMSBC?
IMSBCBULK

15-Second Answer

The "can test" — the complementary shipboard check in IMSBC Code Sec. 8 for Group A (liquefiable) cargoes: half fill a cylindrical can or jar (~0.5–1 litre) with a sample, and bring it down sharply against a hard surface from about 0.2 m, 25 times at 1–2 second intervals. If free moisture or a fluid condition appears on the surface, the moisture content may exceed the Transportable Moisture Limit — do NOT load; require additional laboratory testing. A "passed" can test does not prove safety — it can only raise the alarm; the governing documents remain the shipper's declaration with moisture content and TML certificates (Sec. 4).

60-Second Answer

Group A cargoes (concentrates, nickel ore, certain fines) can liquefy: cyclic vibration and engine/sea motion compact the granular mass, pore-water pressure rises, and a solid-behaving cargo becomes a dense fluid that shifts to one side — the free-surface effect capsizes the ship, often too fast for the crew (Bulk Jupiter 2015, nickel-ore losses off Indonesia/Philippines). The statutory defence is documentary: the shipper's declaration of moisture content (sampled per Sec. 4.5, within 7 days of loading, re-sampled after significant rain) against a certified TML (test within 6 months), with moisture required below TML — the TML itself being 90% of the Flow Moisture Point from laboratory tests (flow-table and alternatives, Appendix 2). The can test is the master's cross-check when appearance raises doubt — visual free moisture, cargo splattering, rain during loading. Procedure exactly as the Code states it; interpretation in the Code's own words: if free moisture or fluid conditions appear, the master should arrange additional laboratory tests before accepting the cargo — and the master's SOLAS right to refuse doubtful cargo (VI/7.2 context) backs the decision commercially. State clearly what the test cannot do: a dry-looking can does not certify moisture below TML; only the lab does that. Follow-through onboard: trim per the schedule, monitor for splatter/fluid state during the voyage where accessible, and treat any observed cargo surface wetness as an emergency stability review.

Governing instrument

IMSBC Code Sec. 8.4 (can test) with Secs. 4.3/4.5 (TML = 90% of FMP; moisture certificate ≤7 days, TML certificate ≤6 months) — mandatory via SOLAS VI.

Key Numbers

  • Can test: half-filled ~0.5–1 L can, ~0.2 m drop, 25 strikes, 1–2 s intervals (IMSBC Sec. 8.4).
  • TML = 90% of FMP; moisture certificate sampling ≤ 7 days before loading; TML test ≤ 6 months.
  • Groups: A liquefy · B chemical hazard · C neither.

Trap point

The can test can only condemn, never clear — a satisfactory appearance does not authorise loading; only the laboratory TML/moisture certificates (≤6 months / ≤7 days) do.

⚖ Regulatory References
IMSBC Code Sec. 8 (8.4)Test procedures for cargoes that may liquefy — complementary shipboard can test
IMSBC Code Sec. 4Shipper's declaration, moisture certificate, TML certificate, sampling intervals
IMSBC Code Sec. 7 & Appendix 2Liquefaction mechanism; laboratory FMP/TML test methods
SOLAS VI & VIIMandatory status of the IMSBC Code for bulk cargoes
CE Oral Tip (Paul): Recite the procedure with its numbers (half-fill, 0.2 m, 25 strikes, 1–2 s), then immediately give the limitation sentence — "it can only trigger lab testing, never clear a cargo" — and the paperwork chain (declaration, moisture cert 7 days, TML cert 6 months, TML = 90% FMP). That combination is a complete Paul answer.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Liquefaction is a stability casualty the engine room feels first — unexplained list, heavy autopilot/rudder demand, rolling character change. The CE's contributions: ballast readiness for corrective action, weather-tight hold bilge monitoring (rising hold-bilge water = pore water draining), and honest fuel/stability data for the master's decisions.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Can test passed — safe to load?" A: No — it cannot verify moisture below TML; only laboratory certification does. Q: "What is TML?" A: Transportable Moisture Limit = 90% of the Flow Moisture Point determined by lab test. Q: "It rains during loading?" A: Stop loading uncovered Group A cargo; re-check moisture — the certificate's sampling validity is broken by significant wetting.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Wrong mechanics (shaking instead of striking); missing the 25/0.2 m/1–2 s details; claiming the test certifies the cargo; not knowing TML = 90% FMP; unable to name Group A/B/C.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

25 strikes · 0.2 m · 1–2 s · TML = 0.9 × FMP · 7 days · 6 months; IMSBC Secs. 4, 7, 8.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Bulk Jupiter (2015, bauxite, 18 dead) and the nickel-ore losses from Indonesian/Philippine anchorages (multiple ships, 2010–2019) — wet-season loading with paper moisture certificates contradicted by the cargo's appearance; the can test exists exactly for that contradiction.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Paul: Onboard IMSBC test → procedure numbers → what a positive result means → TML/FMP relation → shipper documentation timing → master's refusal right → voyage monitoring.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Not applicable hardware on a container ship — answer from the Code and casualties; the transferable discipline is verification culture: paperwork that contradicts physical evidence loses, every time.

QB8_H · Q5 · v1.0
6
LPG cargo — reliquefaction temperature/pressure, plant operation, relief valve setting, discharge pressure for butane and propane.
GASLPGIGC

15-Second Answer

Fully refrigerated carriage keeps cargo at its atmospheric boiling point, tank pressure slightly above atmospheric: propane ≈ −42 °C, n-butane ≈ −0.5 °C. Boil-off from heat ingress is drawn off by the reliquefaction plant — cargo compressor(s) raise vapour pressure so it condenses against seawater in the cargo condenser; condensate returns via float/expansion valve to the tank. Relief valve (MARVS) setting and pump discharge pressures are ship-specific — MARVS per the IGC-approved design (fully refrigerated ships commonly of the order of 0.25 barg class; state "per my Certificate of Fitness/approved drawings"), discharge pressure per the approved pump curves and terminal manifold requirements in the cargo operating manual.

60-Second Answer

Physics first: a fully refrigerated tank is a boiling liquid in equilibrium — tank pressure fixes temperature. Heat ingress through insulation boils cargo; removing that vapour and returning it as liquid holds the state point. Single-stage direct cycle (butane, warm-condensing conditions): compressor suction from tank vapour space → discharge into seawater-cooled condenser → liquid receiver → expansion/float valve → return to tank spray line. Two-stage with interstage cooling (propane): higher compression ratio needed to condense −42 °C boil-off against tropical seawater; two-stage with intercooler limits discharge temperature and improves efficiency. Operation points the examiner probes: incondensables (nitrogen, air) collect in the condenser, raising condensing pressure — purge them; liquid carry-over to the compressor is prevented by suction knock-out/superheat control; oil management per maker; capacity control by speed/cylinder unloading against tank pressure. Relief valves: each cargo tank has at least two pressure-relief valves (IGC Ch. 8); the set pressure is the MARVS on the ship's certificate — a design value, not a universal number; pilot-operated valves with full-flow capacity for the fire case per IGC 8.4. Some ships carry dual-set arrangements (harbour/riser settings) — changeover is a documented, flag-notified procedure per the approved manual. Anchor every number you do give: propane −42 °C, butane ≈ 0 °C, and "MARVS per certificate".

QB8_H · Q6 · v1.0

Type of reliquefaction plant onboard.
GASLPG

15-Second Answer

Two families. Direct cycles — the cargo vapour itself is the working fluid: single-stage (butane and warmer cargoes), two-stage with intercooler (propane/ammonia against warm seawater), and cascade — cargo condensed against a separate closed refrigerant circuit (typically R404A-class) whose own cycle rejects heat to seawater; required for ethylene (−104 °C), where no practical single compression can condense against seawater. Indirect cycles — cargo is cooled without being compressed, via a refrigerant-cooled heat exchanger/coils; rare, reserved for cargoes unsuitable for compression (e.g. easily polymerising products such as certain butadiene/VCM operations per the ship's approval).

60-Second Answer

Answer as a selection logic, then say what YOUR ship has (or, from a container vessel, present the taxonomy and state your plant per the approved cargo operating manual — the manual and the Certificate of Fitness name the plant, its capacity and the cargoes it serves). Single-stage direct: compressor → SW condenser → receiver → expansion to tank; adequate while compression ratio stays modest — butane, and propane in cold-water trades. Two-stage direct: splits the ratio across LP and HP stages with an intercooler (often liquid-injection or open flash type), keeping discharge temperature within limits and improving volumetric efficiency — the standard fully-refrigerated propane/ammonia solution; ammonia additionally demands compatible materials (no copper alloys) and dedicated arrangements. Cascade: the cargo cycle condenses in a cargo condenser cooled not by seawater but by an evaporating closed-loop refrigerant; that refrigerant's own compressor/condenser rejects heat to seawater. This solves the ethylene problem — condensing at ≈ −104 °C needs a low-temperature sink no seawater condenser can offer. Indirect: tank coils or an external exchanger cooled by a refrigeration plant; the cargo never enters a compressor — chosen where compression risks polymerisation or contamination; capacity is limited, so it suits low heat-ingress designs. Semi-pressurised ships pair reliquefaction with pressure-capable Type C tanks, giving flexibility between pressurised and refrigerated carriage. The design driver in every case: condensing temperature vs available cooling medium, and cargo chemistry.

Governing instrument

IGC Code Chs. 5, 7 and 17; the fitted plant, capacity and approved cargo list are stated in the Certificate of Fitness and cargo operating manual.

Key Numbers

  • Ethylene b.p. ≈ −104 °C → cascade mandatory in practice; propane −42 °C → two-stage; butane ≈ 0 °C → single-stage.
  • Plant type, capacity, served cargoes: [ship-specific] — Certificate of Fitness & approved cargo operating manual.
  • Ammonia: no copper/copper-alloy components in cargo systems (IGC Ch. 17 special requirements).

Trap point

Missing WHY ethylene forces cascade (−104 °C cannot condense against seawater) — and forgetting the indirect cycle exists for polymerising cargoes — are the two standard gaps.

LNG cargo — carriage temperature and pressure; how is temperature maintained?
GASLNGIGC

15-Second Answer

LNG is carried fully refrigerated at about −162 °C, at near-atmospheric tank pressure — membrane tanks typically run a small positive gauge pressure of the order of 0.05–0.25 barg [ship-specific per the certificate/CTS]. Temperature is maintained by auto-refrigeration: the cargo is a boiling liquid, so heat ingress converts to latent heat of vaporisation rather than temperature rise; managing the resulting boil-off gas (~0.10–0.15%/day modern designs, design BOR ship-specific) — burning it in the propulsion plant (DF engines/GCU), reliquefying it where fitted, or controlled pressure accumulation — holds the state point. Insulation limits ingress; it cannot stop it.

60-Second Answer

Thermodynamic core (say this first): a liquid in equilibrium with its vapour sits at the saturation temperature for the tank pressure. At ~1 bar abs, methane saturates at −161.5 °C. Heat leaking through insulation cannot warm the bulk liquid while it boils — energy leaves as latent heat in the vapour. So "how is temperature maintained?" = "how is pressure maintained?" — remove vapour at the rate it forms and the temperature holds itself. The BOG destinations: (1) fuel — dual-fuel/ME-GI/X-DF propulsion or the gas combustion unit (GCU) when the plant cannot absorb it; IGC Ch. 16 permits methane as fuel; (2) onboard reliquefaction where fitted (nitrogen reverse-Brayton plants on large conventional ships); (3) a bounded pressure rise within tank limits toward MARVS as a short-term buffer. Supporting management: insulation system performance (membrane interbarrier-space nitrogen sweep, monitored for methane — leak detection and barrier inertisation in one), sloshing limits enforced through filling-level restrictions per the loading manual, cooldown/spray circuits keeping tanks cold on ballast passages using retained heel, and stripping/spray pumps circulating liquid to avoid stratification. Mention rollover awareness (stratified layers of differing density suddenly mixing with a BOG surge) — primarily a terminal-tank phenomenon but a known ship-cargo risk after loading LNGs of different densities; prevention is density management and circulation. Close with document discipline: exact operating pressure band, design BOR and filling limits are Certificate of Fitness / cargo operating manual values.

Governing instrument

IGC Code Chs. 7 (BOG management), 16 (methane as fuel) and 8 (relief/MARVS); ship-specific operating band, BOR and filling limits per the Certificate of Fitness and loading manual.

Key Numbers

  • −162 °C at ~atmospheric; methane saturation −161.5 °C @ 1.013 bar.
  • BOR: ~0.10–0.15%/day modern designs (older ships up to ~0.25) — design value [ship-specific].
  • Operating pressure band & MARVS: [ship-specific]; membrane ships near-atmospheric by design.

Trap point

Saying insulation 'keeps the LNG cold' is the trap — heat ingress leaves as latent heat of boil-off; temperature control IS pressure control, and venting is protection, not an operating method.

LNG containment/tank type — material, properties, structure and construction.
GASLNGIGC

15-Second Answer

IGC Ch. 4 families. Membrane (GTT — dominant today): thin metallic membrane carried by insulation on the inner hull — Mark III: 1.2 mm corrugated 304L stainless primary + reinforced-polyurethane-foam panels + Triplex secondary membrane; NO96: 0.7 mm Invar (36% Ni-Fe, near-zero thermal expansion) primary and secondary + plywood boxes of perlite/glass wool; both need a complete secondary barrier. Independent Type B: self-supporting, e.g. Moss spherical tanks (aluminium 5083 or 9% Ni steel) — refined stress/fatigue/crack-propagation analysis, so only a partial secondary barrier (drip tray + splash shield). Type C: pressure vessels (small-scale/bunker ships) — no secondary barrier. Type A (full secondary barrier) is the LPG prismatic solution, not used for LNG in practice.

60-Second Answer

Material logic first: at −162 °C ordinary carbon steel is far below its ductile–brittle transition — a crack propagates catastrophically. Cryogenic service therefore uses materials with no transition or transition well below service temperature: austenitic stainless (304L), 9% nickel steel, aluminium alloy 5083, and Invar where thermal contraction itself is the enemy. That is why each containment system pairs a material choice with a structural concept. Membrane systems put the load path through the insulation into the inner hull: Mark III's corrugations absorb thermal contraction geometrically in the stainless sheet; NO96 instead absorbs it metallurgically — Invar's expansion coefficient is ~1/10th of steel's, so flat strakes suffice. Interbarrier and insulation spaces are nitrogen-swept and methane-monitored — the secondary barrier (Triplex composite / second Invar skin) must hold cargo for the IGC-defined period (15 days basis) after a primary leak. Type B Moss: a sphere welded from aluminium petals (or 9% Ni plates), supported on an equatorial skirt that also manages the thermal short; because the design is verified by first-principles fatigue and crack-propagation analysis ("leak-before-failure"), IGC accepts a partial secondary barrier — a drip tray under the tank with the hold space inerted/monitored. Sloshing-tolerant (spheres have no flat free surfaces at critical fillings), at the cost of deck space and boil-off performance vs modern membranes. Type C: cylindrical/bilobe pressure vessels designed to full pressure-vessel code stresses; the accumulated-pressure capability makes them standard for bunker vessels and small-scale LNG. Construction quality lives in the details: membrane weld/glue integrity testing (global vacuum/ammonia tests per system), insulation-space tightness, and the pump tower hanging from the liquid dome with its own thermal design. Filling limits and sloshing restrictions are [ship-specific] loading-manual values.

Governing instrument

IGC Code Ch. 4 (containment systems; 4.7 secondary-barrier matrix and 15-day basis) and Ch. 6 (materials of construction); detailed containment approval per IACS/class rules.

Key Numbers

  • Mark III: 1.2 mm 304L corrugated; NO96: 0.7 mm Invar (36% Ni); Moss: Al 5083 / 9% Ni steel.
  • Secondary barrier: membrane & Type A — complete; Type B — partial; Type C — none. 15-day containment basis (IGC 4.7).
  • Invar α ≈ 1.2×10⁻⁶/K — ~an order below carbon steel; the whole reason NO96 works flat.

Trap point

The secondary-barrier matrix is the trap — membrane systems need a COMPLETE barrier, Type B earns a PARTIAL one through leak-before-failure analysis, Type C needs none; mixing these fails the question.

QB8_H · Q9 · v1.0

What is the difference between a chemical or oil-product tanker and a traditional crude oil tanker?
CHEMICALPRODUCTCRUDE
15-Second Answer

The difference follows from one fact: a crude carrier carries one dirty cargo in bulk, and a product or chemical carrier carries many clean cargoes at once, kept apart. From that come all the rest — coated or stainless tanks instead of bare steel; many small segregated tanks with individual deepwell pumps instead of a few large tanks on a common line; cargo heating; far more demanding tank cleaning and previous-cargo control; individual venting rather than a common system; and a Certificate of Fitness under the IBC Code that a crude tanker does not have.

60-Second Answer

Sir, I would put it as one difference with consequences rather than a list. A crude carrier is built around carrying a single, dirty, self-similar cargo in very large parcels: bare mild-steel tanks, a small number of large tanks, a common cargo line and a few big centrifugal pumps in a pump room, crude oil washing, and an inert gas system sized for the whole ship. Nothing about the cargo requires purity, so nothing about the ship is built to protect purity. A product or chemical carrier is built around the opposite problem: many different cargoes, carried simultaneously, each of which must arrive uncontaminated and none of which may meet another. So the tanks are coated — epoxy or zinc silicate for clean petroleum products, stainless steel for aggressive chemicals; there are many more of them and they are smaller; each usually has its own deepwell pump and its own line and its own vent, so that segregation is physical rather than procedural; heating coils are fitted for cargoes that must be kept warm; and tank cleaning, previous-cargo compatibility and wall-wash testing become a central part of the operation rather than a housekeeping task. On the regulatory side, a chemical tanker is certificated under the IBC Code through SOLAS Chapter VII and MARPOL Annex II, and carries an International Certificate of Fitness listing exactly which cargoes she may carry. A crude tanker works under MARPOL Annex I and carries an IOPP certificate.

1. The cargo, and why everything follows from it

Crude oil is a single dirty commodity moved in the largest possible parcels; its value does not depend on purity and a little residue from the last cargo is of no consequence. Clean products and chemicals are the reverse: value is purity, parcels are small and numerous, and residue from the previous cargo is the whole risk. Every design difference below is downstream of that one sentence, and saying it first turns a list into an argument.

2. Tank material and coating

  • Crude: historically bare mild steel — the cargo is not harmed by rust, and crude is mildly protective. That is no longer the modern position and it is a trap. SOLAS II-1/3-11 requires the cargo oil tanks of crude oil tankers of 5,000 DWT and above to be coated in accordance with the Performance Standard for Protective Coatings for cargo oil tanks, res. MSC.288(87), or protected by an alternative means or corrosion-resistant material giving equivalent 25-year protection under res. MSC.289(87) — for ships contracted on or after 1 January 2013. Separately, water ballast tanks are coated under the ballast-tank PSPC, res. MSC.215(82), through SOLAS II-1/3-2. Two different standards; do not merge them.
  • Clean products: epoxy or zinc-silicate coatings, to keep the cargo clean and to stop it picking up colour and iron.
  • Chemicals: stainless steel for aggressive cargoes — acids, alkalis, solvents — because no organic coating survives them. Stainless is why a chemical tanker costs what she costs, and coating or cladding compatibility is checked against the cargo before every fixture.

3. Segregation and pumping — the biggest operational difference

  • Crude: a few large tanks; a common cargo main; a small number of large centrifugal or steam-driven pumps in a dedicated pump room; crude oil washing with the tank's own cargo; stripping via eductors.
  • Inert gas is not a crude-only feature. SOLAS II-2/4.5.5 as amended by res. MSC.365(93) requires an inert gas system on tankers of 8,000 DWT and above constructed on or after 1 January 2016 carrying low-flashpoint cargoes — which brings modern product tankers in. Do not tell an examiner that product tankers have no IGS.
  • Product/chemical: many smaller tanks; typically an individual vertical shaft-driven deepwell pump, impeller at the tank bottom and driver on deck, and dedicated line per tank, so that several grades can be loaded and discharged simultaneously without ever sharing metal. Many modern chemical tankers have no pump room at all, which removes one of the most dangerous enclosed spaces on a traditional tanker — worth saying, because it is an engineering answer rather than a cargo one.
  • Slop and residue handling differs accordingly: Annex I slop tanks and the ODME on the crude carrier; Annex II prewash, residue and stripping-quantity requirements on the chemical carrier, verified against the P&A Manual.

4. Heating, cleaning and the previous cargo

  • Heating: crude may be heated to maintain pumpability; chemical and product cargoes are heated to a specified temperature range, and both overheating and local hot spots can ruin the cargo. Heating coils, their material and their integrity testing are a Chief Engineer's responsibility, and a leaking coil contaminates cargo and puts cargo into the boiler feed.
  • Tank cleaning: crude oil washing on the crude carrier; on the chemical carrier a full cleaning regime — water, chemicals, steam, drying — followed by wall-wash testing for hydrocarbons, chlorides, colour and permanganate time before the cargo is accepted.
  • Previous cargo control: the three-cargo history is a routine charterer's requirement on clean trades and simply does not arise on a crude carrier.

5. Venting, inerting and the atmosphere

The crude carrier has a common inert gas system and, typically, a common venting arrangement with the isolation controls that go with it. The chemical carrier's problem is different: cargoes that must not mix must not share a vapour space either, so venting is far more often individual per tank, and the inerting medium may be nitrogen rather than flue gas, because flue gas contains oxygen, CO2 and SOx that will react with or contaminate many chemical cargoes. Some cargoes need padding, some need drying, and some — the reactive and polymerising ones — need inhibitors with a certificate stating the inhibitor, its concentration and its expiry.

6. The certificate regime

  • Crude tanker: MARPOL Annex I; IOPP Certificate with Form B supplement; ODME, slop tanks, COW and segregated ballast requirements; SOLAS II-2 tanker fire provisions and inert gas.
  • Chemical tanker: SOLAS Chapter VII Part B making the IBC Code mandatory, plus MARPOL Annex II; the International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Dangerous Chemicals in Bulk, which names the permitted cargoes; ship types 1, 2 and 3 by hazard, with survival and tank-location requirements to match; and the P&A Manual.
  • A clean product tanker that carries only petroleum products sits under Annex I and does not need a Certificate of Fitness — but many are built as IMO type 2 or 3 chemical tankers precisely so that they can. That distinction is a common cross-question, and QB8_H#q2 covers the type partition in detail.

The two that mark you as an engineer rather than a reader are individual deepwell pumps and often no pump room, and nitrogen rather than flue gas for inerting. Finish on the certificate, because that is the crispest legal difference: Certificate of Fitness under the IBC Code, against an IOPP certificate. Do not attempt an encyclopaedia of tanker types — he asked for a difference.
CE Relevance, Traps, Failures & Exam Strategy
CE Relevance

The chemical or product carrier loads far more onto the engineering department than a crude carrier does. Deepwell pumps mean many more hydraulic or electric drives and many more seals; heating coils mean integrity testing and the constant risk of cargo in the condensate; nitrogen generation is a plant to run and maintain; and tank cleaning is a heavy consumer of steam, water and pumping capacity on a short port turnaround. If asked what is harder about the ship, those four are the honest answer.

Trap Questions
  • Examiner: “Why stainless steel and not a coating?” Because no organic coating survives aggressive cargoes such as strong acids and some solvents. Coatings are for clean petroleum products and the milder chemicals; stainless is for the rest.
  • Examiner: “Why not use your inert gas system on a chemical cargo?” Flue gas carries oxygen, CO2 and SOx, which react with or contaminate many chemical cargoes. Nitrogen is used instead.
  • Examiner: “Is a clean product tanker a chemical tanker?” Not necessarily. Carrying only petroleum products she is an Annex I ship. Many are nevertheless built and certificated as IMO type 2 or 3 so that they can carry Annex II cargoes as well.
  • Examiner: “What is wall-wash testing for?” To prove the tank is clean enough for a sensitive cargo — hydrocarbons, chlorides, colour and permanganate time — before the surveyor accepts the tank.
  • Examiner: “Which certificate does each carry?” IOPP for the crude carrier; the International Certificate of Fitness under the IBC Code for the chemical carrier, naming the permitted cargoes.
Common CE Failures

Listing differences with no organising idea, so the answer never converges. Saying “chemical tankers have coatings” without knowing which coating for which cargo class. Not knowing what a deepwell pump is or why it exists. Missing the pump-room point entirely, which is the most engineering-relevant difference of all. Claiming the IGC Code — that is gas, not chemicals. Forgetting that a crude carrier does crude oil washing and treating tank cleaning as the same operation on both ships.

Numbers to Memorise

IBC Code mandatory through SOLAS Ch. VII Part B for ships built on or after 1 July 1986; the BCH Code before that. Crude cargo-tank coating: SOLAS II-1/3-11, MSC.288(87) / MSC.289(87), crude oil tankers 5,000 DWT and above contracted on or after 1 January 2013. Inert gas: 8,000 DWT from 1 January 2016, MSC.365(93). Ship types 1, 2, 3. MARPOL Annex II categories X, Y, Z, OS. Crude side: Annex I, IOPP with Form B supplement, ODME, COW, segregated ballast.

Casualty Link

The chemical trade's characteristic casualties are contamination and reaction rather than large spills: a leaking heating coil putting cargo into the condensate system, an inhibitor that expired before the voyage ended and allowed a cargo to polymerise, a wrongly opened valve on a shared line, or a cargo loaded into a tank whose previous cargo was incompatible. Every one of them is a segregation failure, which is why segregation on these ships is built into the steel rather than left to a procedure.

On My Vessel

The two documents that actually govern the day are the Certificate of Fitness, because it bounds what may be loaded at all, and the P&A Manual, because it bounds what may be discharged afterwards. Heating-coil integrity is pressure-tested before a heated cargo rather than after a contamination claim, and the previous-cargo history is checked against the coating resistance list before the fixture is accepted, not after the tanks have been cleaned for it.
