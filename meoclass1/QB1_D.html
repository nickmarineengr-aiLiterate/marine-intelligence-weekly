What was the GRT and DWT of your last vessel?
Tonnage Senthil (Int)
⏱ 15-Second Answer "My last vessel, a Maersk container ship, had a Gross Tonnage (GT) of approximately 95,000 and a Deadweight Tonnage (DWT) of 110,000 tonnes. GT is a dimensionless number representing the ship's total enclosed volume, whereas DWT is the actual physical weight in tonnes the ship can carry when loaded to her summer marks."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, on my last vessel, the GT was [insert exact number] and DWT was [insert exact number]. GT is a mathematical, dimensionless index derived from the total moulded volume of all enclosed spaces under the Tonnage 1969 Convention. It dictates the applicability of MARPOL and SOLAS regulations, manning scales, and port dues. DWT is a measure of the ship's physical weight-carrying capacity in metric tonnes — calculated as summer load line displacement minus the lightship weight. As Chief Engineer, I monitor GT closely because it determines which statutory thresholds apply to my engine room, such as MARPOL Annex I OWS capacity requirements."

Gross and Net Tonnage (TONNAGE 69)

Under the International Convention on Tonnage Measurement of Ships, 1969 (TONNAGE 69), the obsolete terms GRT (Gross Register Tonnage) and NRT (Net Register Tonnage) were replaced by GT and NT. [owaysonline]

  • Gross Tonnage (GT): A dimensionless index indicating the overall size of the vessel based on the moulded volume of all enclosed spaces. The formula is GT = K₁V. [treaties.un]
  • Net Tonnage (NT): A dimensionless index indicating the vessel's earning capacity based on the moulded volume of cargo spaces. The convention dictates that NT cannot be less than 30% of the GT. [imo]

Deadweight Tonnage (DWT)

Deadweight is the actual weight a ship can carry in metric tonnes. It includes cargo, fuel, lubricating oil, fresh water, ballast water, provisions, passengers, and crew.

DWT = Displacement at Summer Load Line − Lightship Displacement

Why Vessel Types Differ

Container ships have large enclosed cargo holds and deckhouses, giving them a very high GT relative to their DWT. Conversely, bulk carriers carry high-density cargo, resulting in a DWT that is numerically much higher than their GT. [owaysonline]

🔧 CE Relevance

While the Master handles the commercial aspect of DWT (cargo loading), the CE uses GT to determine statutory compliance. GT thresholds dictate whether the vessel requires an IOPP certificate (≥ 400 GT), an IAPP certificate (≥ 400 GT), or compliance with the ISM Code (≥ 500 GT).

⚠ Trap Questions

"What are the units for Gross Tonnage?" — GT has no physical units; it is a strictly dimensionless mathematical index number. [owaysonline]

"Why did the industry move from GRT to GT?" — GRT used a system of exemptions that allowed ship designers to exploit loopholes (e.g., leaving shelter decks "open" to avoid tonnage taxes). GT provides a universal, mathematically rigorous formula without exemptions.

✗ Common CE Failures
  • Failing to recall own ship's particulars accurately — examiners use this as an instant credibility check
  • Confusing GT (dimensionless index) with GRT (100 cubic feet of volume), or giving GT in "tonnes"
🔢 Numbers & Regulations to Memorise
  • TONNAGE 69: Entry into force 18 July 1982
  • Formula: GT = K₁V (V = total enclosed volume in m³, K₁ = 0.2 + 0.02 log₁₀V)
  • NT Limit: NT ≥ 0.30 × GT
⚓ Casualty Link

Not directly casualty-driven — TONNAGE 69 arose from the need for a uniform international measurement system rather than a specific incident, replacing conflicting national tonnage rules.

Lifting appliance latest amendments — what changed and how were they previously checked?
SOLAS Ref Paul (Ext)
⏱ 15-Second Answer "SOLAS Chapter II-1 Regulation 3-13 mandates that all shipboard lifting appliances undergo mandatory statutory survey, including annual inspections and 5-yearly proof load tests witnessed by Class. Previously, lifting appliances were not covered by SOLAS and were only checked under ILO Convention 152 and individual Class rules."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, effective 1 January 2026, SOLAS II-1/3-13 brought shipboard lifting appliances under statutory regulation to prevent severe crane accidents. The amendment requires design approval, permanent Safe Working Load (SWL) marking, and a mandatory 'Register of Ship's Lifting Appliances.' Equipment must undergo an annual thorough examination and a 5-yearly proof load test — typically to 1.25 times the SWL — witnessed by a Class surveyor. Before this, compliance was fragmented, relying primarily on ILO Convention C152 (which focused on dockworkers) and various classification society guidelines, leaving non-cargo engine room cranes with inconsistent oversight."

The SOLAS Amendment (Chapter II-1 Reg 3-13)

The new regulation applies to all permanently installed lifting appliances and anchor handling winches on ships. Introduced to combat the high rate of catastrophic crane failures, it shifts lifting appliances from an operational concern to a statutory safety requirement. [marinetegrity]

  • New Appliances: Must be designed, installed, and tested in accordance with Class rules before first use. [bureauveritas]
  • Existing Appliances (Pre-2026): Must comply with the examination, load testing, and marking requirements no later than the first SAFCON renewal survey on or after 1 January 2026.

Survey and Register Requirements

Under the guidelines of MSC.1/Circ.1663, lifting appliances require:

  • Annual Thorough Examination: A detailed visual check of structural integrity, wire ropes, and safety limits (limit switches, brakes). [eagle]
  • 5-Yearly Proof Load Test: The minimum test load is not a flat 1.25× SWL — it is determined from Table 1, paragraph 3.2.1.5 of MSC.1/Circ.1663, based on the appliance's nominated SWL, and witnessed by a recognized Class surveyor.
  • Record Keeping: The CE must maintain the mandatory Register of Ship's Lifting Appliances and Cargo Handling Gear — all loose gear must be certified, proof-tested, and logged. [bureauveritas]
  • Rigging Plan & Block List: A rigging plan and block list showing the correct reeving/rigging arrangement for the appliance and its loose gear must be kept on board alongside the Certificate of Test and Thorough Examination.

Indian Flag Implementation — DGMA MS Notice No. 02 of 2026

For Kochi MMD candidates specifically: DGMA (formerly DG Shipping) issued Merchant Shipping Notice No. 02 of 2026 implementing SOLAS II-1/3-13 for Indian-flag vessels. It confirms that prior certification under ILO C152 or the Dock Workers (Safety, Health and Welfare) Regulations, 1990 remains accepted until the first SAFCON renewal survey on or after 1 January 2026, and specifies which competent persons/RO surveyors may conduct the thorough examination under Indian flag. [DGMA MS Notice 02/2026, PDF]

⚠ Exam Trap Does the new SOLAS rule apply to a small 500 kg SWL engine room monorail? — Appliances with an SWL below 1,000 kg are exempt from strict design approval, but must still be marked with their SWL and incorporated into the SMS.

"If your engine room crane wire parts, can you replace it and lift loads immediately?" — You can replace it with an identical, certified wire rope; log the new certificate and perform a functional test. A full 5-yearly proof load test is only triggered by major structural repairs, not routine wire replacement.

Lifting appliance survey — how is it conducted and what are the requirements?
Survey Paul (Ext)
⏱ 15-Second Answer "A lifting appliance survey under SOLAS II-1/3-13 consists of an annual thorough examination and a 5-yearly proof load test witnessed by a Class surveyor. The CE must ensure all appliances are marked with their Safe Working Load (SWL) and maintain the statutory 'Register of Ship's Lifting Appliances and Cargo Handling Gear' onboard."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, under SOLAS II-1 Reg 3-13 and MSC.1/Circ.1663, lifting appliances require two main survey interventions. First is the annual thorough examination — a detailed visual and operational check of the crane's structure, wire ropes, hooks, sheaves, and limit switches. Second is the 5-yearly proof load test, typically at 1.25 times the SWL, witnessed and certified by a Class surveyor acting as the flag state's Recognized Organization. As CE, my responsibility is to align these surveys with the PMS, ensure SWL is permanently marked on all equipment, and keep the mandatory Lifting Appliance Register updated with valid certificates for the crane and all associated loose gear."

Annual Thorough Examination

According to MSC.1/Circ.1663, the annual thorough examination must be conducted by a competent person (often authorized shore-based personnel or a Class surveyor, depending on flag rules). This examination verifies: [dnv]

  • The structural integrity of the crane jib, slewing ring, and foundation
  • The condition of wire ropes, hooks, and sheaves against discard criteria
  • The correct operation of safety devices — limit switches, slack wire switches, overload alarms

5-Yearly Proof Load Test

  • The Load: Not a flat multiplier — the minimum test load is determined from Table 1, paragraph 3.2.1.5 of MSC.1/Circ.1663 against the appliance's nominated SWL. 1.25× SWL is a commonly-seen figure at typical engine room/provision crane capacities but should not be quoted as a universal rule.
  • The Procedure: Load (often certified water bags or calibrated shore weights) is lifted, slewed, and luffed through the crane's entire operational range
  • Certification: Witnessed by a Class surveyor who issues a Certificate of Test and Thorough Examination, cross-checked against the rigging plan and block list kept on board

Documentation & Registers

The CE maintains the Register of Ship's Lifting Appliances and Cargo Handling Gear. Any loose gear (shackles, chain blocks) must also be proof-tested, marked with its own SWL, and have its certificate logged. A rigging plan and block list showing the correct reeving/rigging arrangement for the appliance and its loose gear positions must also be kept on board.

On Indian-flag vessels, the survey execution details — including which competent persons/RO surveyors are authorised — are set out in DGMA Merchant Shipping Notice No. 02 of 2026. [DGMA MS Notice 02/2026, PDF]

🔧 CE Relevance

The CE is the primary custodian of engine room and provision crane records. If a crane's load test certificate expires, it cannot legally be used — the CE must permanently mark it "Not to be used," secure it, and log the out-of-service status in the Register.

⚠ Trap Questions

"If a hydraulic cylinder for luffing is replaced during a voyage, is a load test required?" — Yes; replacing a major load-bearing component constitutes a major repair requiring an occasional thorough examination and load test witnessed by a surveyor.

"Can the Chief Engineer perform the 5-yearly load test?" — No. It must be witnessed and certified by a Class surveyor acting as the RO.

✗ Common CE Failures
  • Forgetting to mention "loose gear" — surveyors will fail a crane if shackles or slings lack valid, traceable certificates in the Register, even if the crane passed the 5-year test
🔢 Numbers & Regulations to Memorise
  • Frequency: 12 months (annual), 5 years (proof load)
  • Proof Load: per MSC.1/Circ.1663 Table 1 (§3.2.1.5), not a fixed multiplier — 1.25× SWL is common at typical capacities only
  • Guidelines: MSC.1/Circ.1663; Indian flag execution per DGMA MS Notice 02/2026
  • Onboard Documentation: Register of Lifting Appliances + rigging plan/block list, both mandatory
⚓ Casualty Link

In the Lodz 2 incident, catastrophic failure of the crane slew ring resulted in the crane collapsing onto the deck. Proper execution of the annual thorough examination — specifically grease analysis and slew bearing clearance measurements — is designed to prevent this exact failure mode.

Loadline survey — which documents are required?
Load Line Paul (Ext)
⏱ 15-Second Answer "For a Load Line survey, the surveyor requires the International Load Line Certificate (ILLC), the 'Record of Conditions of Assignment,' the Approved Intact Stability Booklet, the Damage Stability Booklet, and the Loading Manual. The survey ensures the vessel's watertight integrity and freeboard marks remain unchanged."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, the core documents required for a Load Line survey are the International Load Line Certificate (ILLC) and the 'Record of Conditions of Assignment of Load Lines.' The surveyor uses the Record of Conditions of Assignment as a master checklist to verify that all structural arrangements — hatch covers, watertight doors, ventilators, air pipes — have not been altered since the freeboard was originally assigned. Additionally, the surveyor reviews the Approved Intact Stability Booklet, the Damage Stability Booklet (SOLAS II-1 Reg 25), the Trim and Loading Manual, and the AFS Certificate. Once documents are verified, physical checks of the deck, hull openings, freeing ports, and painted draft/freeboard marks are carried out."

Core Statutory Documents

The Load Line survey verifies that the ship's assigned freeboard has not been compromised by structural changes or degradation. [classnk]

  • International Load Line Certificate (ILLC): Valid for 5 years, subject to annual endorsement
  • Record of Conditions of Assignment: The most critical technical document — details exact specifications, sill heights, and closing arrangements for all doors, hatches, ventilators, air pipes, and scuppers on the freeboard deck. [sdir]

Stability and Operational Manuals

  • Approved Intact Stability Booklet
  • Damage Stability Booklet (mandatory for cargo ships under SOLAS II-1)
  • Loading / Ballasting Manual (or Trim and Stability Booklet)
  • Anti-Fouling System (AFS) Certificate (often checked concurrently)

Physical Survey Focus

After document review, the surveyor cross-references the Record of Conditions of Assignment against the physical deck:

  • Freeboard marks — correct positioning and visibility
  • Condition of weather-tight hatch covers and securing devices
  • Engine room skylights, fiddley openings, machinery space ventilators
  • Functionality of non-return valves on overboard discharges
  • Clear freeing ports for rapid deck drainage
⚠ Exam Trap If your ship uses two load line certificates, only one ILLC can be in use at a time. Active freeboard marks are painted in; marks for the unused certificate must be painted out or obscured, and the change logged in the Official Log Book.

The CE must ensure all engine room envelope openings — air pipe heads with internal floats, funnel louvers, skylights, and overboard discharge non-return valves — are well maintained. A corroded or stuck-open vent head float fails the Conditions of Assignment and the Load Line certificate cannot be endorsed.

  • Regulations: International Convention on Load Lines 1966 / 1988 Protocol

The loss of the MV Derbyshire (1980) heavily influenced modern Load Line regulations on hatch cover strength and air pipe closing arrangements. Water ingress through unprotected forward ventilators during a typhoon led to catastrophic loss of buoyancy — the reason surveyors rigorously check these items against the Conditions of Assignment.

Load line — what is Fresh Water Allowance, how is it derived, and how do you apply it when loading in dock water?
Load Line Stability
⏱ 15-Second Answer "Fresh Water Allowance is the number of millimetres by which a ship's mean draught increases when she passes from salt water into fresh water at her summer load displacement. It is displacement divided by four times TPC. It is what sets the Fresh and Tropical Fresh marks above the corresponding salt-water marks."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, a ship floating in fresh water sinks deeper than in salt water for the same displacement, because fresh water is less dense. The Load Line Convention allows for this in Regulation 40: the minimum freeboard in fresh water is obtained by deducting displacement divided by forty times TPC, in centimetres, from the salt-water freeboard. Expressed in millimetres that is FWA equals displacement divided by four times TPC, where displacement is the salt-water displacement in tonnes at the summer load waterline and TPC is tonnes per centimetre in salt water at that same waterline. That allowance is the vertical distance between the Summer mark and the Fresh mark, and between Tropical and Tropical Fresh. In dock water of intermediate density I do not use the full FWA — I use the Dock Water Allowance, scaled in proportion to how far the dock water density falls between fresh and salt."

1. What the Allowance Actually Is

A ship displaces her own weight. In fresh water, of relative density 1.000, that same weight needs a greater volume than in salt water, of relative density 1.025 — so she floats deeper. Fresh Water Allowance (FWA) is that increase in mean draught, expressed in millimetres, measured at the ship's summer load displacement. It is a fixed number for the ship, stated on the load line plan and in the stability booklet, not something recalculated every voyage.

2. The Formula and Where It Comes From

FWA (mm) = Displacement / (4 × TPC)

  • Displacement — the salt-water displacement in tonnes at the summer load waterline.
  • TPC — tonnes per centimetre immersion in salt water at the summer load waterline.

The regulatory home is ICLL Annex I, Regulation 40 (Minimum freeboards), which states the deduction in centimetres as displacement divided by 40 × TPC. The familiar 4 and the regulation's 40 are the same quantity — one gives millimetres, the other centimetres.

3. Why It Exists — the F and TF Marks

  • The Fresh (F) mark sits FWA above the Summer (S) mark.
  • The Tropical Fresh (TF) mark sits FWA above the Tropical (T) mark.
  • The logic is commercial as well as statutory: a ship loading in a fresh-water river may legitimately submerge her salt-water marks, because on reaching the sea she will rise to exactly her permitted salt-water draught.

4. Dock Water Allowance — the Practical Case

Real ports are rarely 1.000 or 1.025; they are brackish. The allowance is therefore scaled:

DWA (mm) = FWA × (1025 − Dock Water Density) / 25

with density in kg/m³. The divisor 25 is simply 1025 minus 1000 — the full span between salt and fresh. If the dock water is fresh, DWA equals FWA; if it is fully saline, DWA is zero.

5. Worked Example

  • Summer displacement 20,000 t, TPC 30 t/cm.
  • FWA = 20,000 / (4 × 30) = 166.7 mm.
  • Dock water density 1012 kg/m³: DWA = 166.7 × (1025 − 1012) / 25 = 166.7 × 13 / 25 = 86.7 mm.
  • So the ship may load until her Summer mark is 86.7 mm below the dock water surface, and she will rise to her Summer marks on reaching the sea.

Key Numbers / Memorisation Targets

  • 1.025 / 1.000 — relative density of salt and fresh water.
  • 4 — the divisor giving FWA in millimetres; 40 gives it in centimetres, as Regulation 40 states it.
  • 25 — the density span, in kg/m³, used as the DWA divisor.
⚠ Exam Trap FWA is calculated at the summer load displacement and is a single fixed figure for the ship. Candidates lose marks by offering to recompute FWA for the actual sailing draught. What varies with the port is the Dock Water Allowance, not the FWA.

CE Oral Tip (Kochi MMD): Give the definition and the formula in one breath, then say where the number lives on board — the load line plan and stability booklet — because that is the follow-up. Be ready for "your dock water is 1012, how much can you go down by?" and do the arithmetic out loud; the examiner wants to see you divide by 25, not recite a rule. State clearly that FWA is fixed at summer displacement and only the DWA changes with the port.
🔧 CE Relevance

The Chief Engineer's stake is the ballast and bunker plan. Sailing from a brackish berth to sea, the ship rises as density increases, and any deballasting or bunker transfer planned across that transition changes the mean draught at the same time. If the DWA has been used to its limit, a late bunker delivery can put the ship over her marks alongside — a detainable load line deficiency, and the CE is the one who knows what weight is going into which tank and when.

⚠ Trap Questions

"If FWA is 180 mm and you are in water of 1025, how much extra can you load?" — Nothing. The water is already fully saline, so DWA is zero and the Summer mark governs directly.

"Why divide by 25?" — Because 1025 minus 1000 is the full density span between salt and fresh water; DWA is simply FWA pro-rated across that span.

✗ Common CE Failures
  • Quoting FWA = displacement / (4 × TPC) without being able to say that displacement and TPC are both taken in salt water at the summer load waterline.
  • Confusing FWA with DWA and applying the full fresh-water allowance in brackish dock water — which overloads the ship.
  • Not knowing that Regulation 40 expresses the same deduction in centimetres with a divisor of 40.
🔢 Numbers & Regulations to Memorise
  • FWA (mm) = Displacement / (4 × TPC).
  • DWA (mm) = FWA × (1025 − dock density) / 25.
  • Salt water 1.025, fresh water 1.000.
  • ICLL Annex I Regulation 40 — the fresh-water deduction.
⚓ Casualty Link

Load line overloading rarely appears as a dramatic casualty; it appears as Port State Control detentions and as reduced reserve buoyancy in heavy weather. An overloaded ship has less freeboard, ships green water sooner, and reaches deck-edge immersion at a smaller angle of heel — which is why the freeboard regime is treated as a survivability requirement rather than a commercial formality.

Type B-60 and B-100 ships — what are they, and what must a ship satisfy to earn the reduced freeboard?
Load Line Stability
⏱ 15-Second Answer "A B-60 is a Type B ship allowed to give back up to sixty per cent of the freeboard difference between the Type B and Type A tables; a B-100 gives back the whole difference, so she floats at effectively a Type A freeboard. The reduction is earned by damage survival, not granted for the ship type."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, Load Line Regulation 27 divides ships into Type A and Type B. Type A carries liquid cargo in bulk and gets the lower tabular freeboard; Type B, the dry cargo ship, gets the higher one. A Type B ship that can demonstrate Type A standards of integrity may claim part of that difference back. For B-60 the ship must be over 100 metres long, meet the enhanced hatch cover, crew protection and freeing port conditions, and survive flooding of any one compartment at 0.95 permeability, machinery space excluded — the reduction is then not more than sixty per cent. For B-100 the standard is harder: she must survive flooding of any two adjacent fore-and-aft compartments, and in ships over 225 metres the machinery space itself must be treated as floodable at 0.85 permeability. In return she gets the full reduction."

1. The Starting Point — Type A and Type B

ICLL Annex I, Regulation 27 sets the ship types. A Type A ship is designed to carry only liquid cargoes in bulk, with high deck integrity, small access openings closed by watertight gasketed steel covers, and low permeability of loaded compartments. She is assigned the lower tabular freeboard. Every other ship is Type B, assigned the higher tabular freeboard. The difference between the two tables is the prize that B-60 and B-100 recover.

2. What "B-60" and "B-100" Actually Mean

  • B-60: the assigned freeboard is reduced by not more than 60% of the difference between the tabular Type B and Type A values for that length.
  • B-100: the reduction is the full 100% of that difference — the ship effectively floats to a Type A freeboard while remaining a dry cargo ship.

The names are simply the percentage of the difference recovered. Neither is a separate ship type; both are Type B ships with a conditional reduction.

3. Conditions for B-60

  • Length over 100 m.
  • Hatchway covers in positions 1 and 2 must meet the enhanced strength and weathertightness conditions, and the ship must satisfy the requirements for protection of the crew and freeing arrangements.
  • Loaded to her summer load waterline, she must remain afloat in a satisfactory condition of equilibrium after flooding of any one compartment, at an assumed permeability of 0.95, excluding the machinery space.

4. Conditions for B-100

  • She must satisfy the special conditions of assignment applicable to Type A ships under Regulation 26 — machinery casings, gangway and access, hatchways and ventilator protection.
  • She must survive flooding of any two adjacent fore-and-aft compartments, again at 0.95 permeability.
  • In ships over 225 m in length the machinery space must itself be treated as a floodable compartment, at a permeability of 0.85.

The single step from "any one compartment" to "any two adjacent compartments" is the whole difference between the two regimes. If you remember one thing, remember that.

5. The Equilibrium the Ship Must Reach

"Remain afloat in a satisfactory condition of equilibrium" is defined, not left to judgement. After flooding:

  • Angle of heel from unsymmetrical flooding not more than 15°; up to 17° may be accepted if no part of the deck is immersed.
  • Positive metacentric height in the upright damaged condition.
  • Residual righting lever curve positive through a range of at least 20° beyond equilibrium.
  • Maximum residual righting lever at least 0.1 m, and area under that curve within the 20° range at least 0.0175 m·rad.
  • The final waterline must stay below any opening through which progressive flooding could occur.

Key Numbers / Memorisation Targets

  • 60% / 100% — the share of the Type B to Type A tabular difference recovered.
  • 100 m — minimum length for a B-60 reduction.
  • 225 m — length above which the machinery space becomes a floodable compartment.
  • 0.95 / 0.85 — assumed permeability generally, and for the machinery space.
  • 15° / 17° / 20° / 0.1 m / 0.0175 m·rad — the equilibrium criteria.
⚠ Exam Trap B-60 and B-100 are not ship types and they are not about the cargo carried. They are conditional freeboard reductions earned by a Type B ship through damage survivability and enhanced closing appliances. A tanker's low freeboard is a different matter entirely — that comes from being Type A in the first place.

Then give the one-compartment versus two-adjacent-compartment distinction, because that is the discriminator the examiner is listening for. Have 100 m, 225 m, 0.95 and 0.85 ready. 🔧 CE Relevance

A reduced freeboard is granted against a set of physical conditions that must stay true for the life of the assignment. Several of them are engine-side: watertight and weathertight closing appliances on the freeboard deck, air pipe heads with functioning floats, engine room ventilator coamings and closing devices, freeing port flaps, and non-return arrangements on overboard discharges. If those degrade, the basis of the reduction has gone even though the marks are still painted on the side. That is why the Record of Conditions of Assignment is the working document at every load line survey.

⚠ Trap Questions

"Is a B-60 ship a tanker?" — No. She is a Type B ship, normally a dry cargo ship or bulk carrier, that has earned part of the Type A freeboard by meeting a one-compartment damage standard and enhanced closing conditions.

"What is the difference between B-60 and B-100 in one sentence?" — B-60 survives any one compartment flooded; B-100 survives any two adjacent fore-and-aft compartments, and above 225 m must include the machinery space at 0.85 permeability.

✗ Common CE Failures
  • Describing why tankers have less freeboard and thinking that answers the question — that is Type A assignment, a different regulation limb.
  • Giving the percentages but not the damage standard that earns them.
  • Quoting 0.95 permeability without saying that the machinery space is excluded, or attaching 225 m to the wrong case.
🔢 Numbers & Regulations to Memorise
  • B-60: over 100 m, one compartment, 0.95, machinery space excluded, reduction ≤ 60%.
  • B-100: two adjacent fore-and-aft compartments, 0.95, machinery space floodable at 0.85 above 225 m, reduction 100%.
  • Equilibrium: 15° (17° if deck not immersed), positive GM, 20° range, 0.1 m maximum lever, 0.0175 m·rad area.
⚓ Casualty Link

The freeboard reduction regime exists because reserve buoyancy is what keeps a damaged ship afloat long enough to matter. Bulk carrier losses following hold flooding are the standing argument for tying any reduction in freeboard to a demonstrated single- or two-compartment survival standard rather than to the ship's trade or her owner's economics.

What is a Bonjean curve, and where would you actually use one?
StabilitySOLAS Ref
⏱ 15-Second Answer "A Bonjean curve plots the area of the ship's immersed transverse section against draught, one curve for every station. Draw the actual waterline on the profile, read off each section area and integrate, and you have displacement and LCB for any waterline — including a trimmed one, where the even-keel hydrostatic tables cannot help you."
🎯 60-Second Answer "Sir, the hydrostatic tables are calculated for the ship upright and on even keel. The moment the waterline is not parallel to the baseline — a large trim, a stranding, a flooded compartment, a ship on the ways — those tables stop being valid. Bonjean curves solve that. Each station has its own curve of immersed sectional area against draught, drawn on the ship's profile. You superimpose the actual inclined waterline, take the draught at every station, read the immersed area off that station's curve, then apply Simpson's Rules along the length. Integrating the areas gives the volume of displacement; taking their first moments about a reference gives the longitudinal centre of buoyancy. That is how buoyancy distribution is obtained for longitudinal strength, launching and grounding calculations."

What the curve actually is

For each transverse station along the length, a Bonjean curve gives the area of the immersed part of that section as a function of the draught at that station. A full set is drawn on a profile of the ship, one curve at each ordinate, to a stated area scale. It is a purely geometric property of the hull form — no assumption about attitude is built into it, and that is precisely why it survives conditions the hydrostatic tables do not.

Why the hydrostatic tables are not enough

Hydrostatic particulars are tabulated against a single draught, for the ship upright and on even keel. They answer "what is the displacement at 9.2 m?" They cannot answer "what is the displacement and LCB with 3 m of trim by the stern and the forefoot aground?", because the waterline is no longer parallel to the baseline and every station is at a different draught. Bonjean curves let you work station by station and rebuild the answer from first principles.

How you use them — the method

  • Draw the actual waterline across the profile, at its true angle.
  • At each station, read the draught where that waterline cuts, and take the immersed sectional area from that station's Bonjean curve.
  • Apply Simpson's Rules to the set of areas along the length to obtain the volume of displacement; multiply by density for displacement.
  • Take the first moment of those areas about a chosen reference and divide by the volume to obtain the longitudinal centre of buoyancy.

Where it matters in practice

  • Longitudinal strength. The buoyancy curve along the length comes from the Bonjean areas. Subtract it from the weight curve to get the load curve, integrate once for shear force and again for bending moment. This is the calculation the loading instrument performs continuously.
  • Launching. As the ship travels down the ways the waterline sweeps aft-to-forward and buoyancy builds only over the immersed length. Bonjean areas give the buoyancy distribution at each instant, which is how the stern-lift moment and the pivoting-pressure at the fore poppet are checked.
  • Grounding and stranding. With part of the hull supported by the ground, displacement no longer equals weight. Bonjean curves give the actual buoyancy for the observed drafts, and the difference is the ground reaction.
  • Damage and flooding. Where a compartment is open to the sea and the ship lies at a large trim, sectional areas are the practical route to the flooded condition.

What a Bonjean curve will not give you

It gives immersed sectional areas, so it yields volume-derived quantities — displacement, LCB, and from those the block coefficient. It does not give waterplane properties. The longitudinal centre of flotation, the waterplane area and TPC come from the curve of half-breadths at the waterline, not from sectional areas. Offering LCF as a use of Bonjean curves is a common and avoidable error.

⚠ Exam Trap A Bonjean curve is not a curve of displacement, and it is not one curve for the ship. It is one curve per station, of immersed sectional area against draught. The examiner's follow-up is usually "so why not just use the hydrostatic tables?" — the answer is that the tables assume even keel, and every situation where Bonjean curves earn their keep is a situation where that assumption has failed.
