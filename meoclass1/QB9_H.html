How is an amendment ratified in India, and which authority decides before Parliament?
INDIACONVENTIONS

15-Second Answer

Treaty-making in India is an executive act of the Union: the administrative ministry (for maritime instruments, the Ministry of Ports, Shipping & Waterways on the maritime administration's — DGMA's — technical advice) proposes; the Union Cabinet approves; the instrument of ratification/accession is deposited through the Ministry of External Affairs. Parliament is not required for ratification itself — it enters when implementing legislation is needed (Art. 253: Parliament may legislate to implement any treaty for the whole of India), e.g. the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 giving domestic force to IMO conventions. Most IMO technical amendments then bind India by tacit acceptance at IMO, implemented domestically by rules/notifications under the Act.

60-Second Answer

Separate three layers, because examiners blur them. (1) International plane — ratification/accession: under the constitutional scheme (executive power of the Union, Art. 73, extending to treaty matters in the Union List — entries on entering into and implementing treaties), the decision rests with the Union executive: the sponsoring ministry consults stakeholders, the maritime administration provides the technical case, Cabinet approves, MEA deposits the instrument with the depositary (the IMO Secretary-General for IMO conventions). No parliamentary vote is constitutionally required for this act — that is the direct answer to "which authority decides before Parliament": the Union Cabinet on the sponsoring ministry's proposal. (2) Domestic force: India is dualist — a treaty does not become enforceable law by ratification alone; Parliament legislates where rights/obligations of persons are affected (Art. 253), which is precisely what the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 (Act 24 of 2025, in force 15 March 2026) does for the modern convention set, replacing the 1958 Act; detailed technical content flows through rules and DGMA circulars/notifications under the Act [Part-level reference — exact section numbers pending verification against the 2025 Act text]. (3) IMO amendments: after India is party, most SOLAS/MARPOL technical amendments enter into force by the tacit acceptance procedure — adopted by MSC/MEPC, deemed accepted on a set date unless a specified proportion of parties object — so each amendment does not go back through the full ratification machinery; the administration implements by notification and updates statutory certification.

Governing instrument

Constitution of India Arts. 73 and 253; Merchant Shipping Act 2025 (Act 24 of 2025, in force 15 Mar 2026) as the implementing statute; SOLAS Art. VIII tacit-acceptance procedure for amendments.

Key Numbers

  • Art. 253 — Parliament's power to implement treaties; executive treaty power with the Union (Art. 73 scheme).
  • MS Act 2025: Act 24 of 2025, in force 15 Mar 2026; DG Shipping renamed DGMA.
  • Tacit acceptance: standard SOLAS route — accepted unless objected to by the treaty-specified majority before the set date.

Trap point

Claiming Parliament ratifies treaties is the fail — ratification is a Union executive act; Parliament legislates only to implement (Art. 253), and IMO amendments then bind via tacit acceptance without fresh ratification.

Ship casualty — step by step, who responds, and how does the Flag State conduct its process?
CASUALTYFLAG STATE

15-Second Answer

Immediate: master's emergency response — save life, contain damage, SAR/coastal-State alerting, then reporting: company (DPA under ISM), flag administration, coastal State where required, P&I/class. Investigation: the flag State conducts a marine safety investigation under the Casualty Investigation Code (Res. MSC.255(84), mandatory via SOLAS XI-1/6) — mandatory for very serious casualties (loss of ship, loss of life, severe environmental damage); safety-focused, separate from blame/liability proceedings; report to IMO (GISIS). In India: reporting to DGMA/Principal Officer MMD, with inquiry/formal-investigation machinery under the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 [Part-level; section numbers pending verification].

60-Second Answer

Run it as a timeline. Phase 1 — emergency (master): muster/response per SMS emergency preparedness (ISM Sec. 8), distress/urgency traffic, SAR coordination with the relevant MRCC, pollution response per SOPEP, evidence preservation from the outset (VDR data saved, logs, ECDIS records, no interference with the scene where safe). Phase 2 — notifications: company/DPA immediately (ISM 9 reporting), flag administration per its casualty-reporting rules, coastal State per MARPOL Art. 8/Protocol I for pollution incidents and local regulations, charterers, class (damage affecting class), P&I and H&M insurers. Phase 3 — investigations, in parallel and distinct: (a) the flag State marine safety investigation — obligation from UNCLOS Art. 94(7) and SOLAS I/21, standardised by the Casualty Investigation Code: independent of criminal/civil process, aimed solely at preventing recurrence; mandatory for very serious marine casualties, discretionary otherwise; substantially interested States (coastal, ships' crews' States) cooperate; seafarers' rights per the Code and IMO/ILO fair-treatment guidelines; final report submitted to IMO/GISIS and made public; (b) the company ISM investigation (Sec. 9 non-conformity/accident analysis feeding corrective action); (c) class damage survey; (d) any coastal/port State proceedings, and (e) liability processes — separate tracks. India-specific machinery: report to the maritime administration (DGMA) and the local Principal Officer/MMD; the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 provides preliminary-inquiry and formal-investigation machinery (courts empowered for formal investigations, powers over certificates of officers) — cite at Part level with the note that section mapping from the 1958 Act (old ss. 358–360 family) must not be carried across unverified.

Governing instrument

Casualty Investigation Code (Res. MSC.255(84)), mandatory via SOLAS XI-1/6; UNCLOS Art. 94(7); ISM Code Secs. 8–9; Indian machinery under the MS Act 2025 [Part-level, sections pending verification].

Key Numbers

  • Very serious casualty (Code 2.22): total loss, death, or severe environmental damage → investigation mandatory.
  • Casualty Investigation Code: MSC.255(84), mandatory via SOLAS XI-1/6 (1 Jan 2010).
  • UNCLOS Art. 94(7) — flag-State inquiry duty; reports to GISIS.

Trap point

Merging the safety investigation with blame proceedings is the trap — the Code's investigation exists solely to prevent recurrence, is mandatory only for very serious casualties, and 1958 Act sections must not be cited as current.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): Give the three-phase timeline, then the sentence Nair listens for: "the flag-State safety investigation is separate from blame — its only output is prevention, reported to GISIS." Add VDR preservation in phase 1 (it is the engineer's data too: alarms, RPM, telegraph) and the mandatory trigger (very serious casualty).

In a machinery-related casualty the CE is the primary technical witness: engine-room logs, alarm/DataChief printouts, fuel records, maintenance history — preserved untouched. The CE also keeps the plant safe/stable for investigators and supports the company's ISM Sec. 9 analysis without prejudicing the flag investigation.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "When is investigation mandatory?" A: Very serious marine casualty. Q: "Who investigates if it happens in another State's waters?" A: Flag State leads or co-operates with the coastal State as substantially interested — the Code provides for agreed lead and parallel interests.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Jumping to liability instead of the safety track; no VDR/evidence step; not knowing the mandatory trigger; quoting 1958 Act sections as current Indian law; forgetting GISIS reporting.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

MSC.255(84) · SOLAS XI-1/6 · UNCLOS 94(7) · ISM 8/9 · MS Act 2025 (15 Mar 2026).

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

If a casualty delays your ship, who compensates for that loss?
LIABILITYCOMMERCIAL

15-Second Answer

It depends on who caused what — there is no single payer. Third party at fault (e.g. collision): claim loss of use/detention damages from the other shipowner in proportion to fault (Collision Convention 1910 principle), pursued via the H&M/P&I machinery, subject to the other owner's limitation of liability (LLMC 1976/96). Own casualty: the owner bears the delay unless insured — hull policies cover damage, not delay; the dedicated cover is Loss of Hire insurance. Between owner and charterer: the charterparty allocates it — off-hire clause puts time lost from breakdown on the owner; deviation/laytime/demurrage clauses handle voyage-charter time. General average does not compensate delay (York-Antwerp Rules — loss of time/market expressly excluded).

60-Second Answer

Frame it as "follow the cause, then read the contract, then check the insurance". (1) Tort route: where another ship's negligence caused the casualty, detention/loss-of-use is a recognised head of damage; apportioned by degree of fault (both-to-blame), recovered through the owners'/clubs' correspondents, and capped by the wrongdoer's right to limit under LLMC 1976 as amended by the 1996 Protocol (limits by tonnage, in SDRs; property claims fund covers such losses). FD&D (defence) cover funds the legal fight — it does not pay the loss itself. (2) Contract route: under a time charter, the off-hire clause is the practical answer the examiner wants — machinery breakdown, grounding, detention by average accidents suspend hire, so the delay cost falls on the owner (and any excluded causes stay with the charterer); under a voyage charter, delay lives in laytime/demurrage and exceptions clauses. Cargo interests claiming delay against the carrier face the Hague-Visby regime, which is unfriendly to pure delay claims. (3) Insurance route: Loss of Hire / Loss of Earnings insurance — a separate policy triggered typically by a peril covered under H&M, paying a daily amount after a deductible period of days; without it, an owner's own-fault delay is simply his commercial loss. (4) What does NOT pay: general average contributions cover sacrifices/expenditures for common safety — the York-Antwerp Rules exclude loss of market and delay losses; P&I covers liabilities to third parties, not the member's own trading loss. A management-level close: the CE's casualty documentation (cause, times, repairs) is the evidence every one of these routes runs on.

Governing instrument

Collision Convention 1910 (proportionate fault); LLMC 1976/1996; the charterparty's off-hire and exceptions clauses; York-Antwerp Rules (delay excluded from GA); Loss of Hire policy terms.

Key Numbers

  • LLMC 1976/1996: limitation by GT in SDRs [figures per current amendments — quote the framework, not memorised sums].
  • Loss of Hire: daily indemnity after an excess of [policy-specific] days — commercial policy values.
  • York-Antwerp Rules: delay/loss of market excluded from GA.

Trap point

Naming a single payer is the fail — and remember P&I never pays the member's own delay loss, while general average expressly excludes delay under the York-Antwerp Rules.

Four routes in one breath is the complete answer.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Off-hire disputes are decided on engine-room paper: exact breakdown and restoration times, cause classification, class involvement, repair records. A CE whose logs are precise saves the owner days of hire; sloppy timelines hand the argument to the other side.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Does P&I pay for my ship's delay?" A: No — P&I covers third-party liabilities; the member's own trading loss needs Loss of Hire cover. Q: "Does general average share my delay loss?" A: No — York-Antwerp excludes delay/loss of market. Q: "Other ship 100% at fault — do I recover everything?" A: Detention damages yes, but subject to their tonnage limitation under LLMC.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Naming one payer; confusing P&I with the owner's own-loss covers; not knowing the off-hire concept; claiming GA covers delay; forgetting limitation caps recovery.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Frameworks, not figures: 1910 proportionate fault · LLMC 76/96 SDR-by-tonnage · off-hire · LoH excess days [policy-specific] · YAR delay exclusion.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Ever Given (Suez, 2021): the delay-loss map in one event — SCA claims, GA declared for the salvage/refloating (but not for others' delays), hundreds of delayed ships bearing their own losses or claiming under their own covers — proving there is no universal delay compensator.

If you don't receive salary or sign-off, how do you approach it via a grievance letter — and what if there's no resolution?
MLCWAGES

15-Second Answer

Use the ladder. (1) Onboard complaint procedure — MLC Reg 5.1.5/Std A5.1.5, mandatory on every ship, copy with your SEA: written grievance to head of department/master, right to be accompanied, no victimisation, defined time limits, escalation to the shipowner. (2) Company/DPA and the flag State. (3) Onshore complaint to port State control — MLC Reg 5.2.2: any port's authorised officer takes seafarer complaints; unpaid wages/expired SEA are detainable MLC deficiencies. (4) External: ITF, the RPSL agent's obligations and the Indian Shipping Master/DGMA machinery [MS Act 2025 — Part-level, sections pending verification], financial-security direct claims if it becomes abandonment (A2.5.2: ≥2 months unpaid = a defining limb), and civil enforcement — wages are a maritime lien, enforceable by ship arrest.

60-Second Answer

The letter itself: factual and dated — identify the SEA and CBA, state amounts due with the wage-account extract (MLC Std A2.2 requires monthly accounts), the sign-off entitlement (SEA end date / max service period under 12 months per Std A2.5), the relief requested and a reasonable deadline; deliver up the onboard procedure (HoD → master → shipowner) keeping copies; the procedure must be resolved at the lowest level possible but you may go directly to the master or external authorities in appropriate cases — the Code says the right to complain directly to external bodies is preserved. No resolution: escalate in parallel, not sequentially forever: DPA (ISM link), flag administration MLC complaints channel, and at the next port the onshore complaint — the PSC officer must take initial steps, may inspect, and unpaid wages for a sustained period or an expired unrenewed SEA justify detention until rectified. Involve the ITF inspectorate (especially with an IBF/ITF CBA) and, for Indian seafarers, the recruitment chain: the RPSL-licensed agent has obligations to the seafarer under MLC Reg 1.4 and DGMA's RPSL rules — complaints to DGMA can cost the agent its licence; the Shipping Master's office handles wage disputes under the Merchant Shipping Act machinery [2025 Act — Part-level pending verification]. End of the road: two heavyweight remedies — (a) abandonment financial security (Std A2.5.2): at two months' unpaid wages the definition is met; direct access to the P&I-issued security for up to four months' wages plus repatriation and essential needs; (b) maritime lien for wages: among the highest-ranking liens, surviving change of ownership, enforced by arrest of the ship in an admiralty jurisdiction (in India under the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act 2017). Document everything — the paper trail is the case.

Governing instrument

MLC Regs 5.1.5 and 5.2.2 with Stds A2.1/A2.2/A2.5.2; the DGMA RPSL framework under MLC Reg 1.4; the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act 2017 for the wage lien and arrest.

Key Numbers

  • Monthly wage accounts (Std A2.2); max service before repatriation entitlement <12 months (Std A2.5).
  • ≥2 months unpaid → abandonment limb; security pays up to 4 months wages (A2.5.2).
  • Reg 5.1.5 onboard / Reg 5.2.2 onshore complaint; wages = maritime lien → arrest.

Trap point

Believing the onboard procedure must be exhausted first is the trap — the direct external complaint right is preserved (A5.1.5.2); and ≥2 months' unpaid wages already meets an abandonment limb.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): Give the four-rung ladder (onboard → company/flag → port State onshore complaint → ITF/RPSL/legal), then the two closers that mark seniority: "two months unpaid engages the abandonment security" and "wages are a maritime lien — I can arrest the ship." Nair also expects the letter's anatomy: SEA reference, wage-account figures, deadline, copies retained.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

As a senior officer the CE is often the accompanying/representing officer in juniors' grievances and must run the procedure without victimisation; knowing the external rungs also makes the CE the informed adviser when a whole crew's wages stop — the abandonment scenario's first hours.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Must I exhaust the onboard procedure before complaining ashore?" A: No — the right to complain directly to the master and to appropriate external authorities is expressly preserved (A5.1.5.2). Q: "Can PSC detain for unpaid wages?" A: Yes — MLC deficiencies including wage non-payment are detainable; repeated non-payment is treated seriously across MOUs. Q: "What ranks higher — wage lien or mortgage?" A: The wage lien ranks above the mortgage in the classic priority order.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Not knowing the onboard procedure is mandatory and documented; missing the onshore-complaint route; unable to connect two months' unpaid wages to abandonment; forgetting the RPSL agent's accountability; vague letters with no figures/deadlines.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

How do you assess a junior officer / give an appraisal — what procedure and marking do you use?
MANAGEMENTSTCW

15-Second Answer

Use the company's SMS appraisal system, but describe the method, not just the form: continuous observation against defined criteria — technical competence mapped to the STCW function tables (A-III/1 for an engineer OOW), watchkeeping standards, safety behaviour, teamwork/communication, leadership potential — scored on the company's graded scale (typically 1–5 behavioural anchors), evidenced by specific incidents, discussed face-to-face with the officer, signed by both with the officer's comments recorded, and closed with a development plan. Interim appraisal for weak performance — never a surprise end-of-contract verdict.

60-Second Answer

Procedure: (1) Set expectations at joining — familiarisation completion (ISM 6.3), standing orders acknowledged, the appraisal criteria shown on day one; assessment without published criteria is unfair and indefensible. (2) Gather evidence continuously — watch handovers, UMS call-out performance, PMS job quality, drill performance, defect reporting honesty; note dated specifics, good and bad. (3) Score against anchors — company forms grade dimensions (technical knowledge, watchkeeping, safety compliance, initiative, teamwork, leadership) on a defined scale with behavioural descriptions per grade; mark to the anchors, not to personality; guard against halo/recency bias by using the incident notes. (4) The interview — private, two-way: strengths first with examples, development areas with examples, the officer's self-assessment and response recorded; agree targets (e.g. independent UMS duty, specific system competence) with support and a review date. (5) Sign-off and routing — both signatures, officer's comments field, forwarded per SMS to the company (fleet personnel), feeding promotion boards and training nomination. Marking philosophy the examiner wants: objective evidence over impression; STCW competence tables as the technical yardstick; distinguish competence (can do), performance (does do) and potential (could do at the next rank); interim/adverse appraisals require documented counselling first — natural justice. Poor appraisal handled badly becomes a grievance (Q4's ladder); handled well it is the ship's officer-development engine and the evidence trail behind the CE's promotion recommendations.

Governing instrument

ISM Code Sec. 6 (resources and personnel); STCW Tables A-III/1 and A-III/2 as the competence yardsticks; the company's controlled SMS appraisal procedure.

Key Numbers

  • Yardsticks: STCW Table A-III/1 (operational competence), A-III/2 behaviours for promotion potential.
  • Scale/forms: [company-specific] graded anchors — describe the method, quote your SMS.
  • Both signatures + comments field + development plan — the three marks of a defensible appraisal.

Trap point

An adverse appraisal that surprises the officer is the fail — documented counselling must precede it; and marking personality instead of anchored behaviour is the other classic error.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): The trap is describing a form. Give the five-step procedure with the fairness spine — published criteria, dated evidence, face-to-face, both signatures, development plan — and anchor marking to STCW tables. The sentence "an adverse appraisal must never be the first the officer hears of the problem" is exactly the management maturity Nair scores.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE's appraisals decide who is released to independent UMS duty (Q2 of QB4_J territory), who is recommended for 2/E, and they are the documents the company produces if that officer's competence is ever questioned after an incident — write them as if an investigator will read them.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Officer disagrees with the appraisal?" A: Comments recorded on the form, signature acknowledges receipt not agreement, escalation via the complaint procedure — the disagreement is documented, not suppressed. Q: "Continuous or end-of-contract?" A: Continuous evidence, formal appraisal at defined points plus interim for concerns. Q: "What removes bias?" A: Behavioural anchors, dated incident evidence, and multiple dimensions scored separately.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

"I fill the company form" with no method; marking personality not behaviour; surprise adverse appraisals; no development plan; no link to STCW competence tables.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

ISM Sec. 6 · STCW A-III/1 / A-III/2 · five steps: criteria → evidence → score → interview → sign/route.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Casualty investigations repeatedly surface glowing appraisals of officers whose documented performance said otherwise — appraisal integrity is a safety barrier, which is why investigators request them alongside training records.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: How do you appraise → criteria source → bias control → the difficult conversation → officer disagrees → link to promotion/UMS release → records routing.

How do you evaluate a new company before joining, and ensure MLC compliance?
MLCEMPLOYMENT

15-Second Answer

Check three layers. Recruitment chain: the agent must hold a DGMA RPSL licence (MLC Reg 1.4) — verify on the DGMA site; no fees may be charged to the seafarer for placement. Company/ship record: DOC holder identity, fleet PSC history on Equasis/MOU databases (detentions, MLC deficiencies), flag quality, ILO/IMO abandonment database for the owner/manager. Contract paperwork before signing: Maritime Labour Certificate + DMLC for the ship, a compliant SEA (Std A2.1 — signed original, reviewed before signing, terms per the Code), applicable CBA (ITF/IBF ideally), wage scale and allotment terms, and the two posted financial-security certificates (A2.5.2 abandonment; A4.2 death/disability).

60-Second Answer

Answer as due diligence with sources, not vibes. (1) The agent: India requires recruitment through RPSL (Recruitment and Placement Services Licence) holders regulated by DGMA under MLC Reg 1.4/Std A1.4 — verify the licence number and validity on the DGMA portal; a demand for placement fees is both an MLC breach and the classic fraud flag. (2) The employer: identify who actually employs you (owner vs manager vs manning entity — the SEA must name the shipowner responsible under MLC); look up the fleet on Equasis and the Tokyo/Paris MOU databases — detention rate, MLC-deficiency pattern (wages, SEA, food, hours) is the single most honest indicator of life onboard; check the ILO/IMO abandonment database for the owner's name; note flag (white/grey/black list) and class. Seafarer networks and the ITF's lookup services complete the picture. Wreck Removal Convention — what is strict liability under it?
LIABILITYWRC

15-Second Answer

The Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks 2007 (in force 14 Apr 2015; India a party) makes the registered owner strictly liable for the costs of locating, marking and removing a wreck the affected State determines to be a hazard — liable without proof of fault, simply because the ship became a wreck. Limited exceptions only: act of war/hostilities, a natural phenomenon of an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible character, wholly caused by a third party's intentional act, or wholly caused by the negligence of the authority responsible for navigational aids. Ships ≥300 GT must carry compulsory insurance with a State-issued certificate, and claimants have direct action against the insurer.

60-Second Answer

Strict liability defined: liability that attaches on the happening of the event — the maritime casualty producing the wreck — regardless of negligence; the claimant need not prove the owner did anything wrong, and "we took all care" is no defence. The Convention channels it to the registered owner and closes the escape routes with the short exhaustive exception list above (note the pollution-convention family resemblance — CLC uses the same architecture). Scope: the Convention area is the EEZ of a State Party (extendable by declaration to territorial sea and internal waters — many parties including major flags have opted in); "wreck" covers the sunken/stranded ship, parts, lost objects, and a ship about to sink where effective assistance is not underway. The machinery: the affected State determines the hazard (navigation or environment, against listed criteria), the ship's flag/owner must report, the State marks the wreck and sets a reasonable deadline for owner removal; if the owner fails, the State removes and recovers costs — from the owner strictly, or from the insurer directly. Financial security: compulsory insurance for ≥300 GT to the LLMC limits, certificate issued/verified by a State Party (in India via the administration), carried onboard; direct action means the claimant sues the insurer, defeating owner insolvency — the P&I club stands behind the blue card. Liability may still be limited under the applicable limitation regime (LLMC as national law allows), and the strict wreck-removal liability sits alongside, not instead of, other liabilities (pollution under CLC/Bunkers, cargo, crew).

Governing instrument

Nairobi WRC 2007 — Arts. 5–9 (reporting, hazard, marking, removal) and Arts. 10–12 (strict liability, exceptions, compulsory insurance, direct action); in force 14 Apr 2015; limitation interface with LLMC 1976/1996.

What are the different types of liability in shipping?

15-Second Answer

Classify by basis: fault-based (tort/negligence) — collision, personal injury: claimant proves duty, breach, causation, loss; strict liability — event-based with narrow defences: CLC oil pollution, Bunkers Convention, Nairobi WRC, HNS; contractual — charterparties, bills of lading (Hague-Visby regime), towage, shipbuilding; vicarious — owner answers for master/crew acting in employment; statutory — duties imposed by legislation (MS Act, port/pollution statutes) with penalties; criminal — of individuals and companies (e.g. deliberate pollution, safety offences). Overlay: joint & several allocation among wrongdoers, and the limitation of liability regime (LLMC) capping most claims by tonnage.

60-Second Answer

Answer as a taxonomy with one shipping example each, then the two features that make maritime liability distinctive. Fault: the default — collision claims apportioned by degree of blame (1910 Convention), negligent bunker spills outside the strict regimes, personal injury. Strict: policy shifts the risk to the shipowner for high-consequence activities — CLC 1992 (tanker oil), Bunkers 2001, WRC 2007, HNS (not yet in force) — same architecture each time: channelled to the registered owner, short exception list, compulsory insurance, direct action. Contractual: the commercial web — carrier's obligations and immunities under Hague-Visby incorporated in the B/L, charterparty performance warranties (speed/consumption — the CE's numbers), off-hire, safe-port warranties. Vicarious: the owner/employer's liability for servants' acts in the course of employment — why crew negligence lands on the company; the ISM DOC company is where safety accountability is anchored. Statutory: obligations created by legislation independent of contract or fault, breach bringing penalties/detention — certification, manning, pollution statutes (India: the MS Act 2025 and rules [Part-level, sections pending verification]). Criminal: individuals (master, CE) and corporations for offences — falsified oil record books (the "magic pipe" prosecutions), deliberate discharges, manslaughter in gross-negligence jurisdictions. The maritime overlays: (1) limitation — LLMC 1976/96 lets owners cap most property/personal claims by tonnage unless the loss resulted from their personal act or omission committed with intent or recklessly with knowledge (near-unbreakable test); (2) liens and arrest — certain claims (wages, salvage, collision damage) travel with the ship as maritime liens enforceable in rem. Frame it honestly: marine insurance itself is not a strict-liability regime — it is a contract of indemnity of utmost good faith (uberrimae fidei) under the Marine Insurance Act (India 1963, mirroring UK 1906). The phrase in the examiner's mouth usually means one of two true things: (a) once an insured peril is proved, the insurer pays regardless of the assured's or crew's negligence — negligence of master/crew is itself a covered peril, and only the assured's wilful misconduct defeats the claim (the s.55 logic); (b) insurers stand behind the strict-liability conventions — CLC, Bunkers, WRC — where P&I clubs issue the compulsory certificates and face direct action, so the owner's no-fault liability is transmitted to the insurer. Distinguish those, and the question is answered.

60-Second Answer

Step 1 — correct the premise politely: liability regimes describe when someone must pay a victim; insurance describes who indemnifies the payer. Marine insurance rests on contract: insurable interest, utmost good faith/fair presentation, warranties, and indemnity against proximately caused insured perils. That is a fault-neutral trigger, not strict liability. Step 2 — the kernel of truth (a): under the Marine Insurance Act's causation section (the s.55 framework), the insurer is liable for loss proximately caused by an insured peril even if the loss would not have happened but for the misconduct or negligence of master or crew — the Inchmaree/perils clauses expressly cover crew negligence; what the insurer never pays is loss attributable to the wilful misconduct of the assured himself (scuttling being the classic case). So from the assured's seat the cover behaves "strictly": prove the peril, no need to disprove your crew's negligence. Step 3 — the kernel of truth (b): where conventions impose genuine strict liability on the shipowner (CLC 1992, Bunkers 2001, WRC 2007), the insurance architecture is welded on: compulsory insurance to specified limits, State-issued certificates against the club's blue card, and direct action by claimants against the insurer — the insurer effectively underwrites a no-fault exposure and may only raise the owner's convention defences (plus wilful misconduct). Step 4 — the boundary markers the examiner may probe: P&I is mutual liability cover and generally follows "pay to be paid" except where direct-action regimes override it; breach of warranty/seaworthiness issues (hull, and the MIA voyage-policy warranty logic) can still defeat cover — reminders that the contract, not strictness, rules. Close: "strict liability lives in the conventions; insurance responds to it — and separately, insurance pays proved perils without fault-hunting against the crew."

Governing instrument

Marine Insurance Act 1963 (India)/1906 (UK) — the s.55 causation and wilful-misconduct logic; CLC 1992 Art. VII, Bunkers 2001 Art. 7 and WRC 2007 Art. 12 for compulsory insurance and direct action.

Key Numbers

Capital cost, voyage cost and operating cost — what comes under each, and who pays them under the different charters?
COMMERCIALCHARTERING

15-Second Answer

Capital cost is the cost of owning the ship — purchase or newbuilding price, loan interest and repayment, depreciation, return on equity. Operating cost is the daily cost of keeping her ready to trade — crew, stores, lubricants, routine repairs, insurance, management. Voyage cost is what a particular voyage adds — bunkers, port dues, canal transit, pilotage, towage, agency.

60-Second Answer

Shipping economics splits a ship's costs by what drives them. Capital costs are finance-driven: the purchase or newbuilding price, debt service, depreciation and the owner's required return. Operating costs are time-driven and run whether she sails or lies idle — crew wages and victualling, stores and spares, lubricating oil, routine maintenance and repair, hull and machinery and P&I insurance, registration, flag and management fees. Voyage costs are voyage-driven — bunkers, port and berth dues, canal transit dues, pilotage, towage and agency. Two further heads are normally separated out: periodic maintenance, meaning the dry docking and special survey, and cargo-handling costs. The point of the split is that the charter type decides who carries which head.

1. Capital cost — the cost of owning

  • Purchase price of a secondhand ship, or the newbuilding contract price and yard instalments.
  • Debt service — interest and principal repayment on the mortgage or lease.
  • Depreciation, written down over the ship's economic life to a scrap value.
  • The equity return the owner requires for the capital tied up.
  • Pre-delivery and modification capital — ballast water treatment, scrubber or efficiency retrofits are capital, not running cost.

Character: fixed at the moment of purchase, largely unalterable afterwards, and the single biggest determinant of whether a ship is competitive. Two identical ships bought at different points in the cycle have different break-even freight rates for life.

2. Operating cost — the cost of readiness

  • Crew — wages, overtime, victualling, travel, relief, training and certification.
  • Stores and consumables — engine and deck stores, spares, chemicals, paint.
  • Lubricating oil — conventionally its own head, because cylinder oil consumption is large and separately controllable.
  • Repair and maintenance — routine and running repairs, riding squads, condition monitoring.
  • Insurance — hull and machinery, P&I, war risk, loss of hire.
  • Administration — registration and flag fees, class fees, ship management fee, communications, certification.

Character: a daily rate that accrues whether the ship is loaded, in ballast, waiting or off-hire. It is the head the technical manager actually controls, and the one against which a superintendent is judged.

3. Voyage cost — the cost of the particular voyage

  • Bunkers — normally the largest single voyage cost, and the reason speed and consumption are contractual matters.
  • Port charges — port and berth dues, light dues, mooring, tonnage tax, garbage and waste reception.
  • Canal transit dues — Suez and Panama, plus transit-related towage and pilotage.
  • Pilotage, towage and agency at each port of call.
  • Cargo-handling costs — loading, stowage, dunnage, lashing and discharge — are usually treated as a separate head, because the charterparty terms decide who pays them.

Character: incurred only because a specific voyage is performed, so they disappear when the ship is idle — and they are the head that moves between owner and charterer depending on the contract.

4. Who pays what — the charter decides

This is the part that earns the marks, because the three cost heads map almost exactly onto the three charter types:

  • Voyage charter (BIMCO GENCON 2022) — the owner carries capital, operating and voyage costs, and is paid a freight for carrying an agreed cargo between agreed ports. The owner therefore takes the full risk of delay, weather and bunker price; laytime and demurrage exist to limit it.
  • Time charter (BIMCO NYPE 2015) — the owner keeps capital and operating costs and is paid hire per day; the charterer directs the ship's employment and pays the voyage costs, bunkers and port charges included. This is why speed-and-consumption warranties, off-hire and bunker quality clauses matter so much: they police the boundary between the two parties' cost heads.
  • Bareboat charter (BIMCO BARECON 2017) — the charterer takes possession and full operational and financial responsibility, so he carries operating and voyage costs, including crewing, maintenance, and hull and P&I insurance. The owner is left with capital cost alone, and the arrangement is closer to a finance lease than a transport contract.

What is a ship broker?
COMMERCIALCHARTERING

15-Second Answer

A ship broker is a commercial intermediary who brings owner and charterer, or seller and buyer, together and negotiates the deal — but is not a party to the resulting contract. He acts as an agent for his principal, is paid a commission on the freight, hire or price, and carries no liability for performance. The main types are chartering, sale and purchase, newbuilding and demolition brokers.

60-Second Answer

A ship broker does not own or operate ships. He matches cargo to available tonnage, or a buyer to a seller, negotiates the commercial terms on behalf of his principal, and records the agreement — a fixture note or recap in chartering, a memorandum of agreement in a sale — which is then drawn up on a standard form such as GENCON, NYPE, BARECON or the Norwegian Saleform. His legal position is the important part: he acts as agent, so the contract is between owner and charterer and the broker takes no liability for freight, hire or performance. He is remunerated by a commission, provided for by the commission or brokerage clause in the charterparty itself. The main specialisations are dry cargo and tanker chartering, sale and purchase, newbuilding and demolition. He is distinct from the port agent, who represents the ship in port rather than in the market.

What he actually does

  1. Market intelligence — maintains a live picture of open tonnage, cargoes on offer, freight levels and vessel values, and advises his principal on timing.
  2. Circulation and matching — circulates his principal's position or requirement and identifies the counterparty.
  3. Negotiation — conducts the exchange of offers and counter-offers on main terms, then on the detailed clauses, passing each between the parties.
  4. Fixing — confirms the recap when terms are agreed, and produces the charterparty or memorandum of agreement on the agreed standard form with the negotiated riders.
  5. Post-fixture — follows the voyage, passes notices, and assists with laytime, demurrage and hire statements when disputes arise.

The main types

  • Chartering broker — dry cargo or tanker; may act as the owner's broker or the charterer's broker, and a competitive broker works the market generally rather than for one house.
  • Sale and purchase broker — negotiates the sale of secondhand tonnage, arranges inspection and handles the closing.
  • Newbuilding broker — places orders with shipyards and negotiates the building contract and specification.
  • Demolition broker — sells ships for recycling, frequently through a cash buyer who takes title before the yard.

Legal position and payment

  • Agent, not principal. The broker's authority is to negotiate and conclude on behalf of his principal. He is not a party to the charterparty and, acting within his authority and disclosing his principal, is not personally liable for its performance.
  • Duties as agent — to act with reasonable skill and care, to follow his principal's instructions, to disclose material facts, and not to make a secret profit.
  • Commission — a percentage of the freight, hire, demurrage or sale price, agreed in the fixture and written into the commission or brokerage clause. It is customarily payable by the owner even where the broker was introduced by the charterer.
  • Address commission is different — it is a deduction the charterer retains from freight or hire for his own account, not a payment to a broker, and candidates routinely confuse the two.

Distinguish the broker from the agent

The ship broker operates in the market and is finished once the fixture is concluded and the post-fixture work is done. The port agent is appointed for a particular call and attends to the ship at that port — berth booking, clearance, stores, crew changes, pilots and disbursements. A candidate who treats the two as the same word loses the question immediately. The professional body for the discipline is the Institute of Chartered Shipbrokers, granted its Royal Charter in 1920, and the historic marketplace for dry cargo chartering and freight indices is the Baltic Exchange in London.
