What is the AECS course and who is required to do it?
stcw
15-Second Answer: AECS stands for Assessment, Examination and Certification of Seafarers — a DGMA-approved 10-day/70-hour modular course, based on IMO Model Course 3.12, for people who assess, examine, or certify OTHER seafarers on behalf of the Administration: internal/external MMD examiners, Class society surveyors conducting STCW-related audits, internal auditors, and MTI course in-charges/faculty. It is not a competency course for engineers seeking their own promotion.
60-Second Answer: AECS exists to satisfy STCW Regulation I/6 and the quality-standards obligations of Regulation I/8 — that anyone who trains, assesses, or examines seafarers for STCW certification purposes must themselves be appropriately qualified to do so. Eligibility requires holding a Certificate of Competency no lower than the certificate being examined (Master FG / MEO Class I FG / ETO / GMDSS GOC or equivalent) plus a separate 'Training Course for Trainers and Assessors' (TOTA) certificate. The course itself is run under DGMA's 3-Tier system (e-learning, offline classroom, exit exam) and produces a lifetime-valid certificate. It is aimed at surveyors, internal auditors, and MTI faculty/course in-charges — not at operational engineers preparing for their own oral exam.

What AECS Actually Is

AECS = Assessment, Examination and Certification of Seafarers, a DGMA-approved course based on IMO Model Course 3.12. Its purpose is to train and qualify the people who will themselves assess, examine, or certify other seafarers' STCW competency on behalf of the Directorate General of Shipping.

Who Is Required to Do It

  • Internal and external examiners at MMDs conducting oral examinations.
  • Surveyors from Classification Societies performing STCW-related audits/certification.
  • Internal auditors of Maritime Training Institutes (MTIs).
  • Course in-charges and faculty responsible for assessment/examination on any STCW-approved course.

Eligibility to Attend

  • Must already hold a Certificate of Competency no lower than the certificate/qualification they are to examine — e.g. Master (FG), MEO Class I (FG), ETO, or GMDSS GOC.
  • Must additionally hold a certificate for the Training Course for Trainers and Assessors (TOTA).

Structure

A 10-day, 70-hour course delivered under DGMA's standard 3-Tier e-learning approach: online e-learning module → offline classroom training at a DGS-approved MTI → online exit exam at the MTI. The resulting STCW certificate has lifetime validity.

Chief Engineer Relevance

AECS itself is not something a serving Second Engineer does to prepare for their own MEO Class 1 exam — it is the course a future examiner, surveyor, or MTI faculty member would take. Its practical relevance to a CE is indirect: it explains the qualification standard behind the person who will later assess your own engine room crew's competency, or behind an internal Company auditor reviewing your training records.

Outline the Best Management Practices for a transit through the Strait of Hormuz.
isps
Currentness note (31 Aug 2026): BMP5 has been superseded. It was replaced in 2025 by Best Management Practices for Maritime Security (BMP MS), which also replaced BMP West Africa and the Global Counter Piracy Guidance; the current edition is 2026, and BMP MS is global rather than region-scoped. The technique below — hardening, citadel, muster, reporting — carries forward and is still examinable, but do not present BMP5 itself as the current publication. For what BMP MS is, what it replaced and its status, see QB4_H Q13.
15-Second Answer: Transiting the Strait of Hormuz follows BMP principles adapted to a state-actor/asymmetric threat profile rather than classic Somali-style piracy — registering with UKMTO/IMSC before transit, hardening the vessel, running maximum safe speed, and preparing the citadel with independent engine/steering trip controls, while watching for WBIEDs, UAVs, and fast-craft boarding attempts.
60-Second Answer: Preparation includes updating the Ship Security Assessment against current NAVAREA and company security bulletins, and registering the transit itinerary with UKMTO — reports are auto-forwarded to NAVCENT NCAGS — plus MSCIO, 24 hours before entering the Voluntary Reporting Area. Hardening measures include sealing non-essential access points, razor wire on low-freeboard areas, and citadel provisioning with independent communications and remote engine/steering trip capability. During transit, UMS is cancelled, generators run in parallel to avoid a single-point blackout, extra lookouts are posted, maximum feasible distance is kept from the Iranian coast and a minimum 30 nm standoff from naval vessels, and AIS policy (on with restricted data, or off with two-hourly position reports to UKMTO/NCAGS) is set by the Master per current company/flag guidance rather than left to a fixed rule.

Pre-Transit Preparation

  • Update the Ship Security Assessment against current NAVAREA warnings and company/regional intelligence.
  • Report transit route and ETA to UKMTO and IMSC 24–48 hours before entering the Voluntary Reporting Area.
  • Conduct a full security/citadel briefing and drill with all crew.

Route Selection & the Coastal-State Routing Dispute (April 2026 onward)

This is now a distinct planning question from ordinary TSS lane selection. Since April 2026, Iran has redrawn the Strait of Hormuz traffic scheme and directed all vessels to use IRGC-designated corridors only (the so-called "Tehran Toll Booth" route between the islands of Qeshm and Larak), warning of a "decisive response" against vessels using the previously charted TSS. Oman separately announced its own alternate transit route, stated to be coordinated with the IMO — Iran rejected this as done without its consent, and a vessel was reportedly struck shortly afterward while using the Omani-approved route.

The applicable legal position, per the IMO's own March 2026 statement, is that the right of transit passage through an international strait under UNCLOS Part III cannot be unilaterally suspended or redirected to routes of the coastal state's choosing. Iran has not ratified UNCLOS and disputes this, asserting broader sovereign authority over its portion of the Strait. In practice this means a Master electing to follow an Iran-designated route is doing so as a safety-driven decision under duress, not because of a recognised legal routing obligation — the same "comply for safety, do not characterise it as seeking permission, report immediately" principle that already applies to Iranian Navy VHF hailing, now extended to route selection itself.

Vessel Hardening

  • Seal all non-essential external access points; retain one secured, monitored access.
  • Deploy razor wire on low-freeboard areas; prepare fire hoses on the fire main for immediate deployment; shutter/blind bridge windows where practicable.
  • Ensure the citadel has independent communications, provisions, and remote trip capability for the main engine and steering gear.
  • Safe muster point vs. citadel: BMP5 Section 5 distinguishes a safe muster point from the citadel — where the threat assessment identifies a risk of hull breach on or below the waterline (missile, WBIED, mine), a safe muster point above the waterline must be identified, selected with the likely blast path in mind (the central stairway, protected by the accommodation block, is a common choice). A below-waterline citadel is the correct destination for a boarding/hijack threat, not for a weapon-of-war threat where blast/fragmentation above or at the waterline is the concern — the two are complementary, not interchangeable.
  • Hardening now serves two separate purposes: classic anti-boarding hardening (razor wire, sealed access, single monitored entry) remains necessary because state-actor illegal boarding/seizure has actually occurred in this crisis. Separately, the May 2026 Industry Guidance adds hardening aimed specifically at the kinetic/blast threat — considering ISPS Level 3, a deck-lighting/blackout plan, and, where operationally feasible, sandbagged bridge windows and ballistic protection (Kevlar helmet/vest) for bridge personnel. Do not present these as one undifferentiated checklist in the oral — state which measure defends against which threat.

During Transit

  • Enhanced lookouts (visual and radar) for fast craft, WBIEDs, and low-flying UAVs.
  • UMS cancelled; auxiliary generators run in parallel to prevent a single-point blackout; steering motors in parallel.
  • AIS: policy set by the Master per company/flag/current official guidance under SOLAS V/19 — if left on, consider restricting transmitted data to identity, position, course, speed and safety information; if switched off, position must be reported to UKMTO and NAVCENT NCAGS every two hours. Not a fixed "always on" or "always off" rule, and — despite Iran's broader assertions of authority over the Strait — AIS policy is a Master/company/flag decision, not something the coastal state (Iran) has any recognised legal authority to mandate.
  • If hailed on VHF by Iranian Navy units and directed to divert or submit voyage information — a tactic reported on the southern route — the Master assesses the instruction against flag/company guidance and international law, prioritises safety of vessel and crew, and reports immediately to UKMTO and NAVCENT NCAGS; this is not a request for prior transit permission from Iran, which is not part of any official reporting framework.

Chief Engineer Relevance

Machinery reliability is the CE's contribution to BMP MS in this environment: fuel system optimisation for rapid load changes, verified emergency steering and remote engine trips accessible from the citadel, and generators configured in parallel so no single failure causes a blackout during a high-threat transit.

What security-related courses are required for seafarers under STCW/ISPS, and what does each cover?
stcwisps
15-Second Answer: STCW Chapter VI security training has three tiers: Security Awareness Training (SAT) for all seafarers without designated security duties, Designated Security Duties (DSD) training for those with a specific role under the Ship Security Plan, and the Ship Security Officer (SSO) course for the officer accountable to the Master for the vessel's overall security.
60-Second Answer: SAT (Reg. VI/6 & Section A-VI/6 paras 1–4) is mandatory for every seafarer aboard an ISPS-compliant ship, covering basic threat recognition and security terminology. DSD (Reg. VI/6 para 4 & Section A-VI/6 paras 6–8) is required for crew assigned specific duties in the Ship Security Plan — access control, restricted-space monitoring, search procedures. The SSO course (Reg. VI/5 & Section A-VI/5) qualifies the officer designated by the Company, accountable to the Master, responsible for implementing and maintaining the Ship Security Plan, liaising with the CSO and PFSO, and managing security equipment including the SSAS.

1. Security Awareness Training (SAT)

Target: all seafarers aboard an ISPS-compliant ship without designated security duties. Basis: STCW Reg. VI/6.1 and Section A-VI/6.1–4. Covers basic recognition of security threats, terminology, and identification of weapons/dangerous substances.

2. Designated Security Duties (DSD)

Target: crew assigned specific duties under the Ship Security Plan (deck watches, restricted-space control, search operations). Basis: STCW Reg. VI/6.4 and Section A-VI/6.6–8. Covers emergency procedures, crowd control, recognising bypass attempts, and hardening measures.

3. Ship Security Officer (SSO)

Target: the officer, accountable to the Master, designated by the Company as responsible for the ship's security and implementation of the Ship Security Plan. Basis: STCW Reg. VI/5 and Section A-VI/5. Covers ship security assessments, internal audits, managing security equipment (including the SSAS), and liaison with the Company Security Officer (CSO) and Port Facility Security Officer (PFSO).

Chief Engineer Relevance

The Chief Engineer is not normally the designated SSO (typically the Chief Officer), but must hold at least a DSD-level qualification and ensure every engine department member holds valid SAT/DSD before standing an active watch. The engine room is itself a restricted space under the ISPS Code, so the CE works with the SSO to enforce access control there.

Describe the procedure for testing the Ship Security Alert System (SSAS).
isps
15-Second Answer: SSAS testing transmits a covert alert flagged as "TEST" to the Company Security Officer and Flag Administration, pre-coordinated in advance so it is never mistaken for a real alert, using the terminal's dedicated test mode or physical activation button — with zero audible or visual indication anywhere on board.
60-Second Answer: Before testing, the Master/SSO formally notify the CSO and the Flag Administration of the exact test window and log the intention in the Official Log Book and the confidential ISPS log. The test is executed either via a software "SSAS Test" mode on the satellite terminal (e.g. Inmarsat-C) which appends a TEST flag to the transmitted packet, or by depressing the covert physical button for the manufacturer-specified duration. The system must transmit vessel identity, position, and timestamp without triggering any onboard alarm, light, or indicator. Confirmation of receipt must come back from the CSO/Flag State before the test is considered complete and logged.

Pre-Test Coordination

  • Formal prior notification to the CSO and Flag Administration specifying the exact test time window.
  • Logged in the Official Log Book and the confidential ISPS security log.

Execution

  • Software terminals: select the dedicated "SSAS Test" mode so the transmitted packet is explicitly flagged as a test.
  • Physical button activation: depress the covert button for the manufacturer-specified duration (commonly a few seconds).
  • Covert verification: confirm no alarm, light, or indicator activates anywhere on board — bridge, engine room, or accommodation.

Post-Test

  • Await positive confirmation of receipt from the CSO/Flag State, explicitly marked as a test.
  • Reset any physical latching mechanism per manufacturer instructions.
  • Log the completion date/time and confirmation reference in the vessel's security records.

Chief Engineer Relevance

One of the covert SSAS activation points is frequently sited within the Engine Department (ECR or a workshop area). The CE must keep it unobstructed yet inconspicuous, and ensure electrical/automation staff never disturb SSAS wiring without the Master's explicit authorisation, since an accidental trigger initiates a real international security response.

Certificate of Competency (CoC)

  • Issued/endorsed by the Administration (DGMA for India) authorising an officer to serve at their certified rank.
  • Restricted to management/operational-level officers — Master, Chief Mate, OOW, Chief Engineer, Second Engineer, watchkeeping engineer, ETO.
  • Requires structured sea service, approved courses, and written/simulator/oral examination.
  • Revalidated roughly every 5 years via continued competency and medical fitness.

  • Any STCW-recognised certificate other than a CoC, confirming specific training/competency/sea-service requirements are met.
  • Issued to both officers and ratings.

  • Effect: denial of service — GPS loses lock, ECDIS reverts to dead reckoning, SATCOM links drop.

1. Answer the "why both" question first

The examiner is not asking you to describe two documents. He is asking whether you understand that they are answers to two different questions:

  • CDC — what service have you performed? A continuous record of engagements and discharges, issued by the flag or national administration, used to prove sea time for STCW certification, promotion and employment.
  • SID — who are you? A secure identity credential issued under an ILO convention, designed to be read and trusted by immigration and port authorities of other States.

A CDC carries identity details, which is exactly why candidates assume it is enough. It is not, because its recognition is national. The SID was created precisely to give the seafarer an internationally standardised credential that a foreign border officer can verify.

2. What ILO Convention 185 actually requires

  • It is the Seafarers' Identity Documents Convention (Revised), 2003, revising Convention No. 108 of 1958.
  • A ratifying State must issue an SID to each of its seafarers who applies and meets the conditions, and must maintain a national electronic database of the documents it has issued, with a permanent focal point to answer verification enquiries from other States.
  • Ratifying States must facilitate the entry of foreign seafarers holding a valid SID for the purposes of shore leave, transfer and transit — in principle without requiring a visa for those purposes.
  • The document is not a travel document in place of a passport, and it is not a certificate of competency. It says nothing about your qualifications.

3. The 2016 amendments — the currentness point

The Convention's annexes were amended by the International Labour Conference in 2016, entering into force the following year. The biometric was changed so that the SID now conforms to the ICAO machine-readable travel document standard used for electronic passports — a facial image held in a contactless chip, rather than the earlier fingerprint-template-in-a-barcode arrangement. The practical effect is that the SID can be read by the same border equipment as an ePassport, which was the barrier to its acceptance in the first decade. If you are asked what is new about the SID, this is the answer.

4. India's implementation

India issues a Biometric Seafarer Identity Document (BSID) through the national maritime administration, with the facial-biometric architecture the amended Convention calls for, and integrates it with the e-governance portal that also handles CDC applications and sea-service validation. For an Indian candidate the useful framing is that the two documents come from the same administration and the same digital profile, but they are issued under different legal instruments and are presented to different audiences — the CDC to the administration and the employer, the BSID to a foreign immigration officer.

5. What it is used for in practice

  • Shore leave in a foreign port — the access-ashore right that the ISPS security regime made harder and that both the MLC and the FAL Convention require to be facilitated.
  • Transit to or from a ship — joining and leaving, including travel through a third State.
  • Transfer between ships.
  • Identity verification at the port facility interface, where the ship security officer and the facility must establish who is seeking access.

Note what is not on the list: it does not evidence sea service, it does not evidence competency, and it does not replace the passport for ordinary international travel.

What is BMP MS (Best Management Practices for Maritime Security)? What did it replace, what is its purpose, and does it apply in all areas or only in a war zone?
isps
15-Second Answer: BMP Maritime Security is the shipping industry's single consolidated security guide, published by the main industry bodies. The current edition is 2026. It replaced BMP5, BMP West Africa and the Global Counter Piracy Guidance. It is global, not region-locked, and covers state and non-state threats — so it is not war-zone-only. It is industry guidance, not regulation — SOLAS XI-2 and ISPS are mandatory.
60-Second Answer: Before 2025 the guidance was region-locked — BMP5 for the Red Sea, Gulf of Aden, Indian Ocean and Arabian Sea, BMP West Africa for the Gulf of Guinea, and the Global Counter Piracy Guidance alongside them. A ship trading worldwide had to carry and reconcile several documents, and none was written for missile, drone or sea-mine attack by a state actor. BMP MS consolidated all of them into one publication built on a threat and risk management process: understand the current threat for this voyage, assess the risk to this ship, then apply proportionate measures. It covers threat assessment, voyage planning, mitigation, incident response and post-incident recovery, and it signposts out to the most current regional security intelligence rather than freezing a threat picture in print. The company feeds it into the Ship Security Assessment, the Ship Security Plan and the voyage risk assessment; onboard it drives hardening, lookout, drills and reporting to the regional centre for wherever you are. As Chief Engineer you own the machinery half: citadel services, steering-gear and emergency-generator protection, power for evasive speed, and securing external access to engine-room systems.
What it is, and what it replaced: Full title Best Management Practices for Maritime Security. Published by BIMCO, ICS, IMCA, INTERCARGO, INTERTANKO and OCIMF, supported by over forty maritime stakeholders. The first edition was announced 31 March 2025 and replaced three publications: BMP5, BMP West Africa, and the Global Counter Piracy Guidance for Companies, Masters and Seafarers — often shortened to “Global BMP”, but give the full title if you are asked. Those three are no longer carried on the OCIMF site. The current edition is the 2026 one, announced by the co-publishers in July 2026.
What changed in the 2026 edition: The principal addition is “Actions on Boarding by Activists”, placed in Section 6, Incident Response. It covers preparation, communication with protesters, de-escalation, evidence preservation and safe management of an activist boarding — and it advises against physical confrontation, razor wire, water cannon, and interfering with drones. That is a genuine change of posture: an activist boarding is handled as a documented, de-escalated event, not as a repelled attack. Beyond this, do not quote chapter or page structure — see the CE Oral Tip.
Purpose: To help all vessels plan voyages and to detect, avoid, deter, delay and report attacks and incidents wherever they occur. It supplies the threat and risk management process, a directory of the authorities to contact, and seafarer welfare material. It is an interactive online publication, which is why it can signpost live intelligence instead of printing a threat list that ages.
Why we follow it: Three reasons an examiner will accept. First, it is the single consolidated industry standard — ship, company, charterer, P&I club, underwriter and naval forces all work from one playbook. Second, it turns an abstract SOLAS XI-2 and ISPS duty into concrete, auditable measures a Master and Chief Engineer can implement and evidence. Third, the threat is real: in 2024 ships were attacked more than 100 times with weapons of war in the Black Sea and the Southern Red Sea, and four seafarers lost their lives; separately and globally, 126 seafarers were held hostage and 12 kidnapped in piracy and armed robbery.
Does it apply in all areas? Yes. BMP MS is deliberately global — one publication for anywhere, signposting the regional intelligence and reporting centre relevant to where you actually are. This is the biggest single change from BMP5, which was scoped to a named region. In practice you apply the same process everywhere and let the voyage-specific threat assessment, not a line on a chart, decide how much protection is proportionate.
Is it only for a war zone? No — this is the trap. It covers piracy, armed robbery, illegal boarding, activist boarding, state-actor intervention, missiles, drones, sea mines and cyber-related threats. A war-risk or high-risk area raises the assessed threat and so raises the measures, but it is not the trigger for the guidance existing. A ship alongside where robbery is a problem, or one facing a protest boarding in a quiet port, is inside BMP MS just as much as one crossing a war-risk area.
Status — guidance, not regulation: BMP MS is industry guidance. It is not an IMO convention or code, it has no entry-into-force date and no flag-State survey. What is mandatory is SOLAS Chapter XI-2 and the ISPS Code — Ship Security Assessment, approved Ship Security Plan, SSO and CSO, security levels, and the SSAS required by SOLAS XI-2. Say it in that order: mandatory instrument first, BMP MS as the practice that discharges it well. Reversing that order is the costly error. A charterparty, company procedure or war-risk underwriter may separately make BMP compliance binding for a voyage — that is contractual, not statutory.

Fresh water quality on the ship is challenged — what will be your action as Chief Engineer?
mlc
15-Second Answer: Treat it as a potable-water problem, not boiler chemistry. Stop the suspect supply, cross over to a clean tank, inform the Master, sample and test, and issue bottled water while the system is in doubt. Then work the four stages — production, bunkering, storage, distribution — disinfect, flush, re-test before restoring, and record it. The duty is MLC Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2.
60-Second Answer: Sir, there are two systems: potable and domestic water, and technical water for jacket cooling and boiler feed. A challenge affecting the crew is the potable one, and my duty there is a health duty under MLC Regulation 3.2 — ships shall carry and serve drinking water of appropriate quality, nutritional value and quantity — and under Standard A3.2.7, which requires frequent documented inspections of water supplies and of the spaces and equipment storing and handling them, by or under the Master’s authority. My action is three steps. Contain: isolate the suspect tank or line, cross over, tell the Master, and provide bottled water. Diagnose: sample at the point of complaint and at the tank, check residual disinfectant, and work production, bunkering, storage and distribution. Restore: disinfect, flush, re-test before returning to service, and record it. The technical benchmark is the WHO Guide to Ship Sanitation.

Say which water you are talking about — first sentence

  • Potable and domestic fresh water — drinking, cooking, washing. Governed as a health matter: MLC, the WHO regime, and port health.
  • Technical fresh water — jacket cooling water and boiler feed. Governed by engineering and maker’s chemistry limits: chlorides, hardness, pH, conductivity, treatment dosage. Nothing to do with MLC.
  • They share a production plant and sometimes a tank line, which is exactly why saying which one you mean is the first mark. A question that says “quality challenged … as Chief Engineer” in a conventions panel is the potable one.

The obligation — MLC 2006

  • Regulation 3.2, purpose: “to ensure that seafarers have access to good quality food and drinking water provided under regulated hygienic conditions”; paragraph 1 requires ships to carry on board and serve food and drinking water of appropriate quality, nutritional value and quantity that adequately covers the requirements of the ship and takes into account the differing cultural and religious backgrounds.
  • Standard A3.2.1 — each Member shall adopt laws providing minimum standards for the quantity and quality of food and drinking water.
  • Standard A3.2.2(a) — food and drinking water supplies shall be suitable in respect of quantity, nutritional value, quality and variety, having regard to the number of seafarers, religious requirements and cultural practices, and the duration and nature of the voyage.
  • Standard A3.2.7 — the competent authority shall require frequent documented inspections on board, by or under the authority of the master, of (a) supplies of food and drinking water, (b) all spaces and equipment used for storage and handling of food and drinking water, and (c) galley and other equipment for preparing and serving meals. This is the paragraph to quote — it makes the inspection documented and recurrent, which is what a PSC or MLC inspection looks for.
  • Non-conformity here is inspectable under Title 5 and appears on the DMLC: “Food and catering” is one of the sixteen working and living conditions listed in Appendix A5-I that must be inspected and approved by the flag State before a ship is certified under Standard A5.1.3. Note the heading is food and catering, not “drinking water” — the water obligation lives inside it.

The technical standard — the WHO regime

  • WHO Guide to Ship Sanitation — the reference text for potable-water systems on ships: bunkering, storage, distribution, disinfection and the ship’s water safety plan approach.
  • WHO Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality — the quality benchmark against which shipboard potable water is verified. The corpus already states this in the certificates card, Q9.
  • IHR 2005 and the SSCC/SSCEC regime are the certificate side of the same subject — a port health inspection that finds a contaminated water system is where this question becomes a detention. Again Q9.

Your action, in the order you would actually take it

  • 1. Contain. Isolate the suspect tank or branch and cross over to an unaffected tank. Inform the Master immediately — this is a crew-health matter and the inspection duty under A3.2.7 sits with him. Provide bottled water for drinking and food preparation while the system is in doubt. If anyone is symptomatic, it becomes a medical and reporting matter, not merely an engineering one.
  • 2. Sample and test. Take samples at the point of complaint and at the tank, not just one of them — that is what separates a distribution problem from a storage problem. Check residual disinfectant at the tap, and appearance, odour and taste. Send samples for microbiological analysis ashore if the ship cannot do it; retain a sample.
  • 3. Work the four stages. Production — on a fresh-water generator, distillate quality and the salinity alarm and dump valve, and whether production was taken too close to shore or in a polluted area. Bunkering — source, the hose used, whether it was capped, flushed and dedicated to potable water. Storage — tank coating condition, last internal inspection and cleaning, air-vent screens, and any risk of cross-connection or ingress. Distribution — hydrophore, calorifier temperature, dead legs, and the state of the UV or silver-ion unit if fitted.
  • 4. Correct. Disinfect and flush the affected part — tank, line or whole system as the diagnosis requires — restore the residual, and re-test before returning it to service. Do not restore on a single clear sample if the cause is not understood.
  • 5. Record and prevent. Log the incident, the tests and the corrective action; the record is the A3.2.7 evidence. Then close the loop: is the routine testing interval adequate, is the tank cleaning in the PMS, is the bunkering hose controlled, is the crew trained. Raise it as a non-conformity in the SMS if a procedure failed.

Where the CE’s duty ends

  • The system is yours — production, storage, treatment, distribution and the records of them. The health response is the Master’s, with the company and, if needed, port health and telemedical advice. Say that boundary aloud; claiming the medical side is a common overreach.

CE Relevance

Potable water is the one crew-welfare system that is entirely engineering plant. The Chief Engineer owns the generator, the tanks, the disinfection and the distribution, so a water-quality complaint is technically yours from the first minute even though the health consequences are the Master’s.
