Understand this first

This question is really about one boundary: the line between what a classification society does for the owner under a private contract, and what it does for the flag State under a delegated statutory authority. Limb (a) is life on the owner's side of that line - how a ship becomes classed and stays classed, and what the society can actually do to an owner who lets her slip. Limb (b) then doubles the owner's side and asks what happens to the flag State's side. The answer is: nothing. Class is contractual, so a ship can have two of them; statutory authority is delegated by an Administration, so it cannot be doubled by a commercial arrangement the Administration is not party to. SOLAS II-1/3-1 makes the point in its own wording - it requires compliance with the requirements of a classification society recognised by the Administration, and one recognised society satisfies it. Everything else in this question is detail hung on that boundary.

Model written answer

(a) Requirements for classification, and the role of the society — 8 marks

1. What classification is, and the line between class and statutory work

Classification is a private, contractual certification of technical fitness against a society's own published rules. A classification society develops rules for the structure, machinery and electrical installations of a ship, approves the design against them, surveys the ship during construction and then surveys her periodically in service. If she complies, the society assigns and maintains class and issues a Certificate of Class.

Two consequences follow immediately, and a Class I answer states both.

  • Class is not law. It is a contract between the owner and the society. The society's remedy for non-compliance is to impose a condition, suspend or withdraw class — not to detain the ship.
  • Class is nevertheless commercially compulsory. Hull and machinery underwriters, P&I clubs, charterers, financiers and flag Administrations all require it, so a ship out of class is effectively unemployable and uninsurable. Withdrawal of class is the sharpest sanction in the industry precisely because it is not a legal one.

The statutory work — SOLAS, MARPOL, Load Line, Tonnage, ISM, ISPS, MLC — belongs to the flag Administration. It only reaches a classification society because the Administration delegates it. Keeping those two ideas apart is what limb (b) then tests.

2. The requirements a ship must satisfy to be assigned class

Classification is not a single event. It is a sequence of requirements running from the drawing board to the end of the ship's life, and the marks in this limb are in that sequence.

  1. Application and rule selection. The owner applies to the society; the applicable rule set is fixed by ship type, size, service and intended notations — and for bulk carriers and oil tankers by the IACS Common Structural Rules.
  2. Plan approval. Structural drawings, midship section, scantlings, shafting and propeller, main and auxiliary machinery, piping, electrical single-line diagrams and control systems are submitted and approved against the rules before construction.
  3. Approval and certification of materials and components. Steel, castings, forgings, welding consumables, main engine, boilers, pressure vessels, shafting, anchors and chain cables are made at approved works under survey and issued with certificates.
  4. Survey during construction. The society's surveyor attends the yard to verify that the ship is being built to the approved plans — alignment, welding and NDT, tank testing, sea trials and machinery trials.
  5. Assignment of class. On satisfactory completion the society assigns a class notation and issues the Certificate of Class. The notations record the basis on which she was built — hull and machinery character marks, service restriction, ice class, UMS, and any special notation.
  6. Maintenance of class in service. Class is retained only by a continuing survey regime: annual, intermediate and five-yearly renewal surveys, docking or in-water surveys, tailshaft and boiler surveys, and machinery surveyed either at renewal or on a rolling continuous-survey or approved planned-maintenance basis.
  7. Reporting of damage and of change. The owner must report damage, grounding, defects and repairs affecting class, and must not alter classed items without approval. Failure to do so is itself a breach.
  8. Conditions of Class. Where a defect impairs compliance, the surveyor imposes a time-bound requirement to rectify. Class continues meanwhile; if the condition is not cleared by its due date, class is suspended.

All of that is class practice and the IACS framework. It is not a SOLAS requirement, and stating it as one is the commonest error in this question.

3. Where class rules acquire legal force, and how the society ensures compliance

The society becomes legally relevant at two distinct points, and they are different mechanisms.

First, SOLAS adopts class rules as the construction standard. Regulation II-1/3-1 provides that, in addition to the requirements elsewhere in the regulations, ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society which is recognized by the Administration in accordance with regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration which provide an equivalent level of safety. That single regulation is what converts a private rulebook into the operative construction standard for a Convention ship — and note the words designed, constructed AND MAINTAINED, which is why the survey cycle, not just the newbuilding survey, is a SOLAS matter.

Second, the Administration may delegate the statutory work itself. Regulation I/6 places inspection and survey on officers of the Administration, but permits the Administration to entrust them to nominated surveyors or to recognized organizations. Regulation XI-1/1 then requires that authorisation to be given in accordance with the RO Code (resolution MSC.349(92)), whose parts 1 and 2 are mandatory and part 3 recommendatory. The RO Code sets what an organisation must be and must do to be recognised, and how the Administration must oversee it.

So the society ensures compliance in two capacities that must never be confused: as classification society, enforcing its own rules by conditions of class and ultimately withdrawal; and as Recognized Organization, acting for the flag State under a specific delegation, issuing statutory certificates in the Administration's name. Under regulation I/6 the RO must at minimum be empowered to require repairs and to survey at a port State's request, and must notify the Administration where a ship's condition does not correspond substantially with her certificates.

(b) A ship classed by two societies — management and statutory responsibility — 8 marks

4. What dual class means, and why an owner takes it

Dual class — also called joint or double classification — is where a ship is entered with two classification societies simultaneously, and must comply with both societies' rules and hold a Certificate of Class from each. It is not the same as either of the two things candidates confuse it with:

  • It is not transfer of class, where the ship leaves one society and joins another under the IACS transfer-of-class procedure.
  • It is not the ordinary and universal split between a class society and a flag Administration.

Owners take dual class for commercial and regulatory access rather than for safety. Typical drivers: a national requirement that ships on a particular register or in a particular domestic trade be classed with the national society; charterer or oil-major preference for a specific society; financiers or the shipyard requiring a society the owner would not otherwise use; retaining an existing society's records on a second-hand purchase while adding the buyer's preferred society; and market access to a trade where one society is dominant.

The cost is real: two rule sets to satisfy, two sets of surveys to arrange and pay for, two sets of records, and the risk that the two societies take different views of the same defect.

5. How the arrangement is managed in practice

Management is contractual, and it is settled before the ship is entered, not after a dispute. The recurring elements are these.

  1. A joint-class agreement between the two societies and the owner. It records the scope each society takes, how surveys are shared, how findings are exchanged and how disagreement is resolved.
  2. The stricter rule governs. Where the two rule sets differ, the ship must satisfy both, so in practice she is built and maintained to the more demanding requirement in each area. Design approval is normally led by one society with the other reviewing and accepting.
  3. Division of the survey scope. The commonest shape is that each society surveys an agreed part of the ship — for example one taking hull and the other machinery — or that the societies alternate the survey cycle. Each then accepts the other's survey for its own class purposes.
  4. Mutual acceptance of records. Survey reports, thickness measurement reports, conditions of class and the survey status are exchanged so that both societies' records agree. IACS procedural requirements govern this exchange between IACS members; the arrangement between an IACS and a non-IACS society is purely a matter of the agreement.
  5. One survey status on board. The practical requirement is that the ship carries a single coherent picture of what is due and what is outstanding, whichever society raised it. Two divergent survey-status reports is the failure mode this whole arrangement exists to prevent.
  6. Conditions of class are honoured by both. A condition imposed by either society is a condition on the ship. Clearing it with one does not clear it with the other unless the agreement so provides.

6. Who conducts the statutory surveys and audits, and why this is a flag question

This is the part of the question that decides the mark, and the answer turns on a point of text.

Statutory surveys and audits are the flag Administration's, and only the flag Administration can say who performs them. Under regulation I/6 they are the Administration's own function; a society performs them only where that Administration has recognised and authorised it under regulation XI-1/1 and the RO Code. Dual class does not confer statutory authority on either society. A society may be the ship's classification society and have no delegation from her flag at all.

Regulation II-1/3-1 speaks of the requirements of a classification society — singular and indefinite. The construction standard is satisfied by compliance with one recognised society's requirements; nothing in the regulation contemplates or requires two.

The practical position is therefore:

  • The Administration nominates one RO to act for it in respect of a given ship — normally one of the two societies, and normally the one it has authorised. That society issues the statutory certificates in the Administration's name.
  • Statutory certification is not duplicated. A ship holds one Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate, not two. Two societies issuing the same statutory certificate would be a defect in the delegation, not a feature of dual class.
  • ISM and ISPS audits follow the same rule and are separately delegated. The Administration may authorise a different organisation for audits than for surveys, and the DOC and SMC belong to the company and ship respectively, not to a class society.
  • The Administration may reserve items to itself — commonly initial certification, exemptions, equivalents and detention decisions — and the delegation instrument says which.
  • Class survey and statutory survey may be carried out by the same surveyor on the same attendance, and usually are. That convenience is exactly what obscures the distinction, and the candidate must state it: the surveyor wears two hats and acts under two different authorities.

Conclusion for limb (b). Dual class doubles the classification obligation and leaves the statutory obligation single. The two societies share the class work by agreement between themselves; the statutory work is performed by whichever of them — or which other organisation, or the Administration's own officers — the flag State has authorised, and by nobody else.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question is two questions of eight marks and they are not the same kind of question. Limb (a) is a process question — it rewards a sequence, and the sequence is the ship's own life from plan approval to withdrawal of class. Limb (b) is a distinction question, and it rewards one idea held firmly: class is contractual and can be doubled; statutory authority is delegated and cannot.

Section 1 spends a short paragraph establishing that class is not law. That paragraph is what makes sections 3 and 6 scorable, because both turn on the boundary it draws. Candidates who skip it write eight marks of correct description in limb (a) and then cannot answer limb (b) at all.

The three capacities a classification society can occupy

Almost every error in this question comes from collapsing these into one. Hold them apart:

  • Classification society — acting under its own rules, under a private contract with the owner. Sanction: condition of class, suspension, withdrawal.
  • Recognized Organization — acting for a flag Administration under a delegation given per SOLAS XI-1/1 and the RO Code. Sanction: the Administration's, exercised through the RO.
  • Nominated surveyor — an individual, not an organisation, entrusted under regulation I/6.

A society is not automatically an RO, is not an RO for every flag, and is not an RO for every function of a flag that has authorised it. Each of those three is a separate examinable point.

The textual point most candidates miss

SOLAS II-1/3-1 requires compliance with the requirements of a classification society recognised by the Administration. The indefinite singular is doing work: the Convention's construction standard is met by one recognised society. Dual class is therefore a commercial arrangement layered on top of a statutory scheme that neither requires it nor recognises it. Once that is seen, limb (b) writes itself.

Common mistakes

  • Writing that SOLAS requires a ship to be classed. It does not. It requires compliance with a recognised society's structural, mechanical and electrical requirements or with equivalent national standards of the Administration — and the alternative is in the text.
  • Treating class certificates as statutory certificates. The Certificate of Class is not a Convention certificate and a port State control officer does not detain on it, though he may well ask to see the survey status.
  • Answering limb (b) as though dual class meant transfer of class, or as though it meant the ordinary class-and-flag split.
  • Saying that under dual class the two societies each issue statutory certificates. That would mean two flag delegations issuing the same certificate for the same ship.
  • Listing the survey types in limb (a) and never reaching plan approval, material certification or survey during construction — which is where classification actually begins.
  • Inventing an IACS PR number for the mutual-recognition procedure. Name the mechanism; the number is not required and a wrong one costs more than an absent one.
  • Forgetting that II-1/3-1 says maintained, and treating classification as a newbuilding activity that stops at delivery.

Examiner traps

  • 'Who is responsible?' in limb (b) invites the answer 'the classification society'. The correct answer names the flag Administration first and the society only as its delegate.
  • The stem prints 'classes by two classification societies' for 'classed by'. Answer the question the examiner set; do not comment on the typography.
  • The word audits is in the stem alongside surveys. It is there to test whether the candidate knows ISM and ISPS audits are separately delegated and produce company and ship documents. An answer that treats 'surveys and audits' as one phrase leaves marks on the table.
  • 'Ensuring compliance with safety and construction standards' in limb (a) is an invitation to explain the enforcement mechanism, not just to describe surveys. Conditions of class, suspension and withdrawal are the answer.

Understand this first

The examiner is testing whether you understand that substantial corrosion is a warning, not a failure. The ESP Code's own words are 'wastage in excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins, but within acceptable limits' — the steel is still good. What has been consumed is most of the corrosion allowance, the margin deliberately added to the net scantling to be eaten over the ship's life. So the correct response to a finding is not to cut plate out; it is to find out how far it goes. That is why the Code makes the finding a trigger that enlarges the survey — more thickness measurement, more close-up survey, and the location placed on a suspect-area register that follows the ship for the rest of her life. Renewal only enters when the measurements reach the renewal thickness, at which point the material is no longer in substantial corrosion at all: it is simply unacceptable. Hold that logic and the whole answer — criteria, techniques, steps, repairs and the society's role — falls out of it in order.

Model written answer

1. The definition - and it is printed twice, not once

Substantial corrosion is defined in the 2011 ESP Code (resolution A.1049(27), mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/2), at paragraph 1.2.9. The Code gives two definitions and a Class I answer gives both.

  1. The general definition. Substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that assessment of the corrosion pattern indicates wastage in excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins, but within acceptable limits.
  2. The Common Structural Rules definition. For ships built under the IACS Common Structural Rules, substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that assessment of the corrosion pattern indicates a gauged (or measured) thickness between t-net + 0.5 mm and t-net.

Read the general definition carefully, because both halves of it are examinable. 'In excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins' means most — but not all — of the corrosion allowance is gone. 'But within acceptable limits' means the structure is still acceptable. Substantial corrosion is therefore not a defect requiring immediate renewal. It is a warning state: a declaration that this location is close to its limit and must now be watched more closely and measured more widely.

The CSR definition says the same thing in a different currency. Under CSR the scantling is expressed as a net thickness plus a corrosion addition; substantial corrosion is the last 0.5 mm above the net thickness. Below t-net the steel is no longer acceptable at all.

2. Where the concept sits, and the model it presupposes

The definition only makes sense against the corrosion-allowance model of hull scantlings, and stating that model first is what converts a memorised sentence into an answer.

  • A rule scantling is a net thickness required for strength, plus a corrosion addition for the wastage expected over the ship's life.
  • Allowable margin is that addition. Renewal thickness is the point at which it is exhausted and the steel must be renewed.
  • Wastage is therefore measured not as a percentage of the original plate but as a proportion of the margin. Substantial corrosion is 75 per cent of the way through it.

The Code is deliberate about its own status. Paragraph 1.1.3 states that it contains the minimum extent of examination, thickness measurement and tank testing, and that 'the survey should be extended when substantial corrosion and/or structural defects are found and include additional close-up survey when necessary'. That sentence is the engine of the whole question: a finding of substantial corrosion is the trigger that enlarges the survey.

One further consequence is easy to miss. Paragraph 1.2.2 provides that a space used for both cargo and salt water ballast is treated as a ballast tank when substantial corrosion has been found in it. A finding does not merely add measurements — it can change the survey regime the space falls under.

3. The criteria used to assess it during inspections

The surveyor is not applying one criterion but a small set, and the Code supplies the vocabulary for each.

  1. The measured wastage against the allowable margin. The primary criterion. The gauged thickness is compared with the rule renewal thickness taken from the society's rules and the ship's approved scantling drawings; the result is expressed as a proportion of the margin, and 75 per cent is the threshold.
  2. The corrosion pattern, not a single reading. The Code says 'assessment of the corrosion pattern'. A single low point is not the test. General diminution, grooving, pitting and edge and weld-line wastage are read together across the member.
  3. Coating condition, graded by paragraph 1.2.11 as GOOD (only minor spot rusting), FAIR (local breakdown at edges of stiffeners and weld connections, and/or light rusting over 20 per cent or more of the areas under consideration, but less than POOR) or POOR (general breakdown of coating over 20 per cent or more of areas, or hard scale at 10 per cent or more). Coating condition is what tells the surveyor where corrosion will appear next.
  4. Whether a corrosion prevention system is in place and effective. Paragraph 1.2.10 treats a corrosion prevention system as normally a full hard protective coating, usually epoxy or equivalent. A tank with no effective system attracts a heavier survey regardless of today's measurements.
  5. Whether the location is a suspect area. Paragraph 1.2.8: suspect areas are locations showing substantial corrosion and/or considered by the surveyor to be prone to rapid wastage. Note the 'and/or' — the surveyor's judgement is an independent route into the category.
  6. The ship's history. Paragraph 1.2.7 requires that in selecting representative spaces, account be taken of the service and repair history and of identifiable critical or suspect areas. Previous findings drive present scope.

4. Inspection techniques - how it is actually found

The techniques run from the general to the particular, and the Code's own sequence is the right order to write them in.

  1. Preparation. The space must be cleaned, de-scaled, ventilated, gas-freed, illuminated and made safe for access. A poorly prepared tank is the commonest reason a survey cannot be credited.
  2. Overall survey — a survey to report on the overall condition of the hull structure and to determine the extent of additional close-up surveys required.
  3. Close-up survey — defined at paragraph 1.2.5 as a survey where the details of structural components are within the close visual inspection range of the surveyor, normally within reach of hand. Achieved by staging, rafting, cherry-picker, rope access or remote inspection techniques as the society accepts.
  4. Thickness measurement by ultrasonic gauging, carried out by a company certified for the purpose — the Code carries a dedicated annex of procedures for certification of a company engaged in thickness measurement of hull structures — and witnessed by the attending surveyor.
  5. Simultaneity. Paragraph 1.5 requires that, in any kind of survey, thickness measurements of structures in areas where close-up surveys are required be carried out simultaneously with the close-up surveys. Measuring after the staging has come down is not compliance.
  6. Supplementary techniques where the visual and gauged evidence points to cracking rather than wastage: magnetic particle or dye-penetrant inspection at weld toes and hard spots, and hammer testing to identify heavily scaled or grooved material.
  7. Tank pressure testing of boundaries, and internal examination of the spaces the Code lists.
  8. Recording. Thickness measurement reports on the Code's reporting format, the survey report, and the Condition Evaluation Report which summarises the ship's structural condition at the renewal survey.

5. What a finding does to the renewal survey

The renewal survey is the five-yearly hull survey that re-establishes fitness for the next period of validity of the Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate. Its shape is fixed before substantial corrosion is found, and is then enlarged by the finding.

The baseline. Under section 2, the renewal survey may commence at the fourth annual survey and be progressed during the succeeding year with a view to completion by the fifth anniversary date (2.1.1); the survey programme is dealt with in advance and thickness measurement is not to be held before the fourth annual survey (2.1.2); all cargo holds, ballast tanks, pipe tunnels, cofferdams and void spaces bounding cargo holds, decks and the outer hull are examined and the examination supplemented by thickness measurement and testing, sufficient to discover substantial corrosion, significant deformation, fractures, damage or other structural deterioration (2.1.4); a dry-dock survey forms part of it, with a minimum of two bottom inspections in the five-year period and a maximum interval of 36 months, in dry-dock for ships of 15 years and over (2.2.1–2.2.2); and concurrent crediting to both the intermediate and the renewal survey is not acceptable (2.1.7).

The steps once substantial corrosion is found:

  1. Extend the thickness measurements to the increased extent the Code's dedicated annex prescribes for areas of substantial corrosion. The Code is express, in the corresponding intermediate-survey provision, that these extended measurements must be carried out before the survey is credited as completed — the survey is not closed on a promise.
  2. Extend the close-up survey where necessary, per paragraph 1.1.3, to establish the extent of the affected structure rather than the extent of the first sample.
  3. Record the locations as suspect areas, so that they are examined at every subsequent survey and gauged again. This is the mechanism by which a single finding follows the ship for the rest of her life.
  4. Re-classify the space where 1.2.2 applies, so that a combined cargo and ballast space is thereafter surveyed as a ballast tank.
  5. Assess against renewal thickness. Where measurements are within the substantial-corrosion band the steel is acceptable but monitored; where they are at or below renewal thickness the material must be dealt with now.
  6. Repair or renew, to an approved scheme — section 6 below.
  7. Deal with the cause, not only the symptom. Coating condition graded POOR, a failed or absent corrosion prevention system, or defective anodes are addressed as part of the same event, because renewing steel into a failed coating simply restarts the clock.
  8. Complete the records — thickness measurement reports, survey report and Condition Evaluation Report — and only then credit the survey.

6. Repair methods and their acceptance

This part of the answer is class practice and the IACS framework, not Convention text, and should be written as such. What the ESP Code supplies is the trigger and the record; what the society's rules supply is the acceptance.

  • Cropping and renewal of wasted plating and stiffening is the primary and permanent remedy: the wasted material is cut out and replaced with new material of at least the original rule thickness, of approved grade, with approved welding procedures, qualified welders and approved consumables, and with post-weld NDT.
  • Partial renewal — renewing the wasted strake, face plate, web or bracket rather than the whole member — where the wastage is local and the surrounding structure is sound.
  • Insert plates with full penetration butt welds, sized and positioned so that seams do not fall in high-stress locations or coincide with existing welds.
  • Doubler plates are a temporary and conditional measure only, accepted at the society's discretion, usually with a time limit and a condition of class requiring permanent repair. A doubler conceals the parent material and traps moisture behind it, which is precisely why it is not a repair.
  • Weld repair and build-up for grooving corrosion along weld lines, within the limits the society allows — it is easy to do more harm than good by heat input into thin material.
  • Renewal of the corrosion prevention system: surface preparation, coating to the maker's specification, and renewal of sacrificial anodes.
  • Reinforcement or redesign where wastage is recurrent in the same location, because a repeated failure is a design or drainage problem rather than a maintenance one.

Two conditions govern all of it. First, a repair affecting classed structure requires the society's prior approval of the repair scheme and survey of the completed work. Second, the work must be recorded, because the next surveyor's scope depends on knowing what was done.

7. The role of the classification society

The society is present at every stage of what has just been described, and it is worth naming the roles separately because they are different.

  1. Rule-maker. It sets the scantlings, the corrosion additions and the renewal thicknesses against which everything is measured, and adopts the IACS Unified Requirements and the Common Structural Rules into its own rules.
  2. Survey planner. It agrees the survey programme in advance with the owner, and it determines the extent of close-up survey and thickness measurement — including the extension the finding requires.
  3. Attending surveyor. It carries out the overall and close-up surveys, witnesses the gauging and applies the assessment criteria in section 3.
  4. Approver of the repair. It approves the repair scheme, the materials, the welding procedures and the welders, and surveys the completed repair.
  5. Keeper of the record. It maintains the survey status, the thickness measurement records, the Condition Evaluation Report and the suspect-area register that determines future survey scope.
  6. Enforcer. Where compliance is impaired it imposes a condition of class with a due date; if that is not cleared, class is suspended. Because the Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate is normally issued by the same organisation acting as the flag State's Recognized Organization, the practical consequence reaches the ship's statutory certification too — but through the flag State's delegation, not through class itself.
  7. Certifier of the measuring company. The Code's own annex requires the thickness-measurement company to be certified, and that certification is a class function — a point almost no candidate makes and one that shows the whole chain has been understood.

The commonest failure is to write a good definition and then a vague paragraph about surveys, which collects perhaps five of sixteen marks. Common mistakes

  • Giving only one of the two definitions in paragraph 1.2.9.
  • Saying substantial corrosion means 75 per cent of the plate thickness is lost, rather than 75 per cent of the allowable margin.
  • Treating substantial corrosion as a defect requiring immediate renewal, which contradicts 'but within acceptable limits'.
  • Describing the renewal survey generically and never saying what the finding changes — which is the actual question.
  • Omitting that extended measurements must be completed before the survey is credited.
  • Offering doubler plates as a repair method without saying they are temporary, conditional and normally attract a condition of class.
  • Attributing the ESP Code to SOLAS chapter II-1. It is made mandatory by SOLAS XI-1/2.
  • Presenting repair acceptance criteria as Convention requirements. They are class rules.
  • Quoting a numeric renewal thickness. It is rule- and ship-specific and inventing one is worse than giving the method.

Examiner traps

  • 'Define' is the first word of the stem. An approximate paraphrase will not do; the two-limb definition is the cheapest three marks on the paper and the most commonly half-taken.
  • 'What criteria are used' is plural and is not a repeat of the definition. It wants the assessment framework: pattern rather than point, coating condition, corrosion prevention system, suspect-area status and survey history.
  • 'Including inspection techniques, repair methods, and the role of the classification society' is a three-part instruction embedded in a clause. Each of the three is worth marks and each needs its own section.
  • The ESP Code applies to bulk carriers and oil tankers. Saying so is a mark; silently generalising the definition to all ships is a small error a strong examiner will notice.
  • The Code's language is 'should' throughout, because it is a code applied through class and made mandatory by SOLAS XI-1/2 rather than drafted as SOLAS text. Reproducing it as 'shall' misstates the instrument.

Uncertainty and currency

The corpus holds the base 2011 ESP Code and its instrument log records expressly that the amendment chain after A.1049(27) is not assembled (RQ-26), the Code being amended at almost every MSC session. Everything cited in this answer is read from the base text and no amendment is asserted. For a definition-and-procedure question that is sufficient; for an edition-precise citation it would not be, and the log says so.

The annex that carries the extended measurement extent is numbered differently in the bulk carrier and oil tanker annexes of the Code, so it is named here by its function rather than by a number that would be right for only one ship type.

No numeric renewal thickness, corrosion addition or acceptance criterion for doubler plates is quoted. Those are rule- and ship-specific, the corpus holds no class rulebook, and the method is worth more to a candidate than a figure that would be wrong for his ship.

Model written answer

(a) The importance of gender diversity, and the challenges women face — 8 marks

1. Where gender equality sits, and why it reaches shipping

Sustainable Development Goal 5 — gender equality and the empowerment of all women and girls — is one of the seventeen goals of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, adopted in 2015. It is a cross-cutting goal: it is not a sector programme, it is a condition the other sixteen are expected to be pursued under.

Shipping is caught by it more sharply than most industries for three structural reasons, and naming them is a better opening than repeating the goal.

  • It is one of the most male-dominated occupations in the world of work, and by a very wide margin at sea.
  • It is internationally regulated, so a global norm can be given effect through IMO and ILO instruments in a way that a purely national industry could not achieve.
  • It faces a structural officer shortage. An industry short of qualified officers that recruits effectively from half the population has a supply problem it has partly created.

2. Why gender diversity matters to the industry

The case is worth making as an argument rather than as a slogan, because the examiner is testing management reasoning.

  1. Labour supply. The industry projects a persistent shortfall of certificated officers. Recruiting from the whole talent pool rather than half of it is the largest single untapped source, and it is available without any change to training capacity.
  2. Quality of selection. If a profession draws from half the population it is, on average, taking the median of that half rather than the top of the whole. Widening the pool raises the standard of entry at no cost.
  3. Decision quality and safety culture. Diverse teams are less prone to the group conformity that sits behind a recognisable class of maritime casualty — the bridge or engine-room team in which nobody challenges the person in charge. Diversity of background supports the willingness to speak up that safety management systems depend on and cannot mandate.
  4. Retention and the shore pipeline. Sea-going experience feeds the shore side — superintendents, surveyors, DPAs, port and flag administrations, class. A sea-going population that excludes women produces a shore management population that excludes them a decade later, which is why the effect compounds.
  5. Commercial and reputational expectation. Charterers, financiers and cargo interests increasingly report on workforce composition under environmental, social and governance frameworks, so it has become a commercial fact and not only an ethical one.
  6. Legitimacy. An industry that carries the great majority of world trade and asks for public and regulatory trust is weakened by a workforce profile that no other technical profession would now defend.

3. The challenges women actually face

Separate these into three groups. Running them together produces a list; separating them produces an analysis, and the second scores.

Structural and institutional.

  • Recruitment and cadet-berth allocation, where sponsorship depends on operators who may be reluctant to place a lone woman on a ship.
  • Family, social and cultural expectation about a sea career, which in many labour-supplying countries operates before any employer is involved.
  • The absence of visible role models and of senior women in a position to sponsor juniors — a self-perpetuating shortage.
  • Employer concern about the cost or complexity of accommodating a mixed crew, which is a management problem presented as an operational one.

Shipboard and physical.

  • Personal protective equipment and workwear sized for the average male body. This is not a comfort issue; ill-fitting PPE is a safety defect, and it is precisely the point the MLC now addresses — see section 5.
  • Accommodation, sanitary arrangements and privacy on ships designed and built for a single-sex crew.
  • Isolation: being the only woman on board, often for months, with no peer and no informal support.
  • Harassment, bullying and sexual harassment, aggravated by the closed community, the confined environment, the impossibility of leaving, and a reporting line that may run through the person complained of.
  • Access to medical care and to gender-specific health provision at sea.
  • Manual handling and equipment design based on assumed male anthropometry — a genuine ergonomic issue and one an engineer should identify as a design problem rather than a personal one.

Career and progression.

  • Interrupted sea time around maternity, in a certification system whose revalidation depends on recent sea service.
  • Lack of shore-based career paths that would retain trained women who leave the deep sea.
  • Perception of competence, and the burden of being treated as representative of a category rather than as an individual.
  • Higher attrition at the first-command and first-chief-engineer stage — the point at which the shore pipeline is actually fed.

(b) The IMO's role, and India's initiatives — 8 marks

4. The IMO's role - the programme, the mechanism, and its limits

The IMO's contribution is real and it is worth being precise about what kind of contribution it is.

  1. The Women in Maritime Programme. Established within the Organization's technical co-operation work in the late 1980s, its purpose is to improve women's access to maritime training and to technical and senior positions in the sector, principally through fellowships, training places and institutional support in developing member States.
  2. The regional Women in Maritime Associations. The programme's most durable structure: regional networks that give women in the sector professional contact, mentoring and visibility across national boundaries.
  3. Agenda-setting. The Organization made 'Empowering Women in the Maritime Community' the World Maritime Day theme for 2019, and established an annual International Day for Women in Maritime on 18 May. These are not law, and the value of naming them correctly is that they show the mechanism — the IMO's principal lever here is convening and visibility.
  4. Measurement. The joint IMO and WISTA International Women in Maritime Survey is the industry's principal attempt to measure participation. Measurement matters because in the absence of it the debate runs on anecdote.
  5. Gender mainstreaming in its own work, including in the Strategic Plan and in the technical co-operation programme, and encouragement of member States to report on it.
  6. The competence framework is already neutral. STCW sets standards of competence and makes no distinction of sex. That is worth stating, because it locates the problem correctly: the barrier is not the certification regime, it is access, conditions and retention.

The limit is the honest part of this limb. Almost all of the IMO's gender work is promotional and capacity-building rather than mandatory. There is no IMO convention requirement that a company employ women, and none is proposed. The hardest law in this field is not an IMO instrument at all — which is the next section.

5. The hard legal layer, and what changed ten weeks before this sitting

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is an ILO instrument, amended by the Special Tripartite Committee under Article XIII by the Convention's own simplified procedure — not by IMO tacit acceptance. It is where gender equality in shipping stops being aspiration and becomes an enforceable requirement inspectable by port State control.

The 2022 amendments entered into force on 23 December 2024, ten weeks before this sitting. Among them, Standard A4.3, paragraph 1(b) now requires that the measures a Member adopts for occupational safety and health include 'reasonable precautions to prevent occupational accidents, injuries and diseases on board ship, including through the provision of all necessary appropriately-sized personal protective equipment'.

That single word — appropriately-sized — is the most concrete gender-equality provision in the whole of maritime law, and a candidate who reaches it has answered this question at a different level from one who has not. PPE designed for the average male body does not protect a smaller person; it is a hazard. The obligation is now express, it is in Part A of the Code and therefore mandatory, it takes effect through each Member's national law, and it is verifiable at inspection.

Two further points complete the legal layer. The 2016 amendments, in force 8 January 2019, strengthened protection against shipboard harassment and bullying, and the Convention's guidance directs competent authorities to take harassment and bullying into account in national occupational safety and health guidelines and to have regard to the joint ICS and ITF guidance on eliminating it. And the 2025 amendments, approved in June 2025, are after this sitting and form no part of the answer.

6. India's initiatives to reduce the gender gap

India is one of the largest suppliers of seafarers in the world, so its policy has effect out of proportion to its own fleet. The measures fall into four groups, and they are stated here at the level the evidence supports.

  1. Regulatory and institutional. The Directorate General of Shipping is the competent authority under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and administers seafarer training, certification and welfare. India has ratified the MLC, and its obligations — including Standard A4.3 — take domestic effect through the Merchant Shipping (Maritime Labour) Rules made under the 1958 Act. The competent authority is therefore the route by which the appropriately-sized PPE obligation reaches an Indian ship.
  2. Training access. Pre-sea and post-sea training at DG Shipping-approved maritime training institutes is open to women across the deck, engine and electro-technical streams, and the Indian Maritime University, established by the Indian Maritime University Act, 2008, provides the degree-level route on the same basis. Access to training is the necessary first condition and India's system does not restrict it.
  3. Employment and placement. The persistent Indian bottleneck is not admission to training but the cadet berth — the shipboard training placement without which a certificate cannot be completed. Administrative measures directed at training-berth availability, at conditions for women on board Indian ships and at grievance and harassment redressal are the operative levers, and they are exercised through the Directorate's circulars and orders.
  4. Policy and advocacy. India's national maritime policy documents carry commitments to increase women's participation in the maritime workforce, and India supports the IMO's Women in Maritime work including through the regional association for its area.

A closing judgement, which the command word 'discuss' invites. The regulatory barrier has largely gone: STCW is neutral, Indian training is open, and the MLC now requires equipment that actually fits. What remains is berth allocation, shipboard conditions and retention — and those are decided by shipowners and by senior officers on board, not by regulators. That places the responsibility squarely on the management-level officer this examination is certifying.

Model written answer

(a) The concept and working principles — 6 marks

1. The principle, stated as a contrast

3D printing, properly called additive manufacturing, builds a component by adding material layer upon layer under the direct control of a digital model. The definition is worth stating as a contrast, because that is what makes it examinable:

  • Subtractive manufacturing — turning, milling, grinding — starts with more material than the part and removes what is not wanted.
  • Formative manufacturing — casting, forging, rolling, moulding — takes material and forces it into shape, and needs a pattern, mould or die.
  • Additive manufacturing starts with nothing and places material only where the part requires it.

Three consequences follow directly from that one sentence, and they explain everything in limbs (b) and (c).

  1. Complexity is free. An internal cooling passage, a lattice, a conformal duct costs nothing extra to print, because the machine is only following a different path. In machining or casting each of those is expensive or impossible.
  2. No tooling is needed. There is no mould and no die, so there is no minimum economic quantity. One part costs the same each as a hundred — which is the exact opposite of casting economics, and it is why the technology matters for spares.
  3. The part is a file. The design can be transmitted anywhere and made where it is needed, which turns a physical inventory into a digital one.

2. The working chain, from model to finished part

The stem asks for working principles, and they live in the chain rather than in the machine. Every process family runs the same eight steps.

  1. Digital model. Either an existing CAD model, or a model reverse-engineered by 3D scanning an existing component — which is how obsolete parts with no surviving drawing are recovered.
  2. Tessellation. The solid model is converted to a surface mesh the machine software can process.
  3. Orientation and supports. The part is oriented in the build volume, and support structures are generated for overhangs and to anchor the part against distortion. Orientation is an engineering decision, not a convenience: it fixes the direction of the layers and therefore the direction in which the part is weakest.
  4. Slicing. The oriented model is cut into horizontal layers, typically some tens to a few hundred micrometres thick, and each layer is converted into a tool path.
  5. Build. The machine deposits, melts or cures one layer, lowers or raises the platform by one layer thickness, and repeats until the part is complete.
  6. Removal. The part is separated from supports and build plate — for metals normally by wire cutting.
  7. Post-processing. Stress relief or full heat treatment, hot isostatic pressing where porosity must be closed, machining of mating and sealing surfaces, and surface finishing. An as-built metal part is rarely a finished part.
  8. Inspection. Dimensional check, non-destructive examination appropriate to the defects the process produces, and where required destructive testing of witness coupons built alongside the part.

3. The process families a marine engineer will meet

  • Material extrusion — a thermoplastic filament is melted and laid down. Cheap, tolerant, safe, low strength, temperature-limited. The realistic candidate for a machine on board.
  • Vat photopolymerisation — liquid resin cured by light. Excellent resolution and surface finish, brittle and poorly resistant to ageing. Patterns, models and gauges rather than working parts.
  • Powder bed fusion — a laser or electron beam melts successive layers of metal or polymer powder. The principal route to dense, functional metal parts, and also the most demanding in equipment, atmosphere control, powder handling and post-processing.
  • Directed energy deposition — metal powder or wire melted as it is deposited by a moving head. Suited to large components, to cladding, and above all to repair by adding material to a worn or damaged component, which is its most immediately useful marine application.
  • Binder jetting — a binder is printed into a powder bed and the green part is then debound and sintered. Also used to print sand cores and moulds, so that a conventional casting is produced with no pattern — an indirect route that reaches marine parts sooner than direct metal printing does.

(b) Application in the maritime industry — 5 marks

4. Where it is actually used

Group these by where the machine is, because that is what decides feasibility.

Ashore — in yards, workshops and service centres.

  • Tooling, jigs and fixtures for construction and repair — the largest and least glamorous use, and the one with the clearest payback.
  • Patterns, cores and moulds for castings, removing the pattern-making lead time from propellers, manifolds and housings.
  • Obsolete and long-lead spares: a component for a discontinued engine or pump, reverse-engineered from the worn part and remade — the case with no conventional alternative at all.
  • Repair rather than replacement by directed energy deposition or cold spray: rebuilding worn shaft journals, liner surfaces, valve seats, pump wear rings and impeller edges.
  • Prototyping and design verification of retrofits — a printed mock-up checked for fit in a congested machinery space before anything is fabricated.

On board.

  • Polymer parts printed in service: guards, brackets, covers, handles, gaskets and seals in suitable materials, cable cleats, lagging clips, tools and templates.
  • Gauges and templates for a repair being planned before the next port.
  • Emergency temporary items that keep an auxiliary in service until a proper part arrives — and the honest description of these is temporary.

In the supply chain.

  • Digital inventory. The part is held as a qualified file rather than as steel on a shelf, and is printed at a service centre near the ship's next port. This is the model with the largest potential effect on the industry, and it is a logistics change more than a manufacturing one.
  • Distributed manufacturing near major bunkering and repair ports, which shortens the chain that today runs from an OEM warehouse by air freight.

(c) Advantages and challenges — 5 marks

5. Advantages, stated where they are real

  1. Lead time for the parts that have no alternative. The gain is largest exactly where conventional supply is worst — obsolete parts, single items, and parts whose tooling no longer exists.
  2. No tooling and no minimum quantity. One-offs are economic, which is the normal marine spares case.
  3. Inventory and capital. A digital file occupies no space, does not corrode and is not written off when the ship is sold.
  4. Design freedom. Internal passages, weight reduction, part consolidation — several components replaced by one, removing joints and therefore leak paths and fasteners.
  5. Repair as an alternative to replacement, which is often the fastest and cheapest route and keeps a large expensive component in service.
  6. Resilience and sustainability. Less dependence on long supply chains and air freight, less transport emission, and less material wasted than in machining from bar.

Say where the advantage does not apply. For a standard part available from stock at low cost, additive manufacturing is slower and dearer than buying it. The technology competes on availability, not on unit price.

6. Challenges - and the one that actually blocks adoption

Qualification and approval is the real obstacle, and it should be written first. A part in a classed or statutory application must be of an approved material, made by an approved and repeatable process at an approved works, with demonstrated and reproducible properties and appropriate examination. Additive manufacturing makes each of those harder: the material is created at the same moment as the part, so material approval and process approval collapse into one; the properties depend on machine, parameters, orientation, powder batch and post-processing; and there is no established service history. The classification societies have published guidance in this field, and this answer deliberately does not cite it, because it is not held by the corpus and was not read. What can be said with confidence is the shape of the problem — and that it is a qualification problem, not a printing problem.

The remaining challenges, in the order they will be met:

  1. Material properties and anisotropy. A layer-built part is directional: it is generally weaker across the layers than along them, so build orientation is a design input. Residual stress, porosity, lack-of-fusion defects and rough as-built surfaces all reduce fatigue performance — which matters most in exactly the rotating and pressure-containing components an engineer would most like to print.
  2. Post-processing is unavoidable. Heat treatment, hot isostatic pressing and machining are part of the cost and the lead time, and an answer that presents printing as a one-step process has missed how the part is actually produced.
  3. Size and rate. Build volumes are limited and build rates are slow; large marine components are outside the practical envelope of direct metal printing.
  4. Cost. Machines, qualified feedstock and skilled operators are expensive, and metal powder has handling, storage and shelf-life requirements.
  5. Shipboard practicality. Space, power, environmental control, vibration and ship motion, powder and fume hazards, and the competence to run and inspect the process. This is why polymer extrusion is a realistic shipboard technology and metal powder bed fusion is not.
  6. Intellectual property and liability. Who owns the file? Reverse-engineering an OEM part and printing it raises design-right questions and, more sharply, the question of who is liable when a printed part fails — the OEM has supplied nothing.
  7. Cyber security of the file. Once the part is a file, an altered file is an altered part. The integrity and traceability of the digital chain becomes a safety matter, and it is the point at which this question meets the ship's cyber risk management obligations.
  8. Competence and standards. Operators, designers and inspectors trained for a technology whose defect population is unfamiliar to people trained on castings and forgings.
  9. Management of change. Under the safety management system, introducing a printed part into a machinery system is a change to be assessed, recorded and approved — particularly where the item serves critical equipment.

Balanced conclusion. At present the technology is mature and paying for itself in tooling, patterns, prototypes, polymer items and repair by deposition. Its transformative promise — the qualified digital spare printed near the ship — is credible and is limited not by printers but by qualification, approval and liability. A Chief Engineer's correct position is to use it now where it is already sound, and to require the same evidence of any printed part in a classed application that he would require of any other component.

Why an additively manufactured part is hard to approve

The ordinary approval chain for a marine component is: approved material, from an approved manufacturer, by an approved process, with tested properties, examined appropriately. Additive manufacturing disturbs every link:

  • Material and part are created together, so material approval and process approval cannot be separated as they are for a forging cut from approved bar.
  • Properties depend on the build — machine, parameters, orientation, powder batch, layer thickness, heat treatment. Two parts from the same file are not necessarily the same part.
  • The defect population is unfamiliar: porosity, lack of fusion and residual stress rather than the inclusions and shrinkage an inspector knows from castings.
  • Traceability now includes the file, not only the heat number.

That is the whole difficulty, and it can be reasoned out without citing any guideline — which is exactly how this answer states it.

Common mistakes

  • Describing only fused filament printing and generalising it to metal parts.
  • Presenting printing as a one-step process and omitting heat treatment, HIP, machining and inspection. An as-built metal part is not a finished part.
  • Ignoring anisotropy and build orientation — the most important engineering property of a printed part and the one most likely to be tested orally.
  • Claiming cost savings on standard parts. For a part available from stock, printing is slower and dearer; the advantage is availability.
  • Quoting a percentage lead-time or cost saving from a vendor case study as though it were a general figure.
  • Citing a class guideline by number that has not been read.
  • Omitting repair by deposition, which is the most immediately useful marine application of all.
  • Treating metal powder bed fusion as shipboard-feasible. Powder handling, inert atmosphere and post-processing rule it out.
  • Missing intellectual property and liability, which are the commercial obstacles alongside the technical one.

Understand this first

The whole question rests on one fact: 'perils of the sea' is a narrow legal term, not a description of everything that can go wrong on a voyage. It means fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas — so the loss must be accidental rather than inevitable, and it must be caused by the sea rather than merely happening at sea. Fire proves the point: it is accidental, it happens at sea, and it is not a peril of the seas, which is why every policy lists it separately. Once that narrowness is seen, the rest of the question explains itself. The policy adds a list of other perils by express words because the first head is so narrow. Where even that list does not reach, the owner buys cover elsewhere — extension, separate policy, P&I entry, better terms. And for the machinery perils that the courts had held were not perils of the seas — a broken shaft, a burst boiler, a latent defect, an engineer's mistake — the market wrote the Inchmaree clause, and attached to it the one qualification that makes this an examinable question: the cover is lost if the loss resulted from want of due diligence by shore management, and it is the underwriters, not the owner, who must prove that it did.

Model written answer

1. What 'perils of the sea' actually means

The phrase is far narrower than candidates expect, and the whole question turns on that narrowness.

The statutory definition, carried in the Rules for Construction of Policy in the Schedule to the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, is that the term 'perils of the seas' refers only to fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas, and does not include the ordinary action of the winds and waves.

Two tests follow, and both must be satisfied.

  1. Fortuity. The loss must be accidental — not inevitable, not certain, not the natural consequence of the ship going to sea. Deterioration that was bound to happen is not a peril; it is the passage of time.
  2. Of the sea, not merely at sea. The sea must be the cause, not just the location. A fire in the engine room is not a peril of the seas even though it happens at sea — which is exactly why fire is listed separately in every policy.

What is therefore covered as a peril of the seas: stranding and grounding; sinking and foundering; collision with another vessel; striking a submerged or floating object; heavy weather damage beyond the ordinary action of wind and sea; entry of seawater through an accidental opening; capsizing; and damage from an accidental incursion of the sea into the ship.

What is excluded, and this list earns as many marks as the first: ordinary wear and tear; ordinary leakage and breakage; inherent vice of the subject matter; the ordinary action of the winds and waves; corrosion and gradual deterioration; damage by rats or vermin; and injury to machinery not proximately caused by a maritime peril.

2. The maritime perils that are covered but are NOT perils of the sea

This section is what makes the rest of the question intelligible, and most candidates omit it.

A hull and machinery policy does not insure 'perils of the sea' alone. Perils of the seas is only the first item in a list, and the other items are there precisely because they would otherwise fall outside it. The perils clause of the standard hull form covers, in substance:

  • Perils of the seas, rivers, lakes or other navigable waters
  • Fire and explosion
  • Violent theft by persons from outside the vessel
  • Jettison
  • Piracy
  • Contact with land conveyance, dock or harbour equipment or installation
  • Earthquake, volcanic eruption or lightning
  • Accidents in loading, discharging or shifting cargo or fuel

So the correct answer to 'what perils of the sea are covered' is: the fortuitous casualties of the sea itself — and the policy then adds, by express words, a series of other perils that are not perils of the sea at all.

3. How an insured covers a peril that is not an insured peril - with examples

The stem's second question presupposes that a shipowner faces exposures the perils clause does not reach. Four routes are available, and a good answer gives an example of each.

  1. Extend the existing policy by an additional clause. The risk is written into the policy for an additional premium, usually against warranties or a higher deductible. Example: the additional perils cover that buys back the exclusion on the cost of repairing or replacing a part found to be defective, where the ordinary latent-defect cover pays for the resulting damage but not for the faulty part itself.
  2. Buy a separate policy for the excluded risk. Some risks are excluded from the hull policy as a matter of market structure and are insured elsewhere. Examples: war, strikes, terrorism and confiscation, excluded by the hull policy and bought back by a war risks policy or an association entry; loss of hire, for the owner's own trading loss during repairs, which no hull policy covers; increased value and hull interest, above the sum insured; and freight insurance.
  3. Cover it mutually, through a P&I club. The hull policy insures the ship; it does not insure the owner's liabilities to third parties. Examples: pollution liability, cargo claims, crew injury, illness, death and repatriation, wreck removal, damage to fixed and floating objects, stowaways and fines. This is the single most important route and the one most often left out.
  4. Adjust the terms rather than the perils. Where the problem is quantum rather than scope, the answer is a higher sum insured, a lower deductible, or removal of a restrictive warranty — again for premium.

Two limits should be stated, because they show the routes are not unlimited. An insurer will not write back a loss that is inevitable rather than fortuitous — wear and tear is a maintenance cost, not an insurable risk. And cover for a known existing defect is not available on ordinary terms; that is not insurance, it is a repair bill.

4. The perils whose cover is qualified by due diligence

This is the third demand and it is the one that decides the mark. Alongside the perils in section 2, the hull form covers a second group, and every item in it is expressly qualified. The group is known in the market as the Inchmaree clause, after the nineteenth-century case in which a donkey-engine casualty was held not to be a peril of the seas — which is precisely why the clause had to be written.

The perils in the qualified group are:

  1. Bursting of boilers
  2. Breakage of shafts
  3. Latent defect in the machinery or hull
  4. Negligence of Master, Officers, Crew or Pilots
  5. Negligence of repairers or charterers, provided they are not an assured under the policy
  6. Barratry of Master, Officers or Crew

The qualification is that the loss or damage is covered provided it has not resulted from want of due diligence by the Assured, Owners, Managers or Superintendents or any of their onshore management.

Three points of precision, and each is examinable.

  • The Master, Officers and Crew are NOT in the due-diligence list. Their negligence is an insured peril, not a defence. The proviso reaches shore management. This is the most misunderstood feature of the clause: an engineer's mistake is covered; a superintendent's failure to act on a reported defect may not be.
  • Latent defect cover pays for the damage, not for the defect. The consequential damage is recoverable; the cost of renewing the defective part itself is conventionally excluded, and buying that back is exactly what the additional perils cover in section 3 does.
  • Barratry is a wrongful act wilfully committed by Master or crew to the prejudice of the owner. It is covered because it is a peril to the owner, and it necessarily involves no privity of the owner.

5. Where the onus of proof lies, and why it matters

The general rule is settled and follows the structure of the policy.

  • The assured must prove that the loss was caused by a peril insured against. That is his affirmative case, and it is his burden.
  • The insurer must prove any exception, exclusion or proviso on which it relies to reduce or defeat a claim that otherwise falls within the cover.

The due-diligence provision is a proviso limiting the insurer's liability, not a condition precedent that the assured must satisfy before he can claim. It follows — and this is the express point of the question — that once the assured has proved a loss falling within the qualified group, the onus of proving want of due diligence lies on the underwriters. The assured does not have to prove that he was diligent; the insurer has to prove that he was not.

Why the allocation matters in practice. Want of due diligence is a negative, it concerns the internal conduct of the owner's own organisation, and the evidence of it sits in the owner's records. Placing the burden on the insurer therefore makes it a demanding one, and it is why claims in this group are fought on documents: the planned maintenance system, defect reports, superintendent's reports and correspondence, class survey records and conditions of class, the safety management system's maintenance and non-conformity records, and the record of what shore management was told and what it did about it.

So the direct answer to the stem's third demand is: bursting of boilers, breakage of shafts, latent defect, negligence of Master, Officers, Crew or Pilots, negligence of repairers or charterers, and barratry — covered, but subject to a due-diligence proviso which the insurer must prove is engaged.

6. The Indian frame, and what it means on board

The statute. The governing law for a policy subject to Indian law is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. The UK Marine Insurance Act 1906 is its model and is often quoted in coaching material, but it is not the operative statute here, and the later UK reform of the avoidance remedy does not apply to Indian law. Under the 1963 Act a contract of marine insurance is a contract of utmost good faith (section 19), and the assured must disclose every material circumstance known to him before the contract is concluded (section 20); section 66 defines a general average loss.

The clause layer. The perils themselves are not in the statute. They are in the policy, and Indian hull policies are ordinarily written on the London market forms, so the perils clause and the Inchmaree clause described above are the operative wording. The Act supplies the framework of the contract; the clauses supply the cover.

What this means for a Chief Engineer, which is why the question is set in this paper at all:

  • Your negligence is insured. The claim is not defeated because an engineer made a mistake.
  • The claim is put at risk by shore management's failure to act — and the evidence of that failure, or of its absence, is the paperwork you generate.
  • A defect reported and not repaired is the single most dangerous document in a machinery casualty file, because it converts a fortuitous loss into an argument about diligence.
  • Maintain the records: planned maintenance, defect reports, spares ordered and chased, class conditions and their clearance, and what you told the office and when.
  • Report and preserve. On a casualty, notify promptly, preserve the failed component, photograph the evidence and do not dismantle beyond necessity before the surveyor attends — because the burden the insurer must discharge can only be resisted with evidence.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains three questions and a fourth hidden one. It asks what perils of the sea are covered; what an insured can do about a peril that is not covered; and which perils are covered subject to a due-diligence burden on the insurer. The hidden question is the one in section 2: which covered perils are not perils of the sea at all. Without it the second and third demands make no sense, because both are about the parts of the policy that exist precisely because perils of the seas is narrow.

The structure also puts the burden analysis in a section of its own. That is deliberate: the stem's own words are 'the onus of proof is upon the insurers', so the examiner has told the candidate where the marks are, and an answer that mentions it in a clause rather than developing it has ignored an instruction.

The two tests, and why fire is listed separately

Fortuitous, and of the sea rather than merely at sea. Both must hold.

The best single illustration is fire. A fire on board happens at sea, damages the ship and is plainly accidental — and it is not a peril of the seas, because the sea did not cause it. That is why every hull policy lists fire and explosion separately. Give that example and the definition is proved rather than asserted.

The second best is the Inchmaree case itself: a donkey engine damaged by a blocked valve was held not to be a peril of the seas, and the market responded by writing the clause that now bears the ship's name. The whole of the qualified group in section 4 exists because the definition in section 1 is narrow.

The distinction that most candidates get wrong

The due-diligence proviso names the Assured, Owners, Managers or Superintendents or any of their onshore management. It does not name the Master, Officers or Crew — whose negligence is an insured peril in the very same clause.

Read those two facts together and the commercial logic is clear. The policy accepts the risk of human error by the people operating the ship, because that is what the owner is insuring against. It does not accept the risk of the owner's own organisation failing to run the ship properly, because that would be insuring the owner against himself.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the UK Marine Insurance Act 1906. The Indian statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. This is a standing correction in this corpus and it is checked.
  • Treating perils of the sea as meaning 'anything that happens at sea'. It is a narrow term with two tests.
  • Forgetting that the loss must be fortuitous, and so listing wear and tear, corrosion or inherent vice among the covered perils.
  • Answering the second demand with only 'take out another policy' and missing the P&I club, which is how third-party liabilities are actually covered.
  • Getting the burden backwards and saying the assured must prove he exercised due diligence. He must prove the peril; the insurer must prove want of diligence.
  • Putting the Master and crew into the due-diligence list. Their negligence is the insured peril.
  • Saying latent defect cover pays for renewing the defective part. It pays for the resulting damage; the part itself is bought back by additional perils cover.
  • Quoting an Institute clause number. Several editions run concurrently and their numbering differs; enumerate the perils by substance.

Understand this first

The HNS Convention is the chemical equivalent of the CLC and Fund regime: strict liability on the owner up to a limit, compulsory insurance evidenced by a certificate, and a second tier funded by the receivers of the cargo. Two features make it different and both are examinable. First, it does not list chemicals — article 1.5 defines its scope by pointing at seven other IMO instruments, so the list of covered substances updates itself whenever those instruments are amended. Second, its damage is wider than pollution: the first head is death and personal injury, because a chemical cargo kills by fire, explosion and toxicity as well as by contamination, and no oil convention reaches that. The third limb is about boundaries. CLC is defined by the oil being persistent; Bunkers by the oil being used for the ship's operation or propulsion; HNS by the cargo's category. Both Bunkers and HNS then carve out anything the CLC definition already claims, using the same phrase — 'whether or not compensation is payable' — so the three tile rather than overlap. A products tanker carries gasoline this voyage, fuel oil the next and bunkers throughout, so she needs all three certificates because which regime applies is decided by the cargo of the day, and certificates cannot be obtained at the loading berth.

Model written answer

1. The Convention, and its status at this sitting

The instrument is the International Convention on Liability and Compensation for Damage in Connection with the Carriage of Hazardous and Noxious Substances by Sea, 1996, as amended by the Protocol of 2010. Article 18 of the Protocol directs that the two instruments be read and interpreted together as one single instrument, and the resulting text is what is meant by 'the 2010 HNS Convention'.

Deal with the stem's premise first, because it is a statement about time and this is March 2025. The question asserts that the Convention 'is expected shortly to come into force'. At this sitting the Convention is not in force, and its entry-into-force conditions have not been met. The premise is preserved as the position the paper takes and the answer neither adopts it as a prediction nor contradicts the examiner: the correct statement is that the Convention has been adopted and is not yet in force.

That has a consequence worth stating in one line, because it is what makes the rest of the answer safe: the Convention's substantive provisions were fixed by the 2010 Protocol. Definitions, heads of damage, exclusions, limits and the certificate do not change while the instrument waits, so everything below can be stated with confidence even though nothing below is yet enforceable.

The scheme in one sentence: strict liability on the shipowner up to a limit, backed by compulsory insurance and a certificate, with a second tier funded by receivers of HNS cargo — the same two-tier architecture as the CLC and Fund regime, transplanted from oil to chemicals.

2. (a) The substances covered - the seven categories of article 1.5

The Convention does not list commodities. Article 1.5(a) defines HNS as any substances, materials and articles carried on board a ship as cargo falling within seven categories, and each category is defined by reference to another IMO instrument. The effect is that the HNS list updates automatically as those instruments are amended — which is the single most intelligent point available in this limb.

  1. (i) Oils carried in bulk, as defined in regulation 1 of MARPOL Annex I. Examples: crude oil, fuel oil, heavy diesel oil, lubricating oil.
  2. (ii) Noxious liquid substances carried in bulk, as defined in regulation 1.10 of MARPOL Annex II, together with substances provisionally categorised in pollution category X, Y or Z under regulation 6.3. Examples: vegetable oils, phosphoric acid.
  3. (iii) Dangerous liquid substances carried in bulk listed in chapter 17 of the IBC Code, and products for which preliminary carriage conditions have been prescribed under paragraph 1.1.6. Examples: benzene, styrene monomer, methanol, sulphuric acid.
  4. (iv) Dangerous, hazardous and harmful substances, materials and articles in packaged form covered by the IMDG Code. Examples: drummed and containerised chemicals, corrosives, explosives, flammable solids.
  5. (v) Liquefied gases listed in chapter 19 of the IGC Code, and products prescribed under paragraph 1.1.6. Examples: LNG, LPG, ammonia, ethylene, vinyl chloride monomer.
  6. (vi) Liquid substances carried in bulk with a flashpoint not exceeding 60°C, measured by closed-cup test. Examples: gasoline, naphtha and similar light products.
  7. (vii) Solid bulk materials possessing chemical hazards covered by the IMSBC Code, to the extent that they are also subject to the IMDG Code in effect in 1996 when carried in packaged form. Examples: ammonium nitrate and ammonium nitrate based fertilizers, seed cake.

Article 1.5(b) extends the definition to residues from the previous carriage in bulk of categories (a)(i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii). A tank that has been discharged but not cleaned therefore still carries HNS for the purposes of the Convention — a point of real operational consequence.

3. (a) Bulk HNS and packaged HNS - why the split matters

Article 1.5bis defines bulk HNS as categories (i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii) together with residues. Article 1.5ter defines packaged HNS as category (iv) alone.

The distinction is not academic. It fixes the owner's limit of liability under article 9 — a materially higher limit for packaged cargo — and in the second tier packaged cargo does not contribute to the Fund in the way bulk cargo does. It is also the reason a mixed or indeterminate cargo is treated under the packaged figures.

4. (b) The heads of damage - article 1.6

The stem asks for 'types of pollution and subsequent losses or damages'. The Convention answers it directly: 'damage' under article 1.6 has four heads, and they are wider than pollution.

  1. Loss of life or personal injury, on board or outside the ship, caused by the substances. Note that this head has no pollution element at all — a fire, an explosion or a toxic release injuring people is squarely within it, and this is the largest single difference between HNS and the oil regime.
  2. Loss of or damage to property outside the ship.
  3. Loss or damage by contamination of the environment, subject to an express restriction: compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit from such impairment, is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken. So a fisherman's lost earnings are recoverable as economic loss, while the ecological damage itself is compensable only through the cost of reasonable restoration actually done or to be done.
  4. The costs of preventive measures, and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Article 1.6 also provides for the case where it is not reasonably possible to separate HNS damage from damage caused by other factors.

The four risks a candidate should name, because the stem says 'types of pollution': fire and explosion risk; toxicity to persons; contamination of sea, shore and air; and reactivity — and the Convention's own architecture reflects that the first two produce claims that no oil-pollution convention would reach.

5. (b) What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it

Article 4 does the boundary work, and the mechanism is worth understanding because limb (c) depends on it.

  • 4.1 — the Convention applies to claims other than those arising out of any contract for the carriage of goods and passengers. Cargo and passenger claims travel under their own regimes.
  • 4.2 — it is disapplied where incompatible with workers' compensation or social security schemes.
  • 4.3(a) — CLC pollution damage is excluded: 'pollution damage as defined in the International Convention on Civil Liability for Oil Pollution Damage, 1969, as amended, whether or not compensation is payable in respect of it under that Convention'. The italicised words are the mechanism: the exclusion bites on the definition, not on whether money is actually recoverable. There is no gap and no overlap by design.
  • 4.3(b) — damage caused by radioactive material of class 7 in the IMDG Code or the IMSBC Code is excluded.
  • 4.4 — warships and State ships on non-commercial service are excluded.

So the conventions are drafted to tile rather than to overlap: CLC takes persistent oil pollution damage, HNS takes the rest of the chemical risk including fire, explosion and toxic injury, and the Bunkers Convention takes bunker pollution that CLC does not already claim.

6. (c) Why an oil products tanker needs certificates under all three

The answer is that the three conventions are defined by different things — one by the nature of the oil, one by the use of the oil, one by the category of the cargo — and a products tanker can satisfy all three definitions on the same voyage or on successive voyages.

CLC 1992. 'Oil' means any persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil, whether carried on board a ship as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship (article I.5). A products tanker carrying a persistent product — fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil — is a CLC ship for that voyage, and article VII.1 requires a certificate where she carries more than 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo. Note also article I.1: a combination carrier is a 'ship' only when actually carrying oil in bulk as cargo, and on a following voyage unless it is proved she retains no residues. Whether CLC applies therefore turns on two facts — is she a CLC ship, and is the oil persistent — and it must never be stated absolutely.

Bunkers Convention 2001. Bunker oil is any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues (article 1.5). A certificate is required of the registered owner of a ship of gross tonnage greater than 1000 (article 7.1). But article 4.1 disapplies the Convention to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable under it — so on a voyage when she is a CLC ship, a bunker spill is CLC damage, and the Bunkers certificate covers her on the voyages when she is not.

HNS. A products tanker frequently carries cargoes inside the seven categories that are not persistent oil: gasoline and naphtha fall in category (vi) as liquids in bulk with a flashpoint not exceeding 60°C; chemical and vegetable-oil parcels fall in (ii) or (iii). She then needs an HNS certificate under article 12, which applies to a ship registered in a State Party actually carrying HNS.

The reason all three are needed at once is commercial, and it is the point of the limb. A products tanker's trade is not one cargo. She loads gasoline this voyage, fuel oil the next and a chemical parcel the one after, and she carries bunkers throughout. Certificates are issued in advance and are checked in port; she cannot obtain one at the loading berth for the cargo she has just been fixed on. So she carries all three because which regime applies is decided by the cargo of the day, and residues under article 1.5(b) and CLC article I.1 mean the previous cargo can still reach her on the next voyage.

7. (c) The certificate itself, and the two tiers behind it

Under article 12 the owner of a ship registered in a State Party and actually carrying HNS must maintain insurance or other financial security in the sums fixed by article 9.1, evidenced by a certificate in the Annex I model form, carried on board, mutually recognised between States Parties, and supporting direct action against the insurer under article 12.8. That structure is deliberately identical to the CLC and Bunkers certificates, which is why one officer can be expected to understand all three.

The limits that the certificate must cover, under article 9.1, and note that they differ by the bulk and packaged split from section 3:

  • Bulk HNS — 10 million units of account for a ship not exceeding 2,000 units of tonnage, rising by 1,500 units per ton and then by 360 units per ton, capped at 100 million units of account.
  • Packaged HNS, or a mixed or indeterminate cargo — 11.5 million units of account, rising by 1,725 and then 414, capped at 115 million units of account.

The second tier. Where the owner's limit is insufficient, the HNS Fund, financed by contributions from receivers of HNS cargo in States Parties, pays the balance. Article 14.5(a) caps the whole scheme: the total of Fund compensation and any amount paid under chapter II shall not exceed 250 million units of account.

Liability and time. Article 7.1 imposes strict liability on the owner, with defences in 7.2(a) to (d) including the shipper's failure to furnish information on the hazardous nature of the substances, and article 7.5 channels claims away from crew, pilot, charterer, manager, operator and salvor. Article 37 extinguishes rights three years from the date the claimant knew or ought reasonably to have known of the damage, and in no case later than ten years from the incident.

The standing correction on CLC

Never assert that CLC has no application to a bunker spill. CLC article I.5 covers persistent oil carried as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship, and the Bunkers Convention excludes itself where the damage is CLC pollution damage. So a bunker spill from a CLC ship is CLC damage. Whether CLC applies turns on two facts and must be stated conditionally: is she a CLC ship, and is the oil persistent?

Common mistakes

  • Trying to list chemicals in limb (a) instead of naming the seven referenced instruments.
  • Saying the Convention is in force, or predicting when it will be. It is adopted and not in force, and no date should be offered.
  • Quoting a number of Contracting States. No held source fixes it and none of the three limbs needs it.
  • Treating HNS as an oil-pollution convention. Its first head of damage is death and personal injury, with no pollution element at all.
  • Forgetting article 1.5(b) residues — a discharged but uncleaned tank still carries HNS.
  • Missing the bulk / packaged split, which changes the owner's limit and the Fund contribution.
  • Stating that environmental damage is fully compensable. Compensation for impairment of the environment, other than loss of profit, is limited to the cost of reasonable reinstatement measures actually undertaken or to be undertaken.
  • Saying CLC does not apply to bunkers. It does, where she is a CLC ship and the oil is persistent.
  • Converting units of account into dollars. They are Special Drawing Rights and the rate moves daily.

Understand this first

Model written answer

1. Where the duty comes from, and the numbering that matters

The EEDI lives in chapter 4 of MARPOL Annex VI, as revised by resolution MEPC.328(76), which entered into force on 1 November 2022. Use the post-2021 numbering; the older scheme still circulates in notes and is wrong.

  • Regulation 19 — application: chapter 4 applies to ships of 400 gross tonnage and above, and regulation 19.3 disapplies regulations 22 to 25 to ships with non-conventional propulsion.
  • Regulation 22 — the attained EEDI: calculated for each new ship, ship-specific, recorded in an EEDI technical file, and verified by the Administration or an authorized organization. Regulation 22.2 refers the calculation method out to guidelines adopted by the Organization.
  • Regulation 24 — the required EEDI, and this is where the phased scheme lives.
  • Regulation 5.4 — the initial survey verifying the attained EEDI; regulation 6 — the International Energy Efficiency Certificate.

Regulation 24.1 states the duty in one line: for each new ship, the attained EEDI shall be less than or equal to the required EEDI, where

required EEDI = (1 − X/100) × reference line value

X is the reduction factor taken from Table 1 for the ship's type, size band and phase, and the reference line value comes from Table 2's parameters against the ship's deadweight or gross tonnage.

2. What 'Phase 2' means at this sitting - and the answer is not what the stem assumes

The stem asks about the present EEDI framework under Phase 2. That premise needs handling precisely, and doing so is worth marks rather than costing them.

Table 1 prints two different phase timetables, and the split is by ship type.

Ship typesPhase 2Phase 3 opens
Gas carriers of 15,000 DWT and above; all containerships; general cargo ships; LNG carriers; cruise passenger ships1 Jan 2020 - 31 Mar 20221 April 2022

It follows that at this sitting Phase 2 has closed on both timetables — on the first nearly three years ago, and on the second only ten weeks before this paper. The present phase for a ship contracted today is Phase 3, whichever timetable she is on.

Three further points of precision, and each is a mark.

  1. Phase 2 is nonetheless still operative law, and the stem is not wrong to ask about it. A ship's required EEDI is fixed by the phase she was contracted or built into, so an expired phase continues to govern a large part of the existing fleet for the rest of its life. Phase 0 and Phase 1 are the same.
  2. The phase a ship falls in is fixed by her construction datum, and regulation 24 does not print the datum rule. 'New ship' is regulation 2.2.18 — contract on or after 1 January 2013, or keel laid on or after 1 July 2013, or delivery on or after 1 July 2015. For a converted ship, regulation 5.4 requires the phase to be determined for the original ship under 2.2.18, while regulation 24.2 supplies only the conversion-date datum for ship type and size. Three datum rules in three regulations.
  3. The stem's bracket '20% – 30%' spans two phases. The reduction factors recurring across Table 1 are 0, 10, 20 and 30; 20 per cent is characteristically the Phase 2 figure for the larger bands and 30 per cent the Phase 3 figure. The bracket therefore describes the stringency band the question is aiming at, and the design features that meet it are the same either way — which is why the rest of this answer is unaffected.

Note also that none of Table 1's dates is an amendment or entry-into-force date. They are phase boundaries printed in the enacted text, and regulation 24 is itself unamended.

3. The equation - because every design feature attacks one side of it

The attained EEDI is, in structure, grammes of CO2 emitted per tonne-mile of transport work:

attained EEDI ≈ (engine power × specific fuel consumption × carbon factor) ÷ (capacity × reference speed)

with correction factors for ice class, technical and regulatory power limits, cubic capacity, weather, voluntary structural enhancement and innovative energy efficiency technologies. The full method is referred out by regulation 22.2 to Organization guidelines, so a candidate should give the structure and not attempt the coefficients.

Every design measure therefore does one of four things, and organising the answer by mechanism rather than by a list of gadgets is what earns a first-class mark:

  1. Reduce the power needed for a given speed — sections 4 and 6.
  2. Reduce the carbon factor of the fuel — section 5. The largest single lever.
  3. Reduce fuel consumed per unit of power, or recover energy already paid for — section 6.
  4. Raise the denominator — more capacity, or a reference speed the design can actually sustain — section 7.

4. Design features that reduce required power - hull and propulsion

This is where a new build has freedom that no retrofit has, and it should be written first.

  1. Hull form optimisation for the real operating profile. Computational fluid dynamics and model testing against the speed and draught the ship will actually trade at, rather than a single contract condition. Slender entrance, optimised aft body and buttock flow, reduced wetted surface.
  2. Bulbous bow designed for the operating envelope, not for one condition. Over-designed bulbs at part load are a known cause of poor real-world performance.
  3. Reduced installed power and a lower design speed. Because the numerator carries engine power directly and resistance rises far faster than speed, a modest reduction in design speed is the most powerful single design decision available. New builds have been designed slower for exactly this reason.
  4. A larger, slower-turning propeller with a bigger diameter and lower revolutions, matched to an aperture designed for it. Propulsive efficiency rises with diameter; the constraint is draught and clearance, and on a new build both are design variables.
  5. High-efficiency propeller design — optimised blade area, pitch distribution, skew and section, with the wake field from the hull optimisation used as the design input rather than assumed.
  6. Optimised hull and propeller interaction: shaft line arrangement, stern tube alignment, twin-skeg or single-skeg selection, and rudder profile chosen with the propeller rather than after it.
  7. Low-friction and foul-release coatings specified from new, so that the ship enters service on her design resistance.
  8. Engine selection and tuning: derated, long-stroke, low-revolution main engines matched to the larger propeller, with electronic control giving low specific fuel consumption across the load range rather than at one point.
  9. Reduced auxiliary demand: variable-frequency drives on pumps and fans, correctly sized rather than over-sized auxiliaries, efficient HVAC and lighting, and demand-based control. Auxiliary power appears in the numerator too and is routinely neglected in answers.

5. Design features that reduce the carbon factor - the fuel

The carbon conversion factor sits in the numerator as a straight multiplier, so a fuel with less carbon per unit of energy reduces the attained EEDI directly and by more than any hull measure. On a new build the choice is genuinely open.

  • LNG dual-fuel, using low-pressure or high-pressure gas injection. The largest single EEDI reduction available from an otherwise conventional design, and the reason so many Phase 2 and Phase 3 compliant designs are gas-fuelled.
  • LPG, particularly on gas carriers where the cargo is the fuel.
  • Methanol dual-fuel, with the advantage of a liquid fuel at ambient conditions and much simpler containment.
  • Ammonia and hydrogen capability, or ammonia-ready and methanol-ready designs where the space, tank foundations and safety arrangements are built in for later conversion.
  • Biofuel capability in the fuel system and materials specification.

Two qualifications should be stated, because an unqualified fuel answer is not a Class I answer. First, gas fuelling introduces methane slip, which is a greenhouse gas the EEDI's carbon factor does not fully capture, so the index gain overstates the climate gain. Second, the index is a tank-to-wake, design-condition measure and says nothing about how the fuel was produced.

6. Design features that recover or displace energy

  1. Waste heat recovery. Exhaust gas economiser with a steam turbine, an exhaust power turbine, or a combined turbo-generator feeding the ship's electrical load and, through a shaft motor, the propulsion. The most substantial single item of machinery-side design available.
  2. Shaft generator, taking the electrical load from the more efficient main engine and removing auxiliary engine running at sea; and a shaft motor for power take-in.
  3. Energy saving devices around the propeller, which recover rotational and hub losses: pre-swirl ducts and stators, wake-equalising ducts, propeller boss cap fins, rudder bulbs and twisted rudders. On a new build these are designed with the hull and propeller rather than bolted on, which is why they perform better on a newbuilding than as a retrofit.
  4. Air lubrication — a bubble or air-cavity layer reducing frictional resistance, with the compressor power correctly debited against the gain.
  5. Wind assistance — rotor sails, rigid wing sails, suction wings or kites, integrated with deck arrangement and visibility lines from the design stage.
  6. Solar contribution to hotel load, which is small but admissible.
  7. Battery hybridisation for peak shaving and for load levelling of the auxiliary plant.

Several of these enter the calculation through the innovative energy efficiency technology terms of the formula, which is how the regulation credits a technology that does not simply reduce shaft power.

7. Raising the denominator - and the floor that stops power being cut

The denominator is capacity multiplied by reference speed, so a design that carries more per unit of installed power scores better.

  • Increased deadweight or cubic capacity for the same principal dimensions — lighter structure through high-tensile steel and optimised scantlings, improved hull volume utilisation, and reduced lightship.
  • Beamier, shallower forms at lower speed, which raise capacity and reduce required power together.
  • Design draught and trim optimisation, so that the reference condition and the trading condition are the same ship.

And the limit. Regulation 24.5 carries a minimum propulsion power requirement. It exists because the cheapest way to pass the index is to install less power, and a ship with too little power cannot keep steerage and manoeuvre in adverse conditions — which is a safety matter, and a real one after several underpowered designs. The numerical criteria live in Organization guidelines. Regulation 24.6 carries the review trigger for the phase scheme itself.

This is the paragraph that distinguishes an engineer's answer: the EEDI is a design index, and it can be satisfied by making the ship worse. Regulation 24.5 is the regulation's own acknowledgement of that, and it is the reason the design features above are the legitimate route and power starvation is not.

8. How the result is verified and certified

Design features only count if they survive verification, and the stem's word 'comply' reaches this.

  1. The shipbuilder calculates the attained EEDI and compiles the EEDI technical file — principal particulars, capacity, rated power, specific fuel consumption from the NOx Technical File, and every correction factor claimed.
  2. Preliminary verification at the design stage, against the technical file and the approved drawings.
  3. Final verification at the sea trial, where the reference speed is established from the measured speed-power relationship corrected to the reference condition. This is the only quantity in the whole index that cannot be known on paper, which is why verification is in two stages.
  4. The Administration or an authorized organization verifies the attained EEDI — it does not calculate it and does not approve it.
  5. The result is recorded and the International Energy Efficiency Certificate issued under regulation 6, following the initial survey under regulation 5.4.
  6. Claims for innovative technologies must be substantiated in the technical file; a device fitted but not evidenced earns nothing in the index.

Common mistakes

  • Using the pre-2021 numbering. Regulation 21 is functional requirements, not the required EEDI. The revised Annex renumbered chapter 4 when it entered into force on 1 November 2022.
  • Writing a list of devices with no reference to the equation, so that the answer cannot explain why any of them helps.
  • Omitting the fuel, which is the single largest lever available to a new build.
  • Claiming LNG is a decarbonisation answer without mentioning methane slip.
  • Quoting a reduction factor for a named ship type from memory. Table 1 has 123 such cells across 27 size bands and four phases, and the wrong cell is worse than no cell.
  • Quoting percentage gains for energy-saving devices. They are ship-specific and mostly come from maker's material.
  • Ignoring auxiliary power, which is in the numerator.
  • Missing regulation 24.5, the minimum propulsion power floor, which is the regulation's own answer to the obvious loophole.
  • Treating EEDI as measured performance. It is a design index calculated at a reference condition; only the reference speed comes from trials.
  • Importing the IMO Net-Zero Framework, which was approved in April 2025 — the month after this sitting.

Understand this first

All three notes are about the same problem: the two surfaces that decide a ship's fuel bill are the two you cannot see. At merchant-ship speeds frictional resistance dominates, and friction is what surface condition acts on. The ship tells you when it is happening — more torque for the same revolutions, the engine running heavy — but only if the data is normalised for draught, trim, weather and current. That is the thread: measure, decide, intervene, verify. And it is the cheapest energy-efficiency measure a ship has, because it needs no capital, no approval and no dock, and it can be done again next month.

Model written answer

1. The physical basis common to all three limbs

All three short notes are about one thing: keeping the ship's underwater surfaces hydrodynamically smooth. Establishing why that matters, in three sentences, makes every later point scorable.

  • A merchant ship's resistance divides into frictional resistance and residuary resistance. At normal merchant-ship speeds friction dominates, and friction is the component that surface condition acts on.
  • Roughness works by increasing the momentum deficit in the turbulent boundary layer, thickening it and raising skin friction. The penalty depends not only on the height of the roughness but on its texture and spacing, which is why biological fouling is far more damaging than physical roughness of the same height — slime and weed disturb the flow out of all proportion to their thickness.
  • The consequence is measured in the engine room: for the same speed the ship needs more shaft power, so more fuel and more CO2; or at constant power she loses speed. On the propeller the same roughness raises absorbed torque, so the engine runs heavy and reaches its limit earlier.

(a) Hull Roughness Management — 6 marks

2. What is being managed, and the cycle that manages it

Two kinds of roughness, and they behave completely differently.

Hull roughness management is the deliberate cycle by which both are held down. Four steps, and writing them as a cycle rather than a list is what earns the six marks.

  1. Measure. Average hull roughness is taken at dry-dock with a hull roughness gauge at a defined pattern of stations, as a mean apparent amplitude over a defined sampling length, and tracked dock to dock so the trend is visible. In service the equivalent measurement is performance monitoring — shaft power, revolutions, speed through water and fuel consumption, normalised for draught, trim, wind, sea state, current and water temperature, with the residual trend watched rather than the raw figures. Unnormalised data is the commonest reason a hull-performance programme tells the owner nothing.
  2. Specify and apply the coating properly. Surface preparation to the specified standard, correct film thickness, correct conditions and correct overcoating intervals. Application quality is a roughness decision: an over-applied or badly applied coating leaves the ship rougher than it found her, and successive layers over a ship's life ratchet roughness upward, which is why full blasting back to steel is periodically the right answer however expensive it looks.
  3. Choose the system for the ship's actual trade. Self-polishing copolymer systems hydrolyse at a controlled rate to expose fresh biocide and maintain smoothness. Foul-release silicone or fluoropolymer systems carry no biocide and work by presenting a low-adhesion surface from which fouling is shed by the ship's own motion — excellent for a fast, continuously trading ship and poor for one that idles, because they are activity-dependent. Trading pattern, speed, idle time and water temperature decide the choice, not the brochure.
  4. Inspect, intervene and verify. Diver or remotely operated inspection of the hull and, critically, of niche areas — sea chests and gratings, bow and stern thruster tunnels, rudder and stabiliser recesses, bilge keels, propeller boss and the rope guard — which foul first and are routinely omitted. Then in-water cleaning where justified, and proactive grooming of light slime, which is gentler on the coating and cheaper than reactive cleaning of established hard fouling. Verify the intervention against the performance trend, because a clean that produces no measurable recovery has told you the problem was elsewhere.

Two practical constraints must be stated. Aggressive mechanical cleaning damages the coating and can leave the hull rougher and foul faster afterwards, so method must be matched to coating type — soft brushes or water jets for foul-release systems. And in-water cleaning is increasingly regulated by port and coastal States, for invasive-species and biocide-release reasons: permission is required and is not automatic, and capture and filtration of the debris is often a condition.

3. Why it has become a duty rather than a preference

The stem's own words — 'the recent high stress on the energy efficiency of ships' — invite this, and it is a mark.

  • The SEEMP. Under the revised MARPOL Annex VI, resolution MEPC.328(76) (in force 1 November 2022), every ship keeps a ship-specific ship energy efficiency management plan. Hull and propeller measures are planned, implemented, monitored and evaluated within it, so the work is documented and auditable rather than discretionary.
  • Operational carbon intensity. In-service hull and propeller condition directly affects the ship's attained annual operational carbon intensity and therefore her rating, which is commercial and visible to charterers. This is what turned hull husbandry from an engineering preference into a board-level matter.
  • The Biofouling Guidelines. The IMO's guidance on biofouling recommends a ship-specific biofouling management plan and record book, covering the antifouling system, niche areas and in-water inspection and cleaning. It is guidance and not law, and saying so is part of the answer — but it is what port States and charterers increasingly expect to see.
  • The AFS Convention sets the boundary on chemistry: organotin compounds are prohibited as biocides in antifouling systems, and the control on cybutryne took effect on 1 January 2023 and is operative. A coating choice is therefore constrained by law as well as by trade.

(b) Condition Based Propeller Maintenance — 5 marks

4. Monitoring the condition, and letting it set the interval

Condition-based maintenance means the propeller is worked on when its measured condition says so, not when the calendar says so. That single sentence is the definition, and everything else is how the condition is measured.

What is monitored, in three groups:

  1. Performance indicators from the ship herself. The propeller declares its own condition through the machinery. Watch the power–speed–revolution relationship: a fouled or roughened blade absorbs more torque at the same revolutions, so the engine runs heavy — higher torque and load for given revolutions, exhaust temperatures up, turbocharger speed up, and the load limit reached earlier. Track slip, and shaft power against revolutions from a torsion meter where fitted. All of it must be normalised for draught, trim, weather and current before it means anything.
  2. Direct inspection. Diver or ROV inspection of the blades, boss and rope guard, photographed and graded, and inspection at dry-dock. What is being looked for is fouling and calcareous deposit, cavitation erosion — characteristically on the suction face near the leading edge and the tip — impingement and corrosion pitting, edge damage from debris or ice, and the roughness left by an earlier poor polish or grind.
  3. Machinery condition indicators. Shaft and stern tube bearing wear-down, stern tube oil condition and consumption, seal performance, and shaft vibration signature — because a damaged or unevenly fouled blade is unbalanced and says so through blade-rate vibration. Increasing bearing temperature or an oil sample loaded with bearing metal is propeller-shaft news as much as bearing news.

What the monitoring produces: a trigger rather than a date. When the normalised performance trend degrades past the agreed threshold, or an inspection grades the blade surface below the agreed standard, the intervention — polish, repair, or dock — is scheduled. The economic argument is straightforward: intervention is cheap, the fuel penalty of leaving it is continuous, and the whole point is to spend the money at the moment it repays fastest.

Say what condition-based maintenance is being compared against, because the contrast is the mark: it replaces calendar-based polishing at every dock or every fixed number of months, which either wastes money on a clean propeller or leaves a fouled one running for months.

And state the limits honestly. Condition monitoring needs reliable, normalised data and a baseline — without both, the trend is noise; it needs an agreed threshold, or the trigger is a matter of opinion; and access for inspection or in-water polishing depends on port permission, water clarity, current and diver safety, so a trigger cannot always be acted on when it fires.

(c) Propeller Coating & Polishing — 5 marks

5. Propeller coating - purpose and real limits

Purpose. A propeller coating is applied to the blades and boss to resist fouling and corrosion and to hold a smooth surface for longer, so that the interval between polishes lengthens and the average surface condition across the docking cycle is better.

  • The systems used are typically biocide-free foul-release types — silicone or fluoropolymer — chosen because they present a low-surface-energy, low-adhesion surface from which fouling releases under the very high water velocities at the blade. Hard, smooth non-stick coatings are also used.
  • Application demands more than the hull: the blades must be fully cleaned and prepared, usually blasted and profiled, and coated in controlled conditions at correct film thickness. An improperly applied coating is worse than none, because it will detach unevenly and leave a surface rougher than the bare bronze.
  • Benefits: slower fouling, lower average roughness across the cycle, longer polish intervals, easier cleaning when it is done, and some protection against corrosion and calcareous deposition.

The limits, which must be stated for the answer to be credible. The blade is the most hydrodynamically hostile surface on the ship: high tip velocities, cyclic loading, cavitation and impingement. Coatings therefore wear preferentially at the leading edge and the tip, exactly where surface condition matters most, and cavitation erosion will remove them locally regardless of quality. A coating is a life-extender between polishes, not a substitute for polishing, it requires periodic reapplication in dock, and its cost must be justified against the ship's actual trade.

6. Propeller polishing - method, grades and why it pays

What it is. The controlled removal of fouling, calcareous deposit and surface roughness from the blades, restoring a specified surface finish. It is done in water by divers with rotating discs and progressively finer abrasive pads, or in dry-dock to a higher and more consistent standard.

Method, in the order it is done:

  1. Remove fouling and calcareous deposit from the whole blade, both faces, plus the boss and the rope guard — areas routinely skipped in a hurried in-water job.
  2. Work through progressively finer grades to the specified finish, never leaving the surface with the coarse directional scratching of a single-grade grind, which can be worse than the fouling it replaced.
  3. Give particular attention to the leading edge and the outer radii. The leading edge governs the section's inflow behaviour and the outer radii do most of the work, so the finish there is specified finer than at mid-chord and matters most.
  4. Do not reshape the blade. Polishing removes surface material only. Dressing edge damage, correcting pitch or repairing cavitation erosion is propeller repair, requires an approved procedure and class involvement where classed items are affected, and is a different job with different consequences.
  5. Record it — grade achieved, photographs, areas treated — and verify against the performance trend, which closes the condition-based loop from limb (b).

Why it pays out of proportion to its cost. Three reasons, and giving them turns a description into an argument.

  • The propeller is a small area doing a large job. The blades are a tiny fraction of the wetted surface but they operate at very high relative velocity and they set the efficiency of the entire propulsion chain, so roughness there costs more per square metre than anywhere else on the ship.
  • A polish is cheap, quick and can be done in water without taking the ship out of service, while the fuel penalty it removes is continuous, every hour, until it is done.
  • It unloads the engine. Restoring the blade surface lightens the running condition, moves the operating point back toward the design point and recovers margin the ship had lost — which matters for both fuel and for the ship's operational carbon intensity.

The closing judgement, which ties the three limbs together: hull and propeller maintenance is the cheapest energy-efficiency measure available to a ship in service. It requires no capital modification, no approval and no dry-docking, and unlike a design change it can be done again next month. What it requires instead is measurement — and that is why all three limbs of this question are really about knowing the condition of a surface you cannot see.

Matching the coating to the trade

  • Self-polishing copolymer — hydrolyses at a controlled rate, exposing fresh biocide and maintaining smoothness. Tolerant of idle time.
  • Controlled depletion — the older, simpler biocidal route.

Common mistakes

  • Writing three unconnected notes with no common physical basis.
  • Treating hull roughness as fouling only, and missing physical roughness and its ratchet effect.
  • Quoting a percentage fuel penalty or saving from memory. Those figures are hull-, coating- and trade-specific and mostly come from maker's material.
  • Recommending aggressive mechanical cleaning without saying that it damages the coating and can leave the hull fouling faster.
  • Ignoring niche areas — sea chests, thruster tunnels, rudder and bilge-keel recesses, rope guard — which foul first.
  • Describing performance monitoring with no normalisation for draught, trim, weather and current. Unnormalised data is noise.
  • Answering limb (b) as 'inspect the propeller regularly', which is calendar-based maintenance and the opposite of what was asked.

  • Presenting a propeller coating as a substitute for polishing. It lengthens the interval; it does not replace it.
  • Assuming in-water cleaning is always permitted. Port and coastal States increasingly regulate it.

Model written answer

1. What has actually happened - four events in one casualty

Begin by separating the incident into its legal components, because each triggers different machinery and different people, and the stem asks who as well as what.

  1. A collision — engaging the duties of both masters and the law of collision.
  2. Loss of life — which, as section 6 shows, converts the casualty into a very serious marine casualty and makes a safety investigation mandatory.
  3. Damage to the vessels — engaging seaworthiness, survey, class, detention and possibly salvage.
  4. An oil spill — engaging the pollution response machinery, the Central Government's directions power and the liability conventions.

The statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. It governs at this sitting and throughout the response to this casualty.

And note the constant that runs through the whole answer: the Act is enabling as much as it is prohibitory. Most of what follows is the Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping being given power to direct, and the owner, master and salvor being obliged to comply.

2. Immediate steps on board - the Master, and the order matters

The first steps are the Master's, and they are taken before any authority is involved. The order is life, then ship, then environment, then evidence.

  1. Muster, account for all persons, and search for the missing. With loss of life reported, this is the first and overriding duty.
  2. Render assistance to the other vessel and to persons in danger, so far as he can do so without serious danger to his own ship and those on board, and stand by. Exchange the identifying particulars of ship, port of registry and ports of departure and destination. This duty is in the Act and is not discretionary.
  3. Sound tanks and compartments, assess damage, contain flooding, and assess the ship's stability and residual strength before any decision to move.
  4. Stop the source of the spill — isolate the damaged tank, transfer internally where it is safe to do so, close valves and pump out to intact spaces. Deploy the ship's SOPEP equipment and act on the plan.
  5. Broadcast and report — distress or urgency as appropriate, and a danger message where the casualty is a hazard to navigation.
  6. Preserve evidence. Secure the voyage data recorder, charts, logs, engine records, alarm and printout records, and record the position, time and circumstances. This is the step candidates omit and it decides both the investigation and the liability that follows.

4. Steps for the safety of the ships

The stem asks expressly about the safety of the ships, and the machinery here is largely about the State's power to compel.

  1. Damage assessment and survey. The classification society and the Administration survey the damage. Repairs, temporary or permanent, are approved before the ship proceeds, and conditions of class or a short-term certificate may be imposed.
  2. Detention. The Act's unseaworthy and unsafe ships provisions — sections 334 to 336 — empower the Administration to detain a ship that is unsafe by reason of defective condition of hull or machinery, undermanning, overloading or improper loading. A damaged ship after a collision is squarely within that power, and the initiator is the Director-General of Shipping or the surveyor acting for him.
  3. Salvage. If the ship cannot proceed under her own power, salvage services are engaged — commercially by the owner, ordinarily on Lloyd's Open Form terms with its no-cure-no-pay basis and its environmental provisions. The Act's wreck and salvage Part, sections 390 to 404, and the Merchant Shipping (Wrecks and Salvage) Rules, 1974, provide the statutory framework and the receiver of wreck's functions where wreck arises.
  4. A place of refuge. If she must be moved to sheltered water, the request is made to the coastal State. The current guidance is resolution A.1184(33), Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, adopted by the 33rd Assembly on 6 December 2023 and revoking A.949(23). The decision is the coastal State's, taken on an objective analysis of the risks of granting refuge against the risks of refusing it — and it is the Director-General of Shipping and the maritime administration who coordinate it on the Indian side.
  5. Directions to the master, owner or salvor. Under Part XA the Central Government and the Director-General may direct the ship to move, to take or refrain from specified action, or to be salved — and this is the power that turns a commercial decision into a compellable one.
  6. Navigational safety. Marking the casualty, a navigational warning, and where necessary an exclusion or safety zone around it.

5. Steps for the marine environment

This is the second half of the stem's own question and it has its own actors.

  1. The ship acts first — the SOPEP is implemented, the source is stopped, and containment is attempted with the ship's own means.
  2. The national response is mounted under the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan, for which the Indian Coast Guard is responsible at sea in India's maritime zones. The plan is tiered, escalating from a local response with local resources to a national and, where necessary, international mobilisation of equipment and expertise.
  3. Response operations — aerial and satellite surveillance to establish the extent and drift of the slick, containment with booms, mechanical recovery with skimmers, dispersant application where and only where it is authorised for those waters and that oil, protection of sensitive shorelines and intakes, shoreline clean-up, and waste reception and disposal.
  4. The statutory directions power is the legal engine of all of it. Under Part XA of the Act — the prevention and containment of pollution of the sea by oil, at sections 356J, 356K and 356L — the Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping may give directions to the owner, the master or the salvor for the prevention or containment of pollution, may take the action themselves where the directions are not complied with or where the case requires it, and may recover the cost from the owner. That is the answer to the stem's 'who should initiate': for the environment, the initiator is the Central Government acting through the Director-General of Shipping, with the Indian Coast Guard executing the response at sea.
  5. Coordination with the coastal State machinery — State pollution control boards, fisheries and environment departments, and port authorities where the shoreline or a port is affected.
  6. Funding. The response is funded through the owner's P&I cover and the compulsory insurance behind the CLC or Bunkers certificate, with the certificate supporting direct action against the insurer — which is why the certificates in Q6 on this paper matter operationally and not only on paper.

6. Investigation and inquiry - two systems, and both are engaged

This is where most candidates lose marks, because there are two parallel systems with different purposes and they must not be merged.

The national system, under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. The Act's Part on investigations and inquiries into shipping casualties provides the machinery, and it runs in stages:

  1. A report of the casualty to the Administration.
  2. A preliminary inquiry, conducted by a person appointed for the purpose, to establish the facts and to advise whether more is required.
  3. Where the circumstances warrant it, a formal investigation before a court sitting with assessors of nautical or engineering experience.
  4. The formal investigation may make findings of fault and may cancel or suspend the certificate of a master, mate or engineer whose conduct caused or contributed to the casualty. Its orientation is therefore partly disciplinary.

The international system, under the Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84), made mandatory by SOLAS regulation XI-1/6. Its purpose is expressly not to apportion blame or liability but to prevent recurrence by establishing causal factors.

And it is mandatory here. A marine safety investigation under the Code is required for a very serious marine casualty — one involving the total loss of the ship, a death, or severe damage to the environment. This casualty is stated to involve loss of lives, so the mandatory limb is engaged, and it would be engaged by the pollution limb only if the environmental damage were severe. The flag State — India, for this vessel — is the marine safety investigating State, and where the other vessel is foreign flagged her flag State and any State whose environment or interests are seriously harmed is a substantially interested State entitled to participate. The report is submitted to the IMO.

Hold the two apart in one sentence: the national inquiry can take a certificate away; the safety investigation exists so that the same accident does not happen twice, and its findings are not to be used to apportion blame.

7. Liability, compensation and the certificates behind them

The final group of steps is the one that pays for everything in sections 4 and 5, and it should be stated briefly rather than omitted.

  • Pollution liability. Where she is a CLC ship carrying persistent oil, CLC 1992 imposes strict liability on the owner of the ship for pollution damage, with limitation and compulsory insurance; where she is not, the 2001 Bunkers Convention covers pollution from bunker oil. Whether CLC applies turns on two facts — is she a CLC ship, and is the oil persistent — and it must not be stated absolutely.
  • Collision liability between the two ships, apportioned according to fault, and pursued in the appropriate forum — in India, the admiralty jurisdiction of the High Courts under the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017.
  • Loss of life claims, and the owner's obligations to the crew and their dependants.
  • Limitation of liability for maritime claims, invoked by the owner in the appropriate forum.
  • Wreck removal, if the casualty leaves a wreck, under the Act's wreck and salvage Part and its rules.

The one-sentence answer to the stem's 'who'. The Master initiates everything in the first hour; the owner and company initiate the commercial and technical response through the safety management system; the Director-General of Shipping, for the Central Government, initiates and compels the statutory response and holds the inquiry; the Indian Coast Guard executes the pollution response at sea; and the coastal State decides on a place of refuge. Every one of those is an answer to the question, and an answer naming only one of them is incomplete.

The Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping may give directions to the owner, master or salvor for the prevention or containment of pollution, may take the action themselves where the directions are not complied with, and may recover the cost from the owner. That is the legal engine of the whole environmental response and the direct answer to the stem's question of who initiates.
