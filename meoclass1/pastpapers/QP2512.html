Understand this first

There are two health systems on a ship and the question runs across both. One protects the world from the ship - vectors, infection and contamination crossing borders in a hull - and that is WHO's International Health Regulations, enforced by a port health authority and evidenced by a ship sanitation certificate. The other protects the crew, and that is labour and training law administered by the shipping administration. Keeping those two apart is what makes the India limb straightforward: the Port Health Officer certifies the ship, DG Shipping certifies the seafarer.

Model written answer

1. The governing instrument

Ship health and vector control are governed by the International Health Regulations (2005), a binding instrument of the World Health Organization, as amended in 2014, 2022 and 2024. The 2024 amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025. The IHR work through designated points of entry, competent authorities at those points, and a small set of ship documents.

2. The role of WHO

  • It makes the rules. WHO adopts the IHR and prescribes the model certificates in Annex 3 and the Ship Declaration of Health in Annex 8, so the documents are the same worldwide.
  • It identifies where vector control is needed. Under Annex 5, WHO publishes on a regular basis a list of areas where disinsection or other vector control measures are recommended for conveyances arriving from them, through temporary or standing recommendations.
  • It advises methods and materials. Where WHO has advised methods for disinsection, deratting, disinfection or decontamination, those should be used unless the competent authority is satisfied that others are as safe and reliable (Article 27), and States should accept measures applied elsewhere by WHO-advised methods.
  • It certifies and publishes. On a State Party's request WHO may, after appropriate investigation, certify that a port meets the IHR requirements (Article 20(4)), publishes certification guidelines and the list of certified ports (20(5)), and publishes the lists of authorized ports notified by States Parties (20(3)).
  • It provides technical guidance - notably the WHO Handbook for inspection of ships and issuance of ship sanitation certificates - and declares Public Health Emergencies of International Concern with temporary recommendations.

3. National agencies in India

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare - the health authority for IHR purposes, under which the National IHR Focal Point sits.
  • Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), and under it the Port Health Organisation, headed by the Port Health Officer - the competent authority at the point of entry. It inspects ships, baggage, cargo and containers, supervises control measures and issues the ship sanitation certificates, working under the Indian Port Health Rules, 1955 with the IHR 2005.
  • Directorate General of Shipping, through its Maritime Health Branch, on the seafarer side - approving medical examiners and governing seafarers' medical fitness examination and certification.
  • Port authorities and the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways support these functions at the ports themselves.

4. The ship's certificates

  • Ship Sanitation Control Exemption Certificate (SSCEC) - issued where the competent authority is satisfied that the ship is free of infection and contamination, including vectors and reservoirs, so that no control measures are required (Article 39(6)).
  • Ship Sanitation Control Certificate (SSCC) - issued where control measures were required and have been satisfactorily completed, and it records the evidence found and the measures taken (Article 39(5)). Under Annex 5 the presence of vectors on board and the measures used to eradicate them are recorded on this certificate. If a satisfactory result could not be obtained, the authority notes that on the certificate (Article 39(7)).

Both follow the model in Annex 3, and they replaced the former Deratting and Deratting Exemption Certificates. Alongside them the master submits a Ship Declaration of Health under Article 37, to the model in Annex 8 - a declaration, not a certificate. Separately, and concerning the crew rather than the ship, seafarers hold medical fitness certificates under MLC 2006 and STCW.

5. Who issues them in India, validity and extension

  • In India they are issued by the Port Health Officer at a port authorized for the purpose and notified to WHO. Under Article 20(3) a State Party notifies which ports may issue the SSCC, which may issue the SSCEC only, and which may grant the one-month extension.
  • Validity: a maximum of six months, for both the SSCEC and the SSCC (Article 39(1)).
  • Extension: by one month, where the inspection or the required control measures cannot be accomplished at that port - the extension carries the ship to a port where the certificate can be obtained.
  • Where no valid certificate is produced, or evidence of a public health risk is found on board, the State Party may proceed under Article 27(1) - treating the ship as affected and requiring disinfection, decontamination, disinsection or deratting.
  • Control measures are to be carried out whenever possible when the ship and holds are empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading (Article 39(4)).
  • For Indian coastal trade calling where no Port Health Officer is available, DG Shipping has provided for a Statement of Compliance issued by an authorized medical examiner, to be endorsed at the earliest available opportunity.

So the answer to "validity and extension options" on this paper is one pair of numbers, not two: six months, extendable by one month, issued by the Port Health Officer at an authorized port under the IHR. The stem confines the certificate limb to those issued to a vessel, so the seafarer's own medical certificate - which has its own authority, its own validity and its own extension rule under MLC 2006 Standard A1.2 - is outside what is asked here, even though the opening sentence names the crew. Naming the crew's certificate in one line to show you know the boundary costs nothing; answering it at length spends marks the examiner has not offered.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three questions are asked and each must be visibly answered - WHO's role, the national agencies, and the certificates in India with their validity and extension. Take them in the printed order and head each one. The last limb carries the numbers the examiner is certainly looking for, so never let it be squeezed by a long essay on WHO. Note what this stem does not say: it asks for the certificates "in India", without the qualifier "issued to a vessel for maintenance of the ship in good health" that other sittings of this question print - and its own first sentence names "the ship and the crew". Read plainly, the crew's medical certificate is inside the task. Answer the ship's certificates first and fully, then give the crew's its own short paragraph. That way you are complete on the wider reading and lose nothing on the narrower one.

The currency point that will separate answers

The IHR were amended in 2024 and those amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025 - just two months before this paper was sat. One visible consequence: Article 37 is now the "Ship Declaration of Health". In the original 2005 text it was the "Maritime Declaration of Health", and that older name is what almost every textbook, coaching note and older syllabus still uses. Both texts were compared directly for this answer: the original uses "Maritime Declaration of Health" throughout and never the new name; the consolidated amended text uses "Ship Declaration of Health" throughout and never the old one. Using the current name, and being able to say why it changed, is a cheap and genuine mark.

Ship health and crew health are two different systems

The question asks about "the ship and the crew", and the two run on separate tracks with separate authorities. Ship health is WHO and the IHR, administered in India by the Port Health Organisation under DGHS and the Ministry of Health, and evidenced by the SSCC or SSCEC. Crew health is MLC 2006 and STCW, administered in India by DG Shipping through approved medical examiners, and evidenced by seafarers' medical fitness certificates. Candidates routinely put DG Shipping in charge of ship sanitation certificates. It is not - the Port Health Officer issues those.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the SSCC/SSCEC a Deratting Certificate. Those were the predecessors under the earlier Regulations and were replaced under the IHR 2005 - the scope widened from rodents to all infection and contamination including vectors and reservoirs.
  • Saying the certificates are valid for six months extendable by six months. The extension is one month, and only where the inspection or control measures cannot be accomplished at that port.
  • Confusing the SSCC with the SSCEC. Exemption means no measures were needed; Control means measures were required and were completed.
  • Treating the Ship Declaration of Health as a certificate. It is a declaration by the master, to the Annex 8 model, and a State may dispense with it entirely under Article 37(4).
  • Naming WHO as an enforcement body. WHO recommends, certifies ports on request and publishes; the competent authority at the point of entry enforces.

Examiner traps

"Your certificate expired yesterday and the port cannot inspect - what happens?" The one-month extension in Article 39(1) exists precisely for this, and under Article 20(3)(c) States notify which of their ports may grant it, so that the ship can reach a port where the certificate is obtainable.

"When should the inspection be done?" Whenever possible with the ship and holds empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading - Article 39(4). Answering "any time" misses a printed requirement.

"What does WHO actually do about mosquitoes?" It publishes the list of areas where vector control is recommended for conveyances arriving from them, and advises the methods. States Parties then run vector control programmes at points of entry - to a minimum distance of 400 metres from the operational areas under Annex 5.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Who issues the SSCC in India? The Port Health Officer, at a port authorized for the purpose and notified by India to WHO.
  • Under which ministry does the Port Health Organisation sit? The Directorate General of Health Services, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.
  • Validity and extension? Maximum six months; extendable by one month where the inspection or control measures cannot be accomplished at the port.
  • What is recorded on an SSCC? The evidence found and the control measures taken - including, under Annex 5, the presence of vectors on board and the measures used to eradicate them.
  • What happens if you cannot produce a valid certificate? The State Party may treat the ship as affected under Article 27(1) and require disinfection, decontamination, disinsection or deratting.
  • Which convention covers the crew's medical fitness rather than the ship's sanitation? MLC 2006, with STCW for certification - administered in India by DG Shipping.

Uncertainty and source status

The IHR content in this answer is primary-verified, read in the consolidated WHO text as amended in 2014, 2022 and 2024, and the renaming of the Declaration of Health was confirmed by reading the original 2005 text alongside it rather than by inference. Two areas are weaker. The Indian administrative detail - the position of the Port Health Organisation under DGHS, the Indian Port Health Rules 1955, and the DG Shipping Statement of Compliance route for coastal trade - rests on Government and classification-society restatements rather than on the order and rules texts themselves, and is stated at that level of confidence. The list of authorized Indian ports is deliberately not reproduced: it is notified by the State Party to WHO and changes, so naming ports in a study answer would date badly. A candidate asked for specifics should say that the list is notified to and published by WHO rather than guess at it.

Two clocks, and why the examiner set the word "options"

The printed words are "validity and extension options" - plural. On this stem that plural has a real answer, because two different certificate regimes are in play and each has its own pair of numbers. The ship runs on the IHR: six months, extended by one month, and the extension exists so that a ship can reach a port where the inspection can actually be done. The crew runs on MLC Standard A1.2: two years (one if under 18, six for colour vision), extended by up to three months to the next port of call where a practitioner is available. Notice that the two extensions are built on the same idea - the document expires while the ship is somewhere the issuer is not - and that neither is a favour: both are capped, and both run to the next place the thing can be done properly. If you can set those two side by side in three lines you have answered the last limb completely, and very few candidates will.

Do not put DG Shipping in charge of the ship

This is the error that costs most marks on the India limb, and the widened certificate limb on this paper makes it easier to fall into, because both authorities now appear in the same answer. Keep them apart: the Port Health Officer, under the Port Health Organisation, DGHS and the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, certifies the ship - SSCC and SSCEC, under the IHR and the Indian Port Health Rules 1955. DG Shipping, under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958, approves the medical examiners who certify the seafarer. Health ministry for the hull; shipping administration for the man. Write that sentence and the limb is safe.

Understand this first

Three of these four processes are about vapour and temperature, not liquid, and that is the key to the whole question. In LNG work the hazard is rarely the liquid sitting in a tank - it is the gas you generate by doing something too quickly. Gassing up removes an atmosphere that would freeze solid or refuse to condense; cooling down prevents the violent boil-off a warm tank would produce; vapour management disposes of the gas that filling inevitably creates, and is what actually paces the transfer. The fourth accepts that the connection may still have to be broken, and even then the design aim is unchanged: part the line without releasing what is inside it.

Model written answer

1. The framework these processes sit inside

A ship using LNG as fuel is designed and operated under SOLAS chapter II-1 part G and the IGF Code; the bunkering operation itself is specified by ISO 20519 - equipment, procedures, competence and documentation. Safety rests on a two-stage emergency shutdown system, with the delivery facility and the receiving ship joined by an ESD link so both act together. Before transfer, a safety zone is set, compatibility and mooring agreed, and the checklist and bunkering plan completed.

2. Gassing up

Carried out when a tank is being brought back into service after refit, repair or inerting - before any liquid is introduced. The tank is full of inert gas or nitrogen, and that atmosphere must be replaced with methane vapour.

  • Why. Any carbon dioxide in inert gas would freeze at LNG temperature and block valves, filters and lines. Nitrogen does not freeze but is non-condensable: it accumulates in the vapour space, raises tank pressure, degrades the fuel's calorific value and upsets reliquefaction and gas supply.
  • How. LNG is taken from the supply through a vaporiser to produce warm methane vapour, which is admitted at the top of the tank. Being lighter than nitrogen, it forms a layer that pushes the heavier inert gas down and out through the bottom liquid line.
  • Where it goes. The displaced gas is led to the vent mast with attention to dispersion, or returned to the supply.
  • Completion. Sampling at the outlet continues until the hydrocarbon content reaches the specified value and carbon dioxide falls below its limit. The figures are set by the ship's manual and the containment system, not by a universal rule.

3. Cooling down

Once the tank holds methane vapour it is still warm. Cooling down brings the tank, pipework and manifold gradually towards LNG temperature, about minus 162 degrees Celsius, before bulk filling begins.

  • Why. To avoid thermal shock and excessive thermal stress in the tank structure and insulation; and to avoid the violent flash-off that occurs when LNG meets warm surfaces, which would over-pressurise the tank and swamp the vapour handling capacity.
  • How. LNG is admitted in small controlled quantities through spray nozzles at the top of the tank, evaporating as it falls and cooling the structure evenly.
  • Rate control. The permitted rate of temperature fall is limited by the tank designer and class, and temperatures are watched at fixed sensors through the tank to keep the structure cooling uniformly rather than locally.
  • Completion. When the sensors reach the target and are stable, bulk transfer starts at a low initial rate and is ramped up only once pressures and temperatures are seen to be steady.

4. Vapour management

LNG boils continuously from heat ingress, and filling a tank displaces the vapour already in it. Vapour management is the control of tank pressure within the design limit throughout, and it is the process that actually governs how fast bunkering can proceed.

  • Vapour return line - the primary method. A connection back to the bunker source returns the displaced vapour to its tanks, balancing pressure on both sides and removing the need to vent to atmosphere.
  • Consumption - burning the gas in the ship's engines, boilers or generators where the plant is running.
  • Reliquefaction where fitted, returning boil-off to the tank as liquid.
  • Gas combustion unit - burning off surplus gas safely where it cannot be used.
  • Controlled venting to the vent mast - a last resort only, because released methane is a potent greenhouse gas and venting is operationally and environmentally constrained.
  • Supporting controls. Spray filling to condense vapour; monitoring against the relief valve setting; level alarms and ESD trips; and attention to stratification and rollover where LNG of a different density is loaded onto an existing heel.

5. The emergency release coupling procedure

The emergency release system exists so that the transfer connection can be parted quickly and without spilling if the two vessels move beyond safe limits, if moorings fail, or in fire. Its heart is the emergency release coupling - a dry-break coupling with interlocked isolating valves which close before the halves separate, so the liquid held in the line is contained and the release of LNG and gas is minimised.

  • ESD-1 - stop the pumps and close the manifold valves on both sides. Transfer ceases. Triggered manually from either end or automatically by tank level, pressure, gas detection or fire.
  • Drain and reduce inventory in the transfer line so far as the time available allows.
  • ESD-2 - the release stage. The coupling's isolating valves close first, then the coupling parts at its designed point. Initiation is manual, or automatic from mooring load or vessel position monitoring exceeding its limits.
  • After release - both sides are purged with nitrogen, the parted hose end is made safe, and personnel stay clear of the release zone while residual liquid boils off.
  • Before transfer the ESD link must be connected and function-tested, the coupling confirmed armed and certified, communications and agreed emergency signals established, and the release covered in the bunkering plan and drilled.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four processes are named, so the examiner is marking four visible blocks. The commonest way to lose marks here is to write one long essay on LNG bunkering and leave the marker hunting for the four headings. Head each one exactly as printed. One short framing paragraph first is worth having - it lets you name the IGF Code, ISO 20519 and the ESD link once, so all four processes can then refer back to them without repetition.

The idea that ties the four together

Three of the four are about vapour and temperature, not liquid. That is the whole point. In LNG work the danger is rarely the liquid sitting in a tank - it is the gas you create by doing something too fast. Gassing up removes an atmosphere that would freeze or accumulate; cooling down prevents the flash-off that a warm tank would produce; vapour management disposes of the gas that filling inevitably generates. The emergency release is the fourth because it accepts that despite all of that, the connection may still have to be broken - and even then the design goal is the same, to part the line without releasing what is inside it. The order is also the order of the operation: you cannot cool a tank you have not gassed up, and you cannot fill one you have not cooled.

Why gassing up comes before cooling down

Because the freezing problem has to be solved while everything is still warm. Inert gas from a generator carries carbon dioxide, which becomes a solid well above LNG temperature. Introduce liquid into a tank that still holds carbon dioxide and you produce dry ice inside valves, strainers and spray lines - exactly where you least want a blockage. Gassing up removes it first. Nitrogen is a gentler problem but a persistent one: it will not freeze, but because it does not condense it stays in the vapour space, builds pressure, and thins the gas going to the engines. So gassing up is not a formality before the real work; it is what makes the cooling stage survivable.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the order - cooling down before gassing up. Liquid must not enter a tank still holding inert gas.
  • Saying gassing up removes air. Air was removed earlier by inerting; gassing up removes the inert gas or nitrogen and replaces it with methane vapour.
  • Describing cooling down as "filling slowly". It is spray cooling through nozzles at a controlled rate of temperature fall, not a slow bulk fill.
  • Treating venting as normal vapour management. It is a last resort; the vapour return line is the primary route and methane venting carries a real environmental penalty.
  • Describing the emergency release coupling as a hose that simply breaks. Its defining feature is the interlocked isolating valves that close before separation.
  • Conflating ESD-1 and ESD-2. Stopping the transfer and parting the connection are two different stages, and ESD-2 follows ESD-1.

Examiner traps

"Why not use inert gas from the generator and go straight to loading?" Carbon dioxide in the inert gas freezes at LNG temperature and blocks the system. That single sentence is what the gassing-up limb is really testing.

"What limits how fast you can bunker?" Not the pump - the vapour handling capacity and the tank pressure limit. Bunkering is paced by where the gas can go.

"The bunker vessel starts to drift. What happens first?" ESD-1 stops the transfer and closes the manifold valves; only then does ESD-2 operate the release coupling. Going straight to "the hose disconnects" misses the sequence.

"You loaded a different LNG on top of your heel. What is the risk?" Stratification and rollover - a sudden mixing of layers of different density releasing a large volume of vapour and spiking tank pressure.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the purpose of gassing up? To displace inert gas or nitrogen with methane vapour before liquid is admitted, chiefly to remove carbon dioxide that would freeze.
  • Why is warm vapour admitted at the top of the tank? It is lighter than nitrogen, so it layers above and pushes the heavier gas out through the bottom line.
  • What is the approximate temperature of LNG? About minus 162 degrees Celsius at atmospheric pressure.
  • What is the primary route for displaced vapour during bunkering? The vapour return line back to the bunkering facility.
  • What does an emergency release coupling do that an ordinary quick-release does not? Its interlocked isolating valves close before the coupling parts, containing the line inventory.
  • What links the two ESD systems? A ship-to-ship or ship-to-shore ESD link, so that shutdown on either side acts on both.

The emergency release architecture and the vapour return function are primary-verified against ISO 20519 - the interlocked isolating valves within the coupling, and the interconnected ESD link, are that standard's own requirements. Model written answer

1. The statute these principles come from

For an Indian examination the governing statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. It codifies the law of marine insurance in India and follows the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 closely - the 1906 Act may be named as the source it follows, but it must not be cited in its place, and the later English reforms of insurance law do not apply in India. Section 3 gives the contract itself: a contract of marine insurance is an agreement whereby the insurer undertakes to indemnify the assured, in the manner and to the extent thereby agreed, against marine losses.

The principles below are not decoration. Marine insurance is a promise to pay for something that has not happened yet, made by someone who cannot inspect the ship, about a voyage he cannot watch. Each principle answers one way that arrangement could be abused.

2. Insurable interest

The rule. Section 7 - every person has an insurable interest who is interested in a marine adventure; in particular, a person is so interested where he stands in any legal or equitable relation to the adventure or to any insurable property at risk, such that he may benefit by its safety or due arrival, may be prejudiced by its loss or damage or detention, or may incur liability in respect of it. Section 8 fixes the timing: the assured must be interested at the time of the loss, though he need not be when the insurance is effected - and where the subject-matter is insured "lost or not lost" he may recover even if he acquired his interest after the loss, unless he knew of the loss and the insurer did not. Section 6 supplies the sanction: a contract of marine insurance by way of wagering is void, including a policy made "interest or no interest".

Example. A shipowner has an insurable interest in his ship, and a cargo owner in his cargo. A charterer has an interest in the hire he stands to lose and in liabilities he may incur. But a Chief Engineer has no insurable interest in the ship he serves on - he is prejudiced by her loss only in employment, not in property - so a policy he took out on her hull would be a wager and void under section 6. Conversely, if cargo is sold afloat and the risk passes to the buyer, the buyer acquires the interest and can recover, even for a loss that occurred before he bought, under a "lost or not lost" policy.

3. Utmost good faith - uberrimae fidei

The rule. Section 19 - a contract of marine insurance is a contract based upon the utmost good faith, and if it is not observed by either party the other may avoid the contract. It is mutual, and it operates before the contract is concluded. Section 20 - the assured must disclose to the insurer, before the contract is concluded, every material circumstance known to him, and he is deemed to know every circumstance which in the ordinary course of business ought to be known by him. A circumstance is material if it would influence the judgement of a prudent insurer in fixing the premium or determining whether to take the risk. In the absence of enquiry, need not be disclosed: circumstances diminishing the risk; matters known or presumed known to the insurer; matters he has waived; and matters covered by an express or implied warranty. Sections 21 and 22 extend the duty to the assured's agent and to representations during negotiation.

Example. The vessel is fixed to load in a port where she has already suffered heavy weather damage to her hatch covers, or the owner knows the main engine has an unrepaired crankshaft defect. Both are material and must be disclosed. Failing to do so entitles the insurer to avoid the policy from the outset - and note the consequence, because it is what makes the principle bite: the contract is voidable, not void, the avoidance operates ab initio, and non-disclosure need not be fraudulent. An honest oversight has the same effect as concealment.

4. Indemnity

The rule. From section 3 - the insurer undertakes to indemnify. The assured is to be placed, so far as money can, in the position he occupied immediately before the loss - and no better. Marine insurance is a contract of indemnity and not one of profit. Two sections show it working: section 81, where the assured is insured for less than the insurable value, he is deemed his own insurer for the balance; and the measure-of-indemnity provisions, which fix what is actually recoverable rather than what was lost emotionally.

Example. A twenty-year-old auxiliary engine is destroyed by an insured peril and replaced with a new one. The assured recovers the value of what he lost, not the price of a new machine - the betterment is his. Similarly, a ship insured for one crore whose insurable value is two crore recovers only half of a partial loss, because under section 81 she is self-insured for the other half. Marine insurance is a qualified indemnity in practice, because a valued policy agrees the value in advance and that agreed value is conclusive between the parties - which is worth saying, as it is the one place where the principle is deliberately relaxed.

5. Subrogation

The rule. Section 79 - where the insurer pays for a total loss of the whole, or of an apportionable part of goods, he becomes entitled to take over the interest of the assured in whatever remains of the subject-matter paid for, and is subrogated to all the rights and remedies of the assured in respect of it as from the time of the casualty causing the loss. Where he pays for a partial loss he acquires no title to the subject-matter but is still subrogated to the assured's rights and remedies. Subrogation is the corollary of indemnity: it stops the assured recovering twice for one loss, once from the insurer and once from the wrongdoer.

Example. The ship is damaged in a collision for which the other vessel is wholly to blame. The hull underwriters pay the repair cost, and step into the owner's shoes to pursue the other vessel's owners for that sum - in the owner's name. It follows that the assured must not prejudice those rights: signing away a claim against a stevedore who damaged the cargo, or a repairer who caused the failure, may cost the assured the indemnity itself.

6. Contribution

The rule. Section 80 - where the assured is over-insured by double insurance, each insurer is bound, as between himself and the others, to contribute rateably to the loss in proportion to the amount for which he is liable under his own contract; and an insurer who pays more than his proportion may sue the others for contribution, with the remedies of a surety who has overpaid. Contribution is again the servant of indemnity - the assured may claim in full from one insurer, but he cannot collect the same loss twice, and the insurers settle the sharing among themselves.

Example. A cargo is insured by the seller under an open cover and again by the buyer under his own policy, both policies attaching to the same interest for the same voyage. On a claim the assured need not chase both; he recovers from one, and that insurer recovers a rateable proportion from the other under section 80. If the two policies total more than the insurable value, the assured still recovers only the value of the loss.

7. Proximate cause

The rule. Section 55 - the insurer is liable for any loss proximately caused by a peril insured against, and is not liable for any loss which is not so proximately caused. Proximate means the dominant or effective cause, not the one nearest in time. Section 55 then names exclusions which stand unless the policy provides otherwise, and two are worth carrying: the insurer is not liable for loss attributable to the wilful misconduct of the assured, but is liable for a loss proximately caused by an insured peril even though it would not have happened but for the misconduct or negligence of the master or crew.

Example. A ship strands because the officer of the watch made a navigational error. The proximate cause is stranding, an insured peril, and the negligence of the crew does not defeat the claim - section 55 says so expressly. But if the owner himself sent her to sea knowing she was unfit and she is lost in consequence, the dominant cause is his own conduct and the claim fails. That second half is where this principle meets seaworthiness, and it is the reason the two topics are so often examined together.

8. Mitigation - the duty to sue and labour

The rule. Insurance does not licence the assured to stand and watch. Where the policy contains a suing and labouring clause, section 78 makes the engagement supplementary to the contract of insurance, and the assured may recover from the insurer the expenses properly incurred under it - notwithstanding that the insurer may have paid for a total loss, or that the subject-matter was warranted free from particular average. The assured and his agents are expected to take reasonable measures to avert or minimise a loss.

Example. After a flooding in the engine room, the Chief Engineer runs the emergency bilge suction, arranges shore pumps and lands the damaged spares for cleaning and preservation. Those costs are recoverable as sue and labour, in addition to the claim itself. Doing nothing, and allowing salt water to write off machinery that could have been saved, risks the insurer declining that part of the loss.

The idea that ties them together

Five of the seven exist to enforce one idea: insurance restores, it does not enrich. Indemnity states it. Insurable interest ensures there is a real loss to restore, and not a bet. Subrogation stops the assured recovering the same loss twice, from insurer and wrongdoer. Contribution stops him recovering it twice from two insurers. Mitigation stops him letting the loss grow because someone else is paying. The remaining two protect the bargain itself: utmost good faith ensures the insurer priced the risk he actually took, and proximate cause ensures he pays only for the risk he agreed to carry. Say that in two sentences before the list, and the seven headings stop reading as memorised vocabulary.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the English MIA 1906 for an Indian paper. The 1963 Act governs. Name 1906 only as the model.
  • Listing the principles and omitting the examples. The stem asks for examples expressly.
  • Saying insurable interest must exist when the policy is taken out. Section 8 requires it at the time of the loss; it need not exist at inception.
  • Saying non-disclosure makes the contract void. It is voidable at the insurer's option, avoided ab initio.
  • Saying the non-disclosure must be fraudulent. It need not be - an innocent failure to disclose a material circumstance is enough.
  • Treating proximate cause as the last or nearest in time cause. It is the dominant and effective cause.
  • Saying crew negligence defeats a claim. Section 55 says the opposite - the insurer is liable for a loss proximately caused by an insured peril even though it would not have happened but for the negligence of master or crew.
  • Confusing subrogation (insurer recovers from the wrongdoer) with contribution (insurer recovers a share from another insurer).
  • Omitting the effect of under-insurance under section 81 - the assured is his own insurer for the balance.

Examiner traps

"You are Chief Engineer. Can you insure the ship?" No - no insurable interest in the hull, so the policy would be a wager and void under section 6. You can insure your own effects and your liability. "Cargo was already lost when the buyer bought it. Can he claim?" Under a "lost or not lost" policy, yes - unless he knew of the loss and the insurer did not (section 8). "The Second Engineer's error caused the flooding. Does the claim fail?" No - section 55(2)(a) preserves the claim for a loss proximately caused by an insured peril notwithstanding the negligence of master or crew. "The owner knew she was unseaworthy and sent her anyway." Different answer - that is the assured's own conduct, and it defeats the claim. "The engine was twenty years old and you got a new one." You recover the value lost, not the betterment - indemnity, not profit.

Model written answer

1. What Maslow proposed, and what kind of authority it is

Abraham Maslow set out his theory in A Theory of Human Motivation (1943) and developed it in Motivation and Personality (1954). It is management and psychological literature, not a regulatory requirement - no convention prescribes a theory of motivation, and it should be attributed as what it is. Its claim is that human needs are hierarchical: they arrange themselves in an order of prepotency, so that a lower, unsatisfied need dominates behaviour until it is relatively satisfied, whereupon the next level emerges as the motivator.

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole practical value of the theory: a satisfied need is no longer a motivator, and you cannot motivate at a level above the one a person is actually stuck on. Offering recognition to a man who has not slept properly for a fortnight, or career development to one who has not been paid, does not work - and Maslow explains why.

2. The five levels, read onto a ship

  1. Physiological - food, water, sleep, warmth. On board: adequate rest and hours of rest actually observed, edible and sufficient food, drinking water, a cabin cool enough and quiet enough to sleep in, sanitary facilities that work. This is the level a ship most often fails at, and it is the level that disables every other one.
  2. Safety and security - freedom from danger and from uncertainty. On board: a safe engine room, working PPE, enforced permit-to-work and enclosed-space procedures, machinery guards, but equally job security, wages paid on time, a contract that ends when it says it does, and a credible expectation of relief and repatriation on the due date.
  3. Social - love and belonging. On board: acceptance in the engine-room team and in the ship's company, being spoken to rather than about, contact with family - which today means workable connectivity - a mess where mixed nationalities are not segregated, and recreation.
  4. Esteem - recognition, status, competence, respect from others and self-respect. On board: credit given by name for a job done well, responsibility entrusted, being consulted on the work one performs, rank and role respected, appraisals that are honest and useful rather than a formality.
  5. Self-actualisation - realising one's full potential. On board: promotion and the training that leads to it, a genuinely challenging job, being given charge of a project, mentoring juniors, and support for certificates of competency.

3. What the theory claims, and where it is weak

The mechanism is prepotency plus relative satisfaction - Maslow did not claim a level must be completely satisfied before the next appears, and he allowed movement back down when a lower need is threatened. A man secure and well regarded on Monday can be driven straight back to the safety level on Tuesday by news that the company is not paying wages.

The honest limitations are worth a sentence, because a Chief Engineer applying the theory needs them. The empirical support for a strict five-level ordering is weak; the ordering is culturally variable, and a multinational crew will not rank belonging and esteem identically; the levels overlap rather than queue; and Maslow himself never drew the pyramid the diagram is always given as. Later models - notably Alderfer's compression into existence, relatedness and growth, and Herzberg's separation of hygiene factors, whose absence causes dissatisfaction, from motivators, whose presence causes satisfaction - address some of that. Herzberg's distinction is particularly useful at sea: fixing the food and the accommodation removes dissatisfaction but does not by itself motivate anybody.

4. As Chief Engineer - addressing lack of motivation

Diagnose the level before prescribing the remedy. That is the theory's actual use. Apathy in a motor-man is a symptom; the question is which need is unmet.

  • Start at the bottom, because it is cheapest and most often the cause. Examine rest hours as they are actually worked, not as recorded; a record that is always exactly compliant is a warning rather than a reassurance. Look at watch patterns, port workload, calls at night, food quality and quantity, cabin air conditioning, hot water, mess and laundry.
  • Then safety and certainty. Are wages and allotments paid on time; is the contract end date real; has relief been confirmed; is the engine room actually safe to work in. Uncertainty about going home is one of the strongest demotivators at sea and it is not fixed by encouragement.
  • Then belonging. Include the rating in the daily toolbox talk, use his name, ensure a mixed-nationality crew is not fragmenting at meal times, protect connectivity and recreation time.
  • Then esteem. Delegate real responsibility with the authority to go with it - give a Fourth Engineer ownership of the purifiers or the boiler and let him report on them. Credit him in front of the department and in the appraisal. Ask before deciding on work he does daily.
  • Then growth. Plan on-board training, rotate men through unfamiliar machinery, prepare juniors for their next certificate, mentor deliberately rather than incidentally.
  • Lead by example and be consistent. A Chief Engineer who turns out at 0300 for a blackout, wears the PPE he demands, and applies the same standard to everyone earns the authority that no theory supplies.

5. As Chief Engineer - addressing differences in attitude

A multinational engine room contains real differences in culture, language, hierarchy-distance, age and expectation, and Maslow is a reminder that people at different levels of need behave differently for reasons that are not defiance. The task is not to erase difference but to prevent it becoming misunderstanding or division.

  • Set one visible standard for work, safety and behaviour, applied identically regardless of nationality, rank or seniority. Perceived favouritism destroys esteem faster than anything else on this list.
  • Fix communication first. A working language, briefings confirmed by read-back rather than a nod, written and pictorial checklists, and awareness that in some cultures a junior will not contradict a senior even when he knows the senior is wrong. That is a safety issue as much as a morale one.
  • Use the structured briefing. Toolbox talks and pre-job briefings give every attitude the same information and the same opportunity to object.
  • Deal with conflict early, privately and factually; separate the behaviour from the person; and treat bullying, harassment and discrimination as a formal matter, not a personality clash. MLC 2006 and the on-board complaint procedure exist for it.
  • Mix the teams deliberately on jobs so nationalities and generations work together rather than in blocs, and pair experience with inexperience.
  • Adjust your own style - direct instruction where a task is safety-critical or time-critical; consultation where the man doing the work knows more about it than you do.

6. As Chief Engineer - increasing the sense of competitiveness

The stem asks for competitiveness, and the useful reading is constructive competition - measurable improvement against a target and against the department's own past performance - rather than rivalry between individuals, which on a ship damages the teamwork that safety depends on.

  • Make performance visible and measurable. Fuel and lubricating-oil consumption, specific consumption against the shop trial, boiler and generator running hours, planned-maintenance completion rate, number of overdue jobs, spare-parts stock accuracy, unplanned stoppages. What is measured and displayed improves.
  • Set targets that belong to the department, not to an individual - a month with no overdue PMS jobs, a reduction in generator running hours in port, a clean unannounced inspection.
  • Benchmark against the sister ship or the fleet where the company publishes it. Competing with another ship unites the department; competing with each other divides it.
  • Recognise promptly and specifically. Name the man and the job at the safety meeting; record it in the appraisal; ask the company to recognise it in writing. Recognition delayed to the end of a contract is recognition wasted.
  • Give ownership of an area - each engineer responsible for named machinery and its performance figures - so the comparison is with the equipment's own history and the pride is in the plant.
  • Keep it constructive. Never rank men publicly against each other, never let competition encourage short cuts, under-reporting of defects or falsified records, and be alert to competition suppressing the reporting of near misses. A department that competes on how few defects it reports is more dangerous than one that reports many.

7. The management framework this sits inside

Motivation theory is not the authority for what a Chief Engineer must do; it explains why what he must do works. The obligations come from elsewhere and should be named:

  • ISM Code - the company must ensure the ship is manned with qualified and medically fit seafarers, provide familiarisation for new personnel, identify training needs, ensure personnel understand the safety management system in a working language, and support the master. Reporting of accidents, near misses and non-conformities, and the master's overriding authority, are the mechanisms by which a well-motivated department actually improves the ship.
  • STCW 1978 as amended - the Manila amendments introduced competences in leadership, teamworking and managerial skill, at operational and at management level; for a Chief Engineer these are the management-level competences of Table A-III/2, which is why this is examinable at all rather than being merely good practice.
  • MLC 2006 - the material conditions Maslow's lower levels describe are largely legal entitlements: hours of rest, wages, accommodation and recreational facilities, food and catering, medical care, repatriation, and the on-board complaint procedure. Meeting the MLC is what makes the bottom two levels achievable.
  • The honest summary. Maslow supplies a diagnostic order; the ISM Code supplies the system; STCW supplies the competence; the MLC supplies the entitlements. A Chief Engineer who uses all four is doing management. One who quotes only the pyramid is doing revision.

Say something about the theory's limits

A short, accurate paragraph on the weaknesses is worth real credit at management level, because it shows judgement rather than recitation. The strict five-level ordering has weak empirical support; the ordering is culturally variable, which matters directly on a multinational ship; the levels overlap rather than queue; and Maslow never drew the pyramid. Herzberg is the most useful supplement for a ship: hygiene factors - food, accommodation, pay, working conditions - cause dissatisfaction when absent but do not motivate when present, whereas motivators - responsibility, recognition, achievement, advancement - do. That is exactly why a ship can have excellent food and a thoroughly demoralised engine room.

Model written answer

1. Where India's jurisdiction over a foreign ship comes from

The question contains a trap in its own premise. A foreign ship is under the exclusive jurisdiction of her flag State for the conduct of a safety investigation, so it is worth being precise about why India may inquire at all. The answer is that the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 does not take its jurisdiction here from the flag. Section 358(1) deems a shipping casualty to occur when:

  • (a) on or near the coasts of India, any ship is lost, abandoned, stranded or materially damaged;
  • (b) on or near the coasts of India, any ship causes loss or material damage to any other ship;
  • (c) any loss of life ensues by reason of any casualty happening to or on board any ship on or near the coasts of India;
  • (d) in any place, such a casualty occurs to or on board an Indian ship and a competent witness is found in India;
  • (e) an Indian ship is lost or supposed lost and evidence is obtainable in India.

Limbs (a), (b) and (c) say "any ship" - they are flag-neutral, and they are the limbs that catch a foreign ship in an Indian port. Only (d) and (e) are confined to Indian ships. India's competence therefore rests on the geography of the casualty, not the nationality of the vessel. Section 357 adds that in this Part "coasts" includes the coasts of creeks and tidal rivers, which brings ports and harbours squarely inside it. Alongside this, India acts internationally as the coastal State (Casualty Investigation Code 2.3) and, where its nationals or interests are engaged, as a substantially interested State.

2. (i) The machinery of the Merchant Shipping Act

Part XII of the Act is headed Investigations and Inquiries and runs as a sequence:

  1. s.358(2) - notice. The master, pilot, harbour master or other person in charge - of each ship where two are concerned - must give immediate notice of the casualty, on arriving in India, to the officer appointed by the Central Government.
  2. s.359 - preliminary inquiry. On receiving credible information that a shipping casualty has occurred, that officer shall forthwith report in writing to the Central Government, and may proceed to make a preliminary inquiry, sending a report of it to the Central Government. In practice this is the Mercantile Marine Department surveyor acting under the Directorate General of Shipping.
  3. s.360 - application for formal investigation. That officer, whether he has held a preliminary inquiry or not, may apply to a competent court for a formal investigation, and shall do so where the Central Government directs. The court then shall make the investigation.
  4. s.361 - the court. A Judicial Magistrate of the first class specially empowered by the Central Government, or a Metropolitan Magistrate.
  5. s.362 - conduct of individuals. The court may inquire into any charge of incompetency or misconduct against a master, mate or engineer arising in the course of the investigation, and into any wrongful act or default causing the casualty; the person charged must first be furnished with a statement of the case.
  6. s.365 to s.368 - powers. The court has, for compelling attendance of witnesses and production of documents, the same powers as in its criminal jurisdiction; it appoints not less than two and not more than four assessors conversant with maritime or mercantile affairs (s.366); and it may arrest witnesses and enter ships (s.367).
  7. s.369 - the report. The court transmits to the Central Government a full report of its conclusions together with the evidence, and the Central Government causes it to be published in the Official Gazette.
  8. s.370 to s.372 - outcome. Powers to cancel or suspend a certificate granted by the Central Government, to censure a master, mate or engineer, and to remove a master and appoint a new one.

The provision that answers the foreign-ship point directly is s.369(2). Where the investigation affects a master or officer of a ship other than an Indian ship, holding a certificate under the law of a country outside India, the Central Government may transmit a copy of the report together with the evidence to the proper authority in that country. India investigates and reports; the certificate remains the foreign administration's to act on. The same boundary appears in s.363(1)(b): a holder of a foreign certificate may be proceeded against for incompetency or misconduct outside a formal investigation only where it occurred on board an Indian ship.

3. (i) The international codes that sit above it

  • Casualty Investigation Code - the Code of the International Standards and Recommended Practices for a Safety Investigation into a Marine Casualty or Marine Incident, resolution MSC.255(84), in force 1 January 2010. Part I general, Part II mandatory standards, Part III recommended practices.
  • SOLAS regulation XI-1/6, introduced by MSC.257(84), makes Parts I and II of the Code mandatory. SOLAS regulation I/21 is the older and broader undertaking to investigate; MARPOL article 12 covers casualties with a major deleterious effect on the environment; UNCLOS article 94(7) sits behind all of them.
  • The object is prevention, not blame. A marine safety investigation (2.11) is conducted with the objective of preventing marine casualties and marine incidents in the future, and is to be separate from, and independent of, criminal or other investigations. Chapter 11 requires that it not be subject to external direction.
  • Who investigates. A safety investigation is mandatory into every very serious marine casualty (chapter 6), and the flag State is responsible for ensuring it is conducted. Where India is not the flag State, chapter 7 allows the flag State to agree that another substantially interested State conducts it - and a State conducting or participating in such an investigation discharges its own obligations. Chapters 9 and 10 govern parallel investigations and co-operation.

So the two systems do different work and run side by side. The M.S. Act inquiry is a domestic proceeding which can reach certificates and conduct; the Code investigation is a no-blame safety investigation whose output goes to the IMO. India runs the first over any casualty on its coasts, and takes part in the second as flag State, coastal State or by agreement.

4. (ii) Marine casualty defined

Casualty Investigation Code paragraph 2.9. A marine casualty means an event, or a sequence of events, that has resulted in any of the following which has occurred directly in connection with the operations of a ship:

  • .1 the death of, or serious injury to, a person;
  • .2 the loss of a person from a ship;
  • .3 the loss, presumed loss or abandonment of a ship;
  • .4 material damage to a ship;
  • .5 the stranding or disabling of a ship, or the involvement of a ship in a collision;
  • .6 material damage to marine infrastructure external to a ship, that could seriously endanger the safety of the ship, another ship or an individual;
  • .7 severe damage to the environment, or the potential for severe damage to the environment, brought about by the damage of a ship or ships.

The exclusion is part of the definition and carries a mark: a marine casualty does not include a deliberate act or omission with the intention to cause harm to the safety of a ship, an individual or the environment. Sabotage and terrorism are therefore not marine casualties, and are dealt with elsewhere. Two neighbouring terms complete the picture: a marine incident (2.10) is an event other than a marine casualty which endangered, or if not corrected would endanger, safety; and a very serious marine casualty (2.22) is a marine casualty involving total loss of the ship, or a death, or severe damage to the environment. Only the last of these triggers a mandatory investigation.

5. (iii) What the marine safety investigation report consists of

The Code answers this by definition rather than by leaving it to practice. Paragraph 2.12 - a marine safety investigation report means a report that contains:

  1. a summary outlining the basic facts, and stating whether any deaths, injuries or pollution occurred as a result;
  2. the identity of the flag State, owners, operators, the company as identified in the safety management certificate, and the classification society, subject to national privacy laws;
  3. where relevant, the dimensions and engines of any ship involved, with a description of the crew, work routine and other matters such as time served on the ship;
  4. a narrative detailing the circumstances;
  5. analysis and comment on the causal factors, including any mechanical, human and organizational factors;
  6. a discussion of the findings, including identification of safety issues, and the conclusions;
  7. where appropriate, recommendations with a view to preventing future casualties and incidents.

Note what item .5 requires of an engineer's evidence: causal factors are defined at 2.2 as actions, omissions, events or conditions without which the casualty would not have occurred, or its consequences would not have been as serious - and the Code expects organizational factors to be named alongside mechanical and human ones. That is where the ISM Code and the company's own system come under examination.

6. (iii) How the report reaches the IMO

  1. Draft stage. Under chapter 13 the draft report is sent to substantially interested States for comment, normally allowing 30 days; comments are considered before the report is finalized. Under the recommended practice at 25.2 and 25.3 the same courtesy may be extended to interested parties, again on a 30-day or mutually agreed period.
  2. Submission - the mandatory limb. Chapter 14.1 - the marine safety investigating State shall submit the final version of the report to the Organization for every safety investigation into a very serious marine casualty. Chapter 14.2 - where an investigation into any other casualty or incident produces a report containing information which may prevent or lessen the seriousness of future casualties, that final version shall also be submitted.
  3. The mechanism. Reporting is done electronically into the IMO's Global Integrated Shipping Information System (GISIS), in its Marine Casualties and Incidents module, under the revised harmonized reporting procedures of MSC-MEPC.3/Circ.4/Rev.1, which implement the reports required by SOLAS regulations I/21 and XI-1/6, MARPOL articles 8 and 12 and Load Lines article 23. Basic factual data is populated as soon as practicable after the occurrence; the full report follows.
  4. Publication. Chapter 14.4 - the final report shall be made available to the public and the shipping industry, or the investigating State shall assist in accessing it where published by another State or the Organization. Domestically, s.369(3) of the M.S. Act requires publication in the Official Gazette.
  5. Disagreement. Paragraph 25.6 - where a substantially interested State disagrees with the whole or part of a final report, it may submit its own report to the Organization.
  6. Use. IMO's III Sub-Committee analyses the reports and the lessons feed back into amendments of the instruments - which is the whole purpose of the no-blame system.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints (16) on its opening line and then three numbered limbs, so the opening sentence is a heading for the whole question rather than a fourth task - answer the three limbs. They are very unequal in size: (i) is a two-part discursive limb needing both the M.S. Act sections and the international codes; (ii) is a definition; (iii) is content plus a route. Budget accordingly - roughly half the answer on (i), a short (ii), and a full (iii) - and note that (ii) and (iii) are the limbs where exact recall is possible and therefore expected. A definition you can give in the Code's own terms is worth more than a paragraph of approximation.

The question's own trap

It asks how the Indian Administration investigates casualties on foreign ships. The instinctive answer - that a foreign ship is the flag State's business - is half right and, given as the whole answer, wrong. The resolution is in the words of section 358(1): limbs (a), (b) and (c) say "any ship", and only limbs (d) and (e) say "Indian ship". India's power over a casualty in its ports is territorial, not flag-based. Then the boundary reappears in the right place: under s.369(2) India may report to the foreign administration but does not touch that administration's certificate, and under s.363(1)(b) it may proceed against a foreign certificate holder outside a formal investigation only where the misconduct occurred on an Indian ship. India investigates the casualty; the flag State disciplines the officer. Getting that division right is what the question is for.

Two systems, not one

Candidates routinely merge the M.S. Act inquiry and the Casualty Investigation Code investigation into a single process. They are different in purpose, output and consequence. The Act's inquiry is a domestic legal proceeding before a magistrate, with assessors, criminal-jurisdiction powers over witnesses, and outcomes that reach certificates and conduct. The Code's investigation is a no-blame safety investigation, expressly independent of criminal or disciplinary proceedings, whose output is a report to the IMO for the benefit of everyone else. They can run in parallel over the same casualty - chapters 9 and 10 of the Code exist precisely because they do - and the evidence rules differ, which is why chapter 23 protects marine safety records including VDR data from general disclosure. Saying this in two sentences shows the examiner you understand the architecture rather than the vocabulary.

Common mistakes

  • Answering that India has no jurisdiction over a foreign ship. s.358(1)(a)-(c) says "any ship".
  • Defining very serious marine casualty when the stem asks for marine casualty. They are different paragraphs - 2.22 and 2.9 - and the examiner asked for the wider one.
  • Omitting the deliberate act exclusion from the 2.9 definition. It is part of the definition.
  • Inventing report contents for limb (iii). The Code defines them at 2.12, in seven items.
  • Saying the report goes to IMO 'by letter'. It goes into the GISIS Marine Casualties and Incidents module under MSC-MEPC.3/Circ.4/Rev.1.
  • Saying every casualty report goes to IMO. Mandatory only for a very serious marine casualty (14.1); otherwise only where the report carries information that may prevent future casualties (14.2).
  • Confusing the preliminary inquiry (s.359, an officer) with the formal investigation (s.360-361, a court). And note s.360 allows an application whether or not a preliminary inquiry was held.
  • Forgetting the assessors under s.366 - two to four, conversant with maritime or mercantile affairs. It is a favourite oral question.
  • Citing the Merchant Shipping Act 2025. It had assent but had not commenced at this sitting.

Model written answer

1. The framework Title 1 sits inside

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is built in three parts - the Articles, the Regulations, and the Code. The Code has two halves: Part A Standards, which are mandatory, and Part B Guidelines, which are not, but to which a Member must give due consideration (Article VI). Article VI.3 allows a Member to implement a Part A requirement by substantial equivalence. Title 1 is headed "Minimum requirements for seafarers to work on a ship" and contains exactly the four Regulations this question sets, in this order.

The shape of Title 1 is worth stating before the detail: three of its four Regulations answer the question "may this particular person work at sea at all?" - age, health, competence - and the fourth regulates the system that put them on the ship. That is why recruitment and placement belongs in a Title otherwise about the individual.

2. (a) Minimum age to work on a ship

Regulation 1.1 - purpose: to ensure that no under-age persons work on a ship. No person below the minimum age shall be employed, engaged or work on a ship. Standard A1.1 sets three separate rules, and the marks are in keeping them apart:

  • A1.1.1 - the hard floor. The employment, engagement or work on board of any person under the age of 16 is prohibited. There is no exception to this one.
  • A1.1.2 - night work. Night work of seafarers under 18 is prohibited. Night is defined in accordance with national law, but must cover at least nine hours, starting no later than midnight and ending no earlier than 5 a.m.
  • A1.1.3 - the only exception, and it is to the night rule alone. The competent authority may except a seafarer where effective training under an established programme would otherwise be impaired, or where the specific nature of the duty or a recognized training programme requires night duty and the authority determines, after consultation with shipowners' and seafarers' organizations, that it will not be detrimental to their health or well-being.
  • A1.1.4 - hazardous work. Employment of seafarers under 18 is prohibited where the work is likely to jeopardize their health or safety. The types of such work are determined by national laws or the competent authority, after tripartite consultation, in accordance with relevant international standards.

So there are two ages, not one: 16 is the absolute minimum for working at all, and 18 is the threshold below which night work and hazardous work are additionally restricted.

3. (b) Medical certification for duties on board

Regulation 1.2 - seafarers shall not work on a ship unless they are certified as medically fit to perform their duties; exceptions only as prescribed in the Code. Standard A1.2 supplies the detail.

  • Held before starting. A valid medical certificate attesting fitness for the duties they are to carry out at sea must be held prior to beginning work (A1.2.1). The competent authority prescribes the nature of the examination and the certificate after tripartite consultation (A1.2.2).
  • STCW is accepted. A certificate issued under STCW is accepted for the purposes of Regulation 1.2, so one examination satisfies both instruments (A1.2.3).
  • Who issues it. A duly qualified medical practitioner, or - for a certificate concerning eyesight alone - a person recognized by the competent authority as qualified. Practitioners must enjoy full professional independence in their medical judgement (A1.2.4). In India this means a medical examiner approved by the Directorate General of Shipping, acting under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 and the rules made under it.
  • Right of further examination. A seafarer refused a certificate, or given a limitation as to time, field of work or trading area, must be given the opportunity of a further examination by another independent practitioner or an independent medical referee (A1.2.5).
  • What it must state. That hearing and sight are satisfactory - and colour vision where fitness is liable to be affected by defective colour vision; and that the seafarer is not suffering from a condition likely to be aggravated by service at sea, to render him unfit, or to endanger the health of others on board (A1.2.6).
  • Validity. A maximum of two years; one year if the seafarer is under 18; a certification of colour vision, six years (A1.2.7). These are maxima - a shorter period may be required by the specific duties or under STCW.
  • Expiry at sea. If validity expires in the course of a voyage, the certificate continues in force until the next port of call where a certificate can be obtained, not exceeding three months (A1.2.9). Separately, in urgent cases, the authority may permit work without a valid certificate to the next port, again not exceeding three months, provided the seafarer holds an expired certificate of recent date (A1.2.8).
  • Language. For ships ordinarily engaged on international voyages the certificate must be provided as a minimum in English (A1.2.10).

4. (c) Training and qualification

Regulation 1.3 - purpose: to ensure that seafarers are trained or qualified to carry out their duties on board ship. This limb has a feature worth noticing and stating, because it is the one thing here a well-prepared candidate can say that others will not: there is no Standard A1.3 and no Guideline B1.3. Regulation 1.3 stands alone, without a Part A Standard beneath it. The reason is that the detailed requirements already exist elsewhere - in STCW - and the Convention deliberately does not duplicate them.

  • Reg 1.3.1 - seafarers shall not work on a ship unless they are trained or certified as competent or otherwise qualified to perform their duties.
  • Reg 1.3.2 - seafarers shall not be permitted to work unless they have successfully completed training for personal safety on board ship. This is the basic safety training limb, and it applies to every seafarer, not only to certificated officers.
  • Reg 1.3.3 - the bridge to STCW. Training and certification in accordance with the mandatory instruments adopted by the IMO shall be considered as meeting the requirements of paragraphs 1 and 2. So an STCW certificate of competency and the STCW basic training discharge this Regulation.
  • Reg 1.3.4 - a transitional provision preserving obligations under the Certification of Able Seamen Convention, 1946 (No. 74) for Members bound by it, until IMO provisions covering the subject matter enter into force or five years elapse from entry into force of the MLC, whichever is earlier.

5. (d) Recruitment and placement

Regulation 1.4 - purpose: to ensure that seafarers have access to an efficient and well-regulated seafarer recruitment and placement system. All seafarers shall have access to an efficient, adequate and accountable system for finding employment on board ship without charge to the seafarer. Services operating in a Member's territory must conform to the Code; and a Member must require that shipowners using services based in countries where the Convention does not apply ensure those services conform too (Reg 1.4.3) - the provision that stops the obligation being avoided by recruiting through a non-Party State.

Standard A1.4 regulates the services. A public service must be operated in an orderly manner protecting seafarers' employment rights (A1.4.1). Private services must operate only under a standardized system of licensing or certification or other regulation, established after consultation with shipowners' and seafarers' organizations; and undue proliferation of private services shall not be encouraged (A1.4.2). A Member adopting such a system must at a minimum (A1.4.5):

  • Prohibit blacklisting - the use of means, mechanisms or lists intended to prevent or deter seafarers from gaining employment for which they are qualified.
  • Prohibit fees. No fees or other charges for recruitment, placement or providing employment may be borne directly or indirectly, in whole or in part, by the seafarer - other than the cost of a national statutory medical certificate, the national seafarer's book, and a passport or similar travel document. The cost of visas is expressly excluded and is borne by the shipowner.
  • Require services to maintain an up-to-date register of all seafarers recruited or placed, available for inspection by the competent authority.
  • Require that seafarers are informed of their rights and duties before or during engagement, can examine their employment agreements before and after signing, and receive a copy.
  • Require services to verify that seafarers are qualified and hold the necessary documents, and that agreements accord with applicable law and any collective bargaining agreement.
  • Require services to make sure, as far as practicable, that the shipowner has the means to protect seafarers from being stranded in a foreign port.
  • Require services to examine and respond to complaints and advise the competent authority of any unresolved complaint.
  • Require a system of protection - insurance or an equivalent measure - to compensate seafarers for monetary loss arising from the failure of the service, or of the shipowner under the employment agreement, to meet its obligations.

The competent authority must closely supervise and control all such services, granting or renewing licences only after verification of compliance (A1.4.6), and must ensure machinery exists for investigating complaints about their activities (A1.4.7).

6. How Title 1 is certified and enforced

Title 1 is not merely aspirational: its four subjects are the first four of the sixteen areas listed in Appendix A5-I which must be inspected and approved before a Maritime Labour Certificate is issued, and they are declared in the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance - Part I stating the national requirements and Part II the shipowner's measures. The certificate and DMLC are accepted by a port State as prima facie evidence of compliance (Reg 5.2.1), and the same subjects appear in Appendix A5-III as areas open to a more detailed port State inspection. On board, the practical evidence a Chief Engineer should expect to be asked for is the age and medical certificate of every engine-room rating, the STCW certificates and basic safety training of the watchkeepers, and the manning agent's licence behind the crew list.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

"Write a short note on the following" with four lettered limbs is an instruction about layout as much as content. Four headings, in the printed order, lettered as printed. Roughly four marks each, so roughly equal length - and the commonest failure is to write three pages on medical certificates, because that is the limb most familiar from daily practice, and two lines on recruitment and placement. Resist it. A short framing paragraph naming Title 1 and the mandatory status of Part A is worth having, because it lets all four limbs cite Standards without re-explaining what a Standard is.

The idea that ties the four together

Title 1 answers one question in four ways: who is allowed on the ship in the first place. Age, health and competence are three tests applied to the individual - is this person old enough, fit enough, trained enough. Recruitment and placement is a test applied to the system that supplied them. The Convention's insight is that the first three are worth little if the fourth is unregulated: a manning agent that charges fees, blacklists complainers and cannot be inspected will produce crews who are nominally compliant and actually coerced. Saying that in one sentence turns four disconnected notes into an answer.

The detail that separates a good answer here

There is no Standard A1.3. Regulations 1.1, 1.2 and 1.4 each have a Part A Standard beneath them; Regulation 1.3 does not, and neither does it have a Part B Guideline. That is not an oversight - the Convention points at STCW instead, and Regulation 1.3, paragraph 3 says expressly that training and certification under the IMO's mandatory instruments shall be considered as meeting the requirement. Most candidates will invent a Standard A1.3 to keep the pattern tidy. Stating correctly that none exists, and explaining why, is worth more than a paragraph of invented content - and it demonstrates the candidate has read the instrument rather than a summary of it.

The two ages, and why examiners like them

16 and 18 do different work. 16 is a prohibition on working at all, with no exception anywhere in the Standard. 18 is a threshold for two further restrictions - night work and hazardous work - and the night restriction is the only one carrying an exception, for training. Candidates routinely merge these into a single vague statement that "young seafarers are protected", or attach the training exception to the age-16 rule, where it does not belong. Set them out as three separate rules and the limb is secure.

Common mistakes

  • Inventing a Standard A1.3. It does not exist. Regulation 1.3 stands alone and points to STCW.
  • Saying the minimum age is 18. It is 16; 18 is the threshold for night work and hazardous work.
  • Attaching the training exception to the minimum age. It applies only to the night-work restriction.
  • Giving medical certificate validity as two years without the qualifications - one year under 18, six years for colour vision, and all of them maxima.
  • Confusing the two three-month provisions: A1.2.9 is a certificate expiring during a voyage, which continues in force; A1.2.8 is an urgent case where the authority permits work on a recently expired certificate. Different triggers, same ceiling.
  • Saying the seafarer pays nothing at all. The seafarer may bear the cost of the statutory medical certificate, the seafarer's book and a passport - but not visas, which fall on the shipowner.
  • Omitting Regulation 1.4.3, the provision reaching services based in non-Party States. It is the limb that makes the rest enforceable.
  • Forgetting the insurance or equivalent requirement in A1.4.5 - the financial protection against a manning agent's or shipowner's default.
  • Writing about hours of rest, wages or accommodation. Those are Titles 2 and 3 and earn nothing here.

Examiner traps

"What is Standard A1.3?" - a trap question. There is none; Regulation 1.3 has no Part A Standard, and the requirement is met through STCW. "A cadet of 17 is on the 12-4 watch. Any problem?" Yes - night work under 18 is prohibited, and the watch spans the protected period; it is lawful only under the A1.1.3 training exception, granted by the competent authority after tripartite consultation. "His medical expired yesterday, mid-ocean. Must we divert?" No - A1.2.9 continues it in force to the next port of call where a certificate can be obtained, up to three months. "The manning agent deducted one month's wages as a placement fee." Prohibited outright by A1.4.5(b), directly or indirectly, in whole or in part. "Who pays for the visa?" The shipowner - the exclusion is express.

Model written answer

1. What a time charter is, and where the charterer's power comes from

Under a time charter the owner keeps possession, the crew and the technical management of the ship and hires out her earning capacity for a period. The charterer pays hire for time, not for cargo carried, and in return acquires the right to direct the ship's employment. The division is the key to the whole question and standard forms such as NYPE express it in an employment and indemnity clause: the master and crew, though the owner's servants, follow the charterer's orders as to employment of the vessel.

So the charterer commands where she goes, what she carries and, within limits, how fast; the owner remains responsible for seaworthiness, manning, maintenance, class and safe operation. The charterer's influence on machinery is therefore almost always indirect - exercised through commercial instructions whose engineering consequences fall on the Chief Engineer. He never orders a setting on the governor; he orders a speed, a port rotation and a bunker grade, and the machinery lives with the result.

2. The charterer's influences on propulsion and other machinery

  • Speed instructions. The most direct influence. Orders to slow steam or to run at an economical speed keep the main engine at prolonged low load, with the familiar consequences - fouling of the turbocharger and economiser, poor combustion and deposits, cold corrosion in the liners, exhaust temperature deviations, soot fires in the economiser and the need for auxiliary blowers. Orders to increase speed to make a laycan load the engine near its maximum continuous rating for extended periods.
  • Bunkers. Under most time charters the charterer supplies and pays for the fuel, and therefore chooses the supplier and the grade within the specification the charterparty allows. That decides the quality the plant must digest - viscosity, density, catalytic fines, sulphur content, stability and compatibility with what is already on board - and it drives purifier settings, filter loading, fuel-pump and injector wear, and changeover management.
  • Trading pattern and port rotation. Short passages with frequent port calls mean many manoeuvres, repeated starts and stops, high starting-air demand, more time on auxiliary power and less time at steady sea load. Long ballast legs, waiting at anchor and idle time bring their own problems of layup, standby machinery and lubrication.
  • Cargo-related demand. The cargo the charterer fixes determines the load on ancillary plant - cargo heating and the steam demand it creates, reefer containers and the electrical load, inert gas, tank cleaning and pumping, ballast exchange or treatment, and on some trades the cargo pumps and turbines as a major consumer.
  • Trading limits and voyage areas. Ice, tropical seawater temperature, high ambient, sediment-laden or biologically rich water for the ballast water treatment plant, and emission-control areas requiring compliant fuel changeover - all follow from the charterer's employment orders.
  • Time pressure on maintenance. The commercially valuable ship is the moving one. Pressure to defer immobilisation for overhauls, to shorten port stays and to postpone the withdrawal of a generator or a boiler is real and constant. It is exerted commercially, not by instruction, and it is the influence most likely to cause harm.
  • Efficiency and emissions performance. The charterer's commercial interest in the ship's rating and reported efficiency now shapes instructions on speed, routing, hull and propeller cleaning and voyage optimisation - a further reason for orders that keep the engine off its design point.

3. Where the charterer's influence is priced - warranties and off-hire

Two clauses turn the Chief Engineer's plant into money, and he should know both.

  • The description clause and the speed and consumption warranty. The owner warrants the ship's speed and fuel consumption in stated weather conditions. If she under-performs, the charterer claims the time lost and the fuel over-consumed. This is why accurate noon reports, weather data and consumption figures matter commercially as well as technically, and why a fouled hull, a slipping propeller or a mis-set engine becomes a dispute.
  • The off-hire clause. This is the one that bites on auxiliary breakdown. In the NYPE form, hire ceases for time lost by, among other things, breakdown of machinery, deficiency of men or stores, or damage preventing the full working of the vessel. The ship stops earning while she is unable to perform. A generator failure at sea that forces a speed reduction, or an immobilisation for repair, therefore converts directly into lost hire for the owner - and, where the charterparty is on a net loss of time basis, into an accounting argument about exactly how much time was lost.

That is the commercial reality behind the second half of this question. A history of unforeseen auxiliary breakdown at sea is not merely a technical nuisance; on a time charter it is a recurring off-hire exposure for the owner and a source of claims, and it is what makes the Chief Engineer's pre-voyage work valuable.

4. Taking over the ship - establishing her true condition

Nothing useful can be decided before the plant's actual state is known, and a handover is the one moment when asking every question is expected. Prior to the voyage:

  1. Take a proper handover. A written handover from the outgoing Chief Engineer, and specifically his account of each auxiliary failure - what failed, what was done, and whether the cause was ever established.
  2. Read the history rather than the last entry. The planned maintenance system - overdue jobs, jobs closed without work, running hours against overhaul intervals; the defect list and outstanding work orders; alarm and trip logs; the engine-room log for repeated abnormalities; previous superintendent and internal audit reports and any open non-conformities.
  3. Establish the class and statutory position. Survey status, conditions of class or memoranda, overdue or postponed surveys, and the status of any equipment under a continuous survey cycle. A condition of class is a constraint on what may be deferred.
  4. Look for the pattern. Repeated breakdown at sea of auxiliary machinery usually has a systemic cause rather than several coincidental ones - commonly fuel quality and treatment, cooling water chemistry or fouling, lubricating oil condition, electrical protection and load sharing, governor or automation faults, or simply maintenance not actually performed. Test the plant against each rather than repairing the last symptom.
  5. Check the consumables and the fuel actually on board. Bunker delivery notes, retained samples, the latest fuel analysis - catalytic fines above all - the condition and settings of the purifiers, filter differential pressures and the compatibility of any blend.
  6. Check spares and stores against the plant. Identification of critical equipment and the spares to support it is an ISM obligation, not a preference. Verify physical stock against the inventory, look at lead times, and identify the single points of failure with no spare on board.
  7. Check the people. Familiarisation of newly joined engineers, competence for the specific plant, watchkeeping arrangements, and readiness for unmanned machinery space operation including alarm transfer and response times.
  8. Read the charterparty extracts the company holds - the description and speed and consumption clause, the off-hire clause, the bunker specification and the trading limits - so that the technical advice given is framed in the terms that will actually be applied.

5. The options, and what each one means

Having established the condition, the realistic options range from acceptance to refusal, and the Chief Engineer should present them as a recommendation with consequences rather than as a complaint:

  1. Sail as she is, where the investigation shows the failures were addressed and the cause is understood. Legitimate only if it can be justified on evidence.
  2. Sail with mitigation and a restricted operating envelope - additional condition monitoring, more frequent inspections, redundancy preserved by running an extra generator on critical passages, load limits, avoidance of the conditions that provoked the failure. Record the restriction and tell the master and the company.
  3. Rectify before departure - carry out the outstanding work alongside or at anchorage, with maker's or specialist attendance and, where the defect touches a classed item, class attendance. Ask the company for a superintendent.
  4. Request a deferment of sailing, or of the fixture, until a specific defect is rectified. This is the option candidates avoid naming, and it is a proper one.
  5. Recommend that the vessel not proceed where the plant cannot be made fit for the intended voyage. The master's overriding authority under the ISM Code exists for exactly this, and the Chief Engineer's role is to give him the technical basis for using it.

6. Actions prior to commencement of the voyage

  • Put the assessment in writing to the Company and the DPA. A verbal concern is not a record. State the defect, the risk, the option recommended and what is needed - it is also the mechanism by which the ISM system is supposed to work, and it moves the decision to the level that can fund it.
  • Raise the outstanding items formally in the safety management system as defects or non-conformities so they are tracked rather than remembered.
  • Brief the master fully, in terms of operational consequence rather than machinery detail - what she can and cannot do, what the plant will not tolerate, and what he should not accept in an employment order.
  • Order the critical spares and press for delivery before departure; where they cannot arrive in time, say so explicitly in the written assessment.
  • Test and prove the essential systems before sailing - generators on load and in parallel, automatic start of the standby set, blackout recovery and the emergency generator, steering gear, main engine trials and reversing, the emergency fire pump, bilge and ballast systems, boiler safety devices and alarm transfer.
  • Sample and analyse the bunkers before use, and do not use a stem of doubtful compatibility on a critical passage while the result is outstanding.
  • Set the maintenance plan against the actual voyage - schedule immobilisation and heavy work for ports where it is feasible, and agree it with the master and the company in advance so it is not competing with a fixture later.
  • Bring the crew into it. Brief the engineers on the failure history and the watch response; run drills for blackout and for the specific failure mode seen before.
  • Keep the commercial and technical roles separate. The Chief Engineer advises on the ship's technical condition; he does not negotiate with the charterer, accept employment orders, or comment on hire and off-hire. Those are the master's and the owner's. Where pressure to sail is applied, record it, refer it to the company, and let the master's overriding authority operate.

7. The line the Chief Engineer must not let be crossed

A charterer may direct employment. He may not direct unsafe operation, and no charterparty instruction overrides the owner's obligation to provide a seaworthy, properly maintained ship or the master's authority to refuse. The ISM Code makes the company responsible for the ship's maintenance and for supporting the master, and gives the master overriding authority and responsibility to take any action he considers in the best interests of safety and pollution prevention. The practical discipline is simple: let the commercial pressure be answered commercially and the engineering question answered on engineering evidence, and put the engineering answer in writing before the ship sails, not after she breaks down.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two limbs, and the second is longer than it looks. The first asks for the influences of a charterer on machinery operation - a discussion. The second is a scenario with two explicit demands: state the different option you have and actions you would takes, both prior to the commencement of voyage. That is three things to head, not two, and options and actions are not the same list - options are the courses open to you, actions are what you do regardless of which you choose. Candidates who merge them lose the marks for the one they left out. The words prior to the commencement of voyage also bound the answer: describing how you would fix a generator at sea does not answer this question.

The idea that makes limb one coherent

The charterer never touches the machinery. On a time charter the owner keeps possession, crew and technical management; the charterer buys the ship's time and directs her employment. So every influence in limb one is indirect - a commercial instruction with an engineering consequence. He orders a speed, and the Chief Engineer gets prolonged low load, fouling and cold corrosion. He buys the bunkers, and the Chief Engineer gets the catalytic fines. He fixes a short-sea trade, and the Chief Engineer gets manoeuvres and starting-air demand. Saying that once, at the top, turns a shopping list into an argument - and it is also the sentence that sets up limb two, because it explains why the pre-voyage assessment is the Chief Engineer's real point of leverage.

Know the two clauses that convert engineering into money

At management level the examiner expects commercial literacy. Two clauses do the work. The speed and consumption warranty in the description clause exposes the owner to a claim for time lost and fuel over-consumed if the ship under-performs - which is why noon reports and consumption figures are commercially live documents. The off-hire clause is the one that matters for this scenario: hire ceases for time lost by breakdown of machinery, deficiency of men or stores, or damage preventing the full working of the vessel. A history of unforeseen auxiliary breakdown at sea is therefore a recurring off-hire exposure, not merely a technical nuisance, and naming that is what shows you understand why the company should fund the repairs you are about to ask for.

Options and actions - keep them apart

Set the options out as a ladder with consequences: sail as she is; sail with mitigation and a restricted operating envelope; rectify before departure with maker's or class attendance; request deferment of sailing; recommend that she not proceed. Name the last two. Candidates instinctively avoid them because they sound like refusing to work, but they are proper professional options and the master's overriding authority under the ISM Code exists precisely so that they can be exercised. Then the actions - written assessment to the Company and DPA, defects raised in the SMS, master briefed, critical spares ordered, systems proved before sailing, bunkers sampled, maintenance plan matched to the voyage, crew briefed and drilled. Those you do whichever option is chosen.

Common mistakes

  • Writing about a voyage charter or a bareboat charter. The stem says time charter, and the division of responsibility is what the question turns on.
  • Saying the charterer instructs the Chief Engineer on machinery. He does not - he directs employment, and the machinery consequences follow.
  • Merging options and actions into one list when the stem asks for both.
  • Answering what you would do at sea when it breaks down. The stem says prior to the commencement of voyage.
  • Omitting off-hire entirely. It is the clause that makes auxiliary breakdown expensive and it is the commercial hinge of the scenario.
  • Treating each past breakdown as an isolated event instead of looking for the systemic cause - fuel, cooling, lube oil, electrical protection, automation, or maintenance not actually done.
  • Forgetting conditions of class and survey status, which constrain what may legitimately be deferred.
  • Not putting anything in writing. A verbal concern is not a record and does not move the decision to the company.
  • Straying into the commercial role - negotiating with the charterer, or opining on hire. That is the master's and the owner's ground.
  • Inventing a regulation that governs charterer's instructions. There is none; the constraints are the charterparty, the ISM Code and the owner's obligation to provide a seaworthy ship.

Examiner traps

"The charterer has ordered slow steaming. Can you refuse?" Not as a commercial order - speed is employment. But you advise on the technical consequences, agree load limits and countermeasures, and if prolonged low load would endanger the plant or the ship you say so in writing and the master decides. "The charterer's bunkers are off-specification. Whose problem?" The charterparty allocates it, but the immediate engineering decision is yours - do not burn a doubtful stem on a critical passage while the analysis is outstanding. "The company says sail, you say the generator is unfit." Put the technical assessment in writing to the Company and the DPA and brief the master; the ISM Code gives the master overriding authority. "Is a generator breakdown off-hire?" Under a typical NYPE off-hire clause, time lost by breakdown of machinery preventing the full working of the vessel is - but it is the master's and owner's ground to argue, not yours.

Uncertainty and provenance

Most of this question has no regulatory source, and that is stated rather than disguised. No instrument governs what a charterer may instruct - the constraints are the charterparty, which is a commercial contract between owner and charterer, the owner's obligation to provide a seaworthy ship, and the ISM Code. MIW's evidence-declaration standard governs the charterparty and engineering limbs: the mechanisms are explained, the provenance class is declared, and no regulation has been manufactured to govern charterer's instructions.

Charterparty terms are described generically and by function. NYPE and the other standard forms exist in several editions with materially different wording, and their terms are amended by rider clauses in every fixture. No clause number is cited and no charterparty wording is quoted; the employment clause, the speed and consumption warranty and the off-hire clause are described by what they do. Model written answer

1. What the Merchant Shipping Act means by an unseaworthy ship

The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 carries no definition of unseaworthiness in its definitions section. The description sits inside section 334 itself, which treats a ship as unseaworthy where the following are not such as to render her in every respect fit for the proposed voyage or service:

  • the materials of which she is made and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, boilers and machinery.

Two features carry the marks. The test is relative - fitness for the proposed voyage or service - so a ship adequate for a sheltered coastal run may be unseaworthy for an ocean passage. And it is far wider than material condition: an undermanned ship, one whose officers hold the wrong certificates, or a mechanically sound ship with badly stowed cargo is unseaworthy under the Act. Section 335 implies into every contract of service an obligation on the owner to use all reasonable means to make and keep her seaworthy, notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary; section 336 gives the separate power to detain an unsafe ship.

2. Port State control, and what a deficiency is

Detention is not a power of the Merchant Shipping Act working abroad. It is port State control - the inspection of foreign ships in national ports, conferred by the conventions themselves (SOLAS regulation I/19, Load Lines article 21, MARPOL articles 5 and 6, STCW article X) and operated through regional memoranda of understanding. The procedural detail is the IMO Procedures for Port State Control, 2023, resolution A.1185(33), which is the edition in force at this sitting.

  • Deficiency (1.7.3) - a condition found not to be in compliance with the requirements of the relevant convention. Most deficiencies are simply recorded for rectification.
  • Detention (1.7.4) - intervention action taken by the port State when the condition of the ship or its crew does not correspond substantially with the relevant conventions, to ensure she does not sail until she can do so without danger to the ship or persons on board or unreasonable threat of harm to the environment.
  • Substandard ship (1.7.11) - one whose hull, machinery, equipment or operational safety is substantially below the standards required, or whose crew does not conform to the safe manning document.

3. What makes a deficiency detainable

A detainable deficiency is therefore not a category of defect but a judgement about consequence. Appendix 2 to the Procedures supplies the test. The PSCO applies two criteria (3.1): a ship unsafe to proceed to sea is detained on the first inspection whatever her stay in port; and the re-inspection criterion - she is detained if the deficiencies are serious enough to merit the PSCO returning to satisfy himself they are rectified before she sails. The need to return is what classifies the seriousness.

The PSCO then assesses whether the ship has valid documentation and the crew required by the safe manning document (3.3), and whether ship and crew are able, throughout the forthcoming voyage, to (3.4):

  • navigate safely; safely handle, carry and monitor the cargo;
  • operate the engine-room safely; maintain proper propulsion and steering;
  • fight fires effectively in any part of the ship; abandon ship speedily and safely and effect rescue;
  • prevent pollution of the environment; maintain adequate stability; maintain adequate watertight integrity;
  • communicate in distress situations; and provide safe and healthy conditions on board.

If any of these is answered negatively the ship should be strongly considered for detention, and a combination of less serious deficiencies may also warrant it. Appendix 2 section 5 then lists examples grouped by convention - under SOLAS these include failure of proper operation of propulsion and essential machinery, of the emergency generator, of main and auxiliary steering gear, and non-implementation of the enhanced survey programme. The list is expressly not exhaustive; it illustrates the judgement rather than replacing it. The one exception is STCW, where the listed deficiencies are the only grounds for detention.

4. Serious structural deficiencies in particular

Structural defects - wastage, fractured or detached members, corroded hatch covers, failed watertight boundaries - bear directly on three of the eleven tests: maintain adequate stability, maintain adequate watertight integrity, and the safe carriage of cargo. They are also the class least likely to be repairable alongside, which is what shapes the release procedure. Where the ship is a bulk carrier or oil tanker the enhanced survey programme under SOLAS regulation XI-1/2 and the 2011 ESP Code is the survey regime the PSCO will test the structure against, and Appendix 6 of the Procedures governs the more detailed structural inspection.

5. Securing release where the repair can be done in the port

The governing section is 3.7, Procedures for rectification of deficiencies and release. The PSCO should endeavour to secure the rectification of all deficiencies detected (3.7.1). Where a deficiency is clearly hazardous to safety or the environment, the hazard must be removed before the ship is allowed to proceed to sea (3.7.2). The practical sequence is:

  1. Obtain the inspection report - Form A and Form B (appendix 13) - so the deficiency, the convention requirement relied on and the action code are all in writing.
  2. Notify at once the company, the flag State Administration and the recognized organization which issued the certificates. Under 3.5.2 the PSCO will himself have notified the flag State and the RO in writing.
  3. Agree a repair specification and a survey attendance with class and, where required, the flag State; carry out the repair.
  4. Request re-inspection. The PSCO verifies the rectification and, if satisfied, lifts the detention.
  5. The flag State, the RO and the parties notified of the detention are notified in writing of the release (3.5.2, 4.1.3), the release record including ship's name, IMO number, date and time of release and a copy of Form B.
  6. On receiving the detention report the flag State informs IMO of the remedial action taken, electronically through GISIS or in the form of appendix 17 (4.2.1).

6. Where the repair cannot be done in the port

This is the route that matters for serious structural damage, and it is paragraph 3.7.3. Where the deficiencies which caused the detention cannot be remedied in the port of inspection, the port State authority may allow the ship to proceed to the nearest appropriate repair yard available - chosen by the master and agreed by that authority - provided the conditions agreed between the port State and the flag State are complied with. Those conditions must ensure she does not sail until she can proceed without risk to the safety of passengers or crew, or to other ships, or unreasonable threat of harm to the environment, and may include confirmation from the flag State that remedial action has been taken. The authority notifies the next port of call, the parties in 4.1.4 and any other appropriate authority, using the form in appendix 14; the receiving authority replies using appendix 15.

Two sanctions close the route. If she sails without complying with the agreed conditions, the port State immediately alerts the next port, the flag State and all other authorities it considers appropriate (3.7.5). If she does not call at the nominated repair port, the repair port's authority alerts the flag State and the detaining port State, which may take further action (3.7.6). A conditional-release voyage is a permission with terms, not a release; the detention is discharged only when the repair is completed and accepted.

7. What the Chief Engineer does, and the position on appeal

  • Do not argue the finding on deck. Ask which convention requirement each deficiency rests on and get it recorded; photograph and preserve the evidence before anything is disturbed.
  • Inform the Company and the DPA immediately - the ISM Code makes the response a company obligation, and only the company can commit to a repair plan or lodge an appeal.
  • Work rectification and the release route in parallel, and where a repair-yard voyage is contemplated prepare the master's choice of yard, the voyage conditions and the class attendance in advance.
  • Right of appeal - paragraph 2.3.11. It belongs to the company or its representative, not to the Chief Engineer personally; the appeal does not suspend the detention; and the PSCO must properly inform the master that it exists. Rectification, not appeal, is what gets the ship away.
  • Undue delay - SOLAS I/19 and MARPOL article 7(2) give a right to compensation if a ship is unduly detained or delayed. That is a claim pursued afterwards, and a different remedy from appeal. Neither releases the ship.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem sets three tasks and they are not equal in weight. Define is one, explain is one, and describe the procedure is the third and the largest - it is the only limb with a command verb demanding a sequence. A common failure is to spend half the answer on section 334 because it is the part most easily recalled, and then to give the release procedure four lines. Budget roughly a quarter, a third and the remainder. Heading each limb visibly is worth marks on its own: the examiner is looking for three answers, not one essay.

The hinge the question turns on

There are two different legal systems in this question and the answer must not blur them. Unseaworthiness under section 334 is Indian domestic law applying to an Indian ship, and its consequence is a criminal offence - it looks backwards and punishes. Detention under port State control is international law applied by any port State to a foreign ship, and its consequence is an administrative intervention - it looks forwards and prevents. They overlap in substance and differ completely in machinery. A candidate who writes that a PSCO detains a ship 'because she is unseaworthy within the meaning of the M.S. Act' has merged two regimes; the PSCO applies the conventions, not the Indian Act. Saying that clearly is what separates a good answer from a recited one.

Why a deficiency list is not the answer to limb two

The instinct is to reproduce the Appendix 2 list and stop. But the Procedures say plainly that the list is not exhaustive and is intended to give examples, and that a combination of deficiencies of a less serious nature may also warrant detention. The operative idea is the eleven capability tests in Appendix 2 paragraph 3.4 - can this ship and this crew, over this forthcoming voyage, still do the eleven things a ship must do? A deficiency becomes detainable when the honest answer to one of those is no. Give the test first and the examples second, and the limb reads as understanding rather than recall. The single exception is worth a sentence: under STCW the listed deficiencies are the only grounds for detention, which is the reverse of the general rule.

Why structural damage almost always goes to 3.7.3

Because steel repair needs a yard. A defective quick-closing valve or a failed emergency generator can usually be put right alongside and the ship re-inspected the same day. Cracked frames, wasted side shell or a failed watertight boundary generally cannot, and paragraph 3.7.2 forbids sailing while a clearly hazardous deficiency remains. That is precisely the gap 3.7.3 exists to fill, and the examiner's phrase timely release is pointing at it. Note what the paragraph actually balances: the master chooses the yard, the port State agrees it, and the flag State sets the conditions with the port State. Three parties, each with a distinct role - getting those roles the right way round is a mark in itself.

Common mistakes

  • Citing the wrong Indian statute. The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is the operative law at this sitting - see the temporal note below.
  • Citing the wrong edition of the Procedures. They are re-adopted periodically and each edition revokes the last. The edition in force at this sitting is A.1185(33), Procedures for Port State Control, 2023, adopted 6 December 2023.
  • Treating detainable deficiency as a fixed list rather than a judgement about whether the ship can still be operated safely.
  • Confusing unseaworthy (s.334, an offence) with unsafe (s.336, a power to detain). Every unsafe ship is unseaworthy; the converse does not hold.
  • Saying the appeal suspends the detention. It does not, and 2.3.11 says so expressly.
  • Giving the Chief Engineer the right of appeal. It belongs to the company or its representative.
  • Forgetting that release is notified in writing to the same parties who were told of the detention, and that the flag State reports the remedial action to IMO through GISIS. The paperwork is part of the procedure, not an afterthought.
  • Omitting the master's choice of repair yard - candidates routinely write that the port State directs the ship to a yard.

Understand this first

Ordinary law says that if you are owed money you sue the person who owes it. Shipping cannot work that way: the debtor may be a single-ship company in a jurisdiction you will never reach, and the only asset is a ship that will sail tomorrow. Everything else in this question - the priority list, the arrest, the forced sale - is the machinery for turning a ship into money and then deciding who gets paid out of it, in what order.

Model written answer

1. What a maritime lien is

A maritime lien is a privileged claim against the vessel herself rather than against any person. It is not created by agreement and it is not registered: it attaches to the ship by operation of law at the moment the claim arises, and it does so without notice to anyone. Until it is enforced it is inchoate - a right in the ship that lies dormant; it is crystallised by arrest and realised by forced sale. Its practical significance is its rank: a maritime lien takes priority over a registered mortgage, even one registered earlier and for a far larger sum.

That combination - secret, unregistered, automatic and senior - is what distinguishes it from an ordinary possessory lien, which depends on holding the goods and is lost when possession is given up.

2. How the lien travels with the property

This is the defining characteristic, and the Convention states it in one sentence. Article 8: the maritime liens follow the vessel, notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag.

  • The lien is attached to the res - the ship - and not to the owner, so selling the ship does not shed it.
  • It binds a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. A buyer may acquire a ship with unpaid crew wages or a salvage award still charged against her and have no means of discovering it from any register. This is why it is called a secret lien and why prudent buyers take indemnities and searches.
  • Reflagging and re-registration make no difference: the lien travels with the hull, not with the certificate.
  • It is defeated in only two ways - by a forced sale complying with article 12, which sweeps the ship clean of liens and encumbrances, or by lapse of time under article 9.

Under article 9 the article 4 liens are extinguished after one year unless, before then, the vessel has been arrested or seized and that arrest or seizure leads to a forced sale. Time runs from the claimant's discharge from the vessel for wage claims and from the moment the claim arises for the others, and it runs continuously, without suspension or interruption.

3. In rem and in personam

  • An action in personam is brought against a person - the owner, charterer, manager or operator. The claimant must find that person, establish jurisdiction over them, and then enforce against whatever assets they have. If the defendant is a single-ship company with no other assets, a judgment in personam may be worth nothing.
  • An action in rem is brought against the ship herself, which is named as defendant. The court's jurisdiction is founded on the presence of the res within the jurisdiction, and the ship may be arrested.

The connection between the two limbs of this question is the point the examiner is looking for: the maritime lien is what gives the claimant a right in rem against the ship. Because the lien attaches to the vessel and follows her, the claimant can proceed against the vessel wherever she is found and whoever now owns her.

Arrest is not usually an end in itself. It is a coercive step: it either compels the provision of security - a P&I club letter of undertaking or a bank guarantee - so that the ship may trade on, or, if no security is forthcoming, it produces a fund through forced sale out of which the claims are paid. The two forms of action are not alternatives and may be pursued together.

4. Which convention deals with maritime liens

The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993, adopted at Geneva on 6 May 1993 and in force from 5 September 2004. It is the successor to the earlier Brussels Conventions of 1926 and 1967 on the same subject, neither of which achieved wide acceptance. Its stated purpose is to improve the conditions for ship financing and the development of national merchant fleets - which explains its structure: it protects mortgagees by making mortgages recognisable and enforceable across States, and it does so by defining exhaustively the small class of claims that outrank them.

5. What the convention secures - article 4

Article 4(1) secures five claims, against the owner, demise charterer, manager or operator, by a maritime lien on the vessel:

  • (a) claims for wages and other sums due to the master, officers and other members of the vessel's complement in respect of their employment on the vessel, including costs of repatriation and social insurance contributions payable on their behalf;
  • (b) claims in respect of loss of life or personal injury, whether on land or on water, in direct connection with the operation of the vessel;
  • (c) claims for reward for the salvage of the vessel;
  • (d) claims for port, canal and other waterway dues and pilotage dues;
  • (e) claims based on tort arising out of physical loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel, other than loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects carried on the vessel.

Article 4(2) removes two categories from limbs (b) and (e): damage from the carriage of oil or other hazardous or noxious substances for which compensation is payable under regimes providing strict liability and compulsory insurance, and damage from the radioactive properties of nuclear fuel, products or waste. The reasoning is that those claimants already have a dedicated fund, and there is no need to give them a lien as well.

Note what is not a maritime lien: cargo claims, and the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers and agents. They may be maritime claims founding an action in rem, but they carry no lien under article 4.

6. How the liens rank among themselves - article 5

  • The article 4 liens take priority over registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges, and no other claim takes priority over either, subject to article 12(3) and (4).
  • They rank in the order listed in article 4 - so wages before personal injury, before salvage, before dues, before tort.
  • But salvage liens jump the queue: a lien securing a claim for salvage reward takes priority over all other maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The logic is obvious - the salvor preserved the very fund the others are claiming against.
  • Liens within each of (a), (b), (d) and (e) rank pari passu among themselves.
  • Salvage liens rank in the inverse order of time in which the claims accrued - the later salvage ranks first - each claim being deemed to accrue on the date the salvage operation terminated.

7. The order of settlement after forced sale - article 12

A forced sale in a State Party sweeps the vessel clean: all registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges, except those assumed by the purchaser with the holders' consent, and all liens and other encumbrances of whatsoever nature, cease to attach to the vessel, provided the ship was within that State's jurisdiction and the sale complied with its law and with articles 11 and 12. The proceeds are then distributed in this order:

  • 1. The costs and expenses of the arrest or seizure and of the subsequent sale, paid first out of the proceeds. These expressly include the costs of upkeep of the vessel and the crew, and the wages and other sums under article 4(1)(a) incurred from the time of the arrest or seizure.
  • 2. Wreck-removal costs, where a State Party has so provided. Article 12(3) permits a State to provide that, where a stranded or sunken vessel has been removed by a public authority in the interest of safe navigation or protection of the marine environment, those removal costs are paid before all other claims secured by a maritime lien. This is optional, not automatic.
  • 3. The article 4 maritime liens, in their article 5 order and subject to the salvage rules.
  • 4. The right of retention of a shipbuilder or shiprepairer under article 7. Article 12(4) requires him to surrender possession to the purchaser, but entitles him to be satisfied out of the proceeds after the article 4 lienholders.
  • 5. Registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges complying with article 1.
  • 6. Other maritime liens granted under national law under article 6, which rank after both the article 4 liens and the registered mortgages.
  • 7. The residue, if any, to the owner, and States Parties must ensure the proceeds are actually available and freely transferable.

The purchaser is then entitled to a certificate that the vessel is sold free of encumbrances, on production of which the registrar must delete the registered mortgages and charges and either register the vessel in the purchaser's name or issue a certificate of deregistration.

8. The Indian statutory position, kept separate

In India the subject is governed domestically by the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 (No. 22 of 2017, assented 9 August 2017), which consolidated admiralty jurisdiction and vested it in the High Courts. Its scheme closely follows the Convention, and the differences are worth knowing:

  • Section 9(1) lists the same five maritime liens in the same order, with two widenings: limb (c) covers salvage including special compensation, and limb (d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel.
  • Section 9(2) reproduces the travelling rule - the lien continues notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag - and the one-year extinction, but with a proviso giving TWO years for wage claims under limb (a), running from the date the wage, repatriation cost or social insurance contribution fell due. That is more generous to seafarers than the Convention.
  • Section 10 sets the order of priority of maritime claims in an admiralty proceeding as: (a) claims where there is a maritime lien; (b) registered mortgages and charges of the same nature; (c) all other claims - with claims in a single category ranking equally, and salvage claims in inverse order of time.

(The stem asks about the convention, and the answer above is given at convention level. The Indian Act is set out separately because it is the law an Indian court would apply, and it should not be conflated with the Convention.)

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains five separate demands in sixteen marks - define the lien, explain how it travels, explain in rem and in personam, name the convention, and give the order of settlement. That is roughly three marks each, so none of them deserves an essay and none may be skipped. The two that candidates most often lose are the last: they name the convention vaguely, or give a half-remembered priority list. The order of settlement is the single most concrete thing in the question and it is the easiest to prepare, so it should be written out as a numbered list, not narrated.

The idea that links the whole question

The lien and the action in rem are two halves of one mechanism. The maritime lien attaches to the ship rather than to a person; that is precisely what allows the claimant to sue the ship rather than a person. If you state that link explicitly - the lien is what gives the right in rem - the middle three demands of the question join up instead of reading as three unrelated definitions.

Why the lien is called secret, and why that matters commercially

There is no register of maritime liens. A buyer inspecting the ship's registry sees mortgages and charges but cannot see unpaid crew wages, an outstanding salvage award or a personal injury claim - and under article 8 all of those follow the vessel through a sale, a change of registry and a change of flag. So a purchaser can take delivery of a ship already encumbered by claims ranking ahead of the mortgage financing the purchase. This is why sale contracts carry indemnities and why a forced sale under article 12 is commercially valuable: it is the one transaction that delivers a genuinely clean ship, which is exactly why an arresting creditor's remedy has teeth.

The two salvage rules - state both

Salvage is the exception to almost every ranking rule in the Convention and both exceptions are examinable.

  • Salvage jumps ahead of earlier liens. A salvage lien takes priority over all maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The salvor preserved the fund everyone else is claiming against, so he is paid first out of it.
  • Salvage liens rank in inverse order of time. Where there is more than one salvage, the later ranks ahead of the earlier - each claim accruing on the date its salvage operation terminated. Every other category ranks pari passu.

What is NOT a maritime lien

A quick way to show command of article 4 is to say what it leaves out. Cargo claims are not secured by a maritime lien - article 4(1)(e) expressly excludes loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects. Nor are the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers, agents and chandlers. These may still be maritime claims supporting an action in rem and an arrest, but they carry no lien and therefore rank behind registered mortgages. The distinction between a maritime claim and a maritime lien is exactly what the priority list is about.

The Indian dimension - and the statement to avoid

The examiner asks about the convention, so answer at convention level. The Indian statute is the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, and giving it separately is a strength: its section 9 reproduces the five liens and their order, and its section 10 gives the three-tier priority - liens, then registered mortgages, then all other claims.

Two genuine Indian divergences are worth knowing. Section 9(1)(c) covers salvage including special compensation, and section 9(1)(d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel. Most importantly, section 9(2) gives seafarers two years for wage claims where the Convention gives one - more generous than the Convention.

What to avoid: do not assert whether or not India is a party to the 1993 Convention. It is not necessary to answer any part of this question, and a confident statement either way is a risk for no reward. Say what the Convention provides, say what the Indian Act provides, and let the two stand side by side.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a maritime claim with a maritime lien. Every lien is a claim; very few claims are liens.
  • Saying a maritime lien is lost on sale of the ship. It is not - article 8 is the whole point of the question's second limb.
  • Treating in rem and in personam as alternatives. They may be pursued together.
  • Putting mortgages ahead of maritime liens. Article 5 puts the liens first, which is the commercially surprising and therefore examinable point.
  • Forgetting that arrest and sale costs and post-arrest crew wages are paid before everything, including the liens.
  • Omitting the salvage inverse-order rule, or applying inverse order to all categories rather than only to salvage.
  • Giving a mortgage a rank ahead of the shipbuilder's or shiprepairer's right of retention. Article 12(4) puts retention after the article 4 liens but the Convention leaves mortgages behind both.

Examiner traps

"You buy a ship with a clean registry search. Can you still be caught?" Yes. Maritime liens are unregistered and follow the vessel through a change of ownership, registration and flag. Only a forced sale under article 12, or the lapse of time under article 9, clears them.

"Crew wages or the bank - who is paid first?" Crew. Article 4(1)(a) is the first maritime lien and article 5(1) puts all article 4 liens ahead of registered mortgages. And the wages incurred after arrest are paid even before that, as part of the article 12(2) costs.

"Two salvage services, one in January and one in June. Who ranks first?" June - salvage liens rank in inverse order of time.

"Is a bunker supplier's claim a maritime lien under the Convention?" No. It may be a maritime claim founding an action in rem, but article 4 does not secure it, so it ranks behind registered mortgages.

Q9 Maritime Lien, In Rem and In Personam

In India, the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, sections 9 and 10.
International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993 - adopted Geneva 6 May 1993, in force 5 September 2004		 Confusing a maritime claim with a maritime lien. Bunker suppliers, repairers and agents have maritime claims which will found an action in rem and an arrest, but they are not secured by a maritime lien under article 4, so they rank behind registered mortgages - the opposite of where candidates usually put them.
