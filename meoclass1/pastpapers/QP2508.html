Understand this first

Limiting liability is not the same as insuring against it, and the two are constantly confused. LLMC does not pay anybody. It caps what a claimant can recover from the shipowner or salvor, whatever the true size of the loss, and it does so by reference to tonnage - a measure that has nothing to do with fault, but which can be known in advance and therefore insured. That predictability is the commercial reason the regime exists. The second idea to hold before reading the limbs is that LLMC is the general regime: where a specialised convention brought its own limits it displaces LLMC, and where it brought none it borrows LLMC's.

Model written answer

1. (a) The three principles the LLMC regime is built on

The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 was adopted on 19 November 1976 and entered into force on 1 December 1986. It rests on three organising ideas.

  1. A right to limit that is, in practice, unbreakable. Article 1 gives it to shipowners, charterers, managers and operators, to salvors, to those for whose acts they answer, and to their insurers. Article 4 removes it only for that person's personal act or omission, committed with intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result. Because the fault must be personal, negligent navigation by the master does not break it.
  2. The limit is fixed by tonnage, not by fault and not by the size of the loss. Article 6 computes a global amount from gross tonnage, with a higher tranche for loss of life and personal injury than for property; Article 7 deals separately with passenger claims.
  3. One fund per occurrence, distributed rateably. Articles 11 to 13 allow a single limitation fund; claimants are confined to it and rank rateably, and other assets are protected from arrest for those claims.

2. How LLMC links to the other liability conventions

LLMC is the general, residual regime, and the specialised conventions relate to it in two opposite ways.

  • Conventions with their own limits stand outside it. The CLC 1992 creates strict, channelled liability on the registered owner for tanker oil pollution, with its own limits, compulsory insurance and direct action. LLMC therefore expressly excepts CLC oil pollution claims at Article 3.
  • Conventions with no limits of their own borrow LLMC's. The Bunkers Convention 2001 imposes strict liability but sets no limits; its Article 6 preserves the right to limit under the applicable regime, in practice LLMC. The Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention 2007 does the same at Article 10(2).
  • So a bunker spill and a wreck removal limit in the LLMC fund; a tanker cargo spill is dealt with under CLC. States may, however, reserve against LLMC applying to wreck removal.

3. (b) The liabilities that are subject to limitation

Article 2 lists the limitable claims, whatever the basis of liability may be:

  • loss of life or personal injury, and loss of or damage to property, occurring on board or in direct connexion with the operation of the ship or with salvage operations, including consequential loss;
  • loss resulting from delay in the carriage of cargo, passengers or their luggage;
  • loss resulting from infringement of rights other than contractual rights in direct connexion with those operations;
  • raising, removal, destruction or rendering harmless of a wrecked or sunken ship and its cargo, subject to reservation;
  • claims by a person other than the person liable for measures taken to avert or minimise loss.

4. The claims left outside, and the conduct that breaks the limit

  • Article 3 excepts claims for salvage and for contribution in general average - a salvor's earned award cannot itself be cut down by the regime;
  • CLC oil pollution damage and nuclear damage, each having its own regime;
  • claims by servants of the shipowner or salvor where the law governing the contract of service does not permit limitation;
  • and under Article 4 the limit falls away on proof of the person's own intentional or reckless conduct with knowledge that such loss would probably result - a burden on the claimant, and very rarely discharged.

5. (c) The principles of the Salvage Convention 1989

The International Convention on Salvage, 1989 was adopted on 28 April 1989 and entered into force on 14 July 1996, replacing the Brussels Convention of 1910. Its principles are:

  • salvage is a voluntary service to property in danger, outside any pre-existing duty, and no cure, no pay is the base rule - a reward is earned only by a useful result;
  • the reward is fixed on the Article 13 criteria - salved value, the skill and efforts of the salvors in preventing or minimising environmental damage, measure of success, nature and degree of danger, time and expenses, risk of liability, promptness, availability of vessels and equipment - and may not exceed the salved value;
  • Article 14 special compensation corrects the blind spot in the 1910 regime: where the vessel threatened environmental damage and the salvor earned no reward, or a lesser one, the owner pays his expenses plus up to 30% where such damage was minimised or prevented, which a tribunal may raise to 100% of expenses if fair and just;
  • Article 8 imposes duties both ways - due care and best endeavours to prevent or minimise environmental damage on the salvor, and full co-operation on owner and master. Commercially the regime is worked through Lloyd's Open Form, with SCOPIC as a tariff-rate alternative to assessing Article 14.

6. Why salvors may limit, and the limit that applies

The rationale is a specific failure of the earlier regime. In The Tojo Maru a salvage company's diver, working in the water and not on board the salvage tug, negligently caused an explosion in the casualty. Under the 1957 Convention a salvor could limit only when operating from a vessel and where the act was done on board or in her navigation or management; the diver's act was neither, so the salvor faced unlimited liability.

LLMC 1976 answered it directly. Article 1 makes salvors persons entitled to limit, and Article 1(3) defines a salvor broadly as any person rendering services in direct connexion with salvage operations - which covers the diver in the water. Because such a salvor may have no ship at all, Article 6(4) supplies a notional figure: his limits are calculated according to a tonnage of 1,500 tons.

That deemed tonnage falls in the lowest band, not exceeding 2,000 gross tonnage. In a State party to the 1996 Protocol as amended by the 2012 amendments, in force 8 June 2015, the applicable limits are 3.02 million SDR for loss of life or personal injury and 1.51 million SDR for property claims. A State bound only by the unamended 1976 Convention applies lower figures, so the instrument in force for that State must be identified before any figure is quoted. India is a party through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 - the Act in force at the date of this examination - the Protocol and its amendments being given effect by rules made in 2015 and 2017.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints 5 + 5 + 6 and the route puts two headings under each limb, so an examiner marking limb by limb can see all three answered without hunting. The commonest way to lose marks here is to write a single essay about limitation of liability in which limb (c) never gets a separate treatment, and the specific figure asked for never appears at all.

Limitation is not insurance, and not liability

These three are constantly confused and the distinction is worth holding firmly. Liability asks whether you must pay. Insurance asks who reimburses you when you do. Limitation asks how much you must pay, whatever the true size of the loss - and LLMC pays nobody. It caps recovery from the shipowner or salvor by reference to tonnage, an arbitrary but predictable measure chosen precisely because it can be known in advance and insured against. That predictability is the commercial point of the whole regime: without it, marine risk could not be written.

The architecture in one idea

There is a general regime and there are specialised ones, and the exam-worthy question is always which applies. If the specialised convention brought its own limits - CLC - it displaces LLMC and LLMC excepts it. If the specialised convention created strict liability but brought no limits - Bunkers, Nairobi - it points back at LLMC. Get that one sentence right and limb (a) is largely answered.

Common mistakes

  • Listing conventions by name without saying how any of them links to LLMC, which is what limb (a) actually asks.
  • Saying the right to limit is lost by negligence. It is lost only on the person's own intentional or reckless conduct with knowledge - not the master's navigation.
  • Forgetting that salvage and general average are excepted from limitation altogether, which is the neat counterpoint to limb (c).
  • Quoting a limit figure without saying which instrument the State is bound by. The unamended 1976 figures are lower.
  • Treating special compensation under Article 14 as a second reward. It is compensation for expenses, and it exists because no cure, no pay would otherwise pay nothing for preventing pollution.
  • Omitting the deemed tonnage of 1,500 in Article 6(4), which is the hinge of the last part of limb (c).

Examiner traps

  • 'Three main principles' - the examiner has fixed the number. Give three, clearly separated.
  • 'linkage with two other IMO Liability and compensation Conventions' - two, and the linkage must be explained, not merely named.
  • 'specify the current liability limit applicable to them' - a figure is being asked for. An answer that discusses salvors' limitation in principle and never states 3.02 and 1.51 million SDR has not answered the question.
  • The printed comma splice - 'as established by the IMO, Furthermore, examine' - is on the paper. It joins two demands; answer both.

Understand this first

CII is not a cap on emissions and not a trading scheme. It is an efficiency ratio - carbon per unit of transport work - turned into a public letter grade, and almost everything interesting about it follows from those two facts. Because it is a ratio, it can be improved by increasing the denominator rather than reducing emissions, which is where the shortcomings come from. Because it is a public grade attached to an asset, its real force is commercial rather than regulatory: the regulation itself imposes no fine, but a charterer, a bank and a buyer all read the letter. Keep those two ideas separate and both limbs of the question answer themselves.

Model written answer

1. a) What CII is, and where it sits in MARPOL

The Carbon Intensity Indicator is the operational half of the IMO's short-term GHG measure, imposed by MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28, 'Operational carbon intensity'. It applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above - the threshold of the IMO Data Collection System under regulation 27, on whose fuel data it runs. It must be distinguished from EEXI, a one-off design index applying from 400 gt: EEXI asks what the ship is capable of, CII what she actually did last year.

2. How attained and required CII are set, and how a ship is rated

  • The ship calculates an attained annual operational CII - CO2 per unit of transport work over the calendar year. For most cargo ship types this is the AER, CO2 divided by deadweight capacity multiplied by distance sailed; cruise and ro-pax types use a different metric.
  • The required annual operational CII is the 2019 reference line for that ship type and size reduced by a factor Z: required CII = reference value x (1 - Z/100). Z is 5% for 2023, 7% for 2024, 9% for 2025 and 11% for 2026, and was extended by resolution MEPC.400(83) of 11 April 2025 to 13.625%, 16.250%, 18.875% and 21.500% for 2027 to 2030.
  • The attained value is verified annually and the ship rated A to E, C being the required standard, the rating recorded with the Statement of Compliance issued under the DCS.
  • The factors were set so that, in combination with the rest of Annex VI, shipping reduces CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 compared with 2008.

3. What follows from a poor rating

There is no fine and no detention. The consequence is administrative: a ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E for one year, must develop a corrective action plan showing how the required index of C or above will be achieved. It is incorporated in SEEMP Part III and agreed before the Statement of Compliance is issued, so a ship that ignores it cannot keep her documentation in order. Incentives to A- and B-rated ships are encouraged.

4. The commercial impact on the shipping business

The commercial effect far exceeds the regulatory sanction, because the rating is a public, comparable letter grade on an asset:

  • Chartering. Charterers screen tonnage on rating and some will not fix D or E ships. Because a time charterer controls speed and routing - the very things that set the rating - CII clauses are now standard, notably the BIMCO CII Operations Clause 2022. Who bears a bad rating has become a negotiated term.
  • Operational control shifts. Owners reserve rights over speed, hull and propeller cleaning and voyage optimisation, so slow steaming becomes contractual - with the machinery consequences of prolonged low-load running that follow.
  • Finance. Lenders under the Poseidon Principles report portfolio climate alignment, so rating feeds into the cost and availability of debt.
  • Asset value. A persistent D or E shortens commercial life, depresses second-hand values and brings forward retrofit or scrapping decisions.
  • Cargo owners. Shippers reporting their own scope 3 emissions demand rating data, so pressure comes from the cargo side as well as the regulatory side.

5. b) The shortcomings the industry has identified

  • It rewards capacity, not cargo. The AER denominator is deadweight capacity times distance, not cargo carried, so a ship can improve her rating by sailing further with less on board while absolute emissions rise - a genuine perverse incentive.
  • Waiting is punished, sailing is not. Idling at anchor adds fuel but no distance. Port congestion and 'sail fast then wait' lie outside the ship's control, and the incentive to keep moving can increase emissions.
  • Trade patterns are penalised structurally. Short-sea, multi-parcel chemical and product, offshore and ice-class vessels and ships with long port stays are disadvantaged by a metric built around long laden passages, however efficiently run.
  • Responsibility and control are split. The rating attaches to the ship and the owner's documentation, but a time charterer determines speed, route, waiting and cargo intake.
  • It is an efficiency ratio used as a climate instrument. Being indexed rather than absolute, improving average CII does not itself guarantee a fall in total emissions.

6. The remedial measures adopted, and how far the review had got

The regulation anticipated this. Regulation 28.11 requires a review of the CII requirements to be completed by 1 January 2026, covering effectiveness, reinforced corrective action, enforcement, the data collection system, and revision of the Z factor and reference values.

  • Phase 1 was completed at MEPC 83 in April 2025: implementation experience was gathered and analysed and a list of challenges and gaps identified.
  • Resolution MEPC.400(83), adopted 11 April 2025, amended the G3 Guidelines to set the 2027 to 2030 reduction factors, aligning them with the 2023 GHG Strategy's carbon-intensity ambition.
  • The SEEMP Guidelines were also amended to allow other CII metrics to be developed at future sessions - the door through which a cargo-based metric would have to come.
  • The industry's own remedies have been contractual - the BIMCO clause - and operational, through just-in-time arrival and virtual arrival schemes aimed at waiting time.

The honest position at this sitting is therefore this. The shortcomings had been formally acknowledged and the review was under way but not complete, with its regulation 28.11 deadline of 1 January 2026 still ahead. What had actually been decided was an extension of the reduction factors to 2030 and an amendment permitting alternative metrics to be developed. The metric itself - the source of most of the criticism - had not been changed, and the remaining stages of the review were still to come.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) carries 10 of the 16 marks and two-thirds of candidates spend all of it on the formula. The four steps force the answer up the ladder from instrument to money, and the commercial half is where the differentiating marks are. Limb (b) is short and is best answered as mechanism then remedy, because a list of complaints without the mechanism that causes them reads as grumbling rather than analysis.

Why an indexed measure behaves this way

Any ratio can be improved by growing the denominator instead of shrinking the numerator. CII's denominator is transport work, and transport work was defined using deadweight capacity because capacity is a known, verifiable number that appears on every certificate, whereas cargo actually carried is commercially sensitive, unevenly recorded and open to manipulation. The choice was made for auditability, and the perverse incentives are the price of that choice. This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it explains why the fix is hard: a cargo-based metric would need a trustworthy, global, verifiable source of cargo data that does not currently exist.

CII, EEXI, EEDI and the proposed Net-Zero Framework - keeping them apart

  • EEDI - design index, new ships, regulation 22. What the ship was built to do.
  • EEXI - design index applied retrospectively to existing ships from 400 gt, regulation 23. A one-off calculation, often satisfied by engine or shaft power limitation.
  • CII - operational, annual, ships of 5,000 GT and above, regulation 28. What she actually did.
  • The Net-Zero Framework - the proposed new Chapter 5, a fuel-intensity standard with a pricing mechanism, which at this sitting had been approved and circulated but not adopted. See Q8 of this paper. CII is in force and enforceable; the Net-Zero Framework was not.

What the examiner means by 'remedial measures adopted'

This is the trap in limb (b), and it is a trap about verbs. The industry has asked for a great many things - a cargo-based metric, an exemption for waiting time, relief for short-sea trades. Almost none of that had been adopted. What had been adopted by this sitting was narrower and more procedural: the reduction factors were extended to 2030 by MEPC.400(83), Phase 1 of the review was completed, and the SEEMP Guidelines were amended so that alternative metrics could be developed later. Writing the wish-list as though it were the outcome is the commonest way to lose limb (b), and it is also simply inaccurate.

Common mistakes

  • Calling CII a cap on emissions or an emissions trading scheme. It is neither.
  • Saying a D or E rating attracts a fine or detention. The consequence is a corrective action plan in SEEMP Part III.
  • Saying 'CII is the AER'. AER is the metric for most cargo ships; cruise and ro-pax use a different one.
  • Quoting a reduction factor without its year - the figure changes annually and a bare percentage is unmarkable.
  • Answering limb (b) with what the industry has asked for rather than what was actually adopted.
  • Confusing the 5,000 GT CII threshold with the 400 gt EEXI threshold.
  • Treating the Net-Zero Framework as an existing obligation. At this sitting it was a circulated draft.

Understand this first

Two things have to be held apart in this question. The first is that FSA is not something the ship does - it is the method by which IMO decides whether a rule is worth making, so the question is really 'how did, and how will, the regulator arrive at a proportionate rule for this cargo?'. The second is that a lithium battery fire is not an ordinary fire: thermal runaway generates its own oxidant, so it cannot be smothered, and the only lever the ship holds is cooling to stop it spreading. Model written answer

1. a) What FSA is, and who it is for

Formal Safety Assessment is set out in MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, 'Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment (FSA) for use in the IMO rule-making process', of 9 April 2018. The title states its purpose: a structured, auditable methodology by which the Organization decides whether, and how, to regulate a hazard, balancing safety benefit against cost. It is not a shipboard risk assessment or a permit-to-work system. The question is therefore how a proportionate rule for this cargo was, and was still being, arrived at.

2. Step 1 - identifying the hazards of this cargo

  • Thermal runaway - an exothermic internal reaction that becomes self-sustaining and generates its own oxidant, so it cannot be smothered and continues without external air;
  • propagation cell to cell and module to module, and thence vehicle to vehicle in a close stow;
  • initiators - internal cell defects, mechanical damage in handling or lashing, over-charge, a high state of charge on shipment, and charging on board;
  • vent gas - flammable and toxic, able to accumulate in an enclosed vehicle space and produce a gas explosion rather than a simple fire;
  • re-ignition hours or days after apparent extinction, defeating a normal fire-out judgement;
  • mis-declaration and concealment - cells as cargo may be undeclared or wrongly classified, and a battery electric vehicle is not a labelled package at all, so the energy is invisible to the stowage plan.

3. Step 2 - assessing the risk on a ro-ro deck

Risk is frequency times consequence, estimated from casualty returns, incident and near-miss data, cell testing and fire modelling - with the Rev.2 requirement that the reliability, uncertainty and validity of that data be assessed and reported rather than assumed.

  • Frequency is low per vehicle but rising with fleet electrification, and exposure is concentrated - one deck may hold thousands of units;
  • consequence escalates because a ro-ro space is a large undivided volume, vehicles stowed centimetres apart, with no cargo-hold fire divisions and with ramps and ventilation trunks carrying heat and smoke between decks;
  • on a ro-pax the dominant risk is to life; on a pure car and truck carrier it is total loss of the ship, and a series of well-known casualties supplied the evidence;
  • manual firefighting on a loaded deck is near impossible, so the model must assume that fixed systems and boundaries are what will actually be available.

4. Steps 3 and 4 - risk control options and whether they pay

Risk control options are generated and grouped, then tested by cost-benefit assessment using indices such as the cost of averting a fatality, so a measure is recommended only where the safety gain justifies the cost across the world fleet:

  • detection - earlier, better located, individually identifiable so the seat of fire is known, with combined smoke and heat sensing;
  • suppression - drencher and water-based systems of adequate coverage and capacity, aligned with detection sections so that the right section operates;
  • containment - insulated boundaries, ventilation shut-down and control of openings;
  • operational and design - state-of-charge limits on shipment, segregation and dedicated stowage, restrictions on charging at sea, video monitoring, crew procedures;
  • options are ranked, and those effective but disproportionately costly are rejected transparently - which is why the outcome is a specific set of measures rather than everything technically possible.

5. Step 5 - what the recommendations had become by this sitting

The risk-based work on ro-ro fire safety - notably the EMSA FIRESAFE I and FIRESAFE II studies of fire dynamics, system performance and operational factors - entered the regulatory system in stages, and it is important to state precisely how far it had got:

  • MSC.1/Circ.1615, Interim Guidelines for Minimizing the Incidence and Consequences of Fires in Ro-Ro Spaces and Special Category Spaces, carried key outcomes into guidance while the mandatory work continued. At this sitting this was the operative IMO instrument on the subject;
  • resolution MSC.550(108), amending SOLAS chapter II-2 regulations 7 and 20, and resolution MSC.555(108), amending FSS Code chapters 7 and 9, had been adopted at MSC 108 in May 2024 - but they were not yet in force. Their entry into force was 1 January 2026, some five months after this examination;
  • when they did bite they would require individually identifiable fixed detection with combined smoke and heat detectors, detection aligned with deluge sections, detection on weather decks used for vehicles, an effective video monitoring system with at least seven days of replay at a continuously manned control station, and A-60 boundaries on passenger ships carrying more than 36 passengers;
  • they would apply to ships constructed on or after 1 January 2026, existing ships in scope complying not later than the first survey on or after 1 January 2028;
  • further amendments aimed specifically at new energy vehicles were still in development.

So the honest conclusion at this date is that FSA had done its work and been acted on - the amendments were adopted, dated and known - but the mandatory regime was prospective. What actually governed the ship on the day was SOLAS II-2 as it then stood, the IMDG Code, and the interim guidelines, which is precisely why limb (b) matters so much at this sitting.

6. b) Measures by stakeholder

  • IMO - bring the adopted amendments into force on time, complete the new-energy-vehicle amendments, keep IMDG provisions current and maintain the interim guidance meanwhile;
  • flag State and class - issue guidance ahead of the compliance dates, and prepare to approve and survey the amended detection, suppression and boundary arrangements;
  • shipper, manufacturer and packer - correct IMDG classification, packing and marking, state-of-charge control, testing and quality control, and no offering of damaged, defective or recalled batteries;
  • port and terminal - screen and refuse visibly damaged vehicles, check declared state of charge, separate electric vehicles in marshalling areas, and inform the ship promptly;
  • ship operator - a written stowage and segregation policy, limits on charging on board, training in lithium fire behaviour, vehicle-deck drills, thermal imaging, and a response plan that assumes re-ignition. Where the fixed systems are not yet those the new amendments will require, this is where the gap is actually closed;
  • ship's staff - verify cargo against the declaration, maintain detection and drencher readiness, patrol during and after loading, and fight fire by early activation of fixed systems and boundary cooling rather than by entering a loaded deck;
  • insurers and industry bodies - loss-prevention guidance, incident and near-miss data sharing, and premium signals rewarding compliant stowage - the same data that feeds the next FSA.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The 10-mark limb is a method question, and method questions are marked on whether the method is visible. Naming the five steps and then walking this cargo through them is what separates a top answer from a general essay about battery fires. Step 5 is where most candidates stop short: an FSA that produces no recommendation has not been applied, so ending with what the recommendations actually became - and how far they had got - is what closes the loop.

Why FSA exists at all

Before FSA, IMO regulation was largely reactive - a casualty happened, and a rule followed. FSA was introduced to allow the Organization to act proactively and proportionately, on evidence rather than on the emotional weight of the most recent disaster, and to be able to say publicly why a proposed rule was or was not worth its cost. That is why the Guidelines insist on auditability, traceability and the explicit reporting of uncertainty: an FSA is as much an accountability document as a technical one.

The lithium hazard in engineering terms

Thermal runaway begins when a cell's internal temperature passes the point at which exothermic decomposition of the electrolyte and cathode outpaces heat loss. The decomposition liberates oxygen from the cathode material, which is why the reaction sustains itself without air and why smothering agents - CO2, a foam blanket, an inert atmosphere - do not stop it. Cooling is the only lever the ship holds, and it works by removing heat from cells that have not yet reached onset temperature, so its real objective is to stop propagation to neighbouring vehicles rather than to extinguish the cell already in runaway. That is also why re-ignition is normal rather than surprising: cells that were merely heated may still cross the threshold hours later.

Vehicles, containers and packages are three different regulatory problems

  • Cells and batteries as cargo - dangerous goods under the IMDG Code, class 9, with UN numbers 3480 and 3481 for lithium-ion and 3090 and 3091 for lithium-metal, subject to packing, marking, documentation and segregation.
  • Batteries in or with equipment - the same UN family but different provisions, and often carried under exceptions.
  • A battery electric vehicle - carried as a vehicle, not as a declared dangerous goods package. This is the regulatory gap: the ship may know exactly how many tonnes of declared class 9 she has, and have no idea how much stored energy is sitting on the vehicle decks.

Adopted, in force, and applicable are three different things

This question is a good place to practise a distinction that Q7 of this paper examines in the abstract. Resolutions MSC.550(108) and MSC.555(108) were adopted in May 2024. They entered into force on 1 January 2026. And even then they applied only to ships constructed on or after that date, with existing ships in scope reaching compliance by the first survey on or after 1 January 2028. At this August 2025 sitting the first of those three stages had been passed and the second had not. A candidate who wrote that the new detection and video-monitoring requirements applied to his ship would have been wrong by five months and, for an existing ship, by nearly three years.

Common mistakes

  • Treating FSA as a shipboard risk assessment. It is a tool for IMO rule-making.
  • Naming the five steps and never applying them to lithium batteries - or applying them and never naming them. The marks need both.
  • Saying the fire can be smothered or that CO2 will deal with it. Runaway generates its own oxidant; cooling and boundary protection are the levers.
  • Declaring the fire out. Re-ignition hours later is normal and the plan must assume it.
  • Stating that the SOLAS II-2 and FSS Code amendments applied at this sitting. They had been adopted but were not yet in force.
  • Answering limb (b) as a list of things the ship should do, when the question says stakeholders - the chain runs from the cell manufacturer to the insurer.
  • Forgetting that a battery electric vehicle is not a declared dangerous goods package.

Examiner traps

  • 'Application of formal safety assessment' - the demand is application, not description. A description of the methodology alone answers half the limb.
  • 'in vehicles and containers and in RORO ships' - three carriage situations, and they are regulated differently. Cover the distinction.
  • 'measures to be taken by stakeholders' in limb (b) - plural and deliberately wide. Group the answer by party.

Understand this first

The phrase human element is not a polite word for human error. In STCW it means the whole set of conditions that decide whether a competent person actually performs competently on the day - whether the standard he was assessed against was published and objective, whether he has slept, whether he is medically fit, whether he can work as part of a team, and whether his company has made any of that possible. That is why limb A cannot be answered with rest hours alone: rest is one of four fronts. And it is why limb B is a separate document - the mandatory rule sets a floor for hours, while the Guidelines address the thing the floor cannot reach, which is whether the person is actually rested.

Model written answer

1. A) What the human element means in STCW

STCW refuses to treat competence as something that time served automatically confers. It replaces experience with demonstrated competence, and then addresses the conditions - fitness, rest, teamwork and company responsibility - under which competence can actually be exercised. The instrument is the STCW Convention and Code, 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force from 1 January 2012.

2. Competence-based standards

  • The Part A tables of competence specify, for every function and level, the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods for demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard, not an examiner's impression.
  • Part A is mandatory; Part B is guidance - a distinction worth stating explicitly.
  • The Manila amendments added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking at operational level and leadership and managerial skill at management level - where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention. It is the explicit recognition that technically competent individuals still fail as a team.
  • Regulation I/6 governs training and assessment, and regulation I/8 requires a quality standards system, so the training system itself is audited rather than assumed.
  • Regulation I/11 requires periodic revalidation, and chapter VI mandatory safety familiarisation and basic training before a seafarer is assigned duties.

3. Fitness for duty - the physical human being

  • Regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require that watch systems are arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The Manila-amended A-VIII/1 minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Authorised exceptions are tightly conditioned: weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours in any 7-day period, exceptions from the 77-hour norm may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • The Manila amendments also introduced alcohol limits for masters, officers and other seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards, and regulation VIII/2 with section A-VIII/2 governs watchkeeping arrangements and principles.

4. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring that seafarers hold valid certificates, are familiarised with their duties and with the ship's arrangements, and that documentation is maintained - so the human element is not left as an individual's problem. Administrations operate quality standards systems and are subject to independent evaluation and reporting to IMO.

5. B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The guidance is MSC.1/Circ.1598, 'Guidelines on fatigue', of 24 January 2019, approved at the 100th session of the Maritime Safety Committee and finalized by the HTW Sub-Committee. It supersedes MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, not mandatory - the binding rest-hour requirements remain in STCW chapter VIII and in MLC 2006. The Guidelines are arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party:

  • Module 1 - Fatigue: what it is and its causes, grouped as sleep and rest, the biological clock and circadian rhythm, health and stress, environmental factors such as noise, vibration and motion, sleep disorders and operational factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods.
  • Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company: assessing, mitigating and managing fatigue risk in the safety management system, in manning decisions and in scheduling.
  • Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer: practical guidance for the master, officers and ratings on sleep quality, diet and the limits of countermeasures - notably that caffeine is not a substitute for sleep.
  • Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training.
  • Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design: mitigation designed in - accommodation location, noise, vibration, lighting and habitability.
  • Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities: how regulators and inspectors should approach it.

6. How the guidance and the rules fit together

The Guidelines are explicit that hours of rest are set by a prescriptive formula in STCW chapter VIII, and that complying with the formula is not the same as being rested: managers must allow for circadian rhythm and sleep debt, and - in the Guidelines' own words - rest means rest, not the substitution of a different form of work. The same theme runs through MLC 2006 Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7. A management-level engineer should therefore treat recorded compliance as the floor, and a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant as a warning rather than a reassurance.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two printed limbs of 8 marks each, so split the answer evenly and head each limb clearly. Limb A rewards structure - competence tables, fitness for duty, company responsibility - and limb B rewards naming the actual circular. A candidate who writes generally about tiredness for the second limb without producing MSC.1/Circ.1598 and its module structure will lose most of eight marks, because 'the IMO guidance' is a specific document.

The organising idea for limb A

STCW attacks the human element on three fronts, and saying so gives the limb a spine: can they do it (competence standards, assessed against published criteria), are they fit to do it now (fitness for duty - rest, medical, alcohol), and who is responsible for making that possible (regulation I/14, the company). The Manila amendments strengthened all three and added the fourth dimension the older Convention lacked - can they work as a team, through mandatory leadership and teamworking training.

The numbers you must get right

10 hours in any 24, and 77 hours in any 7 days. Divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods. Exceptions may not go below 70 hours in any 7 days and may not run more than two consecutive weeks. Learn 10, 77, 2, 6, 14, 70 - and be careful, because 70 and 77 are both real figures doing different jobs: 77 is the norm, 70 is the floor under an authorised exception. Quoting 70 as the normal requirement is the classic error, and it is exactly the trap that a pre-2010 copy of the Code will lead you into.

The wrong-edition trap, stated plainly

This is worth a paragraph of its own because it caught this project. A freely available IMO circular copy of section A-VIII/1 - STCW.6/Circ.1 - reproduces the 1995 Code: five paragraphs, 70 hours weekly, and no 14-hour interval provision at all. It looks entirely authoritative, it is on an official domain, and it is superseded. A candidate who revises from it will write 70 where 77 is required and will omit the 14-hour rule altogether. The lesson generalises: for any instrument that has been amended, the question to ask of a source is not 'is this official?' but 'which edition is this?'

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb B without naming MSC.1/Circ.1598 and the circular it replaced, MSC/Circ.1014.
  • Treating the fatigue Guidelines as mandatory. They are recommendatory; the binding requirements are STCW chapter VIII and MLC 2006.
  • Quoting 70 hours as the normal weekly rest. 77 is the norm; 70 is the minimum where an exception has been authorised.
  • Confusing Part A with Part B - Part A is mandatory, Part B is guidance.
  • Writing about fatigue only, and never addressing competence, which is the larger half of limb A.
  • Forgetting regulation I/14 - the company's responsibility - which is what turns the human element from an individual failing into a management obligation.
  • Describing the STCW comprehensive review as though it had produced amendments in force. It had not.

Examiner traps

  • 'How is Human Element issue addressed in STCW code' - the Code, so the Part A tables and section A-VIII/1 are the target, not a general essay on seafarer welfare.

  • The two limbs carry 8 marks each. Fatigue appears in both, and it is easy to spend limb A on rest hours and arrive at limb B with nothing new to say. Keep competence in A and the Guidelines in B.

Understand this first

Model written answer

1. What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship

The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 gives no definition of unseaworthiness in a definitions section. The description sits inside section 334 itself, which treats a ship as unseaworthy when the following are not such as to render her in every respect fit for the proposed voyage or service:

  • the materials of which she is made and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, boilers and machinery.

Two features carry the marks. The test is relative - fitness for the proposed voyage or service - so a ship adequate for a sheltered coastal run may be unseaworthy for an ocean passage. And it is far wider than material condition: an undermanned ship, one whose officers hold the wrong certificates, or a mechanically perfect ship with badly stowed cargo is unseaworthy under the Act.

2. The offence, and the defences the Act allows

  • s.334(1) - every person who sends or attempts to send an Indian ship to sea from any port in India in such an unseaworthy state that the life of any person is likely to be thereby endangered is guilty of an offence, unless he proves he used all reasonable means to insure her being sent to sea in a seaworthy state, or that sending her to sea in that state was, in the circumstances, reasonable and justifiable.
  • s.334(2) - a master who knowingly takes such a ship to sea commits the offence, with only the second defence available to him.
  • s.334(3) permits a person charged to give evidence in his own defence; s.334(4) provides that no prosecution shall be instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government.

Liability is therefore not automatic. The threshold is danger to life, the burden of the defence lies on the accused, and the standard demanded is reasonable means rather than a guarantee of result.

3. What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her

Section 336 is directed at a different problem - stopping the ship rather than punishing anybody. An unsafe ship is one which, by reason of the defective condition of her hull, equipment or machinery, or by reason of overloading or improper loading, is unfit to proceed to sea without serious danger to human life, having regard to the nature of the service for which she is intended. The procedure is staged:

  1. the Central Government, having reason to believe on complaint or otherwise that she is unsafe, may order her provisionally detained for the purpose of survey;
  2. a written statement of the grounds must be served forthwith on the master;
  3. the matter is referred to the court of survey for the port, or a competent person is appointed to survey the ship and report;
  4. on that report she is either released or finally detained.

4. The real difference between the two

  • Unseaworthy is a state; unsafe is the trigger for a power. Section 334 describes a condition and attaches criminal liability to sending a ship to sea in it. Section 336 describes a condition that entitles the Government to stop her.
  • One looks backwards, the other forwards. The offence punishes a decision already taken; detention prevents the voyage from happening at all.
  • Unseaworthiness is the wider concept - it expressly takes in crew numbers and qualifications and the stowage of cargo and ballast. The unsafe-ship definition lists a narrower set of causes.
  • The thresholds differ - life likely to be endangered under s.334; unfitness to proceed without serious danger to human life under s.336.
  • In practice, a ship unsafe within s.336 will invariably be unseaworthy within s.334, but a ship may be unseaworthy - one certificated engineer short - without being unsafe.

5. The owner's obligation to the crew

Section 335 converts seaworthiness from a matter between owner and cargo into a duty owed to the people on board. In every contract of service, express or implied, between the owner of an Indian ship and the master or any seaman, and in every contract of apprenticeship, there is implied - notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary - an obligation that the owner, the master and every agent charged with the loading of the ship, or preparing her for sea, or sending her to sea shall use all reasonable means to ensure the seaworthiness of the ship for the voyage at the time when the voyage commences, and shall keep her in a seaworthy state during the voyage.

Three consequences follow. The duty is non-excludable - a term in the crew agreement waiving it is void. It is a due diligence standard, not a warranty of result. And it is continuing throughout the voyage rather than being discharged at departure. It also binds people other than the registered owner: the loading agent is named in the section.

6. How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force at this sitting

Section 335(2) allows the Central Government, on the owner's request or otherwise, to arrange a survey of the hull, equipment or machinery. In practice the duty is policed through the wider system - class and flag State statutory survey and certification, ISM auditing, and port State control under SOLAS chapter I regulation 19 - with the section 336 detention power as the last resort.

One point of currency should be stated rather than assumed, and it is unusually pointed at this sitting. The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 was the operative law on the examination date. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 received the assent of the President on 18 August 2025 - the same month as this paper - but assent is not commencement: it was brought into force only on 15 March 2026, and it is section 324(1) of that Act which repeals the 1958 Act. The answer is therefore correctly and necessarily given on the 1958 Act.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem asks three questions and prints no limbs, so the examiner has no printed structure to mark against and will mark against the three questions actually asked. Answering them in the order asked, with the middle one made explicitly comparative, is what makes the script easy to mark. The comparison in step 4 is the highest-value paragraph in the answer and the one most often missing: candidates define both terms and then never say how they differ, which is literally what was asked.

Why the Act splits the idea in two

A criminal offence needs fault, a trial and proof beyond reasonable doubt, and it operates after the event - which is no use at all to the crew of a ship that is about to sail. An administrative detention power needs only a reasonable belief and can act within hours. The 1958 Act therefore does both: section 334 attaches consequences to a decision, and section 336 supplies the fast preventive lever, with the survey and the right to be told the grounds as the safeguards against its misuse. Seeing that the two sections exist to solve different problems is the whole of the distinction.

Seaworthiness has three different legal lives - keep them apart

  • Statutory and criminal - MSA 1958 s.334, which is this question. A state of the ship, punishable, with a due-diligence defence.
  • Contractual, owed to the crew - MSA 1958 s.335. Implied, non-excludable, and continuing through the voyage.
  • Insurance and carriage - a warranty or a duty of due diligence depending on whether the policy is a voyage or a time policy and on the contract of carriage. Under Indian law that is the Marine Insurance Act 1963, not the English Act of 1906, and a breach affects recovery rather than attracting a penalty.

The examiner asks about the first two. Answering about the third is the commonest way to lose this question.

Assent is not commencement - and this sitting proves it

This paper happens to sit almost exactly on the seam. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 received assent on 18 August 2025, in the very month of this examination, and a candidate who had read a newspaper that week might well have thought the new Act was the law. It was not. An Indian Act ordinarily comes into force on a date the Central Government appoints by notification in the Official Gazette, and here that notification, S.O. 1244(E), appointed 15 March 2026. Between assent and commencement the old Act governs in full. That distinction - passed, assented, commenced - is exactly the same three-stage problem that Q7 and Q8 of this paper examine for international conventions, and it is worth revising the three together.

Common mistakes

  • Describing seaworthiness in general engineering terms without quoting the section 334 formula.
  • Leaving crew numbers, certification and stowage out of the definition - they are expressly inside it.
  • Treating unseaworthy and unsafe as interchangeable, or saying the difference is only one of degree.
  • Saying the owner guarantees seaworthiness. The duty is to use all reasonable means.
  • Forgetting that the section 335 duty continues during the voyage.
  • Answering under the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 or the doctrine of stages.
  • Answering under the Merchant Shipping Act 2025, which had not commenced and was not the law at this sitting.

Examiner traps

  • 'Within the meaning of the Merchant Shipping Act (MSA), 1958, as amended' - the examiner is asking for the statutory meaning, and a general definition cannot reach the top band.
  • 'What according to you is the difference' invites an opinion, but the marks are for the legal difference, not a preference.
  • 'Obligations of owners to crew' - section 335 also binds the master and the loading agent, so an answer confined to the registered owner is incomplete.
  • The printed words 'with respect seaworthiness' are incomplete on the paper. Read them as 'with respect to seaworthiness' and answer the obvious demand; do not waste time on the typography.

Understand this first

General average is not insurance and not liability - it is a rule of contribution, and it is one of the oldest ideas in maritime law. The problem it solves is a problem of incentives at the worst moment: if the master must sacrifice one interest to save the whole adventure, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, the owner of that interest would be ruined for everyone else's benefit and would fight the decision. Making the loss shared removes the conflict. Everything in limb (a) follows from that purpose - the sacrifice must be deliberate (or there was no decision to protect), for the common safety (or there is nothing to share), and reasonable (or the decision does not deserve protection). And limb (b) is simply that same test applied to an engine: damage suffered because the master chose to risk the machinery to save the ship is a sacrifice; damage suffered in the ordinary course is not.

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

The statement in the question is correct, and the features are not a matter of opinion: they are drawn from the definition itself. Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules provides that there is a general average act 'when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure'.

Note first how the Rules apply. They are not law: they take effect by incorporation into the contract of carriage - the bill of lading or charterparty - and the Rule of Interpretation then makes them apply 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. In India the concept is also recognised in the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

2. The essential features

Every phrase of Rule A is doing work, and each yields one essential feature:

  1. Extraordinary. The sacrifice or expenditure must be outside the ordinary performance of the contract of carriage. Fuel burned on a normal passage, or wear on machinery in ordinary service, is not extraordinary however costly.
  2. Intentional and voluntary. The loss must be deliberately incurred. Cargo washed overboard by a sea is accidental and lies where it falls; cargo jettisoned by decision is a sacrifice. This is the single feature that most sharply separates general from particular average.
  3. Reasonable. Reinforced by the Rule Paramount, which overrides everything else: 'In no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.'
  4. Peril. There must be real danger - not necessarily immediate, but genuine and substantial, and more than the ordinary risk of the sea.
  5. Common safety and common adventure. The act must be for the benefit of the whole adventure, not one interest alone, and there must be more than one interest at risk. A ship in ballast with no cargo on board has no common adventure and can have no general average.
  6. Preservation of property. The purpose must be to preserve the property from that peril. Success matters: there must be something saved to contribute.

3. The lettered rules that qualify the definition

  • Rule C - only losses which are the direct consequence of the general average act are allowed. It expressly excludes demurrage, loss of market, loss caused by delay and any indirect loss whatsoever, and excludes environmental damage and the consequences of escaping pollutants.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected even though the event was due to the fault of one of the parties, without prejudice to any remedies or defences in respect of that fault. So the adjustment proceeds first, and blame is fought separately.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming, who must give notice in writing to the average adjuster and supply particulars, generally within twelve months.

4. How the loss is shared, and what general average is not

Under Rule A.2 the sacrifice is borne by the different contributing interests - ship, cargo and freight at risk - rateably in proportion to their values at the termination of the adventure. The adjustment is prepared by an average adjuster, and security is taken before cargo is released, normally an average bond from the receiver and an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter.

Two distinctions complete the picture. General average is not particular average: particular average is accidental damage, falls on the interest that suffers it and is not shared. And general average is not insurance: it is a rule of contribution between the parties to the adventure, which predates marine insurance entirely, though the contribution is in practice recoverable under the policy.

5. b) The refloating scenario - the rule that governs it

This situation is dealt with expressly, and by a rule written for exactly these facts. Rule VII, 'Damage to Machinery and Boilers', provides:

'Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.'

6. Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not

Yes, the burnt bearings can be allowed in general average - provided four things are shown.

  1. The ship was ashore. She was aground, so the first condition is met on the facts given.
  2. She was in a position of peril. This must be established, not assumed. A vessel lying safely aground on soft mud in fine weather, in no danger and awaiting the tide, is not in peril; one working on a hard bottom, or exposed to weather, tide or the risk of breaking her back, is. This is the condition on which the claim will actually turn.
  3. The machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety. Running the engine ahead and astern to shake her off is precisely that - a deliberate act directed at the safety of the whole adventure, not a routine manoeuvre.
  4. The damage arose at the risk of such damage. The master must have accepted the risk of damaging the engine as the price of refloating. Working an engine hard, astern and ahead, against a grounded hull is known to overload the bearings, and it is that knowing acceptance that makes the loss a sacrifice rather than an accident.

The damage then satisfies Rule A as well: it is extraordinary (not ordinary service), intentional (the engine was deliberately worked in a way likely to damage it), reasonable if refloating was a sensible response, and directed at the common safety. It is also the direct consequence of the act, as Rule C requires. Under Rule D it makes no difference to the right of contribution that the grounding may have been somebody's fault.

The converse must be stated, because it is the trap. Rule VII closes with an absolute prohibition: where the ship is afloat, damage from working the propelling machinery is 'in no circumstances' allowed. So if she had already come off and the bearings burnt while manoeuvring clear, or if she were merely touching and not in peril, the same physical damage would be particular average - the owner's own loss, recoverable if at all under the hull policy and not shared with cargo. The engine damage is identical; the legal answer is opposite; and what decides it is the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is a 'discuss the statement' question, and the highest-scoring way to discuss it is to show that the essential features are not a list somebody invented but the clauses of the Rule A definition read one at a time. That converts a memory answer into an analytical one. Limb (b) is worth the same 8 marks and is decided by a single rule, so the answer must quote Rule VII, apply its four conditions to the facts, and then state the converse. An answer that says 'yes, it is a sacrifice' without naming the rule or testing peril has taken about half the marks available.

Why general average exists at all

It is far older than marine insurance and older than most maritime law - the principle is found in the Rhodian sea law and passed through Roman law into every modern maritime system. The problem it solves is one of incentives at the worst possible moment. If a master must jettison cargo to save the ship, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, then whoever owned that particular cargo would bear everything while the rest of the adventure was saved at his expense. Nobody would consent, and the master's decision would be contested at the moment when hesitation is most dangerous. Making the loss a shared loss removes the conflict: every interest knows that a sacrifice made for the common safety will be contributed to by all. Seen that way, general average is a rule about decision-making under peril, not really a rule about money.

Sacrifice versus accident - the line that decides everything

Almost every general average problem in an examination is a disguised question about whether the loss was chosen. Cargo swept overboard by a sea is an accident; cargo jettisoned is a sacrifice. An engine that fails on passage is an accident; an engine deliberately worked to destruction to refloat the ship is a sacrifice. Water damage from a leak is an accident; water damage from firefighting is a sacrifice. Rule VII is simply that same line drawn for one specific case - and the drafters drew it so firmly that they added an absolute prohibition for the afloat case, because otherwise every hard manoeuvre would generate a claim.

The four conditions of Rule VII, and which one is really in issue

Ashore; in a position of peril; an actual intention to float her for the common safety; at the risk of such damage. In practice the first and third are usually obvious on the facts and the fourth follows from them. The condition that is actually litigated is peril. A ship aground is not automatically in peril - the question is whether she was in real danger, having regard to the bottom, the weather, the tide, her condition and the exposure of the position. That is why a careful answer says the claim 'turns on establishing peril' rather than simply asserting it, and why the master's log, weather records and any surveyor's evidence matter so much afterwards.

Which version of the Rules applies

The York-Antwerp Rules are contractual, not statutory. They apply because the bill of lading or charterparty says they do, which is why the Rule of Interpretation has to declare that they operate 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. The consequence is that the version in force between the parties may be the 1994, 2004 or 2016 text, depending on what the contract incorporates. On this question it does not matter: Rule VII is materially identical in all three. On other questions it can matter a great deal, so the habit of asking which version is incorporated is worth forming.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the features without tying them to Rule A, which is where they come from.
  • Omitting the requirement of a common adventure - with no cargo on board there is nothing to contribute and no general average.
  • Forgetting the Rule Paramount. Even a textbook sacrifice is disallowed if unreasonably made.
  • Answering limb (b) 'yes' without naming Rule VII or testing whether the ship was in peril.
  • Missing the afloat prohibition, which is the other half of the rule and the source of the marks for a complete answer.
  • Saying the owner's fault in grounding the ship defeats the claim. Rule D says it does not, without prejudice to separate remedies.
  • Confusing general average with particular average, or treating general average as a form of insurance rather than of contribution.

Examiner traps

  • 'General average is based on certain essential features' - the word essential invites a definitional answer. Give the features as conditions, each of which must be satisfied.
  • 'Discuss the statement' - agreement plus explanation. Do not merely define general average.
  • 'Give justification to your answer' in limb (b) - the justification is the marks. A bare yes or no scores almost nothing.
  • The facts say 'ahead and astern directions ... to shake the vessel loose'. That wording is doing deliberate work: it establishes both the intention to refloat and the acceptance of risk, so quote it back.

Understand this first

The point of this question is that a convention passes through several distinct stages, and at every stage it is not yet law for your ship. Adoption fixes the words. And even then, what is actually enforced against the ship in an Indian port is the domestic legislation giving effect to the convention. A Chief Engineer who can place an instrument on that ladder can answer 'does this apply to me?' - which is what the question is really about, and which Q8 of this same paper asks in a live case.

Model written answer

1. Adoption is not entry into force

A convention is adopted either by the competent IMO organ - the Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text and does no more: it binds no State, and an adopted convention may never enter into force at all. The text is then opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General of IMO as depositary.

2. How a State expresses consent to be bound

Between adoption and entry into force sits the step that actually matters: each State must express its consent to be bound, by one of three routes -

  • signature alone, where the treaty so provides or the negotiating States so agreed;
  • signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, which is the normal course for multilateral conventions;
  • accession, where the State did not sign within the signature period.

In the second and third cases the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument with the depositary, who records it, counts it towards the entry into force condition, and notifies all other States.

3. The two terms the question names

(ii) Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval. The State signs, but the signature does not bind it; it indicates an intention to proceed. The State becomes a party only on later depositing an instrument of ratification. Its modern purpose is to give the State time to enact the necessary domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements before committing internationally. Acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names, used where a constitution does not demand the fuller procedure: VCLT Article 14(2) provides that consent is expressed by acceptance or approval under conditions similar to those which apply to ratification. In legal effect the three are identical.

(i) Accession. The means by which a State becomes party to a treaty it did not sign - because the signature period has closed, or because it was not a negotiating State. It is a single step: no prior signature, simply deposit of an instrument of accession, permitted by VCLT Article 15 where the treaty so provides. Once deposited it has exactly the same legal effect as ratification. The difference is the route, not the destination.

4. The entry into force provision, and why it matters

Every convention contains a final clause fixing entry into force, normally a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. Its importance is fourfold:

  • it prevents an instrument binding a handful of States while the rest operate to another standard, which would distort competition;
  • it fixes a certain date from which obligations, surveys and certificates run, so Administrations, recognised organisations, owners and yards all work to one deadline;
  • it provides the interval in which flag States legislate, class develops rules, and industry builds or retrofits;
  • it gives legitimacy - a convention in force across most world tonnage can be enforced through port State control against ships of non-parties, which is what makes no more favourable treatment work.

5. Conventions of an important technical nature

For a convention of important technical nature - one dictating construction, equipment or operation - States observe a stiffer double condition: a number of States and a percentage of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping. A count of States alone could be satisfied by many small registries while the major fleets stayed outside, leaving a convention in force on paper and irrelevant at sea.

  • SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, requiring not less than 25 States whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; in force 25 May 1980.
  • The 1978 MARPOL Protocol - in force twelve months after not less than fifteen States constituting not less than fifty per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage had become Parties.

Older conventions typically required two-thirds of contracting States, which occasionally meant more acceptances were needed to bring an instrument into force than to amend it.

6. Keeping the convention current once in force

A technical convention is worthless if it cannot keep pace, and explicit acceptance failed on exactly that point: amendments to the 1960 SOLAS Convention adopted between 1966 and 1973 never received enough acceptances and none entered into force.

IMO's answer was the tacit acceptance procedure, used in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL and the collision regulations. It reverses the default: an amendment to the technical annexes is deemed accepted on a specified date unless objections are received beforehand from an agreed number of Parties. For SOLAS 1974 an amendment is deemed accepted at the end of two years from communication to Contracting Governments, unless objected to by more than one-third of Contracting Governments, or by Governments whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross merchant tonnage. Amendments now enter into force typically within 18 to 24 months.

The sequence can be seen running at this very sitting. Under MARPOL article 16(2)(a) the Secretary-General circulated the draft revised Annex VI on 11 April 2025 for consideration with a view to adoption at an extraordinary session under article 16(2)(b), (c) and (d). Circulated, therefore, but not yet adopted - see Q8. Finally, once in force internationally each flag State must still give the instrument domestic effect, in India through the Merchant Shipping Act and the rules made under it, and it is that domestic instrument which is enforced against the ship.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no limbs but contains four separate demands and then names two terms. The route answers them in the order asked and gives the two named terms a heading of their own, because the examiner has singled them out and an answer that defines them in passing inside a general narrative will not be credited for them. Step 6 is the pay-off: a candidate who can name tacit acceptance and say why explicit acceptance failed has shown he understands the machinery rather than memorised it.

Four words that are not synonyms

Adoption settles the text. Signature may or may not bind, depending on what the treaty says. Ratification, acceptance, approval or accession is the act that binds the State, and it binds on deposit, not on decision at home. Entry into force is when the instrument starts to operate, for those States and thereafter for later parties. Candidates use all four as though they meant the same thing, and the whole question is built on their not meaning the same thing.

The same problem exists in domestic law

This is worth noticing because this paper tests it twice. An Indian Act is passed, then receives assent, then commences on a date appointed by notification - and it is not law until it commences. That is structurally the same three-stage problem as adoption, ratification and entry into force. Q5 of this paper turns on it: the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 received assent in August 2025 and did not commence until March 2026. Revising Q5, Q7 and Q8 together is far more efficient than revising them apart, because they are one idea in three settings.

Why tonnage and not just a headcount

The double condition looks like bureaucratic detail and is in fact the most commercially significant sentence in the answer. A safety convention that binds fifty small registries and none of the major fleets creates a competitive distortion rather than a safety improvement: compliant ships carry the cost and non-compliant ships take the trade. Requiring a percentage of world gross tonnage ensures the instrument binds the fleet that actually carries the cargo, which is what then makes port State control and no more favourable treatment effective against the remainder.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a convention applies once IMO adopts it. Adoption binds nobody.
  • Treating accession as a lesser status. Once deposited it has identical effect to ratification.
  • Saying ratification is deposit with IMO of a signed convention. It is deposit of a separate formal instrument.
  • Giving the entry into force condition as a number of States only, and omitting the tonnage percentage, on a question that expressly asks about conventions of important technical nature.
  • Describing tacit acceptance as automatic amendment. It is deemed acceptance in the absence of objection by a specified proportion.
  • Stopping at entry into force and never mentioning that the flag State must still give domestic effect - which is what actually binds the ship.

Examiner traps

  • 'State the provision and its importance' - two demands. Describing the provision without saying why it matters loses half of them.
  • 'For a convention of important technical nature' - this signals the double condition of States plus tonnage. It is the single most specific thing the question asks for.
  • 'Explain the terms (i) Accession (ii) Signature subject to Ratification, acceptance or approval' - two terms, expressly numbered. Answer them as such, and contrast them: one is a single step, the other is two.
  • The printed comma in 'technical nature state, the general rules' is on the paper. It joins two clauses; do not read it as a new demand.

Model written answer

1. What the revised strategy is, and what it is not

The 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships was adopted by resolution MEPC.377(80) on 7 July 2023 at MEPC 80, replacing the Initial Strategy of 2018. Its vision commits the Organization to reducing GHG emissions from international shipping and phasing them out as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition.

What it is must be stated precisely, because the second half of the question depends on it. The Strategy is an adopted policy resolution. It states intent, ambition and candidate measures. It is not mandatory law and binds no ship. Obligation arises only when a measure is written into MARPOL Annex VI and that amendment is adopted and enters into force. That gap between policy and law is precisely what the new Chapter V was drafted to close.

2. The four levels of ambition

  1. Carbon intensity of the ship to decline - further improvement of the energy efficiency requirements for new ships;
  2. carbon intensity of international shipping to decline - reduce CO2 emissions per transport work, as an average across international shipping, by at least 40% by 2030, compared to 2008;
  3. uptake of zero or near-zero GHG technologies, fuels and/or energy sources to increase - to at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030;
  4. GHG emissions to reach net zero - peak as soon as possible and reach net-zero by or around, i.e. close to, 2050, taking account of different national circumstances.

The ambitions are assessed well-to-wake, under the life cycle guidelines, so emissions cannot be moved off the ship and into the fuel production chain and counted as a saving.

3. The indicative checkpoints and the guiding principles

Two indicative checkpoints are added. Unlike the ambitions, they are expressed in total annual GHG emissions: reduce them by at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, and by at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040, both against 2008.

The guiding principles include non-discrimination and no more favourable treatment as enshrined in MARPOL; common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in the light of different national circumstances; and consideration of impacts on States, particularly LDCs and SIDS. Holding a uniform global measure and differentiated burden together is the central difficulty of the whole exercise.

4. The basket of measures, and why Annex VI must be amended

Short-term measures are already mandatory under Annex VI - EEXI, the CII under regulation 28, and the SEEMP. For the mid-term the Strategy identifies a basket of two complementary elements: a technical element, a goal-based marine fuel standard phasing down the GHG intensity of the fuel used; and an economic element, a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism. They were designed to be delivered as one instrument - the standard sets the trajectory, the price makes falling short dearer than complying - and delivering them means amending MARPOL Annex VI, which is what the question refers to.

5. The proposed new Chapter V - what the draft actually provides

That instrument is the IMO Net-Zero Framework, drafted as a new Chapter 5 of MARPOL Annex VI, 'Regulations on the IMO Net-Zero Framework', regulations 30 to 44. As drafted it would:

  • apply to all ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above, excluding ships trading solely within their own flag State's waters, ships not mechanically propelled, platforms and drilling rigs (reg 30);
  • require each ship, after the end of calendar year 2028 and annually thereafter, to calculate an attained annual GHG Fuel Intensity (GFI) in gCO2eq/MJ on a well-to-wake basis (reg 33);
  • set a target annual GFI in two tiers - a Base target and a stricter Direct compliance target - as a percentage reduction from a 2008 reference value of 93.3 gCO2eq/MJ: 4.0% and 17.0% in 2028, 8.0% and 21.0% in 2030, rising to 30.0% and 43.0% by 2035 (reg 35);
  • require a ship in deficit to buy remedial units by making GHG emissions pricing contributions, priced for 2028 to 2030 at US$100 per tonne CO2eq (Tier 1) and US$380 (Tier 2) (reg 36);
  • allow a ship beating the Direct compliance target to earn surplus units, which may be transferred to another ship, banked, or voluntarily cancelled, and which are valid for two calendar years (reg 36);
  • reward zero or near-zero (ZNZ) technologies, fuels and energy sources - those at not more than 19.0 gCO2eq/MJ, tightening to 14.0 from 1 January 2035 (reg 39);
  • channel contributions into an IMO Net-Zero Fund which rewards ZNZ uptake and supports innovation, capacity building and technology transfer in developing countries, governed by a board with assured representation of SIDS and LDCs (regs 40 and 41);
  • require a review every five years, which may amend the reduction factors and the ZNZ thresholds and may consider extending the chapter to ships of 400 gross tonnage and above (reg 44).

6. Where the amendments stood at this sitting

The stem says the amendments are being considered, and that is exactly right. MEPC 83, which met from 7 to 11 April 2025, considered and approved the draft amendments, including the Net-Zero Framework as new Chapter 5. On 11 April 2025 the Secretary-General transmitted the draft revised consolidated Annex VI to all Parties by Circular Letter No.5005, expressly in accordance with article 16(2)(a) of MARPOL, for consideration with a view to adoption at a second extraordinary session of the Committee, MEPC/ES.2, convened for 14 to 17 October 2025, under article 16(2)(b), (c) and (d).

So at the date of this examination the position was: the Framework was approved and formally circulated, but not yet adopted and not in force. The adoption session had not yet been held. If the amendments were adopted as scheduled and accepted under the tacit acceptance procedure, entry into force would follow after the stated period, with the first GFI reporting period being calendar year 2028.

The accurate summary is therefore in three parts: the Strategy is adopted policy; the short-term measures are in force; and the mid-term measures are an approved and circulated draft awaiting an adoption session. Every figure in Chapter V above is precise, and every one of them is provisional until adoption.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains two demands and prints no limbs, so the standing risk is an undifferentiated essay on decarbonisation. Steps 1 to 3 discharge the Strategy, step 4 is the hinge that explains why a Chapter V is needed at all, and steps 5 to 6 discharge the amendments. Step 6 is what turns a good answer into a full-marks one, because the examiner's own words - amendments being considered - show that he is testing status, not enthusiasm.

Read the stem as a date stamp

Amendments being considered under MARPOL Annex VI, including the inclusion of new Chapter-V is not loose drafting. In August 2025 it was a precise description: the text had been approved at MEPC 83 in April and circulated under article 16(2)(a), and the session convened to adopt it was still four months away. An answer that describes the Framework as being in force contradicts the question itself. An answer that gives the approval and the scheduled adoption is answering the question that was asked.

Strategy, approval, circulation, adoption, entry into force

This question is the live worked example of the machinery examined in Q7 of this same paper, and the two are best revised together. The sequence is: a strategy states intent; a committee approves a draft text; the Secretary-General circulates it to Parties under article 16(2)(a), which is the step that starts the clock; a session adopts it, which is what makes it an amendment; and it then enters into force, ordinarily by tacit acceptance. At this sitting the Net-Zero Framework had completed approval and circulation and had reached, but not passed, adoption. Being able to name which of those five stages an instrument has reached is worth more marks than any amount of detail about fuels.

Why the numbers are precise and provisional at the same time

The figures in step 5 are quoted from the draft regulations as circulated, so they are exact rather than approximate - the reduction factors sit in a table in draft regulation 35, and the two unit prices in draft regulation 36. But a draft is only a proposal until a session adopts it. The right way to write them in an examination is with the qualification attached: as drafted, the draft would require. That costs four words and protects the whole answer.

Common mistakes

  • Describing the Net-Zero Framework as adopted, in force, or as binding law. At this sitting it was an approved and circulated draft.
  • Treating the 2023 Strategy as mandatory. It is a policy resolution and binds no ship.
  • Mixing the levels of ambition with the indicative checkpoints - carbon intensity and fuel uptake against total annual emissions.
  • Dropping the striving for figures, which are part of the adopted wording.
  • Omitting the 2008 baseline, without which every percentage is meaningless.
  • Forgetting the well-to-wake basis, which is what stops emissions being exported upstream into fuel production.
  • Confusing GFI with CII. GFI is the GHG intensity of the fuel, in gCO2eq/MJ; CII is the operational carbon intensity of the ship.
  • Quoting the 2018 Initial Strategy targets. The question says revised.

Examiner traps

  • 'Revised' GHG strategy - the 2023 Strategy, resolution MEPC.377(80). A candidate quoting the 2018 figures has answered a different instrument.
  • 'Amendments being considered' - the status is part of the question, not background to it.
  • 'To achieve the decarbonisation goal and transition to a Net Zero future' invites rhetoric. The marks are in the resolution number, the percentages, the baseline, the regulation numbers and the status.

Understand this first

The phrase in the Exclusive Economic Zone of India tempts everyone into the same error. The EEZ is not Indian territory. India holds only functional rights there - over resources, artificial islands, research and the marine environment - and nothing in that list covers a person going missing from a ship. What happens on board a ship is an internal matter of the ship, and internal matters follow the flag. So the first question is never 'where did it happen' but 'whose flag is she flying' - and only then does India's role become clear: search and rescue always, because that duty is owed to everyone, but investigation and prosecution only if she is Indian.

Model written answer

1. a) The zones, and what the coastal State holds in each

UNCLOS measures the zones from the baselines, and the coastal State's authority thins progressively seaward - from full sovereignty, to defined functional rights, to none at all.

  • Internal waters - landward of the baselines. Full sovereignty, and no right of innocent passage.
  • Territorial sea, up to 12 n.m. - sovereignty, extending to the airspace above and the seabed and subsoil below, subject to innocent passage.
  • Contiguous zone, to 24 n.m. - not sovereignty but control, to prevent and punish infringement of customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws.
  • Exclusive Economic Zone, to 200 n.m. - sovereign rights, which is not the same as sovereignty.
  • Continental shelf - sovereign rights over the seabed and subsoil, existing independently of occupation or proclamation.
  • High seas - no State may validly claim sovereignty; the high seas freedoms apply.

2. What the coastal State may actually do

  • Territorial sea - full prescriptive and enforcement jurisdiction; its criminal and civil law applies. It may legislate on navigation, cables, fishing, pollution and customs, but must not hamper innocent passage, which ceases to be innocent on acts prejudicial to the peace, good order or security of the coastal State.
  • Contiguous zone - powers confined to those four subjects, and only to prevent or punish.
  • EEZ - Article 56 gives sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, living and non-living, together with jurisdiction over artificial islands and installations, marine scientific research and protection of the marine environment. Article 73 permits boarding, inspection and arrest for fisheries offences.
  • The limit is the whole point: outside those purposes the coastal State has no general jurisdiction in the EEZ. It is not territory.

3. The flag State - nationality, freedoms and exclusive jurisdiction

  • Article 91 - each State fixes the conditions for granting its nationality and for registration; a genuine link must exist between State and ship.
  • Article 92 - a ship sails under one flag only and is subject to the flag State's exclusive jurisdiction on the high seas.
  • Article 94 - the flag State must effectively exercise jurisdiction and control in administrative, technical and social matters - construction, equipment, seaworthiness, manning, labour conditions and training - and must hold an inquiry into serious casualties.
  • Article 58 - in the EEZ all States retain the freedoms of navigation and overflight, and Articles 88 to 115, which include Article 92, apply so far as they are not incompatible with the EEZ regime. The flag State's authority therefore travels with the ship into the EEZ.

4. How the two are reconciled - due regard

UNCLOS resolves the overlap by a reciprocal standard rather than a hierarchy. The coastal State exercises its EEZ rights with due regard to the rights and duties of other States; other States exercise their freedoms with due regard to the rights and duties of the coastal State and comply with its laws made in conformity with the Convention. Where the Convention attributes no right to either, the conflict is resolved on the basis of equity and all relevant circumstances. And across every zone, Article 98 obliges every master to render assistance to any person found in danger of being lost at sea.

5. b) The missing cadet - which law applies

The tempting answer is that Indian law applies because the ship was in India's EEZ. That is wrong. The EEZ is not Indian territory; India's authority there is functional - resources, installations, research, the marine environment - and a person missing from a ship engages none of it. Because Article 58 carries Article 92 into the EEZ, matters internal to the ship remain under the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State. The determining question is therefore whose flag the vessel flies, and the paper does not say.

  • Indian-flagged - Indian law applies in full, through the flag. Under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958: s.358, loss of life by reason of a casualty on board is a shipping casualty, and the master must give immediate notice to the officer appointed by the Central Government; s.359, that officer reports in writing to the Central Government and may hold a preliminary inquiry; s.452, the proper officer inquires into the cause of death, endorses the official log book as to whether the stated cause is in his opinion true, and if death appears to have been caused by violence or other improper means reports to the Director-General. Indian criminal law reaches the ship through section 1(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 - any offence committed on a ship registered in India wherever it may be.
  • Foreign-flagged - the flag State's law governs the investigation and any prosecution, and its Administration conducts the casualty investigation. India acts as coastal State and, decisively, as the State responsible for search and rescue within its search and rescue region - a duty owed regardless of flag under Article 98 and the SAR Convention. India may also be a substantially interested State if the cadet was an Indian national.

6. The actions to be taken

  1. Immediate. Raise the man overboard alarm and mark the position; muster and account for every person on board to establish who is missing and when last seen, because the search datum has to be reconstructed. Commence a search, return along the track, post lookouts, and broadcast to the MRCC and to ships in the vicinity.
  2. Preserve. Secure the cabin and the last known location; preserve CCTV, gangway and access records, rest-hour records and personal effects; take statements early; make full official log book entries.
  3. Notify. Owner and manager, the DPA, the flag State, the coordinating coastal State authority, the P&I club, the manning agent, and - through the company - the next of kin.
  4. Report and investigate. Loss of life makes this a very serious marine casualty, so a safety investigation follows under the Casualty Investigation Code alongside the flag State's inquiry; for an Indian ship the s.358 notice and the s.359 report apply, with the s.452 inquiry into cause of death on arrival.
  5. Company review and welfare. Treat it under the ISM Code as an accident and a non-conformity - safety of access, work overside, lifebuoy provision, fatigue, and bullying, harassment or isolation all examined honestly - and discharge the MLC 2006 obligations on repatriation of remains, compensation for death and support to dependants, while supporting the remaining crew.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) asks about both States and most answers describe only the coastal one. Splitting the limb into coastal State, flag State and the due-regard reconciliation guarantees that the flag half is present, and it sets up limb (b), which cannot be answered without it. Limb (b) is worth only 6 marks but it contains the single highest-value insight in the question, so it should be led with the jurisdictional point rather than with a list of actions.

Why the EEZ is the way it is

The EEZ was the great compromise of UNCLOS III. Coastal States - especially newly independent ones - wanted the fish and the oil off their coasts; maritime States wanted their ships and aircraft to keep moving. The zone gives each side what it actually needed: economic control to the coastal State and navigational freedom to everyone else. Understanding that bargain tells you at once what the coastal State can and cannot do, without memorising a list. If the issue is a resource, an installation, research or pollution, it is the coastal State's. If it is about the ship as a ship - her navigation, her crew, her internal affairs - it remains the flag State's.

Sovereignty, sovereign rights, jurisdiction - three different words

  • Sovereignty - the full authority of a State over its territory. Internal waters and territorial sea.
  • Sovereign rights - exclusive rights for a defined purpose. The EEZ and the continental shelf. Exclusive, but purposive.
  • Jurisdiction - the power to prescribe and enforce law over specified subject matter, such as artificial islands or marine scientific research in the EEZ.

The question uses the phrase sovereign rights and jurisdictions deliberately, and an answer that treats the three as interchangeable has missed what is being tested.

Missing is not the same as dead

The scenario says the cadet goes missing, not that he was seen to fall or that a body was recovered, and the distinction has practical and legal consequences. Practically, the search datum must be reconstructed from the last confirmed sighting rather than from a known position, which is why the muster is the critical first step and why gangway records and CCTV matter so much. Legally, a presumption of death normally requires a formal process and takes time, which affects wages, the seafarer employment agreement, insurance and the family's ability to deal with the estate. A candidate who writes as though a death certificate follows automatically has misunderstood the situation the crew is actually in.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming Indian law applies because the incident was in India's EEZ. The EEZ is not territory.
  • Forgetting to ask what flag the ship flies - the question does not say, and that omission is the test.
  • Describing only the coastal State's rights in limb (a) and ignoring the flag State.
  • Saying the coastal State has sovereignty in the EEZ, or that it has no rights there at all. Neither is right.
  • Citing the Indian Penal Code, which was replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 on 1 July 2024 - more than a year before this sitting.
  • Treating search and rescue as depending on jurisdiction. It does not: Article 98 and the SAR Convention apply regardless of flag.
  • Jumping straight to actions in limb (b) without answering the question asked, which is which laws will apply.

Examiner traps

  • 'Coastal state and Flag state' - both are named in the stem and both must be answered.
  • 'In the Exclusive Economic zone of India' - written to invite the wrong answer.
  • 'Which laws will apply' comes before 'what actions will be taken'. Answer them in that order or the analysis reads as an afterthought.
  • The printed phrase 'Explain your answer suitable explanations' is incomplete on the paper. It means give reasons; do not lose time over it.

Q1 LLMC Principles, Liabilities and Salvor Limitation
16 marks		 (a) The three principles the LLMC regime is built on → How LLMC links to the other liability conventions → (b) The liabilities that are subject to limitation → The claims left outside, and the conduct that breaks the limit → (c) The principles of the Salvage Convention 1989 → Why salvors may limit, and the limit that applies LLMC 1976 Articles 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6(4), read with the 1996 Protocol as amended by the 2012 amendments in force 8 June 2015; and the International Convention on Salvage 1989, Articles 8, 13 and 14.
LLMC adopted 19 November 1976, in force 1 December 1986; 1996 Protocol in force 13 May 2004; 2012 amendments in force 8 June 2015		 Answering limb (c) without producing a figure. The stem says 'specify the current liability limit applicable to them', so the marks require the two-step chain - Article 6(4) deems a salvor with no ship to be of 1,500 tons, which falls in the band not exceeding 2,000 GT, giving 3.02 million SDR for loss of life or personal injury and 1.51 million SDR for property under the 1996 Protocol as amended. The second trap is asserting a figure without identifying the instrument: a State bound only by the unamended 1976 Convention applies lower limits. The third is missing that salvage and general average are themselves EXCEPTED claims under Article 3, so a salvor's earned award cannot be cut down by the regime that he himself may invoke.
Q2 CII - Concept, Commercial Impact and Shortcomings

CII applies from 5,000 gross tonnage; EEXI from 400 gt		 Answering limb (b) with the remedies the industry has asked for rather than those actually adopted. By this sitting the adopted remedies were narrow and procedural: Phase 1 of the regulation 28.11 review completed at MEPC 83 in April 2025, the reduction factors extended to 2030 by MEPC.400(83), and the SEEMP Guidelines amended so that alternative metrics could be developed later. A cargo-based metric had not been adopted, and the metric that causes most of the criticism was unchanged. The related trap is describing the review as finished: its regulation 28.11 deadline of 1 January 2026 had not yet arrived when this paper was sat. Finally, do not treat the proposed Net-Zero Framework as an existing obligation - at this sitting it was a circulated draft. See Q8.
Q3 Formal Safety Assessment for Lithium Batteries in RORO Carriage
16 marks		 a) What FSA is, and who it is for → Step 1 - identifying the hazards of this cargo → Step 2 - assessing the risk on a ro-ro deck → Steps 3 and 4 - risk control options and whether they pay → Step 5 - what the recommendations had become by this sitting → b) Measures by stakeholder MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 for the FSA methodology, applied to ro-ro fire safety; with MSC.1/Circ.1615 as the operative IMO instrument at this sitting and resolutions MSC.550(108) and MSC.555(108) adopted but not yet in force.
MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2 dated 9 April 2018; five FSA steps		 Writing that the amended SOLAS II-2 and FSS Code requirements - individually identifiable detection, deluge alignment, video monitoring, A-60 boundaries - applied at this sitting. They had been adopted at MSC 108 in May 2024 but did not enter into force until 1 January 2026, five months after this paper, and then only for ships constructed on or after that date, with existing ships reaching compliance by the first survey on or after 1 January 2028. On the day, what governed was SOLAS II-2 as it then stood, the IMDG Code and the interim guidelines in MSC.1/Circ.1615. The second trap is treating FSA as a shipboard risk assessment rather than an IMO rule-making tool. The third is saying the fire can be smothered: thermal runaway generates its own oxidant, so cooling and boundary protection limit propagation rather than extinguishing the cell.
Q4 Human Element in STCW and IMO Fatigue Guidance
16 marks		 A) What the human element means in STCW → Competence-based standards → Fitness for duty - the physical human being → Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations → B) The IMO guidance on fatigue → How the guidance and the rules fit together STCW regulation VIII/1 with section A-VIII/1 for fitness for duty and hours of rest, and regulation I/14 for company responsibility; with MSC.1/Circ.1598 as the IMO guidance on fatigue.
Manila amendments in force 1 January 2012		 Quoting 70 hours as the normal weekly rest requirement. The norm is 77; 70 is the floor that applies only where an exception has been authorised. This error has a specific and dangerous source: a freely available IMO circular copy of section A-VIII/1, STCW.6/Circ.1, reproduces the pre-Manila 1995 text - five paragraphs, 70 hours weekly and no 14-hour interval provision at all. It looks official because it is official; it is simply the wrong edition. The second trap is treating MSC.1/Circ.1598 as mandatory - it is recommendatory, and the binding requirements are in STCW chapter VIII and MLC 2006. The third is answering limb B with general remarks about tiredness instead of naming the circular and its six modules.
Q5 Unseaworthy and Unsafe Ships under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958
16 marks		 What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship → The offence, and the defences the Act allows → What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her → The real difference between the two → The owner's obligation to the crew → How the obligation is policed, and the statute in force at this sitting Merchant Shipping Act 1958 sections 334, 335 and 336, which were the operative Indian law throughout August 2025 and remained so until 15 March 2026.
MSA 1958 s.334 unseaworthy ship; s.335 owner's obligation to crew; s.336 unsafe ship and detention		 Answering with the English common-law or marine-insurance idea of seaworthiness as a warranty in a contract of carriage. This question is on Indian statute, where unseaworthiness founds criminal liability and a non-excludable duty owed to the crew. The second trap is treating unseaworthy and unsafe as synonyms - they are a state and a power respectively, used by the Act for different purposes. The third is a currency trap in reverse: the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 received assent in the very month of this sitting, but it did not commence until 15 March 2026, so an answer given on the 2025 Act is answering under a statute that was not yet law.
Q6 General Average - Essential Features and a Refloating Claim
16 marks		 a) What a general average act is → The essential features → The lettered rules that qualify the definition → How the loss is shared, and what general average is not → b) The refloating scenario - the rule that governs it → Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not York-Antwerp Rules Rule A with the Rule Paramount for limb (a), and Rule VII, Damage to Machinery and Boilers, for limb (b).
York-Antwerp Rules, Rule A - the definition of a general average act		 Answering limb (b) 'yes, it is a general average sacrifice' without naming Rule VII, without testing whether the ship was in a position of peril, and without stating the converse. Rule VII allows the claim only where the ship is ashore and in peril and the machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety at the risk of such damage - and it then prohibits absolutely, 'in any circumstances', any allowance for machinery damage caused by working the propelling machinery while the ship is afloat. The same burnt bearings are therefore general average or particular average according to the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked. A second trap in limb (a) is forgetting that there must be a common adventure: a ship in ballast with no cargo has no general average, because there is no other interest to contribute.
Q7 Entry into Force of an IMO Convention
16 marks		 Adoption is not entry into force → How a State expresses consent to be bound → The two terms the question names → The entry into force provision, and why it matters → Conventions of an important technical nature → Keeping the convention current once in force The entry into force final clause of the convention concerned, read with the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties Articles 14(2) and 15; and for amendment, the tacit acceptance procedure in SOLAS 1974 and MARPOL article 16.
SOLAS 1974: adopted 1 November 1974, in force 25 May 1980; condition 25 States and 50% of world merchant gross tonnage		 Treating adoption, signature, ratification and entry into force as one event. Adoption settles the text and binds nobody; signature ordinarily does not bind either; the State is bound only when it deposits a formal instrument; and the convention operates only when its own entry into force condition is met. The second trap is answering the technical-convention limb with a number of States and omitting the percentage of world merchant gross tonnage, which is the specific thing that limb asks for. The third is stopping at international entry into force and forgetting that the flag State must still give the instrument domestic effect before it can be enforced against a ship.
Q8 Revised IMO GHG Strategy and the Proposed MARPOL Annex VI Chapter V

Carbon intensity: at least 40% reduction in CO2 per transport work by 2030, compared to 2008		 Writing as though the Net-Zero Framework were law. At this sitting the draft chapter 5 had been approved at MEPC 83 and circulated for adoption, but not adopted and not in force, and the session convened to adopt it had not yet met. Quote the figures as what the draft would require, never as what ships must do. It had not - it lay four months in the future when this paper was sat. The third trap is treating the 2023 Strategy itself as mandatory; it is a policy resolution.
Q9 UNCLOS Maritime Zones and a Missing Cadet in the EEZ
16 marks		 a) The zones, and what the coastal State holds in each → What the coastal State may actually do → The flag State - nationality, freedoms and exclusive jurisdiction → How the two are reconciled - due regard → b) The missing cadet - which law applies → The actions to be taken UNCLOS Articles 2, 33, 56, 58, 91, 92, 94 and 98; for the Indian limb, the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 sections 358, 359 and 452 as they stood at this sitting, with the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 section 1(5).
Territorial sea up to 12 nautical miles; contiguous zone up to 24; EEZ up to 200, all measured from the baselines		 Assuming that because the incident occurred in India's Exclusive Economic Zone, Indian law applies. The EEZ is not territory: India's rights there are functional and concern resources, artificial islands, research and the marine environment, none of which reaches a person missing from a ship. What happens on board follows the flag, and the paper deliberately does not say what that flag is. India's search and rescue responsibility is a separate duty and does apply regardless of flag. A second trap is citing the Indian Penal Code, which the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 replaced on 1 July 2024, more than a year before this paper was sat.
