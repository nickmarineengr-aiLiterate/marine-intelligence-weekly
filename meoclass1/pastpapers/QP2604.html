Understand this first

The thing to hold onto is that MARPOL deliberately runs at two speeds, and Article 16 is where the gearbox lives. The articles are the political bargain between States, so they change only when States expressly say yes. The Annexes are engineering - discharge limits, tank arrangements, certificate forms - and if those needed express ratification every time, the Convention would never keep up with the fleet. So for the technical layer the drafters reversed the default: once an amendment is adopted, saying nothing means accepting it. Everything else in the article - the thresholds, the ten months, the opt-out - is detail around that one inversion.

Model written answer

1. What Article 16 does, and why one convention needs several routes

Article 16 is the amendment machinery of MARPOL. Paragraph 16(1) sets the frame: the Convention may be amended by any of the procedures specified in the article. There are two routes to adoption - after consideration by the Organization (16(2)) and by a Conference of Parties (16(3)) - and, within them, the acceptance mechanism differs according to which part of the Convention is being amended. That is the flexibility the question refers to: the articles are framework and are amended slowly, by express consent; the Annexes and their appendices carry technical detail and are amended quickly, by default.

2. The steps common to every amendment considered by the Organization

  • Proposal and circulation (16(2)(a)) - any Party may propose an amendment. It goes to the Organization, and the Secretary-General circulates it to all Members of the Organization and all Parties at least six months before it is considered.
  • Reference to an appropriate body (16(2)(b)) - in practice the Marine Environment Protection Committee.
  • Participation (16(2)(c)) - Parties to the Convention are entitled to take part whether or not they are Members of the Organization.
  • Adoption (16(2)(d)) - by a two-thirds majority of only the Parties present and voting. Members of the Organization that are not Parties do not vote.
  • Communication for acceptance (16(2)(e)) - the Secretary-General sends the adopted text to all Parties. Adoption is not entry into force; acceptance is the step that follows, and it is here that the routes diverge.

3. Amending an Article - explicit acceptance

Under 16(2)(f)(i) an amendment to an article of the Convention is deemed accepted only on the date it is accepted by two thirds of the Parties, the combined merchant fleets of which constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant fleet. Both conditions must be satisfied - a count of States and a tonnage share. Nothing is presumed: a State that says nothing has not accepted. Protocol II (arbitration) follows this same procedure by 16(2)(f)(v), and by 16(5) so does the adoption of an entirely new Annex. This is deliberately demanding, because these provisions define the obligations themselves.

4. Amending an Annex - tacit acceptance by default

16(2)(f)(ii) is the provision that makes MARPOL efficient. An amendment to an Annex is deemed accepted in accordance with the procedure in (f)(iii) - the tacit procedure - unless the appropriate body, at the time of adoption, determines that the explicit (f)(i) test shall apply instead. Tacit acceptance is therefore the default for the technical Annexes, and explicit acceptance the exception the Committee must positively choose.

The reservation attached to it is examinable. At any time before an Annex amendment enters into force, a Party may notify the Secretary-General that its express approval is necessary before the amendment binds it, and the Secretary-General notifies the other Parties of that notification and its date. Protocol I (reports on incidents involving harmful substances) follows the Annex procedures by 16(2)(f)(iv).

5. Amending an Appendix to an Annex - the tacit mechanism itself

The tacit procedure is drafted in 16(2)(f)(iii), where it applies directly to an appendix to an Annex - the certificate forms, lists and schedules. The amendment is deemed accepted at the end of a period fixed by the appropriate body at adoption, which shall be not less than ten months, unless within that period an objection is communicated to the Organization by not less than one third of the Parties, or by Parties whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of world gross tonnage, whichever condition is fulfilled.

Note the contrast with limb 3, and make it explicitly. The acceptance test for an article is conjunctive - two thirds of Parties and 50 per cent of tonnage. The objection test here is disjunctive - one third of Parties or 50 per cent of tonnage, whichever is met first. Silence accepts; only a sufficiently weighty objection defeats.

6. Amendment by a Conference of Parties

Under 16(3), on the request of a Party concurred in by at least one third of the Parties, the Organization shall convene a Conference of Parties to consider amendments. A Conference adopts by a two-thirds majority of the Parties present and voting, and the Secretary-General communicates the amendment to all Contracting Parties for acceptance. Unless the Conference decides otherwise, acceptance and entry into force then follow 16(2)(f) and (g) - so the Conference route changes the forum, not the acceptance mechanism. This route produced the 1978 Protocol; it is available but rarely used, because the Committee route is faster.

7. Entry into force, and the limits on any amendment

  • 16(2)(g)(i) - for an article, Protocol II, or an Annex or Protocol I not under the tacit route, the amendment enters into force six months after acceptance, and binds only those Parties which declared that they accept it.
  • 16(2)(g)(ii) - under the tacit route the amendment enters into force six months after acceptance for all Parties, except those which had declared that they do not accept it or had made the express-approval declaration under (f)(ii). This is the whole practical difference: silence binds you.
  • 16(4) - for an Optional Annex (III, IV, V and VI), 'Party to the Convention' means a Party bound by that Annex; and a Party declining an Annex amendment is treated as a non-Party only for the purpose of that amendment.
  • 16(6) - unless expressly provided otherwise, an amendment relating to the structure of a ship applies only to ships for which the building contract is placed, or the keel laid in the absence of a contract, on or after the date the amendment comes into force. Existing ships are not required to be rebuilt.
  • 16(7) - an amendment to a Protocol or an Annex must relate to the substance of that Protocol or Annex and be consistent with the articles. The technical fast track cannot be used to change the framework.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question hands you its own structure - 'depending on what part of MARPOL is being amended' - and the marks follow it. An answer organised as article / Annex / appendix / Conference is answering the question asked. An answer that simply describes tacit acceptance for three pages is answering a different, easier one. Give the common procedural steps once (16(2)(a)-(e)), then take each part of the Convention in turn, then close on entry into force. The single most valuable paragraph is the contrast between the conjunctive acceptance test for articles and the disjunctive objection test for the tacit route.

The idea behind the two-speed design

MARPOL is called flexible because it is deliberately built at two speeds. The articles carry the bargain the States struck - scope, jurisdiction, enforcement, dispute settlement. Changing that bargain needs express consent, so it is slow and it binds only those who say yes. The Annexes and appendices carry engineering: oil discharge limits, tank arrangements, sulphur limits, survey and certificate forms. If those needed express ratification by two thirds of States every time, MARPOL would be permanently a decade behind the fleet. So the drafters inverted the default for the technical layer: adoption plus silence equals acceptance. 16(7) is the safeguard that keeps the fast track honest - a technical amendment must stay consistent with the articles, so the quick procedure cannot be used to rewrite the slow one.

Getting the thresholds exactly right

Candidates lose marks by blurring three different numbers. Adoption is a two-thirds majority of the Parties present and voting (16(2)(d)) - a meeting-room vote. Acceptance of an article amendment needs two thirds of the Parties whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross tonnage (16(2)(f)(i)) - two conditions, both required. Defeating a tacit amendment needs objection by one third of the Parties or Parties holding not less than 50 per cent of world gross tonnage, whichever is fulfilled (16(2)(f)(iii)) - either will do. And the tacit period is not less than ten months, fixed at adoption; with the further six months to entry into force under 16(2)(g)(ii), sixteen months is the practical floor, which is why amendments are commonly seen entering into force roughly eighteen months to two years after adoption.

Common mistakes

  • Saying MARPOL 'is amended by tacit acceptance' and stopping. That is true of the Annexes and appendices and false of the articles, and the question expressly asks you to distinguish by part.
  • Confusing adoption with entry into force. Adoption is a vote; acceptance is a separate stage; entry into force is six months later again.
  • Giving the objection threshold as 'two thirds'. Objection is one third of Parties, or the 50 per cent tonnage limb.
  • Treating the two limbs of the article test as alternatives. 16(2)(f)(i) requires two thirds of Parties and that those Parties hold at least 50 per cent of world tonnage.
  • Forgetting that a Party can opt out of tacit acceptance in advance under 16(2)(f)(ii) by requiring its express approval.
  • Omitting the Conference route in 16(3) altogether - the question says 'multiple ways', and there are two routes to adoption, not one.
  • Asserting that a new Annex is added by tacit acceptance. 16(5) puts a new annex on the article procedure.

Examiner traps

'A sulphur limit amendment was adopted last October. Does it bind India automatically?' If it is an Annex VI amendment adopted under the tacit route, then yes - unless India objected within the period fixed at adoption, or had previously notified that its express approval is necessary. Silence binds.

'Could MEPC use tacit acceptance to change Article 4 on violations?' No. Article amendments run under 16(2)(f)(i), and 16(7) requires an Annex or Protocol amendment to relate to the substance of that Annex or Protocol and to be consistent with the articles.

'A new double-hull requirement is adopted this year - does your existing ship have to be rebuilt?' Not by force of Article 16. Under 16(6), unless expressly provided otherwise, an amendment relating to the structure of a ship applies only to ships contracted, or keel-laid in the absence of a contract, on or after the date it comes into force. Note the qualifier: MARPOL amendments frequently do expressly provide otherwise, so check the amendment.

Likely oral follow-up

  • How long before consideration must a proposed amendment be circulated? At least six months (16(2)(a)).
  • Who may vote on adoption? Only the Parties to the Convention present and voting, by a two-thirds majority - whether or not they are Members of the Organization.
  • What is the minimum tacit acceptance period? Not less than ten months, fixed by the appropriate body at adoption.
  • How is a tacit amendment defeated? Objection by not less than one third of the Parties, or by Parties holding not less than 50 per cent of world gross tonnage.
  • How long after acceptance does an amendment enter into force? Six months.
  • Which procedure applies to Protocol I and Protocol II? Protocol I follows the Annexes; Protocol II follows the articles.
  • How is a wholly new Annex adopted? By the article procedure, under 16(5).
  • What does Article 16(4) do for Optional Annexes? 'Party to the Convention' is read as a Party bound by that Annex, and a Party declining an amendment is a non-Party only for that amendment.

Memory framework

Two routes, three targets, two clocks. The two routes to adoption are the Organization (16(2)) and a Conference (16(3)). The three targets are the article, the Annex and the appendix - slow, fast-by-default, fast. The two clocks are the tacit period of not less than ten months and the six months from acceptance to entry into force. For the thresholds: AND for articles, OR against annexes - two thirds of Parties and half the tonnage to accept an article; one third of Parties or half the tonnage to object to a tacit amendment.

Understand this first

Two things must be kept apart, because the question mixes them. Formal Safety Assessment is not a risk assessment you carry out on board - it is the structured method the regulator uses to decide whether a rule is worth making, and whether it would be worth what it costs. So this answer is written from the rule-maker's chair, not the Chief Engineer's. The cargo is what makes it interesting. A lithium battery fire is not an ordinary vehicle fire: it generates its own oxygen, so smothering does not stop it; it can reignite hours after it appears to be out; and it sits on a ro-ro deck, which is close to the worst possible geometry - a long undivided space, vehicles packed tight, and a fixed system designed for a fuel fire. Work the standard steps against those facts and the control options almost choose themselves: detect earlier, contain locally, cool for far longer. And be honest that some options cost more than the harm they prevent, which is precisely what the cost-benefit step is for.

Model written answer

1. a) What FSA is, and who it is for

Formal Safety Assessment is set out in MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment (FSA) for use in the IMO rule-making process, of 9 April 2018. The title states its purpose: a structured, auditable methodology by which the Organization decides whether, and how, to regulate a hazard, balancing safety benefit against cost. It is not a shipboard risk assessment or a permit-to-work system. The question is therefore how a proportionate rule for this cargo was, and is being, arrived at.

2. Step 1 - identifying the hazards of this cargo

  • Thermal runaway - an exothermic internal reaction that becomes self-sustaining and generates its own oxidant, so it cannot be smothered and continues without external air;
  • propagation cell to cell and module to module, and thence vehicle to vehicle in a close stow;
  • initiators - internal cell defects, mechanical damage in handling or lashing, over-charge, high state of charge on shipment, charging on board;
  • vent gas - flammable and toxic, able to accumulate in an enclosed vehicle space and produce a gas explosion rather than a simple fire;
  • re-ignition hours or days after apparent extinction, defeating a normal fire-out judgement;
  • mis-declaration and concealment - cells as cargo may be undeclared or wrongly classified, and a battery electric vehicle is not a labelled package at all, so the energy is invisible to the stowage plan.

3. Step 2 - assessing the risk on a ro-ro deck

Risk is frequency times consequence, estimated from casualty returns, incident and near-miss data, cell testing and fire modelling - with the Rev.2 requirement that the reliability, uncertainty and validity of that data be assessed and reported rather than assumed.

  • Frequency is low per vehicle but rising with fleet electrification, and exposure is concentrated - one deck may hold thousands of units;
  • consequence escalates because a ro-ro space is a large undivided volume, vehicles stowed centimetres apart, with no cargo-hold fire divisions and ramps and ventilation trunks carrying heat and smoke between decks;
  • on a ro-pax the dominant risk is to life; on a pure car and truck carrier it is total loss of the ship, and a series of well-known casualties supplied the evidence;
  • manual firefighting on a loaded deck is near impossible, so the model must assume fixed systems and boundaries are what will actually be available.

4. Steps 3 and 4 - risk control options and whether they pay

Risk control options are generated and grouped, then tested by cost-benefit assessment using indices such as the cost of averting a fatality, so a measure is recommended only where the safety gain justifies the cost across the world fleet:

  • detection - earlier, better-located, individually identifiable so the seat of fire is known, with combined smoke and heat sensing;
  • suppression - drencher and water-based systems of adequate coverage and capacity, aligned with detection sections so the right section operates;
  • containment - insulated boundaries, ventilation shut-down and control of openings;
  • operational and design - state-of-charge limits on shipment, segregation and dedicated stowage, restrictions on charging at sea, video monitoring, crew procedures;
  • options are ranked, and those effective but disproportionately costly are rejected transparently - which is why the outcome is a specific set of measures rather than everything technically possible.

5. Step 5 - what the recommendations actually became

The risk-based work on ro-ro fire safety - notably the EMSA FIRESAFE I and FIRESAFE II studies of fire dynamics, system performance and operational factors - entered the regulatory system in two stages:

  • MSC.1/Circ.1615, Interim Guidelines for Minimizing the Incidence and Consequences of Fires in Ro-Ro Spaces and Special Category Spaces, carried key outcomes into guidance while mandatory work continued;
  • resolution MSC.550(108) then amended SOLAS chapter II-2 regulations 7 and 20 and MSC.555(108) amended FSS Code chapters 7 and 9, both adopted at MSC 108 in May 2024 and in force on 1 January 2026 - that is, on 1 January of the year in which this paper was sat;
  • requirements include individually identifiable fixed detection with combined smoke and heat detectors, detection aligned with deluge sections, detection on weather decks used for vehicles, an effective video monitoring system with at least seven days of replay at a continuously manned control station, and A-60 boundaries on passenger ships carrying more than 36 passengers;
  • they apply to ships constructed on or after 1 January 2026, existing ships in scope complying not later than the first survey on or after 1 January 2028;
  • further amendments aimed at new energy vehicles were still in development, so the regulatory answer is partly delivered and continuing.

6. b) Measures by stakeholder to address the safety challenges

  • IMO - complete the new-energy-vehicle amendments, keep IMDG provisions current, maintain interim guidance meanwhile;
  • flag State and class - approve and survey the amended detection, suppression and boundary arrangements, and issue guidance ahead of the compliance dates;
  • shipper, manufacturer and packer - correct IMDG classification, packing and marking, state-of-charge control, testing and quality control, and no offering of damaged, defective or recalled batteries;
  • port and terminal - screen and refuse visibly damaged vehicles, check declared state of charge, separate electric vehicles in marshalling areas, inform the ship promptly;
  • ship operator - a written stowage and segregation policy, limits on charging on board, training in lithium fire behaviour, vehicle-deck drills, thermal imaging, and a plan that assumes re-ignition;
  • ship's staff - verify cargo against the declaration, maintain detection and drencher readiness, patrol during and after loading, and fight fire by early activation of fixed systems and boundary cooling rather than entering a loaded deck;
  • insurers and industry bodies - loss-prevention guidance, incident and near-miss data sharing, and premium signals rewarding compliant stowage - the same data that feeds the next FSA.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The 10-mark limb is a method question, and method questions are marked on whether the method is visible. Naming the five steps and then walking the cargo through them is what separates a top answer from a general essay about battery fires. Step 5 is where most candidates stop short: an FSA that produces no recommendation has not been applied, so ending with the actual resolutions is what closes the loop.

Why FSA exists at all

Before FSA, IMO regulation was largely reactive - a casualty happened, and a rule followed. FSA was introduced after the Herald of Free Enterprise and Piper Alpha era to allow the Organization to act proactively and proportionately, on evidence rather than on the emotional weight of the most recent disaster, and to be able to say publicly why a proposed rule was or was not worth its cost. That is why the Guidelines insist on auditability, traceability and the explicit reporting of uncertainty: an FSA is as much an accountability document as a technical one.

The lithium hazard in engineering terms

Thermal runaway begins when a cell's internal temperature passes the point at which exothermic decomposition of the electrolyte and cathode outpaces heat loss. The decomposition liberates oxygen from the cathode material, which is why the reaction sustains itself without air and why smothering agents - CO2, foam blanket, an inert atmosphere - do not stop it. Cooling is the only lever the ship holds, and it works by removing heat from cells that have not yet reached onset temperature, so its real objective is to stop propagation to neighbouring vehicles rather than to extinguish the cell already in runaway. That is also why re-ignition is normal rather than surprising: the cells that were merely heated may still cross the threshold hours later.

Vehicles, containers and packages are three different regulatory problems

  • Cells and batteries as cargo - dangerous goods under the IMDG Code, class 9, with UN numbers such as 3480 and 3481 for lithium-ion and 3090 and 3091 for lithium-metal, subject to packing, marking, documentation and segregation.
  • Batteries in or with equipment - the same UN family but different provisions, and often carried under exceptions.
  • A battery electric vehicle - carried as a vehicle, not as a declared dangerous goods package. This is the regulatory gap: the ship may know exactly how many tonnes of declared lithium cells she has in containers, and not know how much energy is sitting on the vehicle deck.

Common mistakes

  • Describing FSA as a shipboard risk assessment. Read the circular's title.
  • Listing the five steps and then never using them - a hazard essay wearing an FSA hat.
  • Saying water must never be used on a lithium fire. Cooling and boundary protection with copious water is the accepted tactic.
  • Saying the fire can be smothered with CO2. The reaction supplies its own oxidant.
  • Declaring the fire out when flames stop. Re-ignition must be assumed and monitored for.
  • Treating a battery electric vehicle as though it were declared dangerous goods, or vice versa.

Model written answer

1. Principles of modern salvage law

Modern salvage law rests on the International Convention on Salvage, 1989, in force since 14 July 1996, which replaced a purely property-based regime with one that also rewards protection of the environment. Its principles are:

  • Voluntary service - the salvor owes no pre-existing duty; a tug already under a towage contract is not salving.
  • Real danger to a vessel or other property in navigable waters.
  • "No cure, no pay" - by Article 12, only operations which have had a useful result give rise to payment.
  • A reward fixed to encourage salvage, on the ten criteria of Article 13(1) - salved value; skill and efforts in preventing environmental damage; measure of success; degree of danger; skill in salving vessel, property and life; time, expenses and losses; risk of liability; promptness; availability of salvage equipment; and its readiness, efficiency and value. The reward may not exceed the salved value.
  • Environmental protection in its own right - where the vessel or cargo threatened environmental damage, Article 14 gives the salvor special compensation (his expenses) even with no Article 13 reward, increasable by up to 30%, and by a tribunal to a maximum of 100% of expenses.
  • Duties both ways (Article 8) - the salvor to exercise due care, minimise environmental damage and accept other salvors when reasonably required; the owner and master to co-operate and accept redelivery when reasonably requested.

In practice salvage is contracted on Lloyd's Open Form; because Article 14 proved slow and contentious to assess, the industry added the SCOPIC clause in 2000 - an agreed tariff with a fixed uplift, which the salvor may invoke in place of Article 14.

2. What General Average is

General Average is the principle that where a sacrifice or extraordinary expenditure is made deliberately to save a common maritime adventure, the loss is shared by all the interests saved rather than left on the party that bore it. Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules: there is a general average act "when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure". In Indian law a general average loss is defined at section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. Every element must be present: a real common peril; an extraordinary act, not one the ship was already bound to do; intentional and voluntary; reasonable; for the common safety, not one interest alone; and property saved to contribute. The Rules are contractual, not statutory - they bind only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them, which is why those documents name the edition.

3. Entitlement

The party who made the sacrifice or incurred the expenditure is entitled to contribution from every interest saved - ship, cargo and freight at risk - rateably on their contributory values at the time and place the adventure ends. It requires the Rule A elements and a loss that is a direct consequence of the act. It is defeated by the actionable fault of the claiming party: a shipowner whose unseaworthiness caused the casualty recovers nothing, because the Rules preserve the other parties' defences. Since cargo is released before the adjustment is finished, the owner exercises a lien on cargo and takes security first - an average bond with a general average guarantee from cargo underwriters, or a cash deposit.

4. Artificial general average

"Artificial" general average describes allowances which the numbered Rules permit although they do not satisfy the strict common-safety test in Rule A. The clearest examples are port of refuge expenses and the wages and maintenance of crew during the detention there: once the ship has reached a place of safety the common safety is already secured, so that further expenditure is incurred for the common benefit of completing the adventure rather than to escape peril. The Rules allow it as a practical commercial compromise. The term is practitioner usage, not a defined category of the Rules, and the examinable point is the distinction it marks - general average in principle rests on common safety, while in practice the Rules also extend it to common benefit.

5. Adjustment

Adjustment turns the casualty into a settled apportionment, carried out by an average adjuster - appointed and paid by the shipowner but required to act impartially between all interests. The adjuster neither declares general average nor determines liability. The work is to collect security before cargo is released; decide which sacrifices and expenditures are allowable under the applicable edition; ascertain contributory values at the termination of the adventure; apportion rateably; and issue the general average statement. Large casualties take years, which is why the security taken at the outset matters.

6. Contestation

The adjustment is not binding. It is a skilled statement of account and strong evidence, but any interest - most often cargo underwriters - may contest it. The usual grounds are that there was no general average act at all; that the expenditure was not extraordinary or not reasonable; that an allowance is not permitted by the incorporated edition; that contributory values or allowances are wrongly assessed; that the wrong edition has been applied; or, most powerfully, that the casualty was caused by the actionable fault of the claiming party, so that no contribution is due at all. It is resolved by negotiation, arbitration, or the courts of the jurisdiction the contract of carriage provides for.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are printed, which is a gift: 4 + 4 + 8. Give salvage a quarter of the answer and no more, define General Average tightly, and then spend half the answer on the four named sub-items. The commonest way to lose marks here is to write a long general essay on general average and then deal with entitlement, artificial, adjustment and contestation in a sentence each. Each of those four is worth about two marks - give each of them its own heading so the examiner can see all four have been answered.

The four sub-items are a lifecycle - use that

They are not four unrelated definitions. Read in order they describe what happens after a casualty: who may claim (entitlement), what may be claimed even though strict principle would exclude it (artificial), how the claim is worked out (adjustment), and how it is fought (contestation). Presenting them as a sequence turns a list into an argument, and it makes them far easier to recall.

Getting "Artificial" right

This is the item most candidates cannot place, and precision matters. Artificial general average is not fraud and not a fictitious claim. It is the name practitioners give to allowances the numbered Rules permit even though the common safety has already been achieved - classically port of refuge expenses and crew wages and maintenance during the detention. The underlying distinction is common safety (the Rule A test) against common benefit (completing the adventure). Say that distinction explicitly and the mark is earned. Be careful to present the term as accepted usage rather than as a defined category of the Rules, because it is not defined in them.

Salvage and General Average are different things

They meet - a salvage award is commonly allowed in general average and apportioned across the interests - but they are not the same. Salvage is a reward to a stranger for a voluntary service to property in danger, governed by a convention. General Average is a sharing of loss between parties already joined in one adventure, governed by contract. A candidate who blurs them will struggle with 'artificial', because the reason salvage and port of refuge expenses need special treatment is precisely that they sit at the join.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the York-Antwerp Rules as law. They are contractual and apply only because a bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them; the edition named in the contract governs.
  • Saying the average adjuster declares general average or decides liability. The shipowner, normally through the master, declares it; the adjuster apportions it.
  • Forgetting security. Without an average bond and a GA guarantee, the shipowner has released the cargo and lost the lien that protects the claim.
  • Omitting the actionable fault defence, which is the single most important limit on entitlement and the strongest ground of contestation.
  • Writing that a salvor is always paid. Article 12 is "no cure, no pay" - and the Article 14 environmental exception exists precisely because that rule was leaving environmental salvage unrewarded.
  • Quoting an Article 13 reward that exceeds the salved value. It cannot.

Examiner traps

"The salvor saved the environment but not the ship - what does he get?" Not an Article 13 reward, because there was no useful result in property terms. He may claim Article 14 special compensation - his expenses, increasable by up to 30% and by a tribunal up to 100% - or, if invoked, SCOPIC remuneration under the contract.

"Is the adjuster's statement final?" No. It is evidence, not a judgment, and it can be contested on the grounds above.

"Which edition of the Rules applies?" Whichever the contract of carriage incorporates. YAR 2016 was adopted by the CMI in May 2016 and BIMCO forms refer to it, but older contracts still commonly incorporate YAR 1994.

Model written answer

1. What the owner is exposed to

Identify the exposures before naming any cover: loss of the ship; loss of the cargo; oil pollution from persistent cargo oil and bunkers; salvage, already under way; wreck location, marking and removal; crew death or injury; and claims by the coastal state and third parties. She sank in the territorial sea, where by UNCLOS Article 2 the coastal state has full sovereignty - so its law and its convention obligations govern what may be demanded of the owner.

2. Hull and Machinery cover

  • H&M insures the owner's own property interest in the ship, on standard terms such as the Institute Time Clauses (Hulls), the International Hull Clauses or the Nordic Plan. Explosion is ordinarily an insured peril.
  • Because the vessel sank, the claim will be an actual total loss where the ship is irretrievably lost, or a constructive total loss where recovery and repair would exceed the insured value - in which case the owner serves a notice of abandonment.
  • Sue and labour expenses reasonably incurred to avert or minimise the loss are recoverable, which is significant here because salvage was in progress.
  • General average contributions and salvage charges falling on the ship are recoverable under the policy.

3. P&I cover

P&I is the mutual cover for third-party liabilities that H&M does not touch, and in this casualty it does most of the work: oil pollution, wreck removal where required by law, cargo liability, crew death, injury and repatriation, damage to fixed and floating objects, and certain fines. Clubs in the International Group share risk through a pooling and reinsurance structure, which is what makes a casualty of this size insurable. The club also issues the "blue cards" on which the state issues the ship's compulsory insurance certificates.

4. Oil pollution liability conventions

  • CLC 1992 governs pollution damage from persistent oil carried in bulk as cargo. Liability is strict and is channelled to the registered owner; the owner may limit by tonnage unless the damage resulted from his personal act or omission committed with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that such damage would probably result. It requires compulsory insurance and a certificate, and gives claimants a direct right of action against the insurer. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and EEZ of a State Party.
  • Fund Convention 1992 provides a second tier where CLC compensation is unavailable or insufficient, and the Supplementary Fund Protocol 2003 a third tier for states that join it. Together these are the reason a tanker owner's exposure is finite while claimants remain compensated.
  • Bunkers Convention 2001 covers bunker pollution where CLC does not - principally non-tankers and tankers in ballast. It imposes strict liability on a broadly defined shipowner and requires compulsory insurance for ships over 1,000 GT. For a laden tanker such as this VLCC, CLC covers bunker-oil damage as well, so Bunkers is the fallback rather than the primary regime.

5. Wreck removal, salvage and global limitation

  • Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks, 2007 (in force 14 April 2015) makes the registered owner strictly liable for locating, marking and removing a hazardous wreck, with compulsory insurance and a certificate for ships of 300 GT and above and a direct action against the insurer. It applies by default in the exclusive economic zone; it reaches the territorial sea only where the state has opted to extend it - so whether it governs here depends on that coastal state.
  • Salvage. The salvors' claim continues under the Salvage Convention 1989 - an Article 13 reward if there was a useful result, otherwise Article 14 special compensation, or SCOPIC remuneration if invoked under Lloyd's Open Form. The sinking does not extinguish the claim.
  • Global limitation. The LLMC 1976, as amended by the 1996 Protocol, allows the owner to constitute a limitation fund by tonnage for most maritime claims. Some states exclude wreck removal claims from limitation by reservation, and limitation is lost on the same conduct test as under CLC.

6. The limits of the protection

None of this is unconditional. Cover can be prejudiced by unseaworthiness or want of due diligence, breach of a class or trading warranty, non-disclosure - the contract is one of utmost good faith, governed in Indian law by sections 19 and 20 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 - and wilful misconduct. A tank explosion at once raises questions about inert gas operation, tank atmosphere control and the safety management system, and those answers decide both whether the policy responds and whether the owner keeps his right to limit. The immediate duties are practical: notify H&M and the club, preserve evidence, appoint surveyors, co-operate with the coastal and flag state investigations, and concede nothing on liability.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question prints its own two limbs, (a) insurance and (b) conventions, so give them roughly eight marks each and keep them visibly separate. The strongest opening is the one used here - a short paragraph identifying the exposures before naming any cover. It shows you understand that insurance and conventions are two different answers to the same question: what happens to the money.

The two-axis idea that organises everything

There are two independent axes and candidates constantly collapse them. Axis one: whose loss. H&M covers the owner's own property; P&I covers his liability to others. Axis two: liability versus compensation. The conventions decide whether the owner is liable and how much he can limit; insurance decides who pays. CLC does not insure anybody - it imposes strict liability and then requires the owner to carry insurance for it. Say that sentence and you have the examiner's attention.

Why CLC and not Bunkers leads here

This is a VLCC and the casualty involved a tank explosion, so it was carrying persistent oil in bulk as cargo. That puts the casualty squarely inside CLC 1992, which then also covers the ship's bunker oil. The Bunkers Convention 2001 exists for the cases CLC leaves out - non-tankers, and tankers in ballast. Leading with Bunkers on a laden VLCC signals that the candidate has learned a list rather than a regime.

The territorial sea detail is deliberate

The examiner put the sinking in the territorial sea of a coastal state, not on the high seas, and that is worth marks. By UNCLOS Article 2 the coastal state has sovereignty there, so its law applies directly. It also creates the one genuine trap in the question: the Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention applies automatically in the EEZ, and to the territorial sea only if the state has opted to extend it. Saying "Nairobi applies" flatly is an overstatement; saying it applies if that state has extended it is precise.

Common mistakes

  • Treating CLC as a form of insurance. It is a liability convention with a compulsory-insurance requirement attached.
  • Confusing the tiers. CLC is the owner, the 1992 Fund is the second tier, the Supplementary Fund is the third for states that join it.
  • Assuming the owner can always limit. Limitation is lost where the damage resulted from his personal act or omission with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that such damage would probably result.
  • Forgetting that the salvors still have a claim after the ship sinks - a useful result may be absent, but Article 14 or SCOPIC may not be.
  • Writing as though cover is automatic. Unseaworthiness, breach of warranty and non-disclosure are exactly the issues a tank explosion raises.
  • Ignoring the cargo interests entirely. The cargo owner has lost a VLCC parcel of oil and will be claiming.

Examiner traps

"The ship sank - is it a total loss?" Distinguish. An actual total loss is where the ship is destroyed or irretrievably deprived; a constructive total loss is where recovery and repair would cost more than the insured value, and it requires a notice of abandonment. The bare fact of sinking does not decide which.

"Who pays for removing the wreck?" The registered owner, strictly, under the Nairobi Convention where it applies - and his P&I club in practice, subject to the certificate and direct action.

"Does the salvage claim die with the ship?" No. Article 13 needs a useful result, but Article 14 special compensation and SCOPIC exist precisely for the case where the property is lost but the environment was protected.

  • When did the Nairobi Convention enter into force, and where does it apply? 14 April 2015; in the EEZ by default and in the territorial sea only where the state extends it.
  • What is a blue card? The club's confirmation of cover, on the strength of which a state issues the ship's compulsory insurance certificate under CLC or Bunkers.
  • What breaks the right to limit? The owner's personal act or omission committed with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result.

Model written answer

1. What biofouling is, and the two problems it creates

The 2023 Guidelines define biofouling as the accumulation of aquatic organisms on surfaces immersed in or exposed to the aquatic environment, and note that it can include pathogens. Microfouling is the biofilm or slime layer; macrofouling is the visible growth that follows - barnacles, tubeworms, mussels, algal fronds, bryozoans and sea squirts. It settles on the hull and, critically, in niche areas: sea chests and gratings, thruster tunnels, propeller and shaft, rudder stock and hinge, dry-dock support strips, anodes and transducers. It creates two separate problems - ship performance for the owner, and invasive aquatic species for the receiving ecosystem - and limb (a) asks about both.

2. (a) Consequences for ship performance

  • Added frictional resistance - hull roughness rises, so more delivered power is needed for the same speed. This begins at the slime stage; macrofouling is not needed for a measurable penalty.
  • Higher fuel consumption or lost speed - the ship either burns more to hold the schedule or slows at constant power.
  • Degraded carbon standing - more fuel per tonne-mile worsens the operational carbon indicator and works against the SEEMP. The Guidelines expressly link biofouling management to energy efficiency and the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy.
  • Machinery consequences - fouled sea chests, gratings and coolers reduce seawater flow, raise pump load and lift cooler outlet temperatures. This is an engine-room problem, not only a hull one.
  • Manoeuvring and appendage effects from growth on propeller, rudder and thruster tunnels.
  • Cost - more frequent cleaning and docking, shorter AFS life, and exposure under charter-party speed and consumption warranties.

3. (a) Consequences for the environment

  • Transfer of invasive aquatic species. This is the reason the Guidelines exist. Biofouling is a major vector: non-native organisms carried on the hull and in niche areas may establish in a new ecosystem and threaten human, animal and plant life, economic and cultural activities and the aquatic environment. Niche areas are the highest risk because they are sheltered and often missed.
  • Increased air emissions. The extra fuel burned to overcome added resistance is additional CO2, NOx and SOx.
  • Impacts of the countermeasure itself. Anti-fouling systems release biocides, which is why the AFS Convention 2001 controls them; and in-water cleaning can discharge viable organisms and biocide-laden paint particles, which is why the Guidelines require captured waste to be disposed of safely and in an environmentally sound manner.
  • Regulatory and commercial exposure. Some States apply their own biofouling requirements on arrival, and a fouled hull can mean cleaning at anchor, delay or refused entry.

4. (b) New approaches to reducing biofouling

  • A risk-based, ship-specific approach - the central change in the 2023 Guidelines. The ship monitors biofouling risk parameters (operating profile, idle time, trading area, temperature, salinity, biofouling pressure) and lets the resulting risk set inspection and cleaning intervals, instead of a fixed regime.
  • Proactive cleaning, or grooming, rather than reactive - frequent gentle removal of biofilm before macrofouling establishes, which protects the coating and avoids releasing established organisms.
  • Better anti-fouling systems - foul-release, low-surface-energy silicone and hydrogel coatings that resist adhesion rather than relying on biocide, with the AFS chosen to match ship type, operating profile and conditions.
  • Hull and propeller performance monitoring - measuring the power-speed relationship so cleaning is triggered by evidence rather than by the calendar.
  • Robotic in-water inspection and cleaning with capture, filtering and retaining removed material, and approval regimes for cleaning systems.
  • Design measures - minimising and giving access to niche areas at newbuilding stage, and fitting MGPS to sea chests and internal seawater systems.
  • Contingency action plans on defined triggers, such as an extended idle period in an area of high biofouling pressure.

5. (c) How a ship-specific Biofouling Management Plan minimises biofouling

The Guidelines recommend that every ship have a ship-specific BFMP, under the responsibility of the shipowner, operator and master, and give it a measurable objective: it should contribute to maintaining a fouling rating of 1 or less. It minimises biofouling not by being a document but by converting management into named duties, thresholds and triggers:

  • It fixes accountability. It identifies the officer or position responsible - the Guidelines give chief engineer as the example - so the plan is implemented rather than filed.
  • It records what is fitted and how it must be operated. Details of the AFS and where it is installed, the operating conditions suited to it including temperature, salinity and speed, and its expected efficacy over its life - so the ship does not unknowingly operate outside the coating's design envelope.
  • It maps where fouling will occur. The hull and niche areas where biofouling may accumulate, so inspection looks in the right places.
  • It turns monitoring into action. A description of the biofouling risk parameters monitored, a schedule of fixed inspections, a cleaning regime, and procedures for reactive cleaning triggered by inspection results.
  • It plans for the abnormal. A contingency action plan based on specific triggers from the monitored parameters.
  • It maintains the defences. A regime for repair, maintenance and renewal of the AFS to the manufacturer's instructions, and a process for monitoring and maintaining the MGPS.
  • It closes the loop. It specifies the documentation required, and the plan itself must be reviewed after inspections and cleaning and updated if the measures prove ineffective or deficient.

6. (d) Entries in the Biofouling Record Book

Record-keeping is the responsibility of the shipowner, operator and/or master. It is recommended that the BFRB be retained on board for the life of the ship; it may be kept physically or electronically, and may be stand-alone or integrated into existing manuals or the planned maintenance system. All biofouling management activities should be recorded, including:

  1. Details of repair and maintenance to the AFS - date, location, areas of the ship affected, and the percentage of the ship recoated - in addition to entries in the International Anti-fouling System Certificate.
  2. Details of repair and maintenance to the MGPS - date, location and areas affected.
  3. In-water inspections - initial date, final date, duration in hours or days and location, including the inspection report.
  4. Cleaning, in water or in dry dock - initial date, final date, duration and location, including a cleaning report.
  5. Details of when the ship has been operating outside its normal operating profile, including any lay-up or extended inactive periods.
  6. Details of the performance monitoring parameters used to determine inspection intervals.
  7. A copy of the cleaning report, if applicable.
  8. A description of contingency actions taken, including date, time and location.

One qualification belongs in the answer and carries marks. The Biofouling Guidelines, the BFMP and the BFRB are recommendatory: MEPC.378(80) adopts the Guidelines and requests Member States to apply them. Unlike the Oil Record Book under MARPOL Annex I, the BFRB is not a statutory record book under a convention - although individual flag States, port States and charterers increasingly require it, which is what gives it practical force.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four printed limbs at four marks each, so the answer must be four visibly separate and roughly equal blocks. The commonest way to lose marks here is to spend half the answer on limb (a) - biofouling consequences are easy to write about at length - and then compress (c) and (d), which are the two limbs where the marks are most reliably earned because they have definite content in the Guidelines. Limb (a) is worth splitting explicitly into performance and environment, because the question names both and an answer that blurs them looks like it only knows one.

The one qualification that separates a good answer

The 2023 Biofouling Guidelines are recommendatory, not mandatory. Resolution MEPC.378(80) adopts them and requests Member States to take urgent action in applying them; it revoked the 2011 Guidelines in MEPC.207(62). There is no MARPOL annex on biofouling. A candidate who writes that the Biofouling Record Book is required under MARPOL, or treats it as the equivalent of the Oil Record Book, has made a legal error in a question that is otherwise mostly descriptive - and it is exactly the kind of error an examiner uses to separate candidates. The honest position, which also shows commercial awareness, is that the instrument is voluntary at IMO level but is increasingly made binding in practice by flag State and port State requirements, by charterers and by regional arrival standards.

Where the engineering actually is

This reads like an environmental question, but half of it is engine-room work. The resistance penalty starts at the slime layer, before anything is visible, which is why performance monitoring rather than visual inspection is what detects it. The seawater side is the part candidates forget: fouling in sea chests, on gratings, in coolers and in the internal seawater system reduces flow, raises pump power and lifts cooler outlet temperatures, and the MGPS exists precisely to manage that. Naming the chief engineer's role is not flattery either - paragraph 10.3.1 of the Guidelines gives chief engineer as its own example of the responsible position, so saying so is citing the instrument, not guessing.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the Guidelines, the BFMP or the BFRB as mandatory. They are recommendatory under MEPC.378(80).
  • Confusing the AFS Convention 2001 (controls the harmful anti-fouling systems themselves) with the Biofouling Guidelines (manage the fouling). They are different instruments with opposite subjects, and only the first is a convention.
  • Confusing biofouling with ballast water. Both transfer invasive aquatic species, but the BWM Convention is a separate mandatory regime for a different vector.
  • Answering limb (a) on fuel consumption alone and never mentioning invasive aquatic species - which is the entire purpose of the Guidelines.
  • Quoting a specific fuel-penalty percentage. The figures published vary widely and the Guidelines set none; state the mechanism instead.
  • Ignoring niche areas. They are the highest-risk locations for species transfer and are named throughout the Guidelines.
  • For limb (d), listing plausible-sounding entries instead of the eight categories in paragraph 11.4. The limb says state the entries, so the marks are in the list.

Examiner traps

'Is the Biofouling Record Book like the Oil Record Book?' No, and this is the trap. The Oil Record Book is required by MARPOL Annex I and false entries are an offence. The BFRB is recommended by a voluntary IMO resolution. Say so, then add that flag States, port States and charterers increasingly require it.

'Your ship has been at anchor for six weeks off a tropical port. What does your plan say?' This is the contingency action plan in 10.3.10 - extended idle time in an area of high biofouling pressure is exactly the trigger it exists for, and the event is recordable in the BFRB as operating outside the normal operating profile.

'Can you just clean the hull in water?' Not without regard to where you are. In-water cleaning can release viable organisms and biocide-laden paint particles, the Guidelines require capture and environmentally sound disposal of the waste, and many States regulate or prohibit it in their waters.

'Which fouling rating should the plan achieve?' An effective BFMP should contribute to maintaining a fouling rating of 1 or less.

Uncertainty and status

The status statement in this answer is taken from the operative clauses of MEPC.378(80) itself: it adopts the Guidelines, requests Member States to apply them, agrees to keep them under review and revokes MEPC.207(62). That is a recommendation, and the answer says so rather than allowing the detail of the BFMP and BFRB to imply a mandatory regime. Two things are deliberately not quantified. No fuel or resistance penalty percentage is given: published figures differ by an order of magnitude depending on fouling rating, hull form, speed and measurement method, and the Guidelines set none, so a number here would be false precision. No national arrival standard is quoted: several States operate their own biofouling requirements, these are live regulatory instruments revised independently of IMO, and reproducing a current threshold would be wrong within months - the same live-external-reference problem already recorded for war-risk listed areas and approved recycling facilities. The technology description in limb (b) rests partly on classification-society and industry guidance rather than on the resolution, and is recorded as authoritative secondary.

Model written answer

1. The governing instrument

Ship health and vector control are governed by the International Health Regulations (2005), a binding instrument of the World Health Organization, as amended in 2014, 2022 and 2024. The 2024 amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025. The IHR work through designated points of entry, competent authorities at those points, and a small set of ship documents.

2. The role of WHO

  • It makes the rules. WHO adopts the IHR and prescribes the model certificates in Annex 3 and the Ship Declaration of Health in Annex 8, so the documents are the same worldwide.
  • It identifies where vector control is needed. Under Annex 5, WHO publishes on a regular basis a list of areas where disinsection or other vector control measures are recommended for conveyances arriving from them, through temporary or standing recommendations.
  • It advises methods and materials. Where WHO has advised methods for disinsection, deratting, disinfection or decontamination, those should be used unless the competent authority is satisfied that others are as safe and reliable (Article 27), and States should accept measures applied elsewhere by WHO-advised methods.
  • It certifies and publishes. On a State Party's request WHO may, after appropriate investigation, certify that a port meets the IHR requirements (Article 20(4)), publishes certification guidelines and the list of certified ports (20(5)), and publishes the lists of authorized ports notified by States Parties (20(3)).
  • It provides technical guidance - notably the WHO Handbook for inspection of ships and issuance of ship sanitation certificates - and declares Public Health Emergencies of International Concern with temporary recommendations.

3. National agencies in India

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare - the health authority for IHR purposes, under which the National IHR Focal Point sits.
  • Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), and under it the Port Health Organisation, headed by the Port Health Officer - the competent authority at the point of entry. It inspects ships, baggage, cargo and containers, supervises control measures and issues the ship sanitation certificates, working under the Indian Port Health Rules, 1955 with the IHR 2005.
  • Directorate General of Shipping, through its Maritime Health Branch, on the seafarer side - approving medical examiners and governing seafarers' medical fitness examination and certification.
  • Port authorities and the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways support these functions at the ports themselves.

4. Certificates issued to a vessel

  • Ship Sanitation Control Exemption Certificate (SSCEC) - issued where the competent authority is satisfied that the ship is free of infection and contamination, including vectors and reservoirs, so that no control measures are required (Article 39(6)).
  • Ship Sanitation Control Certificate (SSCC) - issued where control measures were required and have been satisfactorily completed, and it records the evidence found and the measures taken (Article 39(5)). Under Annex 5 the presence of vectors on board and the measures used to eradicate them are recorded on this certificate. If a satisfactory result could not be obtained, the authority notes that on the certificate (Article 39(7)).

Both follow the model in Annex 3, and they replaced the former Deratting and Deratting Exemption Certificates. Alongside them the master submits a Ship Declaration of Health under Article 37, to the model in Annex 8 - a declaration, not a certificate. Separately, and concerning the crew rather than the ship, seafarers hold medical fitness certificates under MLC 2006 and STCW.

5. Issue in India, validity and extension

  • In India they are issued by the Port Health Officer at a port authorized for the purpose and notified to WHO. Under Article 20(3) a State Party notifies which ports may issue the SSCC, which may issue the SSCEC only, and which may grant the one-month extension.
  • Validity: a maximum of six months, for both the SSCEC and the SSCC (Article 39(1)).
  • Extension: by one month, where the inspection or the required control measures cannot be accomplished at that port - the extension carries the ship to a port where the certificate can be obtained.
  • Where no valid certificate is produced, or evidence of a public health risk is found on board, the State Party may proceed under Article 27(1) - treating the ship as affected and requiring disinfection, decontamination, disinsection or deratting.
  • Control measures are to be carried out whenever possible when the ship and holds are empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading (Article 39(4)).
  • For Indian coastal trade calling where no Port Health Officer is available, DG Shipping has provided for a Statement of Compliance issued by an authorized medical examiner, to be endorsed at the earliest available opportunity.

The currency point that will separate answers

The IHR were amended in 2024 and those amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025 - before this paper was sat, and before the January and March 2026 settings of the same question. One visible consequence: Article 37 is now the "Ship Declaration of Health". In the original 2005 text it was the "Maritime Declaration of Health", and that older name is what almost every textbook, coaching note and older syllabus still uses. Both texts were compared directly for this answer: the original uses "Maritime Declaration of Health" throughout and never the new name; the consolidated amended text uses "Ship Declaration of Health" throughout and never the old one. Using the current name, and being able to say why it changed, is a cheap and genuine mark.

Ship health and crew health are two different systems

The question asks about "the ship and the crew", and the two run on separate tracks with separate authorities. Ship health is WHO and the IHR, administered in India by the Port Health Organisation under DGHS and the Ministry of Health, and evidenced by the SSCC or SSCEC. Crew health is MLC 2006 and STCW, administered in India by DG Shipping through approved medical examiners, and evidenced by seafarers' medical fitness certificates. Candidates routinely put DG Shipping in charge of ship sanitation certificates. It is not - the Port Health Officer issues those.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the SSCC/SSCEC a Deratting Certificate. Those were the predecessors under the earlier Regulations and were replaced under the IHR 2005 - the scope widened from rodents to all infection and contamination including vectors and reservoirs.
  • Saying the certificates are valid for six months extendable by six months. The extension is one month, and only where the inspection or control measures cannot be accomplished at that port.
  • Confusing the SSCC with the SSCEC. Exemption means no measures were needed; Control means measures were required and were completed.
  • Treating the Ship Declaration of Health as a certificate. It is a declaration by the master, to the Annex 8 model, and a State may dispense with it entirely under Article 37(4).
  • Naming WHO as an enforcement body. WHO recommends, certifies ports on request and publishes; the competent authority at the point of entry enforces.

Examiner traps

"Your certificate expired yesterday and the port cannot inspect - what happens?" The one-month extension in Article 39(1) exists precisely for this, and under Article 20(3)(c) States notify which of their ports may grant it, so that the ship can reach a port where the certificate is obtainable.

"When should the inspection be done?" Whenever possible with the ship and holds empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading - Article 39(4). Answering "any time" misses a printed requirement.

"What does WHO actually do about mosquitoes?" It publishes the list of areas where vector control is recommended for conveyances arriving from them, and advises the methods. States Parties then run vector control programmes at points of entry - to a minimum distance of 400 metres from the operational areas under Annex 5.

Understand this first

UNCLOS makes a trade, and holding the trade gives you the whole answer. It grants a flag State something nobody else can have - on the high seas its ships answer to it and to no one else - and in exchange it sets out what the State must actually do to deserve that. So the duties read like the job description of a maritime administration: write the ship into a register, satisfy yourself she is fit, put competent people aboard her, and look into it when something goes wrong.

Two ideas underneath are worth having in plain words before the terms arrive. First, a register is not paperwork. It is the act that gives a ship a nationality, and nationality is what makes everything else enforceable - a ship belonging to nobody could be disciplined by nobody. Second, the State has to exercise real control, not merely bank the registration fee. That control is described under three heads - the paperwork and enforcement side, the physical condition of the ship, and the conditions of the people living and working aboard her - which the Convention calls administrative, technical and social matters. Where the connection between a State and the ships on its register is nominal only, that is the gap the idea of a genuine link is aimed at. And rather than list the technical conventions one by one, UNCLOS simply requires conformity with the generally accepted international rules, which is how SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW are pulled in without ever being named.

Model written answer

1. Where the duty comes from

The flag State's obligations sit in Part VII of UNCLOS, and the scheme is a bargain: a ship must have a nationality, that nationality gives one State exclusive jurisdiction over her on the high seas, and in exchange that State must actually govern her. Article 92 provides that a ship shall sail under the flag of one State only and is subject to its exclusive jurisdiction on the high seas; a ship sailing under two or more flags according to convenience may claim none of those nationalities and may be assimilated to a ship without nationality.

2. (a) Registration and nationality - Article 91

  • Every State fixes the conditions for the grant of its nationality, for registration, and for the right to fly its flag - its own decision, not an international prescription.
  • Ships have the nationality of the State whose flag they are entitled to fly.
  • There must exist a genuine link between the State and the ship. UNCLOS does not define the term and no settled test has emerged, which is the origin of the open-registry debate.
  • The State shall issue to ships to which it has granted the right to fly its flag documents to that effect - in practice the certificate of registry.
  • Article 94(2)(a) makes the register itself an obligation: the flag State shall maintain a register of ships with the names and particulars of ships flying its flag, other than those excluded on account of small size.

3. (b) Control of safety, pollution and social aspects

Article 94(1) is the governing sentence: every State shall effectively exercise its jurisdiction and control in administrative, technical and social matters over ships flying its flag. That is then made specific:

  • Article 94(2)(b) - assume jurisdiction under internal law over each ship, and over its master, officers and crew, in respect of administrative, technical and social matters.
  • Article 94(3) - take the measures necessary to ensure safety at sea as regards (a) construction, equipment and seaworthiness; (b) manning, labour conditions and training of crews, taking into account applicable international instruments - this is the social limb; and (c) use of signals, maintenance of communications and prevention of collisions.
  • Article 94(4) - those measures must include survey by a qualified surveyor before registration and thereafter at appropriate intervals; a master and officers appropriately qualified, in particular in seamanship, navigation, communications and marine engineering; and that master, officers and crew are fully conversant with and required to observe the applicable international regulations on safety of life at sea, collision prevention, marine pollution and radio communications.
  • Article 94(5) - in taking these measures each State is required to conform to generally accepted international regulations, procedures and practices. This is the hinge that pulls SOLAS, MARPOL, Load Lines, STCW, COLREG and MLC into UNCLOS.
  • Article 94(6) - a State with clear grounds to believe proper control is not exercised may report to the flag State, which shall investigate and take remedial action; 94(7) requires an inquiry into every marine casualty on the high seas involving loss of life, serious injury to another State's nationals, or serious damage to ships, installations or the environment.
  • On pollution, Article 217 requires flag States to ensure compliance, adopt laws, prohibit their ships from sailing until they can comply, ensure certificates are carried, inspect periodically, institute proceedings irrespective of where the violation occurred, and provide penalties adequate in severity to discourage violations.

4. (c) India's constitutional and legislative mechanism since ratification

India signed UNCLOS on 10 December 1982 and ratified it on 29 June 1995, the Convention itself having entered into force on 16 November 1994. Ratification binds India as a State, but it does not by itself create rights and duties inside India: India is a dualist jurisdiction, so a treaty obligation takes domestic effect only through legislation. Limb (c) is therefore properly answered in two layers - the constitutional power to legislate, and the statutes actually enacted under it.

  • The constitutional layer. Article 253 empowers Parliament to make any law for the whole or any part of India for implementing any treaty, agreement or convention with another country - and it does so notwithstanding the ordinary distribution of legislative powers, so implementing UNCLOS is not obstructed by the federal division. The subject matter is in any event Union business: Seventh Schedule, Union List entry 14 (entering into and implementing treaties), entry 21 (piracy and crimes on the high seas), entry 25 (maritime shipping and navigation) and entry 57 (fishing beyond territorial waters). Article 297 vests in the Union the resources of the territorial waters, continental shelf and exclusive economic zone and provides for their limits to be specified by law. Article 51(c), a Directive Principle, directs the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations.
  • The maritime zones statute. The Territorial Waters, Continental Shelf, Exclusive Economic Zone and other Maritime Zones Act, 1976 declares India's zones and the rights exercised in them. Note that it was enacted in 1976 - before ratification in 1995 and before the Convention was concluded in 1982 - in anticipation of the Third UN Conference. India legislated first and ratified afterwards, which is worth saying on a question asking about the position 'since its ratification'.
  • The flag State statute, and the currency point. The registration, safety, pollution and social obligations in Articles 91 and 94 are carried into Indian law by the merchant shipping legislation. At the date of this paper the operative statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 (Act No. 24 of 2025), which received assent on 18 August 2025 and was brought into force in its entirety on 15 March 2026 by S.O. 1244(E), repealing the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 by section 324(1). Registration and nationality are administered by the Registrars of Indian Ships at the ports of registry, who issue the certificate of registry; section 15 governs who may own an Indian vessel and section 16 bareboat charter-cum-demise registration.
  • The administration. The Directorate General of Shipping, under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways, working through the Mercantile Marine Departments, discharges survey, certification, manning, training and casualty functions and issues merchant shipping notices as the subordinate instrument layer.
  • Delegation and verification. Statutory survey and certification are largely delegated to recognized organizations under the RO Code with the Administration retaining oversight, which is how Article 94(4)'s survey duty is met in practice. India also exercises port State control under the Indian Ocean MOU, and is audited against the III Code under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme. Casualty investigations and formal inquiries give effect to Article 94(7).

5. How flag State performance is itself checked

UNCLOS creates the duty but provides no audit. That gap is filled by the III Code and the IMO Member State Audit Scheme, mandatory since 1 January 2016, under which every Member State is periodically audited on how it discharges its flag, coastal and port State obligations. With port State control, that is what turns Article 94 into something measurable.

The idea that makes limbs (a) and (b) easy

If you can state the bargain and the hinge, you can reconstruct the rest.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (c) with the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 on a January 2026 paper. It had not commenced.
  • Claiming UNCLOS defines the genuine link. It requires one and does not define it - that indeterminacy is the point, and the origin of the open-registry argument.
  • Saying UNCLOS requires ships to comply with SOLAS and MARPOL by name. It does not name them; Article 94(5) requires conformity with generally accepted international regulations, which is how they come in.
  • Forgetting the social limb, which the question names explicitly. It is Article 94(3)(b) - manning, labour conditions and training - and it is where MLC 2006 and STCW attach.
  • Suggesting that authorising a recognized organization transfers responsibility. It transfers the work; the Article 94 duty stays with the Administration, which must monitor the RO.
  • Omitting Article 217 and answering pollution purely from Article 94. Article 217 is the pollution enforcement article and carries the sailing prohibition and the adequate-penalty requirement.

Model written answer

1. (a) The mandatory basis in SOLAS and MARPOL

  • SOLAS regulation XI-1/6 is the operative provision. Adopted by resolution MSC.257(84), it makes Parts I and II of the Casualty Investigation Code mandatory and took effect on 1 January 2010 - converting investigation from an undertaking into an obligation.
  • SOLAS regulation I/21 is the older and broader provision: each Administration undertakes to conduct an investigation of any casualty occurring to its ships subject to the Convention when it judges that the investigation may assist in identifying what changes to the Convention might be desirable. Its paragraph (b) completes the regulation and is the limb candidates omit: each Contracting Government undertakes to supply the Organization with pertinent information concerning the findings of such investigations, and no reports or recommendations of the Organization based upon that information may disclose the identity or nationality of the ships concerned, or in any manner fix or imply responsibility upon any ship or person. Note precisely where that duty sits - the non-disclosure rule binds IMO's own output, not the investigating Administration's report.
  • MARPOL Article 12 - Casualties to ships: each Administration undertakes to investigate any casualty occurring to its ships subject to the Convention where the casualty has produced a major deleterious effect upon the marine environment, and to supply the Organization with information concerning the findings.
  • Behind both sits UNCLOS Article 94(7), requiring an inquiry into marine casualties on the high seas involving loss of life, serious injury to another State's nationals, or serious damage to ships, installations or the environment. The Code cites SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7) together.

2. (b) The purpose and structure of the Code

The Code - resolution MSC.255(84) - provides a common approach to marine safety investigations. Its defining principle, and the thing to state first, is that a marine safety investigation is conducted to prevent future casualties, not to apportion blame or determine liability. It is separate from, and must not be prejudiced by, any criminal, disciplinary or civil proceedings. It is arranged in three parts:

  • Part I - General provisions (chapters 1 to 3): purpose, definitions and application.
  • Part II - Mandatory standards (from chapter 4): what a State Party must do.
  • Part III - Recommended practices (from chapter 15): what it should do.

3. Salient mandatory standards - Part II

  • An impartial Marine Safety Investigation Authority (chapter 4), properly resourced.
  • Notification (chapter 5) - ship name and flag, IMO number, nature, location, time and date, numbers seriously injured or killed, consequences to persons, property and environment, and any other ship involved.
  • A safety investigation into every very serious marine casualty (chapter 6.1), with the flag State responsible for ensuring it is conducted and completed (6.2).
  • Agreement between substantially interested States (chapter 7) - the flag State may agree that another State conducts the investigation, and participating in it discharges its obligations under the Code, SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7).
  • Powers of investigation, parallel investigations and co-operation (chapters 8 to 10).
  • Independence (chapter 11) - the investigation shall not be subject to external direction.
  • Obtaining evidence from seafarers (chapter 12) - they are informed of the nature and basis of the investigation and may be legally represented.
  • Draft reports (chapter 13) circulated to substantially interested States for comment, normally within 30 days.
  • Reports (chapter 14) - the final report of every very serious marine casualty investigation is submitted to the Organization, and so is any other report containing information that may prevent future casualties.

4. Salient recommended practices - Part III

  • Administrative responsibilities and principles of investigation (chapters 15 and 16), and guidance on investigating casualties and incidents.
  • Confidentiality of marine safety records - statements, communications, medical and private information, analyses and voyage data recorder information - so that people speak freely to investigators.
  • Protection for witnesses and involved parties, notification to parties involved, co-ordination, evidence collection, and the re-opening of an investigation where significant new evidence emerges.

5. (c) Very serious marine casualty

The Code defines it at paragraph 2.22: "A very serious marine casualty means a marine casualty involving the total loss of the ship or a death or severe damage to the environment." Three limbs, any one of which is sufficient. Reading it needs the supporting definitions: a marine casualty (2.9) is an event, or sequence of events, resulting directly in connection with the operation of a ship in death or serious injury, loss of a person overboard, loss, presumed loss or abandonment of a ship, or material damage; a serious injury (2.18) is one causing incapacitation for more than 72 hours commencing within seven days of the injury; and severe damage to the environment (2.19) is damage which, as evaluated by the affected State or the flag State, produces a major deleterious effect upon the environment.

The classification matters because it triggers obligations: a safety investigation is mandatory for every very serious marine casualty, and the final report must go to IMO. Note also that the Code defines only this term - the related expression "serious marine casualty" appears nowhere in it and comes from IMO's separate reporting guidance.

The one sentence that must appear

Getting limb (a) exactly right

Three provisions, and they do different jobs. SOLAS XI-1/6 is the mandatory hook - it makes Parts I and II of the Code mandatory and took effect on 1 January 2010. SOLAS I/21 is the older, discretionary undertaking, where the Administration investigates when it judges the investigation may assist in identifying desirable changes to the Convention. MARPOL Article 12 is the pollution counterpart, and note its threshold - a major deleterious effect upon the marine environment. Citing only XI-1/6 answers half of limb (a), because the question expressly asks for SOLAS and MARPOL.

Common mistakes

  • Giving only the SOLAS provision when the question asks for SOLAS and MARPOL. MARPOL Article 12 is the one - an Article, not a regulation in an Annex.
  • Confusing "very serious marine casualty" with "serious marine casualty". Only the first is defined in the Code; the second comes from IMO's reporting guidance and is not in MSC.255(84) at all.
  • Writing that the purpose of an investigation is to find who was at fault. It is the opposite, and it is the single most important point in the question.
  • Getting the serious-injury definition loosely. It is incapacitation for more than 72 hours, commencing within seven days of the injury.
  • Treating the whole Code as mandatory. Parts I and II are mandatory through SOLAS XI-1/6; Part III is recommended practice, and the question asks about both.
  • Forgetting that the final report of every very serious marine casualty investigation goes to IMO.

Examiner traps

"A crew member died - is that a very serious marine casualty?" Yes. The three limbs are total loss of the ship, or a death, or severe damage to the environment - any one is sufficient, and a death alone qualifies.

"Who investigates if the ship is Indian-flagged but the casualty is in another State's waters?" The flag State is responsible, but chapter 7 allows agreement with another substantially interested State to conduct it, and participation in that investigation discharges the flag State's obligations under the Code, SOLAS I/21 and UNCLOS 94(7).

"Can the investigation report be used in court?" The Code makes safety records confidential and keeps the safety investigation separate from criminal, disciplinary and civil proceedings, precisely so that evidence is given freely. That separation is a design feature, not an oversight.

  • Define a very serious marine casualty. A marine casualty involving the total loss of the ship, or a death, or severe damage to the environment.
  • What is a substantially interested State? Broadly: the flag State, the coastal State, a State whose environment was severely or significantly damaged, one whose nationals died or were seriously injured, or one with a significant interest recognised by the investigating State.
  • What is the difference between a marine casualty and a marine incident? An incident endangers, or would endanger if not corrected, the ship, its occupants or the environment - but it stops short of the outcomes that make a casualty, and it excludes deliberate acts intended to cause harm.
  • What must the notification contain? Ship name and flag, IMO number, nature, location, time and date, numbers seriously injured or killed, consequences, and any other ship involved.
  • Where does the final report go? To the Organization, for every very serious marine casualty investigation.

Model written answer

1. (a) What the human element means in STCW

STCW refuses to treat competence as something time served automatically confers. It replaces experience with demonstrated competence, then addresses the conditions - fitness, rest, teamwork and company responsibility - under which competence can actually be exercised. The instrument is the STCW Convention and Code, 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force from 1 January 2012.

2. Competence-based standards

  • The Part A tables of competence specify, for every function and level, the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods for demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard, not an examiner's impression.
  • Part A is mandatory; Part B is guidance - a distinction worth stating.
  • The Manila amendments added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking at operational level and leadership and managerial skill at management level - where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention. It is the explicit recognition that technically competent individuals still fail as a team.
  • Regulation I/6 governs training and assessment, and regulation I/8 requires a quality standards system, so the training system itself is audited rather than assumed.
  • Regulation I/11 requires periodic revalidation, and chapter VI mandatory safety familiarisation and basic training before a seafarer is assigned duties.

3. Fitness for duty - the physical human being

  • Regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require that watch systems are arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The Manila-amended A-VIII/1 minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Authorised exceptions are tightly conditioned: weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours in any 7-day period, exceptions from the 77-hour norm may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • The Manila amendments also introduced alcohol limits for masters, officers and other seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards, and regulation VIII/2 with section A-VIII/2 governs watchkeeping arrangements and principles.

4. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring seafarers hold valid certificates, are familiarised with their duties and the ship's arrangements, and that documentation is maintained - so the human element is not left as an individual's problem. Administrations operate quality standards and are subject to independent evaluation and reporting to IMO.

5. (b) The impact of fatigue on seafarer performance

The question asks for a critical examination, so state the effects and then say what follows from them. Fatigue is not tiredness; it is a measurable degradation of the capacities the job depends on. It reduces vigilance and sustained attention - precisely what a lookout and a duty engineer on an unmanned machinery space are there for. It lengthens reaction time and impairs judgement, decision-making and risk perception, so a fatigued officer is not merely slower but readier to accept a risk they would refuse when rested. It causes memory lapses, loss of situational awareness, and microsleeps - intrusions of sleep lasting seconds of which the person has no awareness, which is why self-assessment of fitness is unreliable.

  • Sleep debt is cumulative - short sleep across successive days accrues a deficit one long sleep does not repay, so performance can be degraded on a day when last night's rest looks adequate on paper.
  • Circadian timing matters as much as duration - the alertness trough falls in the small hours, coinciding with the 0000-0400 watch, and rest against the body clock is of lower quality hour for hour.
  • The marine environment adds its own loading - motion, noise, vibration, heat, split rest, port workload, inspections and short-sea schedules.
  • It causes casualties. Fatigue is a recognised contributory factor in groundings, collisions and personal injury, and one investigators look for under the Casualty Investigation Code.
  • The critical point, and where the marks are: the regulatory instrument is a prescriptive hours-of-rest formula, and compliance with it is not the same as being rested. A schedule can satisfy 10 in 24 and 77 in 7 and still leave an officer impaired, because it says nothing about circadian timing, sleep quality, accumulated debt or workload. Closing that gap is what company fatigue management is for.

6. (b) IMO measures, and what a shipping company can implement

What the IMO has done. The principal instrument is the Guidelines on fatigue, MSC.1/Circ.1598, approved at MSC 100 and issued on 24 January 2019, superseding MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, and it is deliberately addressed to everyone who can affect the outcome, in six modules - fatigue and its causes; the Company; the seafarer; awareness and training; ship design; and Administrations and port State authorities. Behind it sit the mandatory provisions: STCW regulation VIII/1 with Code section A-VIII/1, requiring minimum rest of 10 hours in any 24 and 77 hours in any 7 days, taken in no more than two periods of which one is at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between them; and MLC 2006 Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7 on hours of work and rest, leave and manning. The IMO has also worked the human element through the ISM Code, which requires the company to ensure the ship is appropriately manned.

  • Man the ship for the work, not for the minimum. The single most effective company measure. A manning level and a voyage plan that only work if everyone runs at their maximum permitted hours has no margin for a port call, a breakdown or an inspection.
  • Put a fatigue policy in the safety management system under the ISM Code, with the Company's responsibility and the master's authority stated, so fatigue is a managed hazard rather than an individual weakness.
  • Schedule against the body clock. Where the trade allows, plan demanding work outside the circadian low, avoid abrupt watch rotation, and protect a consolidated sleep period rather than fragmenting rest.
  • Manage the port and inspection load - the point at which rest hours are actually lost. Plan surveys, bunkering and PSC inspections so that the same officers are not carrying them on top of a full watch cycle.
  • Audit the rest records honestly. Records that are always exactly compliant should be treated as a warning rather than a reassurance; the company should compare recorded rest with the actual work programme.
  • Provide reporting without blame. A seafarer must be able to report that they are unfit to keep a watch without it counting against them, or the policy is decorative.
  • Train for it. Awareness of sleep debt, circadian effects, microsleeps and the unreliability of self-assessment, for masters, officers and ratings alike.
  • Design and maintain for rest - noise and vibration in accommodation, blackout, temperature control and alarm management, which is where a chief engineer's decisions bear directly on whether off-watch rest is restorative.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two printed limbs of 8 marks each, so split the answer evenly and head each limb clearly. Limb A rewards structure - competence tables, fitness for duty, company responsibility - and limb B rewards naming the actual circular. A candidate who writes generally about tiredness for the second limb without producing MSC.1/Circ.1598 and its module structure will lose most of eight marks, because "the IMO guidance" is a specific document.

The organising idea for limb A

STCW attacks the human element on three fronts, and saying so gives the limb a spine: can they do it (competence standards, assessed against published criteria), are they fit to do it now (fitness for duty - rest, medical, alcohol), and who is responsible for making that possible (regulation I/14, the company). The Manila amendments strengthened all three, and added the fourth dimension the older Convention lacked - can they work as a team, through mandatory leadership and teamworking training.

The numbers you must get right

10 hours in any 24, and 77 hours in any 7 days. Divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods. Exceptions may not go below 70 hours in any 7 days and may not run more than two consecutive weeks. Learn 10, 77, 2, 6, 14, 70 - and be careful, because 70 and 77 are both real figures doing different jobs: 77 is the norm, 70 is the floor under an authorised exception. Quoting 70 as the normal requirement is the classic error, and it is exactly the trap that a pre-2010 copy of the Code will lead you into.

Currency - what applies and what does not

The operative regime is STCW 1978 as amended, with the Manila amendments in force from 1 January 2012. A comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code has been under way through the HTW Sub-Committee with completion targeted around 2027 - one of the acknowledged gaps being the treatment of hours of rest. Nothing had been adopted at the date of this paper, and nothing had been adopted when this answer was written. So write the Manila regime as current, and mention the review only as work in progress. Do not present any reviewed text as though it were in force.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb B without naming MSC.1/Circ.1598 and the circular it replaced, MSC/Circ.1014.
  • Treating the fatigue Guidelines as mandatory. They are recommendatory; the binding requirements are STCW chapter VIII and MLC 2006.
  • Quoting 70 hours as the normal weekly rest. 77 is the norm; 70 is the minimum where an exception has been authorised.
  • Confusing hours of rest with hours of work. MLC permits either a maximum-hours-of-work or a minimum-hours-of-rest approach; STCW A-VIII/1 is expressed as minimum rest.
  • Writing about STCW competence generally without mentioning that the Manila amendments made leadership and teamworking training mandatory - that is the human-element addition the question is really pointing at.
  • Forgetting regulation I/14. The human element is not only about the seafarer; the company carries defined obligations.

Examiner traps

"Your rest-hour records show full compliance. Is your crew rested?" Not necessarily, and the Guidelines say so - the formula is prescriptive, and quality of rest depends on circadian rhythm and accumulated sleep debt. Records that are always exactly compliant deserve suspicion, not comfort.

"Is coffee a fatigue countermeasure?" The Guidelines address it directly: caffeine can improve alertness temporarily but is not a substitute for adequate sleep and rest, and can interfere with sleep afterwards.

"Where do the fatigue Guidelines actually bite on board?" Through the safety management system - Module 2 is addressed to the company, so fatigue risk belongs in manning, scheduling and the ISM Code procedures rather than being left to individual willpower.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which circular contains the IMO fatigue Guidelines? MSC.1/Circ.1598 of 24 January 2019, approved at MSC 100, replacing MSC/Circ.1014.
  • How many modules, and who are they addressed to? Six - fatigue itself, the company, the seafarer, awareness and training, ship design, and the Administration and port State authorities.
  • State the minimum hours of rest. 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 in any 7-day period; no more than two periods, one at least 6 hours; intervals not exceeding 14 hours.
  • When do the Manila amendments date from? Adopted 2010, in force 1 January 2012.
  • Which STCW regulation makes the company responsible? Regulation I/14 - valid certificates, familiarisation and documentation.
  • Are the fatigue Guidelines mandatory? No. STCW chapter VIII and MLC 2006 carry the mandatory requirements; the Guidelines are recommendatory.

Uncertainty and source status

The fatigue limb is primary-verified: the circular number, date, approving session, the superseded circular and the six module titles were all read in the text of MSC.1/Circ.1598, as were the sleep-debt definition and the statement about the prescriptive STCW formula. The rest-hour figures carry a caveat worth recording. MIW holds no licensed copy of the STCW Convention and Code. An IMO circular copy of section A-VIII/1 was consulted first and turned out to be the pre-Manila 1995 text - five paragraphs, 70 hours per seven-day period, and no 14-hour interval rule. Taking it at face value would have produced a confidently wrong answer. The Manila-amended figures used here therefore come from a competent-authority notice reproducing the amended regime, which also confirms the 1 January 2012 entry into force. They are consistent across sources and are safe to use, but they sit at authoritative-secondary status rather than primary, and acquiring a licensed STCW Code would promote them. The lesson generalises: for an amended instrument, confirm which edition a source is reproducing before quoting a number from it.
