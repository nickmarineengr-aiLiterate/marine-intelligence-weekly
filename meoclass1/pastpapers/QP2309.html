Understand this first

A ship is not only a place of work; it is also where the crew live for months at a time, with no way of going home at the end of a shift. That single fact is what makes this subject different from the same subject ashore. If a workplace is unwelcoming you can leave it at five o'clock. If a ship is, you cannot. So the question splits naturally into two halves that people often blur together. One half is about getting in - whether a woman can train, qualify, find a berth and be promoted on the same terms as anyone else. Organisations and governments work on this half, and they do it by opening training, funding places, building professional networks and leaning on owners to provide berths. The other half is about staying, and almost none of it is decided ashore. It is decided by whether the cabin locks, whether the overalls fit, whether she gets the interesting jobs or the safe ones, and whether the first crude remark is corrected or allowed to stand. The head of department decides all four. There is also a distinction worth holding on to, because the examiner is testing it: wanting more women at sea is an ambition, but not discriminating against them is already an obligation. The first has no legal force and the second does, and an answer that only ever speaks in the language of ambition never reaches the part that binds the ship.

Model written answer

1. What the goal says, and what equality means on a ship

The United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development sets seventeen goals, and the one this stem quotes is to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls. Applied to a ship, that does not mean pretending the ship is a neutral place. It means that a seafarer's access to the job, to training, to promotion and to a safe working and living environment does not depend on her sex - and, because a ship is also a home, that the accommodation, the sanitary arrangements, the medical provision and the standards of behaviour on board are designed for a mixed crew rather than merely tolerated by a minority within it.

Two distinctions carry the rest of the answer. First, equality of opportunity is not equality of outcome: the obligation is to remove barriers, not to guarantee proportions. Second, and this is what the examiner is really testing, gender balance is an aspiration but non-discrimination is already law. Under the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 each Member must satisfy itself that its law respects the fundamental right to the elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation, and every seafarer has the right to a safe and secure workplace and to decent working and living conditions. Those obligations reach the ship whether or not any programme exists.

2. (a) Why gender balance is required

  • It is a matter of right, not of favour. Excluding half the population from a profession is discrimination in respect of employment and occupation, which the Convention's fundamental rights article requires each Member to eliminate. That reason is first and it is sufficient on its own.
  • The industry needs the people. Shipping recruits officers from a shrinking pool and competes with shore employment offering a normal domestic life. A profession that draws from half its potential workforce is choosing to worsen its own shortage.
  • Selecting from a wider pool raises quality. If the best candidate for a fourth engineer's berth is excluded before she applies, the berth goes to someone less good. That follows from restricting the field and holds regardless of any argument about aptitude.
  • Mixed teams manage risk differently. A crew sharing one background tends to share its blind spots, and the human-element failures behind most casualties - unchallenged authority, poor communication, reluctance to speak up - are exactly those a more varied team is less prone to. Put this as diversity of perspective, never as a claim about the qualities of either sex.
  • Retention. Recruiting women and losing them within a contract or two costs more than not recruiting them. Retention is decided by conditions on board, which is what limb (c) is about.
  • It is a commercial expectation. Charterers, financiers, insurers and terminals increasingly ask about the social element of an operator's practice, so a policy that cannot be evidenced is a commercial exposure as well as an ethical one.
  • It raises the profession's own standards. Accommodation, sanitary arrangements, protective equipment sizing, medical provision and shipboard behaviour improve for everyone once they are designed for a mixed crew instead of assumed for a single one.

One caution is worth a mark in itself: the case does not need exaggerating. Quoted figures for the proportion of women in the seafaring workforce vary considerably between sources. This answer quotes none, because an unsourced statistic in a script is a liability and the argument above stands without one.

3. (b) What the International Maritime Organization does

The Organization's work here is programme work rather than regulation. There is no convention on gender and no ship is certified for it. What the Organization does, through its technical cooperation function, is build the supply of qualified women and make the profession visible to them:

  • a long-standing women in maritime programme under the Integrated Technical Cooperation Programme, directed at access to maritime training and to posts in maritime administrations;
  • support for regional women in maritime associations, giving a professional network across administrations and industry in each region;
  • fellowships and reserved places for women at the Organization's own academic institutions, including the World Maritime University and the International Maritime Law Institute;
  • a World Maritime Day theme devoted to women in the maritime community, used to put the subject before Member States and industry for a full year;
  • gender-parity mechanisms written into ordinary technical programmes - the most durable form, because it does not depend on a separate initiative surviving. The freshest worked example at this sitting comes from an instrument with nothing to do with gender: the capacity-building programme described in the appendix to the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy, adopted on 7 July 2023, provides for two trainees, one female and one male, to go on to a World Maritime University scholarship;
  • the same Strategy's insistence that the human element and the impact on seafarers be addressed in the energy transition - which is where the next generation of shipboard jobs is being created.

What is deliberately not said: no resolution number is given for any of this. The Organization has adopted instruments touching the subject, but no licensed text of one is held for this answer, and inventing a number to look authoritative is exactly the error that costs marks in the oral.

4. (b) What the Government of India does

India's maritime administration is the Directorate General of Shipping, acting under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, which is the statute in force at this sitting. Its instruments are the ordinary administrative ones - training and examination rules, certification requirements, approval of maritime training institutes, and notices to industry - and the measures relevant here work at that level:

  • Admission to training on the same terms. Women are admitted to approved Indian maritime training institutes and to the pre-sea and post-sea courses leading to certificates of competency, in the engineering stream as well as the nautical one.
  • Certification and service in all departments and ranks. Eligibility for a certificate of competency and for shipboard service is not restricted by sex, so there is no legal barrier to a woman holding the Chief Engineer's certificate this very examination leads to.
  • Conditions attached to institute approval. The approval regime is the practical lever: separate accommodation and sanitary facilities, safeguarding requirements and complaint mechanisms are conditions of running a course, not aspirations.
  • Training-berth availability. The recurring obstacle for an Indian cadet is not admission but obtaining a training berth, and administrative and industry pressure on owners to provide them is where policy meets reality.
  • Implementation of the Maritime Labour Convention through national law. India's ratification carries the Article III and Article IV obligations into the domestic regime, which is what gives a woman seafarer on an Indian ship an enforceable position rather than a policy statement.
  • Promotion and visibility - recognition of women officers, participation in the regional women in maritime association, and encouragement of owners to recruit and retain women.

Again, no circular, rule, notice or scheme number is asserted. The administration issues numbered instruments; a candidate who has one in front of him should cite it, and one who does not should describe the measure and say what it does. That scores. Inventing a number does not.

5. What already binds the ship, before any initiative

Limb (c) becomes a management answer rather than a set of good intentions the moment it is anchored on obligations that already exist. Before any programme reaches the gangway the Chief Engineer is working to Article IV - a safe and secure workplace and decent living conditions; Standard A3.1 - the individual sleeping room on ships other than passenger ships, and separate sanitary facilities; Regulation 5.1.5 and Standard A5.1.5 - an on-board complaint procedure, an impartial adviser, a right of direct complaint to the master and to the competent authority, and an express prohibition on victimizing a complainant; and Guideline B4.3.1, which places harassment within occupational safety and health.

6. (c) As Chief Engineer - the physical ship

  • Accommodation and sanitary arrangements. Confirm before she joins that the individual cabin the Convention requires is actually allocated and lockable and that private, secure sanitary facilities exist. If the arrangement depends on sharing, that is my problem to solve before arrival, not hers to raise afterwards.
  • Protective equipment that fits. Boiler suits, gloves, safety shoes, harnesses, breathing apparatus face masks and immersion suits stocked in the full range of sizes. Ill-fitting equipment is a direct safety failure, and it is the most visible and most easily corrected signal that a ship was not expecting her.
  • Workshop and machinery-space practicalities. Lifting aids and correct tooling used as routine rather than as a concession, so that no job in the engine room is done by main force alone. That is good practice for every engineer and it removes the argument before it is made.
  • Medical provision. Ensure the medical locker and the ship's medical arrangements match the crew actually carried, and that confidentiality in medical matters is real.
  • Safety equipment sizing proved in drills, not assumed from the inventory.

7. (c) As Chief Engineer - the allocation of work

  • Allocate work by rank and competence and by nothing else. The most damaging thing a Chief Engineer can do is protect a woman engineer from the dirty or heavy jobs. It looks like consideration; it is exclusion, it destroys her credibility with the ratings, and it leaves her under-prepared for her next certificate.
  • Give the same watch and project responsibility - the same overhauls, the same faults to diagnose, the same ownership of a survey item - and hold it to the same standard.
  • Appraise honestly. Feedback withheld for fear of appearing harsh is the quiet way careers end. She is entitled to the same correction as anyone else and to the same record of her competence.
  • Mentor, and be seen to. A junior who is the only woman on board has no peer to ask. Planned time on machinery and on certificate preparation costs little and is what converts recruitment into retention.
  • Include her in the department's decisions - planned maintenance, the repair list, the pre-arrival plan - so that authority follows the rank rather than the individual.
  • Watch the workload in the hours-of-rest records. A junior given every awkward job, or excluded from every desirable one, shows up there before she says anything.

8. (c) As Chief Engineer - behaviour, complaints and accountability

  • Set the standard explicitly and early. At the departmental meeting on joining, state what is expected and what will not be tolerated - as an instruction from the head of department, not as a policy reading. The standard on a ship is whatever the senior officers visibly accept.
  • Deal with the first incident, however small. A remark left uncorrected is a permission. Correcting it once, immediately and privately, prevents almost everything that would otherwise follow.
  • Make the complaint route real. Ensure every member of the department holds the on-board complaint procedure, knows who the impartial adviser is, and knows that the right to go to the master or to the competent authority is not affected by raising it with me first.
  • Protect the complainant. Victimization is prohibited under the Convention. If a complaint is raised, nothing may change in her work allocation, her appraisal or her treatment because of it - and I must be seen to ensure it.
  • Treat harassment as an occupational safety and health matter, which is what the Convention's own guideline makes it: risk-assess it, take it to the safety committee, record it like any other hazard.
  • Use the safety management system. Report and record under the SMS so the Company and the designated person see the pattern, and so the response does not depend on which Chief Engineer happens to be aboard.
  • Brief the reliefs. A standard that survives one contract and lapses at the next is not a standard. Hand over the department's expectations as deliberately as the machinery condition.
  • Be careful with visibility. Singling someone out as an example isolates as effectively as ignoring her. The objective is that within a few weeks she is simply the fourth engineer.

The honest summary, and a good closing line: the initiatives in limb (b) can only bring a woman to the gangway. Whether she stays in the profession is decided almost entirely by what happens in the six months after she walks up it - and in the engine room, that is decided by the Chief Engineer.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has printed three limbs and an instruction to introduce the subject briefly, so four blocks of marks are already allocated and the order is fixed. Candidates lose marks here in two predictable ways: they write an essay on why diversity is good and never reach limb (c), or they treat limb (c) as a list of pieties. The structure above prevents both. Section 1 is the brief introduction the stem asks for and no more. Section 2 answers (a) as seven separately countable propositions rather than one argument. Sections 3 and 4 split (b) by actor, because the stem names two of them and an answer that blurs the Organization's programme work into the Indian administration's statutory work has answered half a question twice.

Section 5 is the section that separates a management answer from a well-meaning one. Anchoring limb (c) on obligations that already bind the ship converts everything after it from opinion into duty, and it is where the Convention's article and standard numbers earn their marks. Sections 6 to 8 then answer (c) in the order the Chief Engineer actually meets the problem - the ship before she arrives, the work once she is aboard, and the behaviour that decides whether she stays.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a percentage for women in the seafaring workforce. The figures vary widely between sources and an examiner who knows a different one will mark it wrong. The argument does not need it.
  • Inventing a resolution number, circular number or scheme name to look authoritative. Describing what a measure does scores; a wrong number does not, and it invites an oral question you cannot answer.
  • Arguing that women bring particular qualities - better communication, more caution. It is unnecessary, it is a claim about sex rather than about diversity of perspective, and it is easy to attack. Argue from the wider pool and from varied perspective instead.
  • Writing limb (c) as policy statements. The examiner asked what you would do, so every proposition should be an action a Chief Engineer can actually take on a ship.
  • Protecting her from heavy or dirty work and presenting it as consideration. This is the single most common well-intentioned error in the answer and it deserves to be named as an error.
  • Running out of time. Limb (c) carries the applied marks and is the part most candidates leave until there are ten minutes left.

Examiner traps

  • The stem is not a grammatical sentence. It quotes the goal's title without quotation marks. Do not spend time on it and do not correct it; open by naming the goal and move on.
  • Equality of opportunity versus equality of outcome. An answer that promises proportions has claimed something no obligation requires. Say that the duty is to remove barriers.
  • Aspiration versus law. Gender balance is an aspiration; non-discrimination is a binding obligation. Candidates who miss this write the whole answer in the language of policy and never cite anything.
  • Two actors in limb (b). The Organization and the Government of India do different things by different means - programme work under technical cooperation, and administration under a national statute. Answer them separately.
  • The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 governs this sitting. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 commenced 15 March 2026, thirty months after this paper. Do not import it.
  • The 2022 amendments to the Convention are not in force at this sitting - they enter force 23 December 2024. Work from the Convention as amended through the 2018 set.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which article of the Maritime Labour Convention makes non-discrimination a fundamental right, and what does it require of a Member?
  • What does Standard A3.1 require about sleeping rooms, and does it differ on a passenger ship?
  • A rating makes a remark to the fourth engineer in the workshop. What do you do, and when?
  • She raises a complaint against the second engineer. Walk me through the on-board complaint procedure and tell me what protection she has.
  • Who is the impartial adviser, and can she complain to anyone outside the ship?
  • Your predecessor kept her off the purifier overhauls to be kind. What is wrong with that, and what will you do about it?
  • What would you check before she joins, and who is responsible if the cabin is not right?

Understand this first

A flag State promises the rest of the world that its ships meet the conventions, and then has to make good on that promise on ships that are scattered across the planet and hardly ever come home. Almost no administration has surveyors everywhere its ships trade, so it hires organisations that do - normally the classification societies, which already have a global surveying network built for their own commercial work. That is the whole idea, and everything else follows from one uncomfortable feature of it: the organisation doing the checking is paid by the owner being checked. So the rules that govern the arrangement are mostly about keeping that from mattering - the organisation must be genuinely independent, must not certify something it designed or maintains, must run a quality system that somebody unconnected with it certifies, and must open its internal instructions and its files to the flag State rather than handing over only the certificates it chose to issue. There is also a distinction that unlocks the subject. Being judged fit is one thing; being handed a particular job is another, and they happen at different times. An organisation can be assessed as competent overall and still not be permitted to touch a chemical tanker or issue a particular certificate, because the permission is written down job by job. And the thing that never moves, whatever is delegated: the flag State is still the one who answers for the certificate. The work can be handed out. The responsibility cannot.

Model written answer

1. What a Recognized Organization is

A Recognized Organization is an organization to which a flag Administration has delegated authority to carry out statutory survey and certification work on its behalf - most commonly, but not necessarily, a classification society.

Two points carry the marks in the definition. First, the delegation is of function, not of responsibility: the Administration remains responsible for the statutory certification of its ships and cannot answer a casualty by pointing at its RO. Second, classification society and Recognized Organization are not synonyms. A society classes ships under its own rules as a commercial matter; it becomes an RO only where an Administration has delegated statutory work to it, only to the extent of that delegation, and only for that flag. The same society may be an RO for one flag and not for another, and may hold different scopes from different flags. One surveyor attending one ship may therefore be acting in two distinct capacities during the same visit - as class, and as the flag State.

2. Recognition and authorization are two separate acts

The Code turns on a distinction candidates routinely blur, and stating it early makes the rest of the answer coherent. Recognition is the flag State's assessment of an organization against the Code's mandatory requirements. Authorization is the separate and later act of delegating specified statutory functions to an organization that is already recognized. It follows that an organization may be recognized and still not authorized for a particular ship type or a particular convention, and that capability is assessed function by function rather than once and for all. The enabling provision expressly forbids a flag State to authorize functions beyond an RO's capabilities.

3. The RO Code, and how a code becomes binding

The Code for Recognized Organizations was adopted by resolution MSC.349(92) of 21 June 2013 and by resolution MEPC.237(65). Its purpose is to provide a harmonised, transparent and independent international standard against which organizations are assessed for recognition and authorization, together with guidelines for flag State oversight - so that a delegation is measured against one international benchmark instead of each flag's own judgement. Before it, the criteria were spread across Assembly resolutions and applied unevenly.

It was made mandatory from 1 January 2015 by resolutions MSC.350(92), MSC.356(92) and MEPC.238(65), which amended SOLAS, MARPOL Annexes I and II and the 1988 Load Line Protocol; the SOLAS hook is regulation XI-1/1. Recognise the technique, because it is worth a mark in the oral: a code is drafted as a standard and then made binding by amending the conventions that refer to it - exactly as the ISM Code and the III Code were.

4. Salient feature - three parts, and which of them bind

  • Part 1 - general provisions: purpose, definitions, application, the power to delegate, and the duty to notify the Organization.
  • Part 2 - mandatory requirements, on the organization and on the flag State alike.
  • Part 3 - guidelines for flag State oversight of ROs.
  • Parts 1 and 2 are mandatory; Part 3 is recommendatory. This is the single most examinable fact about the Code's structure and it is often got wrong.

The consequence is visible in the drafting: Part 2 says 'shall' and Part 3 says 'should'. The adopting resolution recommends Governments to use Part 3 as a basis for their standards unless their national requirements provide at least an equivalent degree of safety - which is why flag States' oversight programmes differ in form while converging in substance. The Code's scope paragraph establishes four things, and they map onto this question exactly: what an organization must satisfy to be recognized; what an RO must satisfy when performing the work; what flag States must do when authorizing; and guidance on oversight.

5. Salient feature - what the organization itself must satisfy

Part 2 opens with the controlling proposition that delegation is subject to confirmation of the organization's capability to deliver high standards of service and to comply with the Code and applicable national legislation. The demonstration is the organization's; the confirmation is the flag State's. The assessment then runs through:

  • General requirements - legal personality, technical, managerial and research capability, a qualified surveyor and auditor cadre, a code of ethics, and independence.
  • Management and organization - a documented quality, safety and pollution-prevention policy, a quality manual, control of documents and records, organizational structure, communication internally, with the flag State and between ROs, and management review.
  • Resources - personnel, infrastructure and work environment adequate to the tasks assigned.
  • Processes - design and development control, control of service provision, subcontracting and service suppliers, control of measuring devices, and a complaints and appeals process.
  • Performance measurement - internal audit, vertical contract audit, and control of non-conformities including statutory deficiencies.
  • Certification of the quality management system - a system no less effective than the ISO 9000 series, periodically assessed and certified by a qualified body accredited by a body signatory to the International Accreditation Forum arrangement and independent of the ROs and their associations.

Two of these deserve naming. Independence is the head the Code is really defending, because the structural weakness of the whole arrangement is that the body surveying the ship is paid by the owner of it; the revenue-dependence rule and the prohibition on certifying what you designed, supplied or maintain are the direct answer. And the vertical contract audit is what distinguishes a real assessment from a paper one, because it follows a single ship's certification right through the organization instead of sampling processes in isolation. Qualification is not left general either: an appendix sets out the training and qualification of plan-approval, survey and audit staff, and another specifies the functions ROs perform by elementary module.

6. Salient feature - what the flag State must do when it authorizes

This is the only part of the Code that is a procedure rather than a standard, and it is where marks are most often left behind.

  • Rest the delegation on its conventional basis - SOLAS regulation I/6, Load Lines article 13, MARPOL Annex I regulation 6, MARPOL Annex II regulation 8 and TONNAGE article 6 - and only for ships entitled to fly its flag.
  • Specify the authority conferred. The word to underline is specify: a general authorization to 'act on our behalf' does not satisfy the Code. Scope is written down function by function and convention by convention, and it must cover the unglamorous end too - corrective actions and the withdrawal or cancellation of certificates. A flag State that has not said who may withdraw a certificate has not finished authorizing.
  • Make a formal written agreement. An appendix sets out its minimum elements: application and purpose; general conditions; execution of the functions, including general and special authorization and cooperation with port States to rectify reported deficiencies; the legal basis of those functions, with interpretations, deviations and equivalent solutions; reporting to the flag State; exchange of information and rule development; and other conditions.
  • Notify the Organization. The flag State communicates to and deposits with the Secretary-General a list of its ROs together with the specific responsibilities and conditions of the delegated authority. This is what lets a port State know, before it inspects, exactly what the certificate in front of it represents.

7. How Administrations monitor - the oversight programme

Monitoring is easiest to reproduce as before, during and after the delegation.

Before - assess the organization against Part 2, confirm capability function by function, and reduce the delegation to a written agreement defining scope and limits, applicable national law and interpretations, the instructions to be followed where a ship is found unfit to proceed to sea, and the records to be kept.

During - Part 3 supplies four mechanisms, and they should be given as such:

  • Supervision of the delegated duties, whose common thread is access: to the RO's quality management system documentation, to its internal instructions, circulars and guidelines, to its documentation relevant to the flag State's fleet, to cooperation with the flag State's own inspection work, and to information and statistics such as damage and casualties relevant to that fleet. Oversight is impossible if the flag State sees only the certificates the RO chose to issue.
  • A system to ensure the adequacy of the statutory work - procedures for communication and for reporting by the RO and the processing of those reports; additional ship inspections by the flag State; technical consultation between ROs where certificate validity may be affected; acceptance of the RO's independent QMS certification; and verification of the statutory work itself.
  • Auditing. The flag State should satisfy itself that the RO has an effective quality management system and may rely upon audits carried out by an accredited certification body. That permission is what makes oversight affordable, and it is precisely why the certifying body must be accredited and independent. The auditor advances three principles - ethical conduct, fair presentation (including reporting significant obstacles and unresolved diverging opinions) and due professional care.
  • Managing the programme - objectives and extent scaled to the size, complexity and location of the authorizations; responsibilities, resources, procedures and records; and periodic monitoring and review of the programme itself. The flag State's own additional ship inspections sit inside this programme, not beside it: inspecting a sample of the RO's ships is one monitoring activity, not a substitute for oversight.

8. Performance indicators, and the duty that cannot be delegated

The stem asks how Administrations monitor, and monitoring without measurement is inspection. The Code offers indicators for evaluating an RO's performance: port State performance of the RO; the results of its internal audits; the results of third-party quality management system audits; the results of previous performance monitoring; and the condition and compliance of the ships it surveys and certifies.

Where a shortfall is found, the response runs along the authorization that was granted. Because the scope was specified, it can be narrowed - by ship type, by convention, by function - as readily as suspended or withdrawn, and corrective action can be required under the written agreement. Because the RO's reporting duties are conditions of the authorization rather than courtesies, a failure to report a detainable deficiency or a withdrawal of class is itself a compliance failure and not merely poor service.

The chain then closes above the Administration, and this is the point that finishes the answer. The flag State's own performance is not unexamined: under the IMO Member State Audit Scheme it is audited against the III Code, whose Part 2 requires an oversight programme for delegated authority, and against the obligations set out in the Assembly's non-exhaustive list - at this sitting resolution A.1157(32). So the RO answers to the Administration and the Administration answers to the Organization.

End on the proposition the whole scheme rests on: the flag State may delegate the work; it cannot delegate the responsibility. The certificates carry its name, the obligation to the other Contracting Governments remains its own, and Part 3 exists because an assessment made on the day of appointment says nothing about the state of the organization five years later.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem is three questions in one sentence and the examiner has weighted it at sixteen marks with no internal split. That is a trap in itself: candidates spend the time on the definition, which is worth least, and arrive at monitoring with two lines left. The structure above allots one section to the definition, one to the distinction that makes the rest coherent, three to the Code and three to monitoring - roughly the proportions the three questions deserve.

Note what section 2 is doing. Recognition and authorization are separate acts, and once that is on the page every later point has somewhere to attach: capability is assessed function by function because authorization is function-specific; the scope must be specified because authorization is not general; the delegation can be narrowed rather than withdrawn because it was specified in the first place. A candidate who omits the distinction has to argue each of those separately and usually does not.

The before/during/after framing in section 7 is deliberately not the Code's own section order. It is the order an Administration actually works in, it is far easier to reproduce under time pressure, and it makes the Part 3 mechanisms fall out as a list rather than having to be recalled by number.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 'classification society' and 'Recognized Organization' as the same thing. They are different capacities, and the same society is an RO only for the flags that have delegated to it and only within that delegation.
  • Saying the Administration delegates its responsibility. It delegates the function. The responsibility stays, and this is the single proposition the examiner most wants to see.
  • Getting the mandatory boundary wrong. Parts 1 and 2 are mandatory, Part 3 is recommendatory. An answer that makes the oversight guidelines binding has misdescribed the instrument.
  • Listing the Part 2 headings without saying what the Code is defending. The heads exist to protect independence, and an answer that never identifies that has described the requirements without understanding them.
  • Answering 'how do Administrations monitor' with 'they audit them'. The question is worth several marks and wants mechanisms - access, reporting, additional inspections, reliance on accredited audits, and a managed programme with indicators.
  • Spending the marks on the definition. It is the shortest of the three questions and it is worth the least.
  • Importing 33rd Assembly resolution numbers. See the traps below.

Examiner traps

  • The Assembly obligations list at this sitting is A.1157(32), not A.1187(33). A.1187(33) was adopted 6 December 2023, three months after this paper, and it revoked A.1157(32). Circulating material written for a 2024 or later sitting cites the later number and it is wrong here.
  • Recognized and authorized are not the same. Expect to be asked whether an RO recognized by a flag may survey any ship of that flag. It may not - only within its specified authorization.
  • Part 3 is guidance. Expect 'is the flag State obliged to follow Part 3?' The answer is that the resolution recommends it as a basis unless national requirements give at least an equivalent degree of safety.
  • The quality system certifier must be independent of the ROs and their associations. The obvious wrong answer is that the RO's own association can certify it.
  • 'Specify' is the operative word in authorization. A general authorization does not satisfy the Code, and the scope must extend to withdrawal and cancellation of certificates.
  • The RO Code is mandatory from 1 January 2015 - not from its adoption in 2013. Adoption and entry into force are different events and this question invites the confusion.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which resolutions adopted the RO Code, and when did it become mandatory? By what route?
  • Your ship is classed with a society. Is that society your flag State's RO? How would you find out?
  • What is a vertical contract audit and why does it tell the flag State more than a process audit?
  • Who certifies the RO's quality management system, and why can the RO's own association not do it?
  • Give me three things the written agreement between flag State and RO must cover.
  • The RO finds a detainable deficiency and does not report it. Is that poor service or a compliance failure?
  • How does IMO check that the flag State is doing any of this?
  • Can a flag State narrow an authorization instead of withdrawing it? On what basis?

Understand this first

There are only three ways to make a ship emit less, and the examiner has named all three - which is a gift, because it gives the answer its own structure. You can build her better, so that the hull, propeller and machinery need less fuel for the same work. You can run her better, which is mostly about speed, routeing, trim and keeping the hull and propeller clean. Or you can make emitting expensive and let the owner work out his own answer. The first two are prescriptive: someone writes down what the ship must achieve and a surveyor checks it. The third is not - it mandates nothing at all, it just changes the arithmetic, and that is precisely why it is both the most powerful and the hardest to agree. The other thing to hold on to is a difference in status, because the question asks what is ongoing. The build-better and run-better measures already exist as enforceable requirements that a ship is surveyed against today. The make-it-expensive measures do not exist at all yet; what exists is a decision that they will be created, a description of what shape they must take, and a deadline. Those are two completely different kinds of statement, and an answer that reports them in the same voice - as though a carbon price were already sitting alongside the design index - has got the question wrong even where every individual fact in it is right.

Model written answer

1. The 2023 Strategy - the frame every measure now serves

The governing instrument is the 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships, resolution MEPC.377(80), adopted at MEPC 80 on 7 July 2023 - two months before this examination - and it revoked the 2018 Initial Strategy from that date. Note what it is: a policy instrument, not law. It binds nobody directly. It works by committing the Committee to develop measures which then become law as amendments to MARPOL Annex VI.

Vision: to phase out GHG emissions from international shipping as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition.

Levels of ambition: carbon intensity of new ships to decline through strengthened design requirements; carbon intensity of international shipping to decline, reducing CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 against 2008; uptake of zero or near-zero GHG technologies, fuels and energy sources to reach at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030; and GHG emissions to reach net zero by or around, i.e. close to, 2050.

Indicative checkpoints - absolute, where the ambitions are intensity-based: total annual GHG emissions to fall against 2008 by at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, and by at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040. Two features change everything downstream: the targets are GHG and not CO2 alone, so methane and nitrous oxide count, and they are measured well-to-wake, so a fuel that is clean at the funnel but dirty to produce earns no credit.

2. The three classes of measure, and what separates them

The examiner has named three classes and they are not three flavours of the same thing. Say what divides them before describing any of them:

  • a technical measure acts on the ship as built - it forces efficiency into the hull, propeller and machinery and is verified once, by calculation, against a design index;
  • an operational measure acts on how the ship is run - speed, routeing, trim, hull and propeller condition, cargo and ballast planning - and is verified continuously, from reported data;
  • a market-based measure acts on price. It mandates no design and no operating practice; it attaches a cost to emitting and lets the owner choose how to respond.

The second division is the one that decides how this answer is written. The technical and operational measures are in force and can be stated as law. The market-based measures are not adopted and can only be stated as a work programme. A question about ongoing developments is asking the candidate to know which is which.

3. The technical measures in force - EEDI and EEXI

The Energy Efficiency Design Index, under Annex VI chapter 4, requires a new ship's attained EEDI to be no greater than the required EEDI for her type and size, the required value tightening in successive phases. It is a design measure, applied at the drawing board.

The Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index, regulations 23 and 25, in force 1 January 2023, extends the same design-type calculation to existing ships, which was the gap in the original scheme. Compliance is assessed once, at the first annual, intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2023, and is commonly achieved by engine or shaft power limitation or by fitting energy saving devices. Both indices are one-off design compliance: neither limits how the ship is subsequently operated, which is exactly why an operational measure was needed alongside them.

4. The operational measures in force - SEEMP Part III and the CII

The Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan, regulation 26, now has three Parts. Part I is the ship-specific energy efficiency plan; Part II is the fuel oil consumption data collection plan; and Part III, required on board on or before 1 January 2023, describes how the ship will achieve her required CII, with implementation, monitoring and self-evaluation. The operative guidance is the 2022 SEEMP Guidelines, resolution MEPC.346(78).

The operational carbon intensity indicator, regulation 28, applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above and is the genuinely new operational measure. The ship calculates her attained annual operational CII, compares it against the required CII - a reference line reduced by an annual reduction factor - and receives a rating from A to E. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E for a single year, must develop a plan of corrective actions in SEEMP Part III. 2023 is the first full data year, so at this sitting no ship has yet been rated: the scheme is running but has produced nothing.

5. The data foundation the whole scheme rests on

Regulation 27 has required ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above to collect and report fuel oil consumption data since calendar year 2019. The Administration verifies the data, issues a Statement of Compliance and transmits the data to the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database. It is not itself a reduction measure, and candidates often omit it for that reason - which is a mistake. Every carbon-intensity calculation and every future policy decision depends on it, the Strategy relies on it for annual emission estimates, and no market-based measure could be administered without it.

6. The market-based measures - what is under development

This is where the word ongoing does its work, and it is the part of the answer most candidates never reach. The Strategy requires a basket of candidate mid-term measures comprising both of the following, together and not as alternatives:

  • a technical element - a goal-based marine fuel standard regulating the phased reduction of the GHG intensity of marine fuel; and
  • an economic element - a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism.

The logic of pairing them is worth a mark: the standard compels the transition while the price funds it and closes the cost gap between fossil and zero-carbon fuel. Neither alone would do both.

The economic element is not yet chosen, and three structurally different forms are under discussion:

  • a levy or contribution per tonne of GHG emitted - it fixes the price and leaves the emissions uncertain, is simple to administer, and generates large predictable revenue whose distribution then becomes the whole negotiation;
  • an emissions trading system, cap and trade - it fixes the emissions and leaves the price uncertain, and is administratively heavier;
  • a feebate - a fee on high-intensity fuel funding a rebate on low-intensity fuel, which transfers less money overall but requires a benchmark that must ratchet or the incentive decays.

Real proposals before the Committee are hybrids of these - a levy returning part of its revenue as a reward for low-intensity fuel is a feebate in a levy's clothing - so treat the three as analytical categories rather than watertight boxes.

The Strategy fixes the process as firmly as the substance: the basket is to be finalized and agreed by the Committee by 2025, with entry into force to be defined thereafter; it must be developed on a well-to-wake basis under the life cycle guidelines; and it must be accompanied by a comprehensive assessment of impacts on States, with particular attention to least developed countries and small island developing States. The guiding principles constrain the design and are why this has taken so long - non-discrimination and no more favourable treatment from MARPOL, set against common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in the light of different national circumstances from the climate regime. Reconciling those two is the negotiation.

7. The rest of the ongoing work programme

  • A review of the short-term measures - the technical and operational measures now in force - is required, to be completed by 1 January 2026.
  • Life cycle assessment. The Guidelines on life cycle GHG intensity of marine fuels, resolution MEPC.376(80), were adopted alongside the Strategy on 7 July 2023 and underpin the well-to-wake basis of everything in the basket.
  • Safety. The Maritime Safety Committee is developing a safety regulatory framework for new technologies and alternative fuels - the safety counterpart to the environmental measures, and the reason an ammonia or hydrogen answer must never be given on environmental grounds alone.
  • Other candidate mid-term work named in the Strategy: measures addressing methane and nitrous oxide, incentives for first movers, onshore power supply and port-side developments, national action plans, and research and development.
  • Long-term measures are deliberately left undefined - measures finalized and agreed beyond 2030, to be developed as part of the 2028 review, once the basket is in force and its effect can be measured.

8. Where the work stands at this sitting

Stating the boundary is part of a correct answer to a question about ongoing work, and it is what separates a candidate who has read the Strategy from one who has read about it.

  • The Strategy is two months old. It was adopted on 7 July 2023 and it replaced the 2018 Initial Strategy on that date. An answer written on the Initial Strategy is out of date by two months.
  • No mid-term measure has been adopted. There is no IMO carbon price, no levy, no emissions trading system and no marine fuel GHG-intensity standard in force anywhere. The basket exists as a requirement with a deadline, not as an instrument.
  • The impact assessment is not complete, and the Strategy makes agreement conditional on it.
  • No CII rating has been issued to any ship; 2023 is the first data year.
  • The only carbon pricing that reaches shipping at all is regional, not IMO. The European Union has legislated to extend its emissions trading system to maritime transport from 1 January 2024 - which is after this sitting. Identify it as regional, note that it is one of the pressures making a global measure urgent, and do not present it as an IMO measure or as something already applying to the ship.

The closing proposition: at this sitting the technical and operational measures are on board and enforceable, and the market-based measures are a mandate with a date on it. That is precisely what 'ongoing developments' means here, and saying it plainly is worth more than any additional detail.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has named three classes of measure and asked what is ongoing. Two things follow. First, the answer must be organised so that all three classes are visibly covered - an answer that describes EEXI and CII beautifully and never reaches market-based measures has left roughly a third of the marks on the table. Second, and less obvious, the word 'ongoing' makes the status of each measure part of the answer rather than background to it.

That is why sections 2 and 8 exist. Section 2 gives the candidate a one-line test for each class - ship as built, how she is run, price - and then draws the line that matters: in force versus under development. Section 8 then closes the answer by stating exactly what has and has not happened. Between them they convert a recitation into a position statement, and they are cheap to write.

Section 5 is included deliberately even though the data collection system is not a reduction measure. It is the foundation every other measure stands on, no market-based measure could be administered without it, and candidates who omit it usually do so because they have learned the measures as a list rather than as a system.

Common mistakes

  • Never reaching market-based measures. The examiner named them; a third of the answer is missing without them.
  • Describing the European scheme as the answer. It is regional, and at this sitting it does not yet apply to shipping at all.
  • Writing the answer on the 2018 Initial Strategy. It was revoked on 7 July 2023, two months before this paper.
  • Confusing the two elements of the basket with the types of economic instrument. The basket is technical plus economic; the economic element may take the form of a levy, an emissions trading system or a feebate.
  • Treating the Strategy as law. It is a policy instrument; it becomes law only through amendments to MARPOL Annex VI.
  • Saying ships are being rated A to E. The scheme is running but 2023 is the first data year and no rating has been issued.
  • Giving a carbon price or levy rate. None had been agreed and an invented figure is indefensible.
  • Treating EEXI as an operating restriction. It is one-off design compliance, even where it is met by power limitation.

Examiner traps

  • MEPC 81 has not happened at this sitting. It concluded 22 March 2024, six months later. Almost all circulating material on market-based measures is written around it, and importing it is the single most likely contamination on this question.
  • The European emissions trading system does not yet apply to shipping. It applies from 1 January 2024. Material written for any 2024 sitting states, correctly for itself, that it already applies - which is wrong here.
  • Adopted is not in force, and a Strategy is neither. The Strategy was adopted 7 July 2023 and is operative as strategy; nothing in the basket is adopted at all.
  • GHG, not CO2. The targets cover methane and nitrous oxide, which is what makes LNG a more complicated answer than it looks.
  • Well-to-wake, not tank-to-wake. A fuel clean at the funnel and dirty to produce earns no credit, and this is the whole reason the life cycle guidelines were adopted alongside the Strategy.
  • The 40% and the 20/30% figures are different kinds of number. The first is carbon intensity per transport work; the second is total annual emissions. Both are against 2008 and candidates routinely merge them.
  • 'Striving for' is not a target. It is the compromise wording that allowed adoption, and describing the higher figure as the commitment overstates the instrument.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which resolution is the current GHG Strategy, when was it adopted, and what did it replace?
  • Give me the four levels of ambition and the two indicative checkpoints, and tell me which are intensity and which are absolute.
  • What is the difference between EEDI and EEXI, and is either an operating restriction?
  • How is your CII rating calculated, and what happens if you are rated D three years running?
  • What is in the basket of mid-term measures, and by when must it be agreed?
  • Levy, emissions trading or feebate - what does each fix and what does each leave uncertain?
  • Why does the Strategy insist on a well-to-wake basis?
  • Is the European emissions trading system an IMO measure? Does it apply to your ship today?

Understand this first

The Organization is easiest to hold in the head if you separate who decides from who does the technical work. At the top sit the member governments themselves, meeting only occasionally, who approve the money, the programme of work and the membership of the smaller body that runs things between their meetings. That smaller executive body can do almost everything the full membership can, with one pointed exception: it cannot itself make recommendations to governments about safety and pollution, because that is the one power the membership kept for itself. Neither of those bodies, though, writes the rules an engineer actually meets. Those come from the committees - one for safety, one for the marine environment, and three others - and beneath them from specialist groups that grind through the technical drafting and then hand the result upwards. The specialists never adopt anything; they prepare, and someone above them decides. That separation is deliberate, and it keeps expertise and authority in different hands. The second half of the question is really about one idea: whether a document binds you has nothing to do with what it is called. A treaty binds because states have signed up and enough of them have done so. A technical manual binds only if some treaty reaches out and says it does - which means the very same manual can be advisory in one context and compulsory in another. And a formal decision of a committee can be a mere suggestion or the instrument that changes a treaty, depending entirely on what the committee was doing when it took it. Read the route, never the cover.

Model written answer

(a) The structure of IMO

1. What the Organization is, and the organs through which it works

The International Maritime Organization is the specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and for the prevention of pollution from ships. It works through a two-tier governing structure - an Assembly and a Council - together with five main committees supported by seven sub-committees, all served by a permanent Secretariat headed by the Secretary-General.

The division of labour is worth stating at the outset because it organises everything that follows: the governing organs decide policy, budget and membership; the technical output that actually reaches a ship comes almost entirely from the committees.

2. The Assembly - the governing body

  • The highest governing body. All Member States sit in it.
  • Meets in regular session once every two years.
  • Approves the work programme, votes the budget and determines the financial arrangements.
  • Elects the Council.
  • Receives the reports of the Council and of the committees.
  • Adopts Assembly resolutions - recommendations, guidelines and codes. Worked example at this sitting: resolution A.1155(32), the Procedures for Port State Control, adopted at the 32nd Assembly in December 2021.

3. The Council - the executive organ

  • 40 Members, elected by the Assembly for a two-year term, in three categories: (a) ten States with the largest interest in providing international shipping services; (b) ten States with the largest interest in international seaborne trade; and (c) twenty States elected to secure geographical representation, having special interests in maritime transport or navigation.
  • Between Assembly sessions it performs all the functions of the Assembly, with one reservation: the function of making recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention is reserved to the Assembly itself. That single exception is the most examinable fact about the Council.
  • Supervises the work of the Organization and co-ordinates the activities of its organs.
  • Considers the draft work programme and budget and submits them to the Assembly with its comments.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General, subject to the approval of the Assembly.

One live boundary should be flagged rather than glossed. Resolution A.1152(32), adopted December 2021, would enlarge the Council from 40 to 52 Members. It amends the Convention on the Organization and requires acceptance by two thirds of Members; it was not in force at this sitting, so the figure to write is 40.

4. The five main committees and what each owns

CommitteeWhat it is responsible for
Maritime Safety Committee (MSC)The highest technical body. Navigational safety, construction and equipment, manning from a safety standpoint, casualty investigation, salvage and rescue
Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC)Prevention and control of pollution from ships - MARPOL and its amendments, ballast water, anti-fouling, and greenhouse gas measures
Legal CommitteeAny legal matter within the scope of the Organization - the liability and compensation conventions in particular
Technical Cooperation CommitteeThe Organization's technical assistance work, particularly for developing States
Facilitation CommitteeElimination of unnecessary formalities and red tape in international shipping - the FAL Convention

All five are open to all Member States. MSC and MEPC are the two that adopt amendments to the mandatory instruments, which is why they are the committees a marine engineer meets.

5. The seven sub-committees and the support they provide

The sub-committees are HTW (Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping), III (Implementation of IMO Instruments), NCSR (Navigation, Communications and Search and Rescue), PPR (Pollution Prevention and Response), SDC (Ship Design and Construction), SSE (Ship Systems and Equipment) and CCC (Carriage of Cargoes and Containers).

Their role is preparatory and technical. A committee sits for about a week and cannot itself draft detailed regulation, so it remits the work to the sub-committee holding the relevant expertise. The sub-committee develops the draft text, guideline or unified interpretation, resolves the technical argument, and reports back to its parent committee - MSC, MEPC, or both where a subject straddles them.

The sentence that earns the mark here: a sub-committee adopts nothing. It prepares, the parent committee approves, and the committee or the Assembly adopts. That division is what keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands.

(b) Conventions and the other instruments

6. What a convention is

A convention is a formal, legally binding treaty between States. It is negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference or by the Assembly; it is then opened for signature, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession; and it enters into force only when the conditions written into the convention itself are satisfied - typically a stated number of Contracting States representing a stated share of world gross tonnage. It binds only those States that have become party to it, and it reaches a ship through the law of her flag State.

Worked example: the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974.

Three distinctions belong in the definition because the examiner is testing them: adoption is not signature, signature is not ratification, and ratification is not entry into force. They are four separate events and a convention adopted years ago may still not be in force.

7. Protocol, Code and Resolution distinguished, with examples

InstrumentWhat it isExample
ProtocolA separate treaty instrument that amends, supplements or modifies a parent convention, used where the change is too large for the ordinary amendment procedure. It is itself subject to ratification, and a State may need to be party to bothThe 1988 Protocols to SOLAS and to the Load Lines Convention, which introduced the harmonized system of survey and certification; the 1978 and 1997 Protocols to MARPOL
CodeA detailed technical instrument. Of itself it is advisory; it becomes mandatory only when a convention makes it so. Mandatory status is a property of the route, never of the word on the coverThe ISM Code, adopted by resolution A.741(18) and made mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX. Contrast the Code for Recognized Organizations, made mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/1 from 1 January 2015
ResolutionA formal decision of an organ of the Organization - the Assembly, MSC or MEPC. It may be advisory, or it may be the vehicle by which a mandatory amendment is adopted. The word tells you the form, not the forceA.1155(32), the Procedures for Port State Control - advisory. 8. The pattern that ties the four together

Finish on the point the four examples exist to make, because it is what turns a list into an explanation: binding force is a property of the route an instrument has taken, not of the name it carries.

  • A convention binds because States have ratified it and the entry-into-force conditions are met.
  • A protocol binds on the same footing, because it is itself a treaty requiring ratification.
  • A code binds only where a convention has reached out and made it mandatory - so the same document can be recommendatory for one purpose and mandatory for another.
  • A resolution may be a recommendation with no binding force at all, or it may be the instrument through which a mandatory convention amendment is adopted and brought into force under tacit acceptance.

That last mechanism is worth naming. Under tacit acceptance an amendment adopted by MSC or MEPC enters into force on a stated date unless a specified proportion of Contracting Governments objects. It is what allows the technical instruments to be kept current without re-ratification, and it is the practical reason the committees, rather than the Assembly, are where a marine engineer's regulation comes from.

Why this structure scores

This is a two-limb question of equal weight, and the commonest failure is to spend twelve marks' worth of time on limb (a), which is largely recall, and reach limb (b) with nothing left. Limb (b) is the harder limb and it is worth exactly the same. Budget the time before starting.

Within limb (a) the printed stem asks four things - the structure, the Assembly, the Council, the main committees, and then the sub-committees' supporting role. Understand this first

Think about what a heap of dry grain will and will not do. Pour it out and it forms a cone; tilt the floor and, past a certain slope, the top layer slides down and settles into a new cone. That slope is the only thing holding it in place, and it is not very steep. Now put that heap in a ship's hold. Two things then matter, and they are easy to miss. The first is that grain consolidates after loading, so even a hold packed right up to the deckhead develops a gap underneath it within a day or two - and a gap is the space the surface needs in order to move at all. The second, and this is the one that decides everything, is that when the ship rolls back, the grain does not roll back with her. Gravity carried it down the slope; nothing carries it up again. So the ship does not swing back to upright. She keeps a lean, permanently, and the next heavy roll to that same side can move a little more. That is completely unlike slack water in a tank, which sloshes both ways and does no permanent harm to which side the ship sits on - and it is why the rules treat the two problems with different arithmetic. What the ship has lost is not simply uprightness. Her reserve of righting effort - the effort available to push back against a gust or a wave - is smaller, it starts from a leaning position instead of an upright one, and it runs out at a smaller angle. So the rules do not try to stop the grain moving, which cannot be guaranteed. They accept that it will move, and then insist the ship still be upright enough to work, strong enough in reserve to absorb a squall, and stiff enough at the outset for either of those to be true.

Model written answer

1. What is being carried, and why grain is regulated apart

Grain in this context means wheat, maize, oats, rye, barley, rice, pulses, seeds and their processed forms having similar behaviour. It is a free-flowing granular cargo, and its defining property for the naval architect is that a heap of it will not stand steeper than its angle of repose.

Two boundaries are worth stating at the outset because they earn marks and prevent the wrong answer:

  • Bulk grain has its own instrument. It is governed by the International Code for the Safe Carriage of Grain in Bulk - the International Grain Code, adopted by resolution MSC.23(59) - which is made mandatory through SOLAS chapter VI. Grain is expressly outside the IMSBC Code, so an answer built on cargo groups, transportable moisture limits and individual schedules is answering about a different regime.
  • A shifting cargo is not a liquefying cargo. In liquefaction the cargo takes on the character of a fluid and can slosh to and fro. Grain does neither: it slides once to a new angle of repose and stays there. That single difference dictates everything below.

2. Why grain shifts, and why the shift does not come back

  • It settles. Grain loaded into a hold consolidates in the first days of a passage, conventionally taken as of the order of two per cent by volume. Even a compartment filled and trimmed to the deckhead therefore develops a void beneath the deck head.
  • The void gives the surface somewhere to go. Until the void exists there is nothing to shift into; once it exists, the exposed surface can move.
  • Rolling does the work. As the ship heels, grain on the high side slides down across the void to the low side, and the surface settles at its angle of repose relative to the new waterline.
  • The shift is irreversible - this is the crucial point. When the ship rolls back, the grain does not run back up the slope. Gravity moved it down; nothing moves it up. So the ship does not return to upright: she takes up a permanent angle of heel, and the next roll to the same side can move more grain again. A liquid free surface, by contrast, is symmetrical and reversible, which is why it is handled by a virtual rise of G and grain is not.
  • The centre of gravity moves twice. The cargo's centre of gravity moves transversely, which is what generates the heeling moment, and it also generates a small vertical component as the heap redistributes. Both reduce stability.

3. From heeling moment to heeling arm

The Code makes the effect calculable rather than leaving it to judgement. Grain loading information approved for the ship gives, for every compartment and every loading condition, an assumed volumetric heeling moment - assumed, because it is a standard calculated allowance rather than a measurement of what any particular cargo will do. The assumption distinguishes a filled and trimmed compartment, where the cargo is confined and the surface can move only a little, from a partly filled compartment, where a large free surface is exposed and the assumed movement is correspondingly greater. That is why the Code presses so hard on filling and trimming: it is buying a smaller assumed moment.

The moments for all compartments are totalled and converted to a heeling arm:

  • the heeling arm at zero degrees is the total assumed volumetric heeling moment divided by the product of the stowage factor and the ship's displacement;
  • the heeling arm at forty degrees is taken as 0.8 times the value at zero;
  • and between them the heeling arm is assumed to vary as a straight line.

That straight line is a deliberate simplification - the real heeling arm is a curve - and it is conservative in the region that matters. What the candidate must remember is that the grain heeling arm is a straight line falling from its value at zero to 0.8 of that value at forty degrees, and that it is drawn on the same axes as the righting arm.

4. Drawing the diagram

Draw angle of heel horizontally, from zero to about sixty degrees, and lever in metres vertically. Then, in this order:

  1. Plot the ship's curve of statical stability - the righting lever GZ against angle of heel - for the loaded condition, with GM already corrected for the free surface of liquids in tanks.
  2. Mark the angle of flooding on the horizontal axis if it lies below forty degrees, and mark forty degrees.
  3. Plot the heeling arm: a point on the vertical axis at the zero-degree value, a point at forty degrees at 0.8 of it, and a straight line joining them, extended as necessary.
  4. Mark the first intersection of the two lines. This is the angle of heel due to the shift of grain - the ship's new equilibrium - and it is the angle criterion 1 limits.
  5. Find the angle of maximum difference between the two ordinates. Take whichever is least of that angle, forty degrees, and the angle of flooding.
  6. Shade the area enclosed between the righting arm curve and the heeling arm line, from the equilibrium angle to that least angle. This shaded area is the residual, or net, area and it is what criterion 2 measures.
  7. At the origin, draw the tangent to the GZ curve and carry it to the vertical erected at 57.3 degrees, i.e. one radian. The height at which it cuts that vertical is the initial metacentric height GM, which criterion 3 limits.

Label the shaded area, the equilibrium angle and GM. All three criteria can then be read off one drawing, which is exactly why the examiner has asked for a neat diagram rather than three sentences.

5. What the shift does to the curve of statical stability

  • The effective righting lever at every angle is reduced by the heeling arm. The curve the ship actually works to is the residual curve - righting arm minus heeling arm - not the curve in the stability book.
  • The ship no longer floats upright. Equilibrium moves from zero to the angle at which the two curves cross, and she lies at that angle as a steady condition rather than as a transient.
  • The maximum righting lever is reduced, and the angle at which it occurs moves outwards.
  • The range of stability is reduced. The residual curve returns to zero at a smaller angle than the original curve, so the ship has less angular margin before capsize.
  • Dynamical stability is reduced - the area under the residual curve is smaller, so there is less energy available to absorb a gust or a heavy roll. This is the reduction that actually capsizes ships, and it is why the second criterion is expressed as an area rather than as a lever.
  • Because the initial heel is permanent, the loss is not recovered. The ship sails at the heeled condition, her deck edge is closer to the water on the low side, and any further shift compounds the first rather than cancelling it.
  • The initial GM is also reduced by the small vertical rise of the cargo's centre of gravity, which steepens nothing and flattens the whole curve near the origin.

In one sentence for the examiner: grain shift converts a symmetrical righting arm curve into a smaller, one-sided residual curve that starts at a heeled equilibrium, peaks lower, ends sooner and encloses less area.

6. The three minimum intact stability criteria

The Code requires the intact stability characteristics of any ship carrying bulk grain to be shown to meet, throughout the voyage and after allowing for the heeling moments due to grain shift, at least the following:

  1. Angle of heel. The angle of heel due to the shift of grain shall not be greater than 12 degrees - or, for ships constructed on or after 1 January 1994, the angle at which the deck edge is immersed, whichever is the lesser. The purpose is to keep the deck edge out of the water and the ship workable and manoeuvrable.
  2. Residual dynamical stability. In the statical stability diagram, the net or residual area between the heeling arm curve and the righting arm curve, measured from the angle of equilibrium up to the angle of maximum difference between the ordinates, or 40 degrees, or the angle of flooding, whichever is the least, shall in all conditions of loading be not less than 0.075 metre-radians. This is the reserve of energy the ship keeps after the grain has done its worst.
  3. Initial metacentric height. The initial GM, after correction for the free surface effects of liquids in tanks, shall be not less than 0.30 metres. This underwrites the first two, because a ship with too little initial stiffness will fail them at almost any heeling arm.

Two features of the set are worth a sentence each. They are cumulative - all three must be satisfied, and satisfying two is failing. And they must hold throughout the voyage, not merely on departure, which is why consumption of fuel and water from double bottoms has to be planned: burning off bottom weight raises G and can put a ship that sailed compliant outside the criteria days later.

7. Reading the three criteria off the one diagram

  • Criterion 1 is a point on the horizontal axis - where the two curves first cross. It must fall at or inside 12 degrees, or inside deck edge immersion for a ship of the later construction date, whichever comes first.
  • Criterion 2 is the shaded area - bounded below by the heeling arm line, above by the GZ curve, on the left by the equilibrium angle and on the right by the least of the maximum-difference angle, 40 degrees and the flooding angle. It must be at least 0.075 metre-radians.
  • Criterion 3 is the slope at the origin - the tangent, read at 57.3 degrees, giving a fluid GM of at least 0.30 metres.
  • The three are not independent. A larger heeling arm pushes the crossing point right, which simultaneously eats into the left-hand end of the shaded area. So a marginal ship usually fails criteria 1 and 2 together, and the way to recover both is the same - reduce the heeling moment.

8. Reducing the heeling moment, and the document that proves it

The criteria are met by controlling the cargo, not by hoping. The measures a candidate should be able to name:

  • Fill and trim. A filled, trimmed compartment carries a far smaller assumed heeling moment than a partly filled one. Trimming the surface level and filling every void that can be filled is the cheapest stability the ship will ever get.
  • Minimise the number of slack compartments, and where a partly filled compartment is unavoidable, place it low and secure its surface.
  • Secure the surface of a partly filled compartment - overstowing with bagged grain or other suitable cargo, or strapping or lashing the surface, or the traditional saucer and bundling arrangements.
  • Use longitudinal divisions where fitted - a centreline bulkhead or shifting boards halve the transverse travel and so the heeling moment.
  • Plan consumption. Keep the ship's GM within the criteria for the whole passage, allowing for the rise of G as double bottom fuel and water are used.
  • Watch the weather and the rolling, since it is rolling that mobilises the surface in the first place.

Finally, the paperwork, because it is what a surveyor or a port State control officer will actually ask for. A ship loading bulk grain must hold a Document of Authorization issued by or on behalf of the Administration, certifying that she is capable of complying with the Code, together with the approved grain loading information - the stability data and the assumed volumetric heeling moments - by which the master demonstrates, before loading, that the intended condition will satisfy all three criteria for the whole voyage.

The proposition to close on: the criteria do not prevent the grain from shifting. They accept that it will, and require the ship to be able to survive it - upright enough to work, with enough residual energy to absorb a gust, and stiff enough at the start for both of those to be true.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains two commands joined by a comma - explain how the shift affects the curve, and with a neat diagram explain the minimum criteria. Both must be answered, and candidates habitually do one. The structure above answers the mechanism in sections 1 to 3 and 5, and the criteria in sections 6 and 7, with section 4 - the diagram - deliberately placed between them so that it serves both.

That placement is the single most useful decision in the answer. Drawn once, the diagram carries the equilibrium angle, the residual area and the initial GM, so every criterion in section 6 can be pointed at rather than described in the abstract. The examiner asked for a neat diagram and it is worth real marks; a diagram that appears after the criteria, as decoration, earns far less than one that the criteria are read from.

Section 2 is where the understanding marks are. Almost anyone can list three numbers; the candidate who explains why a dry cargo shift is irreversible, and therefore why it produces a permanent list rather than a symmetrical loss of GM, has shown that he knows why the regime exists. Section 8 then closes the loop from theory to the ship, which is what a management-level paper is testing.

Common mistakes

  • Treating grain shift as a free surface effect and correcting it as a virtual rise of G. A liquid free surface is symmetrical and reversible; a grain shift is one-way. The Code uses a heeling arm precisely because the free surface model does not fit.
  • Answering from the IMSBC Code - cargo groups, transportable moisture limit, can test. Grain is outside that Code and has its own.
  • Confusing shifting with liquefaction. Different mechanism, different cargo, different failure.
  • Drawing the heeling arm as a cosine curve. The Code's assumed heeling arm is a straight line from its value at zero to 0.8 of that at forty degrees.
  • Giving the residual area limit without saying between which angles it is measured. The bounds are half the criterion.
  • Forgetting that GM must be the fluid value, corrected for free surface in tanks.
  • Stating the criteria as alternatives. All three must be met, in all conditions of loading, throughout the voyage.
  • Omitting the diagram, or drawing it without labelling the shaded area and the equilibrium angle. The stem asks for it by name.

Examiner traps

  • Twelve degrees is not the whole of criterion 1. For ships constructed on or after 1 January 1994 it is twelve degrees or the angle of deck edge immersion, whichever is the lesser. Quoting twelve alone is a half answer.
  • The upper bound of the residual area is the LEAST of three angles - the angle of maximum difference between the ordinates, forty degrees, and the angle of flooding. Candidates routinely say forty and stop.
  • 0.075 is in metre-radians, not metre-degrees. The unit is part of the mark.
  • Throughout the voyage, not on departure. Burning double bottom fuel raises G and can put a compliant ship outside the criteria mid-passage. This is a favourite oral follow-up.
  • The 0.8 factor applies to the heeling arm at forty degrees, not to the righting arm and not to the area.
  • Grain settles, so even a filled and trimmed compartment shifts. Saying that a full hold cannot shift is wrong and invites the follow-up about the void.
  • The Document of Authorization is a specific certificate. It is not the stability booklet, though the approved grain loading information accompanies it.

Uncertainty and evidence limits

  • Where this answer's authority comes from, and where it stops. SOLAS chapter VI has been read at source: regulation 8 defines the International Grain Code as the Code adopted by resolution MSC.23(59) and defines what counts as grain, and regulation 9 requires a ship carrying grain to comply with the Code and to hold a Document of Authorization, the Code's requirements being treated as mandatory. The Grain Code itself is not held, so the three numerical criteria, the 0.8 heeling-arm factor and the assumed shift-angle distinction are given as settled, universally published professional knowledge rather than as text read at source. They are what the question asks for and they are given in full; check them against your ship's approved grain loading manual, which states them for her.
  • The mechanism is derived, not cited.   • The approximately two per cent settlement figure is a conventional working value from grain loading practice, given as such and not as a Code value.
  • No stowage factor, bulk density or angle of repose is given for any grain. These are cargo properties varying by commodity and shipment, and MIW does not assert them from unverified sources.
  • The assumed shift angles for filled and partly filled compartments are described by the distinction they draw rather than pinned to quoted values, because the distinction is what carries the marks and the values are not held.

A liquid free surface is symmetrical and reversible - it sloshes both ways and is handled by a virtual rise of G. A grain surface slides down to a new angle of repose and stays there, because nothing carries it back up. The result is a permanent list, not a symmetrical loss of stiffness, so the Code models it as a heeling arm rather than a GM correction.

The heeling arm at zero degrees is the total assumed volumetric heeling moment divided by the product of stowage factor and displacement. The heeling arm at forty degrees is 0.8 of that value. The two are joined by a straight line, drawn on the same axes as the GZ curve. It is a straight line, not a cosine curve.

The ship works to the residual curve - righting arm minus heeling arm. Equilibrium moves from upright to the crossing angle and stays there; the maximum righting lever is reduced and moves outwards; the range of stability is shortened; and the area under the curve, the dynamical stability, is reduced. The loss is not recovered because the list is permanent.

Understand this first

Start with what the load line on the ship's side is actually promising. It is not a rule about how much cargo you may carry for its own sake; it is a statement that above the water there remains a certain volume of sealed, buoyant hull - the part that lifts her again when a sea presses her down. That volume is the whole safety margin. Now notice the assumption hiding inside it. That volume is only buoyant while it stays sealed. A hull sitting high in the water with a jammed-open engine room vent, or a hatch whose packing has gone hard, has no margin at all in the one condition where the margin was supposed to matter - it simply fills. So the mark on the side cannot be earned by the shape of the hull alone. It is granted on condition that every hole through that sealed volume - doors, hatches, vents, air pipes, discharge lines, scuttles, even the rubbish chute and the pipe the anchor cable runs through - can be shut and will stay tight in bad weather. Write that bargain down as a list of requirements and you have the conditions of assignment. Two consequences follow and they are the rest of the question. First, the requirements have a pattern: put the opening high enough that water running across the deck does not reach it, make the thing that closes it strong, tight and impossible to leave behind, and give the water on deck somewhere to run away to. Second, and this is why they must be looked at again and again: the hull's dimensions never change, but a gasket, a cleat, a hinge and a valve all do. The freeboard was calculated once; the things it depends on wear out continuously.

Model written answer

1. Where the term comes from - what a freeboard assumes

A ship is assigned a freeboard - the distance from the waterline to the freeboard deck - and that freeboard is a measure of her reserve buoyancy: the enclosed, intact volume above the waterline that will lift her when she is pressed down by a sea.

But that reserve is only real if the enclosed volume stays enclosed. A hull with a generous freeboard and an open engine room skylight has no reserve buoyancy at all in the only condition that matters. So the freeboard cannot be assigned on the hull form alone. It is assigned on the assumption that every opening in the hull and superstructure can be closed, and kept closed and tight, in the weather the ship will meet.

The conditions of assignment are that assumption written down as enforceable requirements. Hold that sentence and the whole question follows from it.

2. A. The definition

The conditions of assignment are the structural and closing-appliance requirements of chapter II of Annex I to the International Convention on Load Lines, 1966 as modified by its 1988 Protocol - the chapter is headed 'Conditions of assignment of freeboard' - which a ship must satisfy before a freeboard is assigned to her, and must continue to satisfy for that freeboard to remain valid.

Four points complete the definition and are worth stating separately, because each is markable:

  • They are conditions, not recommendations. A freeboard is assigned subject to them, so a ship that no longer meets them no longer properly holds the freeboard she is marked for.
  • They are continuing, not a one-off entrance test. They are verified at the initial survey and re-verified at every survey thereafter, which is the whole subject of limb C.
  • They are ship-specific and recorded. The Convention provides a Form of Record of conditions of assignment of load lines which lists, fitting by fitting, the deck each is on, its number, its material, its coaming dimensions and height, its type and its closing appliances down to the number of clips. That record is what makes them auditable rather than a matter of opinion.
  • They are the price of the freeboard. A ship assigned a smaller freeboard is required to meet stricter conditions - regulation 17, for example, requires double doors to the machinery casing on ships assigned freeboards less than those based on the tabular value, with an inner sill of 230 mm in conjunction with the outer sill of 600 mm. Less freeboard is bought with more protection.

3. A. What the conditions consist of

Chapter II runs from regulation 10 to regulation 26 and covers, in order: the information to be supplied to the master; superstructure end bulkheads; doors; the position of hatchways, doorways and ventilators; cargo and other hatchways and their covers; machinery space openings; miscellaneous openings in freeboard and superstructure decks; ventilators; air pipes; cargo ports and other similar openings; scuppers, inlets and discharges, with garbage chutes and spurling pipes; side scuttles, windows and skylights; freeing ports; protection of the crew; and special conditions of assignment for type A ships.

Note the first of these, because candidates always omit it. Regulation 10 is a condition of assignment too: the master must be supplied with approved stability information, and with loading information relating to ship strength, in a form approved by the Administration or a recognized organization, and it must be carried on board at all times together with evidence that it has been approved. Paperwork, on this instrument, is a condition of the freeboard in exactly the same way a hatch cover gasket is.

4. B. Protecting the openings - position and sill height

The first mechanism by which the conditions produce watertight integrity is geography. The Convention divides the exposed decks into position 1 and position 2 according to how exposed to boarding seas an opening is, and then requires more protection where the risk is greater. The pattern is consistent throughout the chapter:

  • Machinery space access doors - sills at least 600 mm above the deck in position 1, at least 380 mm in position 2.
  • Companionway doorways - the same 600 mm and 380 mm; and 600 mm to doorways in deckhouses on the freeboard deck where there is no alternative access from above, reduced to 380 mm where there is.
  • Ventilator coamings - at least 900 mm above the deck in position 1, at least 760 mm in position 2, of steel or equivalent, efficiently connected to the deck, and specially supported where the coaming exceeds 900 mm.
  • Air pipes - the height from the deck to the point where water may have access below at least 760 mm on the freeboard deck and 450 mm on the superstructure deck.
  • Side scuttles - sills not below a line whose lowest point is 2.5 per cent of the breadth, or 500 mm, whichever is the greater, above the summer load line; and windows are not permitted below the freeboard deck at all.

The principle is simple and is worth stating: lift the opening above the water that will run across the deck, and where you cannot lift it far enough, do not put an opening there.

5. B. Closures, coamings and the valves that stop back-flooding

Height alone is not enough, so the second mechanism is the closure itself, and the third is control of the penetrations through the shell.

  • Structural enclosure. Machinery space openings in position 1 or 2 must be properly framed and efficiently enclosed by steel casings of ample strength. The casing is part of the buoyant envelope, not a weather shelter.
  • Permanently attached closures. Covers to fiddley openings must be of steel or equivalent material and permanently attached in their proper positions. A cover that can be left ashore is not a closing appliance, and this is why the Convention keeps using the word 'permanently'.
  • Weathertight hatch covers, secured by gaskets and clamping devices or by equivalent means to the Administration's satisfaction, and located in the closed position by means capable of withstanding horizontally acting loads in any sea conditions - because a cover that lifts and shifts in a seaway has failed even if it has not come off.
  • Air pipe closing devices. Air pipes must be provided with automatic closing devices; pressure-vacuum valves may be accepted on tankers. This is the fitting that stops a tank being downflooded by a wave breaking over the deck.
  • Cargo ports and shell openings. Doors so designed as to ensure the same watertightness and structural integrity as the surrounding shell plating, opening outwards so that sea pressure seats them, kept to the minimum number compatible with the design and proper working of the ship, and with the lower edge not below a line at least 230 mm above the upper edge of the uppermost load line.
  • Scuppers, inlets and discharges. This is where integrity is most often lost from the inside. The Convention grades the non-return valve arrangement by how high the inboard end sits above the summer load line, using 0.01L and 0.02L thresholds, and requires the valve controls to be readily accessible and provided with indicators showing whether the valve is open or closed. Pipes needing substantial thickness are given minimum scantlings - 7 mm, 10 mm and 12.5 mm at external diameters of 80 mm or less, 180 mm, and 220 mm or more.
  • Scuppers from cargo spaces. Scuppers led through the shell from enclosed superstructures used for carrying cargo are permitted only where the freeboard deck edge is not immersed when the ship heels 5 degrees either way - a neat example of the Convention writing a stability condition into a piping rule.
  • Garbage chutes and spurling pipes, the two penetrations everyone forgets. Chutes require interlocked gate valves with controls clearly marked 'Keep closed when not in use', and the inboard end above the waterline formed by an 8.5 degree heel and not less than 1,000 mm above the summer waterline. Spurling pipes require permanently attached closing appliances to minimize water ingress.

6. B. Draining the deck, and protecting the crew who must close things

  • Freeing ports. Where bulwarks form wells on weather decks, ample provision must be made for rapidly freeing the decks of water and draining them, and the Convention sets the minimum area: A = 0.7 + 0.035l square metres per side per well where the bulwark length in the well is 20 m or less, and A = 0.07l where it exceeds 20 m, halved for wells on superstructure decks, with an adjustment of 0.004 square metres per metre of well length for each 0.1 m by which the average bulwark height differs from the stated thresholds. The reasoning is direct: water trapped on deck is a large free surface of weight in the worst possible place, and a ship can be lost to it without a single opening having leaked. Note too that gutter bars over 300 mm on tanker weather decks in way of cargo manifolds are treated as bulwarks, and their closures must be arranged so that jamming cannot occur while at sea.
  • Protection of the crew. Guard rails, lifelines, gangways and safe access are conditions of assignment in their own right, and the connection to integrity is not decorative. Every closing appliance on this list has to be shut by a human being, often in the weather that makes it necessary. If the crew cannot reach the ventilator head or the freeing port shutter safely, the closure is theoretical.
  • Information to the master. Regulation 10's approved stability and loading information is what lets the ship be loaded so that the freeboard she is marked for is the freeboard she actually floats to, and so that she is not stressed into a condition none of the above was designed for.

7. C. Why they need periodic inspection

  • They are the only part of the freeboard that can deteriorate. Length, breadth, depth, sheer and superstructure length do not change over a ship's life. Gaskets, cleats, coamings, valves and shutters change continuously. So the freeboard's validity depends entirely on the part of the calculation that decays.
  • They are worked every day. Hatch covers, weathertight doors and freeing port shutters are operating machinery handled by the crew in every port. Wear is not a fault; it is certain, and it is why the interval is periodic rather than once.
  • They corrode in the worst places. Coamings, compression bars, drain channels and cleat pads are wet, salt-laden and difficult to paint properly.
  • They are damaged by cargo work. Grabs, forklifts, spreaders and steel cargo strike coamings, air pipe heads, guard rails and ventilator heads routinely.
  • They are defeated for operational convenience. This is the important one, and it is where inspection earns its place: the crew that lashes a freeing port shut to keep spray off the deck, or paints a deadlight open, has removed a condition of assignment deliberately and for what felt at the time like a good reason.
  • They are lost. Portable covers, deadlights and ventilator closing appliances go missing. The Convention's repeated insistence on permanently attached closures is a direct response, and inspection is how the missing item is found.
  • Ships are modified. Conversions, new penetrations, removed bulwarks and altered gutter bars change the arrangement recorded in the Form of Record, and an unapproved modification is only ever caught by someone comparing the ship to that record.
  • There is a record designed to be checked against. Because the Form of Record lists each fitting with its dimensions and closing appliances, inspection is an item-by-item verification against a document rather than a general impression - which is exactly what makes the annual survey around the anniversary date, the renewal survey, and port State control able to find things at all.

8. C. Specific instances where they are found less than fully effective

The examiner has asked for specific instances, so name them. These are the findings that recur:

  • Hatch covers - gaskets permanently compressed, hardened, painted over or missing in the cross-joints; compression bars corroded, wasted or set over so they no longer bear on the packing; cleats slack, seized, bent or removed; and the non-return valves in the drain channels missing or seized open, which turns the drainage arrangement into a direct route into the hold. A hose test or ultrasonic test on a cover that looks perfectly sound routinely fails on these.
  • Weathertight doors - gasket painted, hardened or torn; dogs and clips seized, missing or wound back so the door merely rests closed; hinges worn so the door does not pull up square; the door leaf itself set in from an impact so it cannot compress the gasket evenly.
  • Ventilators - closing appliances not permanently attached and simply absent; mushroom heads seized; coamings wasted at the deck connection; a ventilator cut and re-sited without approval.
  • Air pipes - the automatic closing device defective: float seized, ball missing, seat corroded, or the gauze so corroded that the head is effectively blanked. Air pipe heads on deck are also the fitting most often struck by cargo gear and left distorted.
  • Freeing ports - shutters seized on corroded hinge pins, jammed by dunnage or cargo residue, or deliberately secured shut. Where gutter bars are involved, closures found jammed at sea, which is the specific outcome the Convention writes against.
  • Scuppers, inlets and discharges - non-return valves seized open or with the flap missing; positive-closing valve controls inaccessible behind later pipework; indicators missing or reading the opposite of the valve's actual position; ship-side pipework wasted below the required substantial thickness.
  • Side scuttles and windows - deadlights missing, painted open, or hinges corroded solid; deadlights fitted where the space now requires them after a conversion, and not fitted.
  • Garbage chutes - covers left open, interlocks disconnected or defeated, and the required marking painted over.
  • Protection of the crew - guard rails slack or with wires parted, stanchions bent by cargo work, gangway lighting and safe access degraded so that closing appliances cannot be reached safely in the conditions that require them.
  • Regulation 10 information - the approved stability and loading information superseded by a conversion, or the evidence of approval no longer on board.

The proposition to close on: the freeboard is a calculation, and the conditions of assignment are what keep the calculation true. A ship whose closing appliances have quietly degraded is not marked with the wrong load line - she is marked with a load line she is no longer entitled to, and that is why the inspection is periodic and why the record exists to inspect against.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are printed - 5, 5 and 6 - and they are not equal. Limb C is the largest and it is also the one that asks for something a textbook cannot supply: specific instances. Budget accordingly and do not let limb A, which is a definition worth five, absorb a third of the answer.

Section 1 exists to earn limb A rather than to introduce it. If the answer opens by explaining that a freeboard is a measure of reserve buoyancy, and that reserve buoyancy is only real while the enclosed volume stays enclosed, then the definition in section 2 is a consequence rather than a recitation - and an examiner can see immediately that the candidate knows why the conditions exist. That single idea is the spine of all three limbs.

Limb B is answered as three mechanisms - lift the opening, close it properly, drain the deck - rather than as a list of regulations. That is deliberate: the regulations are numerous and a list of them is hard to remember and harder to mark, while three mechanisms with examples hung on them covers the same ground and is reproducible under pressure. Limb C is split into reasons and instances because the stem asks for both, and candidates who merge them typically give reasons and forget the instances, which is where half the limb's marks sit.

Common mistakes

  • Defining conditions of assignment as the load line marks, or as the freeboard itself. They are the requirements the ship must meet in order to be assigned that freeboard.
  • Treating them as a one-off entrance test. They are continuing conditions, which is the premise of limb C.
  • Omitting regulation 10. The approved stability and loading information carried on board is a condition of assignment, and it is the one candidates never mention.
  • Answering limb B with a list of regulation numbers and no mechanism. Say what each requirement does.
  • Giving reasons in limb C and no specific instances, when the stem asks for instances by name.
  • Confusing weathertight with watertight. The Convention uses both, and they are not interchangeable.
  • Forgetting the openings nobody thinks about - garbage chutes, spurling pipes, air pipe closing devices. Naming these signals real shipboard knowledge.
  • Discussing SOLAS damage stability instead. This is a Load Line question.

Examiner traps

  • Weathertight is not watertight. Weathertight means water will not penetrate into the ship in any sea condition; watertight is the stronger requirement, capable of resisting head pressure. Cargo ports below the freeboard deck are required to match the shell for watertightness; a hatch cover on deck is weathertight.
  • Position 1 and position 2 are the organising idea of the whole chapter. Nearly every sill and coaming figure comes in a pair, and the higher number is position 1.
  • Less freeboard means stricter conditions. Regulation 17's double doors with the 230 mm inner sill apply to ships assigned less than the tabular freeboard. This is the point that shows you understand the trade.
  • Freeing ports keep water OUT of the ship by letting it OFF the deck. A crew securing them shut to reduce spray has inverted the purpose, and it is a favourite oral scenario.
  • Cargo port doors open outwards so that sea pressure seats them rather than forcing them open.
  • Closing appliances must be permanently attached. The word recurs throughout the chapter and it is the answer to 'the cover is ashore in the store'.
  • The Form of Record is a real document. Knowing it exists, and that it lists fittings down to the number of clips, is what makes limb C a survey answer rather than a general one.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What exactly is a freeboard, and what does it measure?
  • What is the difference between weathertight and watertight? Give me a fitting that must be each.
  • What are positions 1 and 2, and give me a sill height for each.
  • Why must a cargo port door open outwards?
  • What is fitted to an air pipe and why?
  • Your freeing port shutters are lashed shut because the deck was flooding on passage. What do you say?
  • What is recorded in the Record of Conditions of Assignment?
  • You are assigned a freeboard less than the tabular value. What extra is required at the machinery casing?
  • Name three penetrations of the buoyant envelope that a surveyor commonly finds defective.

Understand this first

Two completely different kinds of checking happen on a ship and they are easy to confuse because the same organisation often does both, sometimes on the same day. One asks is the ship in the state she is supposed to be in - is the steel thick enough, does the pump run, is the lifeboat sound. Someone looks at the thing, measures it, tests it, and what he finds is a fault in an object. The other asks a quite different question: is there a working system here, and are people actually following it? That cannot be answered by looking at objects, because a ship can be immaculate and still be run by improvisation, and it can be shabby and still be run by a system that knows exactly what is wrong and when it will be fixed. So the second kind of checking works by sampling evidence - reading records, asking the third engineer what he would do, watching whether what the manual says actually happens - and what it finds is not a broken object but a gap between what the system promises and what is being done. That difference explains why the repair that closes the first kind of finding does not close the second: fixing the pump does not answer why the system let it fail unnoticed. The second half of the question is about a limit on authority. When a flag state hires an outside organisation to do this checking for it, that organisation gets to make the professional judgements - is this a gap, is it serious, has it really been fixed. What it does not get is the flag state's own power to decide the terms of the certificate it issues in the flag state's name. So on any request that would change those terms - extend it, alter what it says - the organisation gathers the facts and hands the decision upward. Sort each of the four situations into judgement or terms, and the answers fall out.

Model written answer

1. (a) Audit and survey distinguished

A survey is an examination of the ship herself - her structure, machinery, equipment and arrangements - against prescriptive technical requirements, to establish that her physical condition satisfies the relevant convention. A safety management audit is a systematic and independent examination to determine whether the safety management system and the activities carried out under it conform to planned arrangements, are implemented effectively, and are suitable to achieve the objectives.

Put shortly: a survey looks at the ship; an audit looks at the system and at the people who run it.

SurveyAudit

2. (a) Why the distinction matters on a real ship

  • A ship can pass every survey and fail an audit, and the reverse. Equipment in perfect order that nobody has been trained to use, or maintenance carried out but never recorded, is a system failure with no physical defect. A wasted plate is a physical defect with no system failure - unless the planned maintenance system should have found it, in which case it is both.
  • A deficiency is corrected; a non-conformity requires a cause to be found. That is the practical difference for the Chief Engineer. Repairing the item closes a survey deficiency. Closing a non-conformity means establishing why the system permitted it and correcting that, which is why corrective action under the Code is the Company's responsibility and not the auditor's.
  • An audit samples; a survey examines. An auditor cannot see everything and does not pretend to - he takes objective evidence and reasons from it. This is why an audit finding is expressed as a conformity judgement about a system rather than as a list.
  • They are done by different people to different standards, and the same visit may involve both - which is exactly why a candidate must be able to say which is which.

3. The position of the recognized organization

All four scenarios in limb (b) turn on one point, so state it once. Where the Administration has delegated ISM certification, the Administration remains responsible for verifying compliance with the Code and for issuing the Document of Compliance and the Safety Management Certificate. The recognized organization acts on its behalf, within a scope specified in the authorization.

So the RO makes the technical judgements that verification requires - is this a non-conformity, is it major, has corrective action been taken and is it effective. It does not have authority to alter the terms on which the flag State certified the ship. That distinction decides every one of the four answers below: where the question is one of verification the RO acts, and where the question is one of the certificate's terms the RO refers to the Administration.

4. (b)(i) Where there is evidence of a major non conformity

A major non-conformity is an identifiable deviation posing a serious threat to the safety of personnel or the ship, or a serious risk to the environment, requiring immediate corrective action - and it includes the lack of effective and systematic implementation of a requirement of the Code.

  • The certificate must not be issued, and an existing certificate must not be endorsed for the annual or intermediate verification.
  • The finding must be downgraded to a non-conformity by immediate corrective action before a certificate can be issued or endorsed. Downgrading is a judgement on evidence, and it is the RO's to make.
  • Where the ship is the one holding a major non-conformity, the Administration and the port State are informed in accordance with the established procedures for observed major non-conformities.
  • Where corrective action is not taken, the RO reports to the Administration, which decides on withdrawal.

5. (b)(ii) Where non-conformities are found

An ordinary non-conformity is an observed situation where objective evidence indicates the non-fulfilment of a specified requirement, but without the serious threat that makes it major. The consequences are materially different.

  • The certificate may still be issued or endorsed.
  • The Company - not the RO - is responsible for determining and initiating the corrective action needed to correct the non-conformity or its cause, within an agreed time.
  • Corrective actions and any consequent changes to the system are recorded, and their completion is verified, normally at the next verification or earlier if the finding warrants it.
  • Failure to correct may itself become a major non-conformity, because it evidences a lack of effective and systematic implementation. That is the sentence which links this scenario to the previous one and it is worth stating.

6. (b)(iii) Where an extension of the Safety Management Certificate is requested

Answer this one by first saying what is not there. Neither the ISM Code nor the guidelines contain a general power to extend a Safety Management Certificate. The Code provides for verification and for renewal before expiry, and it fixes what the validity of a renewed certificate is; it does not create a discretion to prolong a certificate that is running out.

  • It follows that the RO has nothing of its own to grant. The request lies outside the delegated verification function.
  • The RO's action is to refer the request to the Administration, which alone may decide what to do with a certificate it issued, and to advise on the conditions under which any short extension might be contemplated - which is a technical judgement and therefore properly the RO's.
  • It also verifies the ship's actual position - when the renewal verification is due, what is outstanding, and whether the system is being maintained - so that the Administration decides on evidence rather than on assertion.
  • It does not endorse or annotate the certificate on its own authority.

7. (b)(iv) Where revision of an entry on a certificate is requested

The entries on a Document of Compliance or a Safety Management Certificate record what the flag State certified - the Company and its address, the ship, and the ship types covered. The RO issues on the Administration's behalf and therefore may not amend that record on its own authority. Its first action is to establish which kind of change is being asked for:

  • Administrative - a change of company name or address, or of ship name, where the underlying facts of certification are unchanged. The RO verifies that the change is genuine and supported, refers it to the Administration, and on its authority the certificate is re-issued with the corrected entry.
  • Substantive - a change of Company, or the addition of a ship type to the scope. This is not a correction at all: it changes what was certified, so it requires verification appropriate to the change - which for a change of Company means the new Company's system must be verified, and an interim certificate may be the proper instrument while that is done.

In both cases the RO records the change and reports it to the Administration. It does not simply amend the certificate in hand.

8. (c) When the DOC and the SMC may be invalidated

The Document of Compliance may be withdrawn or cease to be valid where:

  • the annual verification is not requested - it must be carried out within three months before or after each anniversary date;
  • there is evidence of major non-conformity with the Code;
  • it expires without renewal verification having been completed - the period of validity is five years;
  • the Company ceases to be the Company responsible for the operation of the ships, or the conditions on which it was issued otherwise fall away.

The Safety Management Certificate may be withdrawn or cease to be valid where:

  • the Document of Compliance is withdrawn - all associated Safety Management Certificates and interim certificates are withdrawn with it. This is the trigger candidates most often miss and it is the most examinable point in the limb: the ship's certificate depends on the Company's.
  • the intermediate verification is not requested - at least one is required between the second and third anniversary dates;
  • there is evidence of major non-conformity with the Code;
  • it expires without renewal verification - again a five-year period of validity;
  • there is a change of Company or of flag, the certificate having been issued to that ship operated by that Company under that Administration.

Underlying all of these: failure to correct non-conformities may affect the validity of the Document of Compliance and of the related Safety Management Certificates, and failure to take adequate corrective action may itself be treated as a major non-conformity. Note finally the direction of authority, which is the closing proposition: the RO establishes the facts and reports; the Administration withdraws.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are printed and they have shifted from the way this question is usually set. Limb (a) is worth four, not two, so a one-line contrast will not do - it needs a worked comparison, which is why the answer gives a table and then a section on why the distinction bites. Limb (b) is four separate two-mark parts, so each scenario must be a self-contained answer with its own action; a flowing narrative that covers all four in one breath will be marked as one. Limb (c) is worth four rather than six, so the grounds are given cleanly without extended commentary.

Section 3 is the structural decision that makes limb (b) work. All four scenarios turn on the same point - whether the question is one of verification, which the RO may decide, or one of the certificate's terms, which it may not. Stating that once, before the scenarios, means each of the four can then be answered in the two marks' worth of space it actually has, instead of re-arguing the principle four times.

Limb (c) is written as two separate lists because the DOC and the SMC have different triggers and the examiner is looking for both. The single highest-value sentence in the whole answer is that withdrawal of the DOC carries all associated SMCs and interim certificates with it.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb (a) with one sentence. It is worth four marks at this sitting, which is a quarter of the question.
  • Saying an audit checks the ship and a survey checks the paperwork. It is the other way round in substance: the survey examines the ship, the audit examines the system.
  • Treating a major non-conformity as merely a serious non-conformity. It has a definition, and it includes the lack of effective and systematic implementation of a Code requirement - which is how a series of small failures becomes major.
  • Saying the RO withdraws the certificate. It does not. It verifies, reports and refers; the Administration withdraws.
  • Inventing a power to extend the Safety Management Certificate. There is no general extension power, and saying so is the correct answer to (b)(iii).
  • Treating a change of Company as a clerical correction. It changes what was certified and requires verification, not re-typing.
  • Forgetting that withdrawal of the DOC withdraws every associated SMC.
  • Giving the verification windows loosely. Three months either side of the anniversary for the DOC; between the second and third anniversaries for the SMC intermediate.

Examiner traps

  • The operative guidelines at this sitting are the 2017 edition, resolution A.1118(30). The 2023 Guidelines, A.1188(33), were adopted 6 December 2023 - three months AFTER this paper - and revoked A.1118(30). Any study material written for a 2024 or later sitting cites the later resolution, correctly for itself and wrongly for this one.
  • Do not quote a guideline paragraph number. The paragraph numbering of the operative edition is not something you can safely reproduce, and the substance you need is in the Code itself.
  • Deficiency versus non-conformity. The word tells the examiner which process you are describing, and using the wrong one signals that the limb (a) distinction has not landed.
  • Downgrading a major non-conformity is a real mechanism. Immediate corrective action can reduce it to a non-conformity and allow the certificate to be issued. Knowing this separates a good answer from a list.
  • The Company owns corrective action. Not the RO, not the auditor. The auditor verifies that it was taken.
  • Five years for both certificates, with different intermediate arrangements - annual for the DOC, at least one intermediate between the second and third anniversaries for the SMC.
  • Interim certificates exist, and an interim SMC is the right instrument when a ship changes Company or flag. Reaching for it in (b)(iv) shows real familiarity.

The second half is the part candidates forget, and it is how an accumulation of small failures becomes major.

It does not issue the certificate and does not endorse an existing one. The Administration and the port State are informed under the established procedures for observed major non-conformities. If corrective action is not taken, it reports to the Administration, which decides on withdrawal.

There is no general power to extend an SMC in the Code or the guidelines - the Code provides for verification and for renewal before expiry, not for prolonging a certificate. The RO therefore has nothing of its own to grant. It refers the request to the Administration, advises on the technical conditions, and verifies what is actually outstanding. It does not endorse or annotate the certificate on its own authority.

All associated Safety Management Certificates and interim certificates are withdrawn with it. Understand this first

The sea is not one legal space; it is a series of belts running outwards from the coast, and a country's grip loosens with every belt it crosses. Close in, the water is simply part of the country - the same authority applies there as ashore, with one important concession: foreign ships may pass through, provided they behave. A little further out there is a narrow band where the country is not in charge, but may still reach out to stop or punish smuggling, tax evasion, illegal entry and disease-related breaches connected with its own territory - and nothing else. Further out again is a much wider belt where the country's interest is economic rather than territorial: the fish, the oil, the wind and the seabed minerals are its to exploit and manage, but ships and aircraft still come and go freely, because the entitlement is over resources and not over the water. The seabed carries a separate story, because it belongs to the land it is geologically attached to, whether or not anyone claims it or works it. And beyond everything is water no country owns at all, where the only authority over a ship is the country whose flag she flies. Running across all of these belts is one duty that does not weaken with distance: every state must protect and preserve the marine environment. That is where the second half of the question sits, and the point to grasp is that this framework treaty contains no discharge limits, no equipment standards and no survey requirements. It obliges states to make those rules through a competent international body and then to enforce them - which is precisely why the detailed rules a marine engineer works to exist somewhere else entirely, and why they bind him at all.

Model written answer

1. (a) The general obligation, and what makes Part XII unusual

The environmental provisions are in Part XII, and it opens with one of the shortest and most important sentences in the Convention. Article 192: States have the obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment.

Three things make that opening unusual and each is worth a mark:

  • It is a free-standing obligation, not a limitation on a right. It is owed by every State, everywhere, and it is not confined to any zone. The zones in limb (b) allocate rights; article 192 imposes a duty that runs across all of them.
  • The sovereign right to exploit natural resources is expressly made subject to it. A State may exploit its resources pursuant to its environmental policies, but in accordance with its duty to protect and preserve - so development and protection are not balanced against one another; the second conditions the first.
  • Article 194 turns the duty into an obligation of conduct. States shall take all measures necessary to prevent, reduce and control pollution of the marine environment from any source, using the best practicable means at their disposal and in accordance with their capabilities - a standard that is demanding and differentiated at the same time - and shall ensure that activities under their jurisdiction or control do not cause damage by pollution to other States and their environment.

2. (a) The sources of pollution, and the rule-making pattern

Part XII then works through pollution source by source: land-based sources including rivers, estuaries, pipelines and outfalls; seabed activities under national jurisdiction and activities in the Area; dumping; vessels; and the atmosphere. A separate article addresses the use of technologies and the intentional or accidental introduction of alien or new species - which is the treaty root of the ballast water regime.

The pattern running through them is the highlight a marine engineer should name, because it explains the whole architecture of his own professional world:

  • UNCLOS sets the duty and delegates the detail. For pollution from vessels, States act through the competent international organization or general diplomatic conference to establish international rules and standards - which is UNCLOS pointing at IMO without naming it.
  • National law must be no less effective than those internationally agreed rules and standards. That is the hinge by which MARPOL and the rest reach the ship.
  • The rules are to be re-examined from time to time, so the framework is built to be updated rather than fixed.
  • States must not transfer damage or hazards from one area to another, or transform one type of pollution into another - the provision that forbids solving a problem by moving it.

This is why UNCLOS is properly called the constitution for the oceans: it does not contain a single discharge limit, and every discharge limit exists because of it.

3. (a) Enforcement - the three-way division

The most original contribution of Part XII, and the one that reaches the ship most directly, is that it distributes enforcement between three States:

  • The flag State shall ensure compliance by vessels flying its flag with the applicable international rules and standards, shall adopt laws and take other measures for their implementation, and shall provide for effective enforcement irrespective of where a violation occurs.
  • The port State may, where a vessel is voluntarily within its port or at an offshore terminal, undertake investigations and institute proceedings in respect of a discharge from that vessel outside its own internal waters, territorial sea or exclusive economic zone. This is the striking one: it is jurisdiction over a violation that did not happen in the port State's waters at all, and it is the treaty foundation of modern port State control.
  • The coastal State may institute proceedings in respect of violations occurring within its territorial sea or exclusive economic zone, with powers graded by the seriousness of the violation and the evidence available - from requiring information, through inspection, to detention.
  • Beyond all of them, after a maritime casualty a State may take and enforce measures beyond the territorial sea proportionate to the actual or threatened damage, to protect its coastline and related interests.

4. (a) Responsibility, liability and the limits

  • States are responsible for the fulfilment of their international obligations concerning protection and preservation of the marine environment and shall be liable in accordance with international law.
  • They must also ensure that recourse is available under their legal systems for prompt and adequate compensation in respect of pollution damage caused by persons under their jurisdiction - which is the treaty basis on which the civil liability conventions were later built.
  • The enforcement powers are not unqualified. Part XII contains its own safeguards limiting how they may be exercised, which is what prevents an environmental power from becoming an unlimited right to interfere with navigation.
  • The Part also requires monitoring, environmental assessment, scientific and technical cooperation and assistance to developing States - obligations of capacity as well as of restraint.

5. (b)(i) Territorial sea

A belt of sea adjacent to the coast, extending beyond the land territory and internal waters, over which the coastal State has full sovereignty - extending also to the air space above it and to its bed and subsoil - exercised subject to the Convention and to other rules of international law.

Breadth: up to 12 nautical miles, measured from the baselines. The one qualification that must be stated, because it is what distinguishes this zone from land territory: sovereignty here is subject to the right of innocent passage of ships of all States.

6. (b)(ii) Contiguous zone

A zone contiguous to the territorial sea in which the coastal State does not have sovereignty, but may exercise the control necessary to:

  • prevent infringement of its customs, fiscal, immigration or sanitary laws and regulations within its territory or territorial sea; and
  • punish infringement of those laws committed within its territory or territorial sea.

Limit: may not extend beyond 24 nautical miles from the baselines. Two points carry the marks. The four subjects are exhaustive - it is not a general enforcement zone. And the control is referable to the land or territorial sea: it protects against infringements there, rather than creating new offences in the zone itself.

7. (b)(iii) Exclusive economic zone

An area beyond and adjacent to the territorial sea, under a specific legal regime, extending not beyond 200 nautical miles from the baselines. The coastal State does not have sovereignty; it has:

  • sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, living and non-living, of the waters, the seabed and its subsoil, and for other economic activities such as the production of energy from the water, currents and winds;
  • jurisdiction over artificial islands, installations and structures, marine scientific research, and the protection and preservation of the marine environment.

Other States retain the freedoms of navigation and overflight and of laying submarine cables and pipelines. The distinction to state expressly: sovereign rights are resource rights, not sovereignty - a merchant ship navigates through an EEZ as of right.

8. (b)(iv) Continental shelf

The seabed and subsoil of the submarine areas extending beyond the territorial sea, throughout the natural prolongation of the land territory to the outer edge of the continental margin - or to 200 nautical miles from the baselines where the outer edge does not reach that far. The margin consists of the shelf, the slope and the rise, and does not include the deep ocean floor with its oceanic ridges. Where the margin does extend beyond 200 miles the entitlement is not open-ended: the outer limit must not exceed 350 nautical miles from the baselines, or 100 nautical miles from the 2,500 metre isobath, whichever line the coastal State establishes.

The coastal State has sovereign rights for exploring and exploiting its natural resources, and three features distinguish these rights from every other zone:

  • they are exclusive - if the coastal State does not explore or exploit, no one else may without its express consent;
  • they do not depend on occupation, effective or notional, or on any express proclamation - they exist automatically;
  • they cover the seabed and subsoil only, not the water column above it, which is why the shelf and the EEZ are different things even where they occupy the same distance.

9. (b)(v) High seas

Defined by exclusion: all parts of the sea that are not included in the exclusive economic zone, the territorial sea or the internal waters of a State, or in the archipelagic waters of an archipelagic State.

They are open to all States, whether coastal or land-locked, and no State may validly claim sovereignty over any part of them. The freedoms comprise:

  1. freedom of navigation;
  2. freedom of overflight;
  3. freedom to lay submarine cables and pipelines;
  4. freedom to construct artificial islands and other installations permitted under international law;
  5. freedom of fishing;
  6. freedom of scientific research.

All are qualified in the same way, and the qualification is the mark: they shall be exercised with due regard for the interests of other States in their exercise of the same freedoms, and with due regard for rights relating to activities in the Area. A ship on the high seas is not beyond law - she is subject to the exclusive jurisdiction of her flag State, which is what makes the flag State's duties in limb (a) load-bearing.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The mark split has moved and it matters. Limb (a) is worth six here, not eight, and limb (b) is worth ten split into five separately weighted parts. The commonest way to lose marks on this question is to write a long essay on Part XII and then dispose of five zones in five lines. Reverse that instinct: the definitions are worth more than the essay, and each carries its own two marks.

Every zone in sections 5 to 9 is answered with the same three elements - the outer limit, what it is measured from, and the character of the coastal State's rights there. That consistency is deliberate. It makes each answer complete in about two marks' worth of writing, it makes them fast to produce under time pressure, and it forces the candidate to state the thing examiners actually test: that sovereignty, sovereign rights and control are three different strengths of entitlement.

Limb (a) is compressed to four themes - the obligation, the sources and rule-making pattern, enforcement, and liability. The enforcement theme is the one to protect if time runs short, because the three-way division is UNCLOS's own contribution and it is what connects the Convention to the candidate's working life.

Common mistakes

  • Over-writing limb (a) and under-writing limb (b). At this sitting the five definitions are worth ten of the sixteen marks.
  • Saying the coastal State has sovereignty over the EEZ. It has sovereign rights over resources and jurisdiction over three named matters - not sovereignty.
  • Giving the zone limits without saying what they are measured from. Every one of them runs from the baselines, and saying so is part of the definition.
  • Measuring the contiguous zone as 24 miles from the edge of the territorial sea. It is 24 miles from the baselines, so it is 12 miles wide where the territorial sea is 12.
  • Treating the continental shelf as a 200-mile rule. It is natural prolongation to the outer edge of the margin, with 200 miles as the entitlement where the margin falls short.
  • Confusing the shelf with the EEZ. The shelf is the seabed and subsoil; the EEZ includes the water column.
  • Defining the high seas positively. The Convention defines them by exclusion, and reproducing that is the cleanest two marks on the paper.
  • Forgetting the qualification on the high seas freedoms - due regard for the interests of other States.

Examiner traps

  • Sovereignty, sovereign rights, jurisdiction and control are four different things. Territorial sea - sovereignty. EEZ and shelf - sovereign rights plus jurisdiction. Contiguous zone - control only, and only over four named subjects. Getting this ladder right is what the whole of limb (b) is testing.
  • The contiguous zone subjects are exhaustive - customs, fiscal, immigration, sanitary. It is not a general enforcement or security zone.
  • Continental shelf rights do not require proclamation. They exist automatically and are exclusive. This is a favourite oral question.
  • Port State enforcement under UNCLOS reaches discharges outside the port State's own waters. That is counter-intuitive and it is the treaty root of port State control.
  • UNCLOS contains no discharge limits. It obliges States to act through the competent international organization, and requires national law to be no less effective than the international rules. Naming that mechanism is worth more than any list.
  • Innocent passage qualifies sovereignty in the territorial sea. Omitting it makes the territorial sea sound like land.
  • The Convention is unamended. Do not attribute later instruments to it.

Understand this first

A treaty only binds the countries that have signed up to it, and it only covers the ships it says it covers. Both of those are perfectly ordinary features of international law, and together they would wreck the whole system of ship safety if nothing were done about them. Think about why. If a country simply declines to join, its ships carry no obligations under the treaty - yet they load at the same berths and compete for the same cargoes as ships that have paid for compliance. And if a treaty only applies above a certain size, a ship just under that size carries no certificates at all. In both cases the ship that is outside the rules is cheaper to run than the ship inside them, and once that is true, joining becomes a commercial mistake and the treaties start to empty out. So the conventions carry a repeated clause that closes the hole from the other end. It does not try to bind the non-member country, which no treaty can do. Instead it puts a duty on the ports: when such a ship turns up, you must not give her an easier time than you would give a ship that is properly covered. That flips the incentive completely - staying outside no longer saves anything, and may cost more, because the ship gets looked at harder. The two practical situations follow directly. A ship from a non-member country may well be carrying official-looking certificates, but they cannot be convention certificates, because only the convention could give them that force and her flag is not bound by it. A small ship may be carrying nothing at all, quite properly. In neither case can the inspector do what he normally does, which is read the documents and stop there. In both he has to do the harder thing: look at the ship.

Model written answer

1. Where the port State's power comes from, and what it is not

Port State control is the inspection of foreign ships in national ports to verify that the condition of the ship and her equipment, and the competence of her crew, comply with the requirements of the international conventions. Two propositions frame the whole answer and should be stated first:

  • It is a safety net beneath the flag State, not a substitute for it. It does not transfer flag State duty and it does not certify the ship. The flag State remains responsible for her.
  • The power is conferred by the conventions themselves, each of which carries its own control article - SOLAS regulation I/19, MARPOL articles 5 and 6, Load Lines article 21, STCW article X, TONNAGE article 12, AFS article 11, BWM article 9 - with UNCLOS above them all, under which a State may act against a vessel voluntarily within its port. The consolidated IMO Procedures for Port State Control then bring the practice together, and regional memoranda of understanding organise it. India is a member of the Indian Ocean MOU, and inspections are carried out under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 by the Directorate General of Shipping.

2. The problem all three limbs exist to solve

A treaty binds only its Parties, and it applies only to the ships it says it applies to. Left there, the system would contain two enormous holes:

  • a State could decline to ratify and its ships would trade the same routes free of the standards everyone else pays for; and
  • a ship could sit below a convention's size threshold and carry no certificate at all.

In both cases the ship would be advantaged by not being covered. That is the free-rider problem, and the three limbs of this question are the three answers to it: limb (i) removes the value of a non-Party's paperwork, limb (ii) removes the exemption of small ships from scrutiny, and limb (iii) is the principle that makes both of them work.

3. (i) Certificates issued by non-party States to their ships

A non-Party flag State may perfectly well issue its own ships documents that look like convention certificates - and often does, because its ships must be accepted commercially. The port State's position on them rests on one point of treaty law:

  • A convention certificate has treaty standing only because the issuing State is bound by the treaty. The certificate's legal force comes from the Convention, not from the paper. A State that is not a Party has no authority under the Convention to issue anything under it.
  • It follows that a certificate issued by a non-Party is not a convention certificate, whatever it is headed, and the port State does not accept it as having the status of one.
  • In particular it is not accepted as prima facie evidence of the ship's condition. For a Party's ship a valid certificate is normally accepted at face value and inspection stops there unless there are clear grounds; for a non-Party's ship that presumption does not arise at all.
  • The port State is not obliged to reject the document either. It may be examined for what it is worth as information - it may record a survey that was actually carried out - but it carries no entitlement.

4. (i) What the officer actually does with such a ship

  • He proceeds directly to a more detailed inspection, without needing clear grounds, because the trigger that would normally be required - a doubt about a valid certificate - has no application where there is no valid certificate.
  • He satisfies himself that the condition of the ship and her equipment, and the manning, are substantially equivalent to what the Convention would require of a Party's ship of that description.
  • The burden sits differently. On a Party's ship the officer must find something wrong; on a non-Party's ship the ship must show that she is up to standard.
  • If she is not, the port State takes the same action it would take against any ship - up to and including detention where the deficiencies are clearly hazardous to safety, health or the environment - and reports accordingly.
  • The practical consequence for the operator is severe and worth stating: a non-Party ship attracts a fuller inspection, more often, and her paperwork buys her nothing. That is the intended effect.

5. (ii) Inspection of ships below convention size

Every convention has a threshold of application. In broad terms - and these are the figures a candidate should be able to give - SOLAS certification applies to cargo ships of 500 gross tonnage and upwards on international voyages; the Load Lines Convention to ships of 24 metres in length and upwards; MARPOL Annex I to oil tankers of 150 gross tonnage and above and other ships of 400 gross tonnage and above; and the Tonnage Convention to ships of 24 metres and upwards. A ship below the relevant threshold does not carry the corresponding certificate, and she is not in breach for not carrying it.

The provisions made are therefore these:

  • The port State cannot demand a certificate the Convention does not require. An officer who detains a ship for the absence of a document she was never obliged to hold has exceeded his authority, and this is the first thing to say.
  • But he is not disabled from inspecting her. He may satisfy himself that the ship does not present a hazard to safety, health or the environment, which is a condition-based judgement rather than a documentary one.
  • The reference standard becomes national and regional. The flag State's own national requirements for a ship of that size apply, together with any applicable regional memorandum procedures and the port State's own law - in India, the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 and the rules under it.
  • And the principle in limb (iii) reaches her. A ship below convention size must not be treated more favourably than a ship above it: being small is a reason for a different documentary regime, never a reason for a lower standard of safety.
  • Where she is found hazardous, the port State acts. The power to prevent an unsafe ship from proceeding to sea does not depend on which certificate she does or does not hold.

6. (iii) No more favourable treatment

This is the principle the other two limbs depend on, and it should be stated in the conventions' own terms: Parties shall apply the requirements of the Convention as may be necessary to ensure that no more favourable treatment is given to the ships of non-Parties.

It is not a single provision but a repeated provision, written into instrument after instrument:

  • MARPOL article 5(4);
  • The 1988 SOLAS Protocol, article I(3);
  • STCW article X(5);
  • MLC 2006 Article V(7), which puts it most plainly - a Member must ensure that ships flying the flag of a State that has not ratified do not receive more favourable treatment than ships flying the flag of a State that has;
  • and the same obligation appears in the later environmental conventions and is reflected in the III Code's port State provisions and in the IMO Procedures for Port State Control.

Three points complete the limb:

  • It reaches two classes of ship, not one. Non-Party ships and below-size ships. Candidates almost always give the first and omit the second, and the second is half the principle.
  • It is an obligation on the port State, not a right. The wording is that Parties shall apply the requirements as necessary. A port State that waved a non-Party ship through would itself be in breach.
  • Its purpose is to remove the commercial advantage of staying outside. If non-ratification bought cheaper operation, ratification would be a competitive disadvantage and the conventions would unravel. This provision is what makes the whole system self-supporting, and it is why a ship cannot escape a convention by choosing a flag.

7. How the three interlock

Set out as a single mechanism, which is what a good answer does at this point:

  • The principle in limb (iii) says the standard must be applied regardless of flag or size.
  • Limb (i) is how that is done where the ship has paperwork with no legal standing - discount the paper and inspect the substance.
  • Limb (ii) is how it is done where there is no paperwork to have - inspect the substance against national and regional requirements instead.
  • In both cases the answer is the same: the port State stops relying on documents and looks at the ship. That single sentence is the heart of the question.

8. The practical limits, and the position at this sitting

  • The port State applies the convention standard; it does not make the ship a Party's ship. It cannot issue a convention certificate to a non-Party's ship, and it cannot confer status.
  • The jurisdiction is territorial and occasion-based. It bites while the ship is voluntarily in the port or at an offshore terminal; it is not a roving power over the flag.
  • Inspection must not be an instrument of discrimination. The requirement is that a non-Party ship is treated no more favourably - not that she is treated worse, and not that she is harassed. The standard applied is the convention standard, no higher.
  • Undue delay attracts a remedy. Where a ship is unduly detained or delayed she is entitled to compensation for loss or damage suffered, which is the balancing provision on the whole regime.
  • At this sitting the consolidated procedures in force are the 2021 edition adopted by the 32nd Assembly - resolution A.1155(32). The 33rd Assembly's revision was adopted on 6 December 2023, three months after this examination, and must not be cited here.

The proposition to close on: a convention binds its Parties, but port State control applies its standard to everyone in the port. Limbs (i) and (ii) are the two ways the gap is closed, and limb (iii) is the reason it must be.
