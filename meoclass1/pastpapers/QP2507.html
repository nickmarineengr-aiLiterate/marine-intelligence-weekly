Understand this first

A wreck is not primarily an environmental problem in this Convention — it is an unallocated bill. Before it entered into force a ship could sink outside a coastal State's territorial sea, in water that State polices but does not own, and there was no clear international power to compel the owner to move it and no certainty that anybody solvent would pay. UNCLOS gave the coastal State rights in the exclusive economic zone but no removal machinery. The Nairobi Convention supplies the machinery, and the whole of it turns on one word: HAZARD. A wreck that endangers nothing is left alone. Once it is a hazard, four things follow mechanically — report it, locate it, mark it, remove it — and the registered owner pays, whether or not anyone was at fault, backed by insurance the State can sue directly. Read the question that way and limb (c) writes itself: the owner carries the cost and the liability, the coastal State carries the judgement and the power to act, and the environment is the reason both exist.

Model written answer

1. The Convention and where it applies

The instrument the stem refers to is the Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks, 2007, adopted on 18 May 2007 and in force since 14 April 2015. It operates in the Convention area — the exclusive economic zone of a State Party, or an equivalent zone not extending beyond 200 nautical miles (Article 1(1)). A State Party may extend it to its own territorial sea by declaration under Article 3(2). Domestically at the date of this examination the governing Indian statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

2. What "wreck" means - the four categories

Article 1(4) defines a wreck as something arising following upon a maritime casualty. Four categories are identified:

  • a sunken or stranded ship;
  • any part of a sunken or stranded ship, including any object that is or has been on board;
  • any object lost at sea from a ship that is stranded, sunken or adrift at sea;
  • a ship that is about, or may reasonably be expected, to sink or to strand, where effective measures to assist the ship or property in danger are not already being taken.

Two points are commonly lost. First, the phrase "following upon a maritime casualty" is part of the definition, not scene-setting: a ship deliberately abandoned, or an object thrown overboard in the ordinary course, is not a wreck under this Convention. Second, the fourth category is anticipatory — a ship that has not yet sunk is already a wreck for these purposes if she may reasonably be expected to sink or strand and effective assistance is not already under way. That is what allows a coastal State to act before the casualty completes itself, which is usually the only cheap moment to act at all.

3. Hazard - the switch that turns the Convention on

A wreck engages the removal machinery only if it is a hazard — Article 1(5): a condition or threat that poses a danger or impediment to navigation, or that may reasonably be expected to result in major harmful consequences to the marine environment or damage to coastal and related interests. Article 6 gives the criteria the affected State applies:

  • the size, type and construction of the wreck, and the depth of water over it;
  • proximity to shipping routes, port approaches and anchorages, and the density and frequency of traffic;
  • the type and quantity of cargo, and the quantity and type of oil — bunkers included — on board;
  • the tidal range, currents and prevailing weather, and the vulnerability of port facilities;
  • the proximity of offshore installations, pipelines and telecommunication cables, and of areas of particular environmental sensitivity;
  • whether the wreck is likely to shift or deteriorate materially, and any damage already suffered.

The determination is therefore a reasoned assessment, not a reflex. A wreck lying deep, clear of routes, with negligible bunkers and stable structure may properly be left in place — and the Convention imposes no removal duty on its owner at all.

4. Objectives and key provisions

The Convention was written to close a gap. UNCLOS gave coastal States rights in the exclusive economic zone but no removal machinery, so a hazardous wreck beyond the territorial sea could sit there with nobody clearly obliged to move it and no guarantee that anyone solvent would pay. States were left choosing between an unfunded removal and a permanent danger. The objectives are therefore:

  • a uniform international rule for the prompt, effective and proportionate removal of hazardous wrecks beyond the territorial sea;
  • cost recovery from the owner and the owner's insurer rather than from the coastal State's public funds — the polluter-pays principle applied to obstruction as well as to pollution;
  • legal certainty: owners and insurers know the extent of the exposure, States know the powers they hold, and both know the time limits;
  • financial security that is real — compulsory insurance with a right of direct action, so the remedy does not evaporate with a single-ship company.

The operative provisions then run as a sequence, and it is worth learning them in that order because it is the order in which a real casualty unfolds:

Behind that sequence stand the financial provisions — strict liability (Art 10), compulsory insurance with direct action (Art 12) and the time bar (Art 13). Article 9 also preserves the owner's right to contract with any salvor or contractor of his choice, subject to the State's conditions, which keeps commercial salvage inside the regime rather than displacing it.

5. The shipowner - strict liability and its limits

Article 10 makes the registered owner strictly liable for the costs of locating, marking and removing the wreck. Fault need not be shown. The exceptions are narrow: act of war or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; damage wholly caused by a third party acting with intent; or wholly caused by the negligence of an authority responsible for navigational aids. Article 11 stands the Convention down where CLC 1992, the HNS, Bunkers or nuclear regimes govern the same damage.

Liability is backed by Article 12: ships of 300 GT and above must maintain insurance or other financial security, carry a certificate to that effect, and the claim may be brought directly against the insurer. The security must be at least equal to the limits of liability under the applicable limitation regime, and an insurer sued directly may invoke the defences the owner could have invoked — but not the owner's insolvency or winding up. The practical consequence is that the coastal State need not find the owner, prove negligence, or hope a single-ship company still exists: it establishes the hazard, sets the deadline and, failing compliance, removes and claims against a certified insurer whose identity it already holds. Article 10(2) preserves the owner's right to limit liability under the applicable limitation regime, and Article 13 bars claims brought more than three years after the hazard was determined, and in no case later than six years after the casualty.

6. The coastal State - judgement, notice and intervention

The affected State decides whether the wreck is a hazard on the Article 6 criteria; informs the flag State and the registered owner; warns mariners; sets a reasonable deadline for removal and may attach conditions; and, if the owner fails to act — or immediately, where the hazard is severe — may remove the wreck itself and recover the cost. Those powers are not unlimited: measures must be proportionate to the hazard and exercised consistently with international law, and the State may not interfere with the rights of other States beyond what removal requires. Where the wreck lies in the territorial sea of a State that has not opted in under Article 3(2), the Convention does not govern and the State acts under its own domestic law — in India, the wreck and salvage provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, administered through the Receiver of Wreck.

The division is therefore clean and should be written that way: the State supplies the judgement and the compulsion; the owner supplies the work and the money. The State does not become the remover of first resort, and the owner does not decide whether the wreck is dangerous.

7. Environmental implications, and what the regime does not reach

A wreck is a continuing environmental source rather than a single event, and that is what distinguishes it from a spill:

  • Progressive release. Bunkers, lubricating and hydraulic oils and cargo escape as tanks corrode, sometimes years after the casualty and long after the response has stood down.
  • Cargo hazards. Chemicals, packaged dangerous goods and containers that break free and drift, each becoming an object within category three of the definition.
  • Structural contaminants. Antifouling coatings, refrigerants, batteries and other hazardous materials built into the ship.
  • Physical damage. Smothering and scouring of the seabed, reefs and fishing grounds; obstruction of ports, approaches and fairways; risk to fishing gear and to cables and pipelines.
  • Navigational risk feeding back into environmental risk. An unmarked wreck causes the next casualty, which is why marking under Article 8 sits inside an environmental instrument at all.

The Convention's contribution to all of this is essentially financial and procedural: strict liability plus compulsory insurance is what converts an environmental risk into a funded removal, promptly and without first litigating fault. It changes who pays and how quickly, rather than changing the physics of the casualty.

Its reach is bounded. It applies only to wrecks that are hazards, only within the Convention area unless a State has opted in for its territorial sea, and the owner's liability remains subject to the applicable limitation regime — so an exceptionally costly removal can still exceed the available fund and leave a residue for the coastal State to carry.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The limbs carry 5, 5 and 6 marks and the six-mark limb names two actors. Splitting sections 5 and 6 by actor is therefore not a stylistic choice — it is what makes both halves of the largest limb visible to the examiner. Sections 1 to 3 build the definition in the order the Convention itself uses: instrument, then definition, then trigger. Answering "define wreck" without "hazard" produces a technically correct definition that explains nothing about how the Convention works.

Note also that this sitting lifts the convention into a stem lead-in rather than naming it inside limb (a). That changes where you write the instrument's full title, not whether you write it: supply Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks, 2007 once, in section 1, because the printed stem does not name it.

The two-part definition, and why it is two parts

Article 1(4) tells you what a wreck is. Article 1(5) tells you when anyone has to do anything about it. Most of the Convention — reporting, locating, marking, removal, liability — hangs off the hazard determination in Article 6, not off the definition. A wreck in deep water clear of routes, with no cargo of consequence, may be left where it is.

Strict liability, and the word that must be used carefully

Strict liability means liability without proof of fault — it does not mean absolute liability, because Article 10 admits three defences. Note the contrast with the Bunkers Convention, where the liable "shipowner" is defined widely to include the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator jointly and severally, and only the insurance duty falls on the registered owner alone. Under Nairobi and under CLC the registered owner is the correct focus; under Bunkers it is not, and that distinction has been examined.

So the commercial salvage market still does the work — it is the obligation and the bill that the Convention allocates.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting the fourth category — a ship about to sink or strand — and so defining only what is already on the bottom.
  • Saying the Convention applies in territorial waters. It applies in the EEZ; the territorial sea is an opt-in under Article 3(2).
  • Treating every wreck as removable. Without a hazard there is no removal obligation.
  • Writing limb (c) as one paragraph about pollution, leaving the coastal State's responsibilities unstated.
  • Confusing the Nairobi regime with salvage. Salvage is a voluntary service for reward; wreck removal is a compulsory duty imposed on the owner.
  • Failing to name the instrument at all, because this sitting's stem refers only to "the Wreck Removal Convention". Give the full title once.

Examiner traps

  • Asking which conventions displace Nairobi — CLC 1992, HNS, Bunkers and the nuclear conventions, under Article 11.
  • Asking whether liability is unlimited. It is not: Article 10(2) expressly preserves limitation under the applicable regime.
  • Asking for the time limits and expecting only one. There are two — three years and six years, measured from different events.
  • Asking who reports the wreck. The master or operator of the ship, to the affected State — not the coastal State to the owner.
  • Asking whether the HNS Convention is in force. At this sitting it is not, which is why Article 11 names it as a displacing regime in principle rather than in current practice.

Understand this first

The whole subject exists because of one accounting mistake that was easy to make and expensive to keep. If you measure a ship's greenhouse gas emissions at the funnel — which is what every operational measure in force at this sitting does — then a ship burning ammonia scores zero for carbon dioxide, whether that ammonia was made from natural gas with the carbon vented to atmosphere or from renewable electricity. The emissions did not disappear; they moved ashore, into a plant the shipowner does not own and the regulation does not see. That is carbon leakage, and a decarbonisation rule that rewards it is worse than no rule, because it funds the wrong technology with real money. Life cycle analysis is the fix. Count the emissions of the fuel from the well or the field, through production, transport and bunkering, and on through combustion in the engine — and express the result as one number, the greenhouse gas intensity of the energy the ship actually uses. The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy adopted exactly that basis, and the 2024 LCA Guidelines are the method for computing it. Everything else in this question — the stages, the boundaries, the well-to-tank and tank-to-wake split, and the argument about feasibility — is machinery for producing that one number honestly.

Model written answer

1. The problem that makes life-cycle accounting necessary

Every mandatory greenhouse gas measure in force at this examination — the EEXI, the CII and the fuel oil Data Collection System — counts emissions at the funnel. That is simple to administer and it produces a serious distortion. A ship burning ammonia or hydrogen emits no carbon dioxide, because there is no carbon in the molecule, whether the fuel was made from natural gas with the carbon vented or from renewable electricity. Measured at the funnel the two are identical.

The emissions have not gone; they have moved ashore, into a production plant the shipowner does not own and the regulation does not see. That is carbon leakage, and a rule that permits it does not merely fail to reduce emissions — it directs investment towards the wrong technology. Funnel-only accounting also mis-ranks the real candidates: LNG looks better than it is because methane slip and upstream leakage are invisible, and a biofuel looks the same whatever its feedstock.

2. What life cycle analysis of a marine fuel is

Life Cycle Analysis is the systematic, quantified assessment of the greenhouse gas emissions associated with a fuel across its entire life, from the resource in the ground or the field through to its conversion in the ship's engine. Its output is a single comparable figure: the fuel's GHG intensity, in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per megajoule of energy delivered. It is equivalent because methane and nitrous oxide are converted to a carbon dioxide basis at their global warming potentials, which matters greatly for gas fuels.

The IMO basis at this sitting. The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy, resolution MEPC.377(80), requires its levels of ambition and checkpoints to take account of well-to-wake emissions as addressed in the LCA guidelines. The operative guidelines are the 2024 Guidelines on Life Cycle GHG Intensity of Marine Fuels, resolution MEPC.391(81), adopted 22 March 2024, whose paragraph 5 expressly revokes the earlier LCA Guidelines in MEPC.376(80). Cite the 2024 edition; the 2023 edition is revoked, not merely superseded. Note what these instruments are: a strategy and a set of guidelines. Neither is mandatory on a ship.

3. The four methodological stages of an assessment

An assessment is conducted in four stages, iterative rather than strictly sequential:

StageWhat is done
1. Goal and scope definitionWhy the study is being done and for whom; the functional unit — here, one megajoule of energy delivered for propulsion; the system boundary; and the assumptions and allocation rules
2. Inventory analysisQuantify every input and output crossing the boundary at each stage — energy consumed, feedstock used, emissions released, co-products generated
3. Impact assessmentConvert the inventory into impact categories. For fuels the dominant category is global warming potential, with methane and nitrous oxide expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent over a stated time horizon
4. InterpretationTest the sensitivity of the result to the assumptions, state the uncertainty, check completeness and consistency, and draw conclusions honest about their limits

4. The physical stages of a marine fuel pathway

Alongside the method, the fuel chain has stages, and these are what the inventory is built on:

  • Feedstock — extraction, recovery or cultivation. Fugitive methane from gas production, and land-use change where a crop is grown for fuel, both enter here.
  • Processing and conversion — refining, liquefaction, synthesis or electrolysis. The electricity mix is decisive for hydrogen and its derivatives, and any carbon capture is credited here.
  • Transport, storage and distribution — moving the fuel to the bunkering port, including boil-off and handling losses.
  • Bunkering — transfer to the ship.
  • Onboard conversion — combustion in the engine or conversion in a fuel cell, including methane slip, nitrous oxide and the pilot fuel in a dual-fuel engine.

Cutting across all five are accounting decisions that decide the answer as much as the physics does: allocation between a process's main product and its co-products, the treatment of biogenic carbon, and end-of-life.

5. Well-to-Tank, Tank-to-Wake and Well-to-Wake

TermCoversControlled by
Well-to-Tank (WtT)Feedstock, production, distribution and bunkering — ending when the fuel is in the ship's tankThe fuel producer and supplier. The shipowner buys it
Tank-to-Wake (TtW)Conversion onboard — combustion or fuel-cell operation, including methane slip, nitrous oxide and pilot fuelThe shipowner and the crew
Well-to-Wake (WtW)The sum of the two — the complete figureNobody alone, which is the administrative difficulty

The relationship is arithmetic: WtW = WtT + TtW. Well-to-tank is therefore a component of well-to-wake rather than a rival to it, and the choice the question poses is really between the whole and one of its parts. Say so before choosing.

6. Why well-to-wake is the feasible basis

  • Well-to-tank alone is not usable for shipping at all. It measures the fuel supply chain and says nothing about the ship — two ships burning the same fuel at entirely different efficiency would carry the same figure.
  • Tank-to-wake alone is measurable but incentivises the wrong thing. It is easy to administer, which is exactly why CII was built that way — but it permits carbon leakage and treats grey and green ammonia as the same fuel.
  • Well-to-wake is the only defensible basis. It closes leakage, because an emission can leave the boundary only by not occurring; it ranks the candidate fuels correctly; and it captures both ends of the methane problem — upstream leakage and onboard slip — which neither half does alone.

It is also the basis the Organization has already chosen: MEPC.377(80) states the ambitions on a well-to-wake footing and MEPC.391(81) exists to make that operable. At MEPC 83 in April 2025 the Committee approved a draft new MARPOL Annex VI chapter with a well-to-wake GHG Fuel Intensity mechanism — approved only; it has not been adopted and is not law. The first binding well-to-wake regime is regional: FuelEU Maritime, Regulation (EU) 2023/1805, applying since 1 January 2025 to ships calling at European ports.

Conclusion. Well-to-wake is the feasible methodology. Well-to-tank is indispensable as its upstream half but is not a metric for a ship, and tank-to-wake alone would reward the appearance of decarbonisation over the fact of it.

7. What makes it hard, and how the difficulty is managed

  • The shipowner does not control, observe or audit the upstream half of his own fuel's emissions.
  • Upstream figures vary with feedstock, plant, electricity grid mix, season and route, and much of the data is commercially confidential.
  • Allocation between co-products and the treatment of land-use change are methodological choices that can change the result by more than the technology does.
  • Verification requires certification schemes and audited chains of custody, with a real risk of double counting where regional and global regimes overlap.

The practical resolution is a two-part system. The upstream half uses conservative default values for each pathway, which any supplier may improve upon by certifying an actual value through an audited scheme — so the burden of proof sits with the party who benefits from the better number. The onboard half uses measured consumption, which ships already record. That is why the guidelines matter as much as the strategy: without an agreed method for the well-to-tank half, a well-to-wake obligation could be declared but not administered.

The commonly cited resolution is MEPC.376(80), the 2023 LCA Guidelines, because the 2023 Strategy is what made the subject famous. It is not the operative instrument. A revoked one has been withdrawn by the body that made it. Citing MEPC.376(80) as the current LCA framework in a 2025 answer is citing an instrument that had been revoked for more than a year.

Common mistakes

  • Describing the four methodological stages and calling them the fuel life cycle, or the reverse.
  • Treating WtT as an alternative to WtW rather than as part of it.
  • Forgetting tank-to-wake because the printed stem does not name it. WtW minus WtT is exactly TtW.
  • Omitting methane slip and pilot fuel from the onboard stage.
  • Citing MEPC.376(80) as the current LCA guidelines. It was revoked by MEPC.391(81) on 22 March 2024.
  • Describing the LCA guidelines as mandatory. They are guidelines, and the Strategy is a strategy.
  • Calling FuelEU Maritime an IMO measure.
  • Answering limb (b) without ever choosing a methodology.

Examiner traps

  • Asking what zero-carbon fuel means. It means no carbon in the molecule, not zero emissions — lifecycle emissions depend on the production pathway.
  • Asking why CII does not solve this. Because it is a funnel measurement and sees nothing upstream of the bunker manifold.
  • Asking what a functional unit is, and why it must be energy delivered rather than tonnes of fuel. Fuels differ in calorific value, so mass is not comparable.
  • Asking who verifies the upstream figure — a certification scheme, not the ship.
  • Asking which is bigger, well-to-tank or tank-to-wake. It depends entirely on the fuel: for conventional fuel oil the onboard half dominates; for green hydrogen almost the whole burden is upstream.
  • Asking about the status of the GHG Fuel Intensity mechanism. At this sitting it is approved and not adopted. Saying "in force", or even "pending entry into force", is wrong — an instrument that has not been adopted has no entry into force to be pending.

Understand this first

Three of these four limbs are really one question asked three times: when does a commercial party who is not the shipowner end up paying? A charterer hires the ship but does not own her, and the whole of maritime liability law is built around the registered owner as the identified, insured defendant. So the interesting answer in each limb is the exception. In limb (b) it is that the charterer's biggest insured risk is not some third party at all — it is damage to the ship he chartered, caused by sending her to an unsafe berth or filling her with bad bunkers. In limb (c) it is that he answers to a cargo holder only if he is the carrier on the bill, which is decided by who signed it and in what capacity, not by the charterparty the cargo holder has never seen. And in limb (d) it is that CLC deliberately channels him out of pollution liability altogether — the Convention names charterers among the protected persons — while the Bunkers Convention does not channel at all and defines 'shipowner' widely enough to catch a bareboat charterer alongside the manager and the operator. Hold the pattern — owner by default, charterer by exception, and name the exception — and all four limbs have a spine.

Model written answer

(a) Standard charter parties and their advantages — 4 marks

1. What a standard charter party is

A charter party is the contract by which a shipowner places a ship, or her carrying capacity, at a charterer's disposal. A standard charter party is one written on a pre-printed industry form published by a recognised body — shipowner and charterer associations, trade bodies and the oil and dry-bulk majors — whose wording has been negotiated between representative interests and settled by long use, rather than drafted from scratch for the fixture.

In practice the parties take the printed form and add a rider of additional clauses and amendments, so a fixture is the standard form as amended. Three families answer three different commercial questions:

  • Voyage charter — the ship is engaged for a stated voyage with a stated cargo. Dry-cargo and tanker voyage forms are the everyday examples, and freight, laytime and demurrage are the commercial heart of them.
  • Time charter — the ship is at the charterer's disposal for a period, the charterer directing employment and paying hire and, ordinarily, for bunkers and port charges. The New York Produce Exchange form and the oil-major time forms are the standard examples.
  • Bareboat or demise charter — the charterer takes possession, mans and operates the ship as if owner for the period. This is the form that matters most for limbs (c) and (d), because the demise charterer steps into the owner's shoes.

2. The advantages of using a standard form

  1. Certainty of meaning. The wording has been litigated and arbitrated for decades, so both parties know what it means before they argue about it. That is the single largest advantage and it cannot be bought any other way.
  2. Speed and cost of fixing. A fixture can be concluded on a form and a short rider in hours. Negotiating a bespoke contract for each voyage would make the chartering market unworkable.
  3. Balance. The forms are drafted or approved by bodies representing both owners and charterers, so the printed allocation of risk starts from a recognised middle rather than from whichever party drafted first.
  4. Compatibility with the rest of the industry. Brokers, P&I clubs, underwriters, banks, arbitrators and courts all know the forms. Cover, security and finance attach to them without special explanation, and standard clause sets for war risks, sanctions, security and emissions can be bolted on as published.
  5. Completeness. A printed form covers the unglamorous eventualities — off-hire, lien, general average, arbitration, notices — that a hurriedly drafted contract omits and then has to litigate.
  6. Fewer disputes, and cheaper ones. Where a dispute does arise, the issue is usually confined to the rider clause the parties actually negotiated rather than to the whole contract.

The corresponding caution, worth one line: the printed form is a starting point. Riders routinely reverse the printed allocation of risk, and it is the amended contract, not the form's reputation, that governs.

(b) Risks covered under charterer's liability insurance — 4 marks

3. What the cover is, and what it reaches

A charterer is not the owner of the ship, so the hull and machinery policy is not his and the owner's P&I entry does not answer for his liabilities. Charterer's liability insurance — written as a charterer's entry with a P&I club or by the fixed-premium market — is the cover that fills that gap. The risks it customarily reaches are these.

  • Damage to the chartered ship herself. Ordinarily the charterer's largest single exposure, and the one candidates miss. It arises from breach of the safe port and safe berth warranty, from unsafe or damaging cargo, and from bunkers supplied off-specification or contaminated where the charterer supplies them.
  • Cargo liability — claims by cargo interests where the charterer is the contracting carrier under the bill of lading, and claims passed to him through the charterparty indemnity chain. See limbs (c) below.
  • Pollution liability arising from the charterer's own operations, including a spill during a bunkering he arranged and pollution from the cargo he ordered carried.
  • Third-party liabilities — damage to fixed and floating objects such as berths, cranes, buoys and pipelines; and contributions towards wreck removal.
  • Personal injury, illness and death claims where the charterer's own act, order or operation gives rise to liability.
  • General average and salvage contributions on interests the charterer owns — his bunkers and, where he carries the risk, freight.
  • Loss of or damage to the charterer's bunkers on board.
  • Fines and penalties — customs, immigration and pollution penalties attributable to the charterer's operation, so far as insurable.
  • Defence cover — the legal costs of pursuing and defending charterparty disputes: hire and off-hire, laytime and demurrage, speed and consumption, bunker quality and cargo claims. This is usually a separate but closely allied cover and it is where most charterers actually use their insurers.

Two limits should be stated. The cover answers for liabilities, not for the charterer's own commercial losses — an unprofitable fixture or unpaid hire is a credit risk, not an insured peril. And it responds to the charterer's legal liability, so it does not reach loss caused by his own wilful misconduct or by trading in breach of a warranty of the entry.

(c) The charterer and cargo loss or damage under the bill of lading — 4 marks

4. The answer turns on who the carrier is

Yes, a charterer can be liable — but not because he is a charterer. He is liable if, and only if, he is the carrier under the bill of lading, because the bill is a contract of carriage between the holder and the carrier and it is the carrier who answers to the holder for cargo loss or damage.

So the question is who is the carrier on this bill, and that is decided by the bill itself rather than by the charterparty, to which the cargo holder is a stranger.

  1. An owner's bill. Where the bill is signed by the master, or by an agent expressly for and on behalf of the master, it is ordinarily the shipowner's contract. The shipowner is the carrier, and the cargo claimant sues him. The charterer is not liable to the holder.
  2. A charterer's bill. Where the charterer or his agent signs in his own name as carrier, or the form identifies the charterer as carrier, the charterer is the contracting carrier and is liable to the holder for cargo loss or damage.
  3. A bareboat charter. The demise charterer possesses and operates the ship as if owner, so bills issued for her are ordinarily his contracts and he is the carrier.
  4. Identity-of-carrier and demise provisions. Many forms carry printed provisions designed to identify the shipowner as carrier whoever signed. Their effect is a matter of construction of the particular bill.

Where the charterer is the carrier, his liability is governed by the applicable cargo regime — the Hague or Hague-Visby Rules as given effect by the applicable law, and in India by the Indian Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, 1925 for carriage covered by a bill of lading. He then owes the carrier's obligations: to exercise due diligence to make the ship seaworthy before and at the beginning of the voyage, and properly and carefully to load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care for and discharge the cargo. He has the carrier's defences — the catalogue of excepted perils — and the carrier's limitation per package or unit.

5. And the indemnity chain can put the claim back on him anyway

This is the part that turns a competent answer into a good one. Even where the bill is an owner's bill, so that the shipowner is sued by the cargo interest, the loss frequently ends with the charterer, by two routes:

  • The employment and indemnity obligation. A time charterer directs the ship's employment and requires the master to sign bills as presented; in return the owner is entitled to be indemnified against the consequences of complying with those orders. If the charterer's orders produced the exposure, the charterer bears it.
  • Inter-club apportionment. Time charters on the New York Produce Exchange form customarily incorporate an inter-club agreement under which cargo claims are apportioned between owner and charterer by the cause of the loss — unseaworthiness on one side, loading, stowage, handling and discharge operations on the other, with a residual split for everything else. It is a mechanical allocation designed to settle claims without litigating fault.

Conclusion for limb (c). The charterer is liable to the cargo holder where he is the carrier on the bill — a charterer's bill, or a demise charter. Where he is not, he is not liable to the holder; but he may still end up paying, through the indemnity and apportionment machinery of the charterparty.

(d) Charterers and oil pollution compensation under the IMO conventions — 4 marks

6. Channelling, and the one convention whose definition reaches a charterer

Here the answer is largely no, and the reason is a deliberate design feature of the conventions — but there is one important exception and it must be identified precisely.

CLC 1992 — no. The Convention makes the registered owner strictly liable, and Article III(4) then channels liability to him: claims for pollution damage may not be made against a list of protected persons which expressly includes any charterer, howsoever described, including a bareboat charterer, together with the servants and agents of the owner, the crew, the pilot, the salvor and those taking preventive measures. Channelling is the point of the regime: the cargo claimant gets a single, identifiable, compulsorily insured defendant and does not have to litigate against a chain of commercial parties.

The exception is narrow and is the examinable detail. The protection is lost where the damage resulted from that person's own personal act or omission, committed with intent to cause such damage, or recklessly and with knowledge that such damage would probably result. That is a very high threshold, and ordinary negligence by a charterer does not come near it.

The 2001 Bunkers Convention — yes, but only a bareboat charterer. This is the point most candidates get wrong in both directions. The Bunkers Convention does not channel to the registered owner. Its Article 1(3) defines 'shipowner' widely, to include the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager and operator of the ship, and those persons are jointly and severally liable for pollution damage caused by bunker oil. So a bareboat charterer is squarely within the liable class. A time or voyage charterer is not, because he is neither bareboat charterer, manager nor operator. Note also that the compulsory insurance duty falls on the registered owner alone, so the certificate is his even though the liability is shared.

The pattern across the family. The Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention likewise makes the registered owner strictly liable. The HNS Convention 2010 would channel similarly, but it was not in force at this sitting and cannot be relied on. So the design is consistent: one identified, insured defendant per casualty, and the Bunkers definition is the deliberate exception because a bunker spill can as easily be the act of the party operating the ship as of the party owning her.

And one honest qualification. Channelling bars claims for pollution damage under the Convention. It does not make a charterer immune from everything: he can still be liable to the owner under the charterparty — for breach of the safe port warranty or for supplying off-specification bunkers — and national liability regimes outside the IMO conventions may reach charterers on their own terms. What the charterer is protected from is the pollution claimant, not from the owner's recourse.

Conclusion for limb (d). Under CLC, no — liability is channelled to the registered owner and charterers are expressly protected, save for intent or recklessness with knowledge. Under the Bunkers Convention, a bareboat charterer yes, jointly and severally with the registered owner, manager and operator; a time or voyage charterer no. Under HNS, the question does not arise at this sitting because the Convention is not in force.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four limbs of four marks each, and they are not four questions about the same thing — they run from commercial document to insurance to cargo liability to pollution liability. The commonest failure is to spend half the answer on limb (a), which is the cheapest and most familiar, and then to give one thin paragraph each to (c) and (d), which are the two limbs that actually discriminate between candidates. Six sections, weighted so that (c) and (d) get two sections and one long one respectively, prevent that.

Both (c) and (d) begin with the word 'Discuss if'. That is an invitation to give a conditional answer with the condition stated — not a yes and not a no. In (c) the condition is who the carrier is; in (d) it is which convention and which kind of charterer. An answer that says simply 'yes' or simply 'no' to either has missed what was being tested.

The two hinges

Limb (c) hinges on the identity of the carrier. The bill of lading is a contract with the carrier, and the cargo holder is a stranger to the charterparty. So the charterer is liable to the holder only if the bill is his contract — a charterer's bill, or bills under a demise charter. Master's signature, or an agent signing for the master, ordinarily makes it the owner's.

Limb (d) hinges on channelling, and on one definition. CLC channels liability to the registered owner and expressly protects charterers; the Bunkers Convention does not channel and defines 'shipowner' widely enough to catch a bareboat charterer. Hold those two side by side and the limb writes itself.

Common mistakes

  • Spending half the answer on limb (a). It is four marks and it is the easiest four.
  • Answering (c) with a flat yes or no. The stem says 'discuss if' — state the condition, which is the identity of the carrier.
  • Saying the charterer is liable under the bill because he chartered the ship. The cargo holder is a stranger to the charterparty; only the bill matters to him.
  • Saying charterers are never liable for oil pollution. A bareboat charterer is within the liable class under the Bunkers Convention.
  • Saying charterers are liable under CLC. They are expressly protected by the channelling provision.
  • Treating the CLC exception as though ordinary negligence defeated the protection. It requires intent, or recklessness with knowledge that such damage would probably result.
  • Listing the HNS Convention as an operative source of charterer liability. It was not in force at this sitting.
  • Omitting damage to the chartered ship from limb (b). It is usually the charterer's biggest exposure.
  • Quoting a clause number or edition of a standard form, or an apportionment percentage. Several editions run concurrently and the agreement has been revised.

Examiner traps

  • The stem prints 'Charter's Liability Insurance' for 'Charterer's'. Answer the question the examiner set; do not comment on the typography.
  • 'Enumerate' in limb (a) is a command for a list. Give numbered advantages, not a paragraph.
  • 'Discuss if' appears twice, in (c) and (d). Both want a reasoned conditional answer, and both have a clean condition available.
  • Asking who the carrier is on a bill signed by the port agent. It depends on the capacity in which he signed — for the master, or for the charterer.
  • Asking whether a time charterer is liable under the Bunkers Convention. No — only a bareboat charterer is within the definition, alongside the manager and the operator.
  • Asking who holds the Bunkers certificate. The registered owner, even though liability is joint and several.
  • Asking what the charterer's insurance would not pay. Unpaid hire and an unprofitable fixture — those are commercial losses, not liabilities.

Understand this first

A solid bulk cargo does not float in water and it does not dissolve. What makes it dangerous is something subtler: it is only a solid because of friction between its particles, and that friction depends on the particles pressing against each other rather than against water. Load a fine, moist ore into a hold and it looks and behaves like rock. Then run the ship for two days: the main engine vibrates the hull, the sea rolls her, and the cargo compacts. As the void spaces between particles shrink, the water that was sitting in them has nowhere to go, so the pore water pressure rises. Every increment of pore pressure takes an equal increment off the contact force between grains. When the contact force reaches zero the grains are no longer touching — they are floating in their own water — and the cargo flows. Nothing was added and nothing was spilled; the same material simply stopped being a solid. That is why the whole regulatory answer is about a number measured before loading — the moisture content against the transportable moisture limit — and why the master's can test, crude as it is, matters: once the ship is at sea there is nothing anyone can do about it.

Model written answer

1. The mechanism - why a solid starts behaving as a liquid

A fine-particled bulk cargo is a solid only because of inter-granular friction and the capillary forces between wet particles. It does not need to be dry, and it does not need to be strong; it needs its grains to be pressing on each other.

Once at sea the cargo is subjected to continuous vibration from the machinery and propeller and to rolling and pitching. Both progressively compact the mass, reducing the void volume between particles. The water occupying those voids cannot drain away at the same rate, so the pore water pressure rises. Rising pore pressure removes, in equal measure, the effective stress — the actual contact force — between adjacent particles. When the effective stress approaches zero the grains are supported by the water rather than by each other, and the mass flows.

Nothing has been added and nothing has escaped. The same material has changed state because the water already inside it moved — which is why the cargo can look perfectly sound at the moment of loading and be a liquid two days later.

2. The conditions and the contributing factors

Three conditions must coincide.

  1. A cargo of a type that can liquefy — established from the declared Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and its current individual schedule in the IMSBC Code, never from the commodity name used in conversation.
  2. A moisture content above the transportable moisture limit for that cargo.
  3. Sufficient ship motion and vibration to compact the mass.

Remove any one and the cargo will not liquefy — which is why the whole regulatory scheme concentrates on the second condition, the only one that can be measured and controlled before the ship sails.

Contributing factors on the supply side: fine particle size with a proportion of clay or very fine material; open stockpiles exposed to weather; rain during storage, transfer or loading; wet mining and washing processes; barge transfer in an open roadstead; poor drainage at the terminal; and loading in the monsoon or wet season. On the ship side: engine and propeller vibration, rolling and pitching, long voyages, and heavy weather. And separately, sampling error: a stockpile is not homogeneous, and a certificate is only as good as the sample behind it.

3. TML and FMP - the numbers that decide acceptance

TermMeaning
Flow Moisture Point (FMP)The moisture content at which the cargo begins to lose shear strength and behave as a fluid under the prescribed test conditions
Transportable Moisture Limit (TML)The maximum moisture content of the cargo considered safe for carriage. For most cargoes it is ninety per cent of the FMP - but the individual schedule governs, and some cargoes use a different determination

Determination is by laboratory test under Appendix 2 — the flow table test, the penetration test, or the Proctor-Fagerberg test. Two certificates then matter and they are not interchangeable: the TML certificate, which is a property of the material and is valid for a period before loading, and the moisture content certificate, which must be taken much closer to loading because moisture changes with the weather while the TML does not.

The master's can test — a sample in a cylindrical can, struck repeatedly against a hard surface, watched for free moisture or fluid behaviour at the surface — is a complementary check on board. It can raise an alarm and justify stopping loading, but it is not a substitute for the certified laboratory determination and it cannot establish that a cargo is safe.

4. Liquefaction is not dynamic separation

Two phenomena are routinely confused and it is worth separating them:

  • In liquefaction, the cargo mass itself loses shear strength and flows.
  • In dynamic separation, moisture migrates upward through the cargo and forms a liquid slurry above a still-solid mass — a wet base with a free surface — without the bulk of the cargo liquefying at all.

The consequence for the ship is comparable and equally dangerous: a free surface where the design assumed none, and a mobile upper layer that can slop and shift. The distinction matters for two reasons. The bauxite casualty most often cited as a liquefaction case is now generally understood as dynamic separation; and the Code addresses the two through different schedules and provisions, so the correct precautions follow from the correct diagnosis.

5. The dangers to the ship

The danger is not that the cargo is wet. It is that the ship's stability has been calculated on an assumption that has silently become false.

  • A free surface appears in a hold designed on the assumption that solid cargo cannot move. The virtual rise in the centre of gravity reduces GM.
  • The mass shifts to the low side and does not come back. Roll becomes asymmetric and a progressive list develops.
  • The cargo can re-solidify at the angle of list, locking the ship heeled and removing the reserve she needs for the next sea.
  • The process compounds. Each shift increases the list; each increase in list makes the next shift larger.
  • Capsize can follow with very little warning, which is what makes these among the most sudden losses in the bulk trades — and why the survival rate is so poor.
  • Secondary consequences: loss of steering and manoeuvrability; structural damage as cargo works against bulkheads and hatch coamings; inability to launch survival craft from a heavily heeled ship; and extreme danger to anyone in or near the hold.

The instrument. Before and during loading.

  • The shipper declares the cargo under its Bulk Cargo Shipping Name and supplies the section 4 information, with certificates of TML and of moisture content.
  • The ship checks the declaration against the individual schedule and the section 7.3 conditions, and checks the dates on both certificates.
  • The acceptance rule: a cargo which may liquefy is not accepted unless its moisture content is below the TML, or the ship is specially constructed or fitted for such carriage, or the cargo is carried under the specific alternative conditions the Code allows.
  • Inspect the cargo and the barges: watch for splatter, free water, and a surface that flattens or will not hold a slope.
  • Carry out the can test as a complementary check.
  • Suspend loading in rain and close the hatches; trim as the schedule requires.
  • Refuse or stop loading if the cargo does not match its declaration. This is the master's only real protection, and it exists only before the ship sails.

On passage. At discharge. Expect the cargo to have compacted or set, possibly at an angle; brief on the hazards of working a shifted or crusted cargo, including voids and collapse; and record and report what was found, so that the loading terminal, the shipper and the charterer are held to the certificate they issued. Why this structure scores

Limb (a) asks for the phenomenon "including the conditions under which it occurs and its contributing factors" — that is an instruction to explain why, not what, and a definition alone cannot satisfy it. Limb (b) expressly asks for "examples of incidents", so the casualties get their own section rather than a parenthesis. Limb (c) carries the largest allocation and names three stages — loading, transportation and discharge — so section 7 is organised on those three stages, in that order, using the examiner's own words.

The one sentence of soil mechanics that carries the question

Effective stress equals total stress minus pore water pressure. Everything else follows: compaction raises pore pressure, pore pressure eats effective stress, effective stress is what makes the cargo a solid. A candidate who writes that one relationship has explained liquefaction; a candidate who writes "the cargo becomes wet and slides" has described it.

Why TML and moisture content are certified separately

The TML is a property of the material and does not change with the weather, so its certificate is valid for a longer period. The moisture content is a property of the consignment on the day, and it changes with every shower — which is why its certificate must be much closer to loading, and why the Code requires re-checking after significant rain. The whole question rests on one fact: 'perils of the sea' is a narrow legal term, not a description of everything that can go wrong on a voyage. Model written answer

1. What 'perils of the sea' actually means

The phrase is far narrower than candidates expect, and the whole question turns on that narrowness.

The statutory definition, carried in the Rules for Construction of Policy in the Schedule to the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, is that the term 'perils of the seas' refers only to fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas, and does not include the ordinary action of the winds and waves.

Two tests follow, and both must be satisfied.

  1. Fortuity. The loss must be accidental — not inevitable, not certain, not the natural consequence of the ship going to sea. Deterioration that was bound to happen is not a peril; it is the passage of time.
  2. Of the sea, not merely at sea. The sea must be the cause, not just the location. A fire in the engine room is not a peril of the seas even though it happens at sea — which is exactly why fire is listed separately in every policy.

What is therefore covered as a peril of the seas: stranding and grounding; sinking and foundering; collision with another vessel; striking a submerged or floating object; heavy weather damage beyond the ordinary action of wind and sea; entry of seawater through an accidental opening; capsizing; and damage from an accidental incursion of the sea into the ship.

What is excluded, and this list earns as many marks as the first: ordinary wear and tear; ordinary leakage and breakage; inherent vice of the subject matter; the ordinary action of the winds and waves; corrosion and gradual deterioration; damage by rats or vermin; and injury to machinery not proximately caused by a maritime peril.

2. The maritime perils that are covered but are NOT perils of the sea

This section is what makes the rest of the question intelligible, and most candidates omit it.

A hull and machinery policy does not insure 'perils of the sea' alone. Perils of the seas is only the first item in a list, and the other items are there precisely because they would otherwise fall outside it. The perils clause of the standard hull form covers, in substance:

  • Perils of the seas, rivers, lakes or other navigable waters
  • Fire and explosion
  • Violent theft by persons from outside the vessel
  • Jettison
  • Piracy
  • Contact with land conveyance, dock or harbour equipment or installation
  • Earthquake, volcanic eruption or lightning
  • Accidents in loading, discharging or shifting cargo or fuel

So the correct answer to 'what perils of the sea are covered' is: the fortuitous casualties of the sea itself — and the policy then adds, by express words, a series of other perils that are not perils of the sea at all.

3. How an insured covers a peril that is not an insured peril - with examples

The stem's second question presupposes that a shipowner faces exposures the perils clause does not reach. Four routes are available, and a good answer gives an example of each.

  1. Extend the existing policy by an additional clause. The risk is written into the policy for an additional premium, usually against warranties or a higher deductible. Example: the additional perils cover that buys back the exclusion on the cost of repairing or replacing a part found to be defective, where the ordinary latent-defect cover pays for the resulting damage but not for the faulty part itself.
  2. Buy a separate policy for the excluded risk. Some risks are excluded from the hull policy as a matter of market structure and are insured elsewhere. Examples: war, strikes, terrorism and confiscation, excluded by the hull policy and bought back by a war risks policy or an association entry; loss of hire, for the owner's own trading loss during repairs, which no hull policy covers; increased value and hull interest, above the sum insured; and freight insurance.
  3. Cover it mutually, through a P&I club. The hull policy insures the ship; it does not insure the owner's liabilities to third parties. Examples: pollution liability, cargo claims, crew injury, illness, death and repatriation, wreck removal, damage to fixed and floating objects, stowaways and fines. This is the single most important route and the one most often left out.
  4. Adjust the terms rather than the perils. Where the problem is quantum rather than scope, the answer is a higher sum insured, a lower deductible, or removal of a restrictive warranty — again for premium.

Two limits should be stated, because they show the routes are not unlimited. An insurer will not write back a loss that is inevitable rather than fortuitous — wear and tear is a maintenance cost, not an insurable risk. And cover for a known existing defect is not available on ordinary terms; that is not insurance, it is a repair bill.

4. The perils whose cover is qualified by due diligence

This is the third demand and it is the one that decides the mark. Alongside the perils in section 2, the hull form covers a second group, and every item in it is expressly qualified. The group is known in the market as the Inchmaree clause, after the nineteenth-century case in which a donkey-engine casualty was held not to be a peril of the seas — which is precisely why the clause had to be written.

The perils in the qualified group are:

  1. Bursting of boilers
  2. Breakage of shafts
  3. Latent defect in the machinery or hull
  4. Negligence of Master, Officers, Crew or Pilots
  5. Negligence of repairers or charterers, provided they are not an assured under the policy
  6. Barratry of Master, Officers or Crew

The qualification is that the loss or damage is covered provided it has not resulted from want of due diligence by the Assured, Owners, Managers or Superintendents or any of their onshore management.

Three points of precision, and each is examinable.

  • The Master, Officers and Crew are NOT in the due-diligence list. Their negligence is an insured peril, not a defence. The proviso reaches shore management. This is the most misunderstood feature of the clause: an engineer's mistake is covered; a superintendent's failure to act on a reported defect may not be.
  • Latent defect cover pays for the damage, not for the defect. The consequential damage is recoverable; the cost of renewing the defective part itself is conventionally excluded, and buying that back is exactly what the additional perils cover in section 3 does.
  • Barratry is a wrongful act wilfully committed by Master or crew to the prejudice of the owner. It is covered because it is a peril to the owner, and it necessarily involves no privity of the owner.

5. Where the onus of proof lies, and why it matters

The general rule is settled and follows the structure of the policy.

  • The assured must prove that the loss was caused by a peril insured against. That is his affirmative case, and it is his burden.
  • The insurer must prove any exception, exclusion or proviso on which it relies to reduce or defeat a claim that otherwise falls within the cover.

The due-diligence provision is a proviso limiting the insurer's liability, not a condition precedent that the assured must satisfy before he can claim. It follows — and this is the express point of the question — that once the assured has proved a loss falling within the qualified group, the onus of proving want of due diligence lies on the underwriters. The assured does not have to prove that he was diligent; the insurer has to prove that he was not.

Why the allocation matters in practice. Want of due diligence is a negative, it concerns the internal conduct of the owner's own organisation, and the evidence of it sits in the owner's records. Placing the burden on the insurer therefore makes it a demanding one, and it is why claims in this group are fought on documents: the planned maintenance system, defect reports, superintendent's reports and correspondence, class survey records and conditions of class, the safety management system's maintenance and non-conformity records, and the record of what shore management was told and what it did about it.

So the direct answer to the stem's third demand is: bursting of boilers, breakage of shafts, latent defect, negligence of Master, Officers, Crew or Pilots, negligence of repairers or charterers, and barratry — covered, but subject to a due-diligence proviso which the insurer must prove is engaged.

6. The Indian frame, and what it means on board

The statute. The governing law for a policy subject to Indian law is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. The UK Marine Insurance Act 1906 is its model and is often quoted in coaching material, but it is not the operative statute here, and the later UK reform of the avoidance remedy does not apply to Indian law. Under the 1963 Act a contract of marine insurance is a contract of utmost good faith (section 19), and the assured must disclose every material circumstance known to him before the contract is concluded (section 20); section 66 defines a general average loss.

The clause layer. The perils themselves are not in the statute. They are in the policy, and Indian hull policies are ordinarily written on the London market forms, so the perils clause and the Inchmaree clause described above are the operative wording. The Act supplies the framework of the contract; the clauses supply the cover.

What this means for a Chief Engineer, which is why the question is set in this paper at all:

  • Your negligence is insured. The claim is not defeated because an engineer made a mistake.
  • The claim is put at risk by shore management's failure to act — and the evidence of that failure, or of its absence, is the paperwork you generate.
  • A defect reported and not repaired is the single most dangerous document in a machinery casualty file, because it converts a fortuitous loss into an argument about diligence.
  • Maintain the records: planned maintenance, defect reports, spares ordered and chased, class conditions and their clearance, and what you told the office and when.
  • Report and preserve. On a casualty, notify promptly, preserve the failed component, photograph the evidence and do not dismantle beyond necessity before the surveyor attends — because the burden the insurer must discharge can only be resisted with evidence.

Why this structure scores

The stem contains three questions and a fourth hidden one. It asks what perils of the sea are covered; what an insured can do about a peril that is not covered; and which perils are covered subject to a due-diligence burden on the insurer. The hidden question is the one in section 2: which covered perils are not perils of the sea at all. Without it the second and third demands make no sense, because both are about the parts of the policy that exist precisely because perils of the seas is narrow.

The structure also puts the burden analysis in a section of its own. That is deliberate: the stem's own words are 'the onus of proof is upon the insurers', so the examiner has told the candidate where the marks are, and an answer that mentions it in a clause rather than developing it has ignored an instruction.

The two tests, and why fire is listed separately

Fortuitous, and of the sea rather than merely at sea. Both must hold.

The best single illustration is fire. A fire on board happens at sea, damages the ship and is plainly accidental — and it is not a peril of the seas, because the sea did not cause it. That is why every hull policy lists fire and explosion separately. Give that example and the definition is proved rather than asserted.

The second best is the Inchmaree case itself: a donkey engine damaged by a blocked valve was held not to be a peril of the seas, and the market responded by writing the clause that now bears the ship's name. The whole of the qualified group in section 4 exists because the definition in section 1 is narrow.

It does not accept the risk of the owner's own organisation failing to run the ship properly, because that would be insuring the owner against himself.

The examiner has set a social-policy question in an Engineering Management paper, and that is the key to answering it well. The marks are not for sympathy; they are for argument and for law. Model written answer

(a) The importance of gender diversity, and the challenges women face — 8 marks

1. Where gender equality sits, and why it reaches shipping

Sustainable Development Goal 5 — gender equality and the empowerment of all women and girls — is one of the seventeen goals of the United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, adopted in 2015. It is a cross-cutting goal: it is not a sector programme, it is a condition the other sixteen are expected to be pursued under.

Shipping is caught by it more sharply than most industries for three structural reasons, and naming them is a better opening than repeating the goal.

  • It is one of the most male-dominated occupations in the world of work, and by a very wide margin at sea.
  • It is internationally regulated, so a global norm can be given effect through IMO and ILO instruments in a way that a purely national industry could not achieve.
  • It faces a structural officer shortage. An industry short of qualified officers that recruits effectively from half the population has a supply problem it has partly created.

2. Why gender diversity matters to the industry

The case is worth making as an argument rather than as a slogan, because the examiner is testing management reasoning.

  1. Labour supply. The industry projects a persistent shortfall of certificated officers. Recruiting from the whole talent pool rather than half of it is the largest single untapped source, and it is available without any change to training capacity.
  2. Quality of selection. If a profession draws from half the population it is, on average, taking the median of that half rather than the top of the whole. Widening the pool raises the standard of entry at no cost.
  3. Decision quality and safety culture. Diverse teams are less prone to the group conformity that sits behind a recognisable class of maritime casualty — the bridge or engine-room team in which nobody challenges the person in charge. Diversity of background supports the willingness to speak up that safety management systems depend on and cannot mandate.
  4. Retention and the shore pipeline. Sea-going experience feeds the shore side — superintendents, surveyors, DPAs, port and flag administrations, class. A sea-going population that excludes women produces a shore management population that excludes them a decade later, which is why the effect compounds.
  5. Commercial and reputational expectation. Charterers, financiers and cargo interests increasingly report on workforce composition under environmental, social and governance frameworks, so it has become a commercial fact and not only an ethical one.
  6. Legitimacy. An industry that carries the great majority of world trade and asks for public and regulatory trust is weakened by a workforce profile that no other technical profession would now defend.

3. The challenges women actually face

Separate these into three groups. Running them together produces a list; separating them produces an analysis, and the second scores.

Structural and institutional.

  • Recruitment and cadet-berth allocation, where sponsorship depends on operators who may be reluctant to place a lone woman on a ship.
  • Family, social and cultural expectation about a sea career, which in many labour-supplying countries operates before any employer is involved.
  • The absence of visible role models and of senior women in a position to sponsor juniors — a self-perpetuating shortage.
  • Employer concern about the cost or complexity of accommodating a mixed crew, which is a management problem presented as an operational one.

Shipboard and physical.

  • Personal protective equipment and workwear sized for the average male body. This is not a comfort issue; ill-fitting PPE is a safety defect, and it is precisely the point the MLC now addresses — see section 5.
  • Accommodation, sanitary arrangements and privacy on ships designed and built for a single-sex crew.
  • Isolation: being the only woman on board, often for months, with no peer and no informal support.
  • Harassment, bullying and sexual harassment, aggravated by the closed community, the confined environment, the impossibility of leaving, and a reporting line that may run through the person complained of.
  • Access to medical care and to gender-specific health provision at sea.
  • Manual handling and equipment design based on assumed male anthropometry — a genuine ergonomic issue and one an engineer should identify as a design problem rather than a personal one.

Career and progression.

  • Interrupted sea time around maternity, in a certification system whose revalidation depends on recent sea service.
  • Lack of shore-based career paths that would retain trained women who leave the deep sea.
  • Perception of competence, and the burden of being treated as representative of a category rather than as an individual.
  • Higher attrition at the first-command and first-chief-engineer stage — the point at which the shore pipeline is actually fed.

(b) The IMO's role, and India's initiatives — 8 marks

4. The IMO's role - the programme, the mechanism, and its limits

The IMO's contribution is real and it is worth being precise about what kind of contribution it is.

  1. The Women in Maritime Programme. Established within the Organization's technical co-operation work in the late 1980s, its purpose is to improve women's access to maritime training and to technical and senior positions in the sector, principally through fellowships, training places and institutional support in developing member States.
  2. The regional Women in Maritime Associations. The programme's most durable structure: regional networks that give women in the sector professional contact, mentoring and visibility across national boundaries.
  3. Agenda-setting. The Organization made 'Empowering Women in the Maritime Community' the World Maritime Day theme for 2019, and established an annual International Day for Women in Maritime on 18 May. These are not law, and the value of naming them correctly is that they show the mechanism — the IMO's principal lever here is convening and visibility.
  4. Measurement. The joint IMO and WISTA International Women in Maritime Survey is the industry's principal attempt to measure participation. Measurement matters because in the absence of it the debate runs on anecdote.
  5. Gender mainstreaming in its own work, including in the Strategic Plan and in the technical co-operation programme, and encouragement of member States to report on it.
  6. The competence framework is already neutral. STCW sets standards of competence and makes no distinction of sex. That is worth stating, because it locates the problem correctly: the barrier is not the certification regime, it is access, conditions and retention.

The limit is the honest part of this limb. Almost all of the IMO's gender work is promotional and capacity-building rather than mandatory. There is no IMO convention requirement that a company employ women, and none is proposed. The hardest law in this field is not an IMO instrument at all — which is the next section.

5. The hard legal layer, and exactly where it stood at this sitting

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is an ILO instrument, amended by the Special Tripartite Committee under Article XIII by the Convention's own simplified procedure — not by IMO tacit acceptance. It is where gender equality in shipping stops being aspiration and becomes an enforceable requirement inspectable by port State control.

The 2022 amendments entered into force on 23 December 2024, and are therefore fully operative at this sitting. Among them, Standard A4.3, paragraph 1(b) now requires that the measures a Member adopts for occupational safety and health include 'reasonable precautions to prevent occupational accidents, injuries and diseases on board ship, including through the provision of all necessary appropriately-sized personal protective equipment'.

That single word — appropriately-sized — is the most concrete gender-equality provision in the whole of maritime law, and a candidate who reaches it has answered this question at a different level from one who has not. PPE designed for the average male body does not protect a smaller person; it is a hazard. The obligation is express, it is in Part A of the Code and therefore mandatory, it takes effect through each Member's national law, and it is verifiable at inspection.

The 2016 amendments, in force 8 January 2019, strengthened protection against shipboard harassment and bullying, and the Convention's guidance directs competent authorities to take harassment and bullying into account in national occupational safety and health guidelines and to have regard to the joint ICS and ITF guidance on eliminating it.

And there is a further set, whose status must be stated exactly. The International Labour Conference, at its 113th Session in June 2025, approved a further set of amendments to the Convention. That approval is a fact and it precedes this examination. But the amendments are not in force, and nothing turns on them yet: under the Convention's own procedure an approved amendment takes effect only after the prescribed period and acceptance conditions. Approved is not in force, and an answer that describes the 2025 amendments as operative law is wrong — as is an answer that omits their approval altogether, now that it has happened.

6. India's initiatives to reduce the gender gap

India is one of the largest suppliers of seafarers in the world, so its policy has effect out of proportion to its own fleet. The measures fall into four groups, and they are stated here at the level the evidence supports.

  1. Regulatory and institutional. The Directorate General of Shipping is the competent authority under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and administers seafarer training, certification and welfare. India has ratified the MLC, and its obligations — including Standard A4.3 — take domestic effect through the Merchant Shipping (Maritime Labour) Rules made under the 1958 Act. The competent authority is therefore the route by which the appropriately-sized PPE obligation reaches an Indian ship.
  2. Training access. Pre-sea and post-sea training at DG Shipping-approved maritime training institutes is open to women across the deck, engine and electro-technical streams, and the Indian Maritime University, established by the Indian Maritime University Act, 2008, provides the degree-level route on the same basis. Access to training is the necessary first condition and India's system does not restrict it.
  3. Employment and placement. The persistent Indian bottleneck is not admission to training but the cadet berth — the shipboard training placement without which a certificate cannot be completed. Administrative measures directed at training-berth availability, at conditions for women on board Indian ships and at grievance and harassment redressal are the operative levers, and they are exercised through the Directorate's circulars and orders.
  4. Policy and advocacy. India's national maritime policy documents carry commitments to increase women's participation in the maritime workforce, and India supports the IMO's Women in Maritime work including through the regional association for its area.

A closing judgement, which the command word 'discuss' invites. The regulatory barrier has largely gone: STCW is neutral, Indian training is open, and the MLC now requires equipment that actually fits. What remains is berth allocation, shipboard conditions and retention — and those are decided by shipowners and by senior officers on board, not by regulators. That places the responsibility squarely on the management-level officer this examination is certifying.

Understand this first

The examiner is testing whether you understand that substantial corrosion is a warning, not a failure. The ESP Code's own words are 'wastage in excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins, but within acceptable limits' — the steel is still good. What has been consumed is most of the corrosion allowance, the margin deliberately added to the net scantling to be eaten over the ship's life. So the correct response to a finding is not to cut plate out; it is to find out how far it goes. That is why the Code makes the finding a trigger that enlarges the survey — more thickness measurement, more close-up survey, and the location placed on a suspect-area register that follows the ship for the rest of her life. Renewal only enters when the measurements reach the renewal thickness, at which point the material is no longer in substantial corrosion at all: it is simply unacceptable. Hold that logic and the whole answer — criteria, techniques, steps, repairs and the society's role — falls out of it in order.

Model written answer

1. The definition - and it is printed twice, not once

Substantial corrosion is defined in the 2011 ESP Code (resolution A.1049(27), mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/2), at paragraph 1.2.9. The Code gives two definitions and a Class I answer gives both.

  1. The general definition. Substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that assessment of the corrosion pattern indicates wastage in excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins, but within acceptable limits.
  2. The Common Structural Rules definition. For ships built under the IACS Common Structural Rules, substantial corrosion is an extent of corrosion such that assessment of the corrosion pattern indicates a gauged (or measured) thickness between t-net + 0.5 mm and t-net.

Read the general definition carefully, because both halves of it are examinable. 'In excess of 75 per cent of allowable margins' means most — but not all — of the corrosion allowance is gone. 'But within acceptable limits' means the structure is still acceptable. Substantial corrosion is therefore not a defect requiring immediate renewal. It is a warning state: a declaration that this location is close to its limit and must now be watched more closely and measured more widely.

The CSR definition says the same thing in a different currency. Under CSR the scantling is expressed as a net thickness plus a corrosion addition; substantial corrosion is the last 0.5 mm above the net thickness. Below t-net the steel is no longer acceptable at all.

2. Where the concept sits, and the model it presupposes

The definition only makes sense against the corrosion-allowance model of hull scantlings, and stating that model first is what converts a memorised sentence into an answer.

  • A rule scantling is a net thickness required for strength, plus a corrosion addition for the wastage expected over the ship's life.
  • Allowable margin is that addition. Renewal thickness is the point at which it is exhausted and the steel must be renewed.
  • Wastage is therefore measured not as a percentage of the original plate but as a proportion of the margin. Substantial corrosion is 75 per cent of the way through it.

The Code is deliberate about its own status. Paragraph 1.1.3 states that it contains the minimum extent of examination, thickness measurement and tank testing, and that 'the survey should be extended when substantial corrosion and/or structural defects are found and include additional close-up survey when necessary'. That sentence is the engine of the whole question: a finding of substantial corrosion is the trigger that enlarges the survey.

One further consequence is easy to miss. Paragraph 1.2.2 provides that a space used for both cargo and salt water ballast is treated as a ballast tank when substantial corrosion has been found in it. A finding does not merely add measurements — it can change the survey regime the space falls under.

3. The criteria used to assess it during inspections

The surveyor is not applying one criterion but a small set, and the Code supplies the vocabulary for each.

  1. The measured wastage against the allowable margin. The primary criterion. The gauged thickness is compared with the rule renewal thickness taken from the society's rules and the ship's approved scantling drawings; the result is expressed as a proportion of the margin, and 75 per cent is the threshold.
  2. The corrosion pattern, not a single reading. The Code says 'assessment of the corrosion pattern'. A single low point is not the test. General diminution, grooving, pitting and edge and weld-line wastage are read together across the member.
  3. Coating condition, graded by paragraph 1.2.11 as GOOD (only minor spot rusting), FAIR (local breakdown at edges of stiffeners and weld connections, and/or light rusting over 20 per cent or more of the areas under consideration, but less than POOR) or POOR (general breakdown of coating over 20 per cent or more of areas, or hard scale at 10 per cent or more). Coating condition is what tells the surveyor where corrosion will appear next.
  4. Whether a corrosion prevention system is in place and effective. Paragraph 1.2.10 treats a corrosion prevention system as normally a full hard protective coating, usually epoxy or equivalent. A tank with no effective system attracts a heavier survey regardless of today's measurements.
  5. Whether the location is a suspect area. Paragraph 1.2.8: suspect areas are locations showing substantial corrosion and/or considered by the surveyor to be prone to rapid wastage. Note the 'and/or' — the surveyor's judgement is an independent route into the category.
  6. The ship's history. Paragraph 1.2.7 requires that in selecting representative spaces, account be taken of the service and repair history and of identifiable critical or suspect areas. Previous findings drive present scope.

4. Inspection techniques - how it is actually found

The techniques run from the general to the particular, and the Code's own sequence is the right order to write them in.

  1. Preparation. The space must be cleaned, de-scaled, ventilated, gas-freed, illuminated and made safe for access. A poorly prepared tank is the commonest reason a survey cannot be credited.
  2. Overall survey — a survey to report on the overall condition of the hull structure and to determine the extent of additional close-up surveys required.
  3. Close-up survey — defined at paragraph 1.2.5 as a survey where the details of structural components are within the close visual inspection range of the surveyor, normally within reach of hand. Achieved by staging, rafting, cherry-picker, rope access or remote inspection techniques as the society accepts.
  4. Thickness measurement by ultrasonic gauging, carried out by a company certified for the purpose — the Code carries a dedicated annex of procedures for certification of a company engaged in thickness measurement of hull structures — and witnessed by the attending surveyor.
  5. Simultaneity. Paragraph 1.5 requires that, in any kind of survey, thickness measurements of structures in areas where close-up surveys are required be carried out simultaneously with the close-up surveys. Measuring after the staging has come down is not compliance.
  6. Supplementary techniques where the visual and gauged evidence points to cracking rather than wastage: magnetic particle or dye-penetrant inspection at weld toes and hard spots, and hammer testing to identify heavily scaled or grooved material.
  7. Tank pressure testing of boundaries, and internal examination of the spaces the Code lists.
  8. Recording. Thickness measurement reports on the Code's reporting format, the survey report, and the Condition Evaluation Report which summarises the ship's structural condition at the renewal survey.

5. What a finding does to the renewal survey

The renewal survey is the five-yearly hull survey that re-establishes fitness for the next period of validity of the Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate. Its shape is fixed before substantial corrosion is found, and is then enlarged by the finding.

The baseline. Under section 2, the renewal survey may commence at the fourth annual survey and be progressed during the succeeding year with a view to completion by the fifth anniversary date (2.1.1); the survey programme is dealt with in advance and thickness measurement is not to be held before the fourth annual survey (2.1.2); all cargo holds, ballast tanks, pipe tunnels, cofferdams and void spaces bounding cargo holds, decks and the outer hull are examined and the examination supplemented by thickness measurement and testing, sufficient to discover substantial corrosion, significant deformation, fractures, damage or other structural deterioration (2.1.4); a dry-dock survey forms part of it, with a minimum of two bottom inspections in the five-year period and a maximum interval of 36 months, in dry-dock for ships of 15 years and over (2.2.1–2.2.2); and concurrent crediting to both the intermediate and the renewal survey is not acceptable (2.1.7).

The steps once substantial corrosion is found:

  1. Extend the thickness measurements to the increased extent the Code's dedicated annex prescribes for areas of substantial corrosion. The Code is express, in the corresponding intermediate-survey provision, that these extended measurements must be carried out before the survey is credited as completed — the survey is not closed on a promise.
  2. Extend the close-up survey where necessary, per paragraph 1.1.3, to establish the extent of the affected structure rather than the extent of the first sample.
  3. Record the locations as suspect areas, so that they are examined at every subsequent survey and gauged again. This is the mechanism by which a single finding follows the ship for the rest of her life.
  4. Re-classify the space where 1.2.2 applies, so that a combined cargo and ballast space is thereafter surveyed as a ballast tank.
  5. Assess against renewal thickness. Where measurements are within the substantial-corrosion band the steel is acceptable but monitored; where they are at or below renewal thickness the material must be dealt with now.
  6. Repair or renew, to an approved scheme — section 6 below.
  7. Deal with the cause, not only the symptom. Coating condition graded POOR, a failed or absent corrosion prevention system, or defective anodes are addressed as part of the same event, because renewing steel into a failed coating simply restarts the clock.
  8. Complete the records — thickness measurement reports, survey report and Condition Evaluation Report — and only then credit the survey.

6. Repair methods and their acceptance

This part of the answer is class practice and the IACS framework, not Convention text, and should be written as such. What the ESP Code supplies is the trigger and the record; what the society's rules supply is the acceptance.

  • Cropping and renewal of wasted plating and stiffening is the primary and permanent remedy: the wasted material is cut out and replaced with new material of at least the original rule thickness, of approved grade, with approved welding procedures, qualified welders and approved consumables, and with post-weld NDT.
  • Partial renewal — renewing the wasted strake, face plate, web or bracket rather than the whole member — where the wastage is local and the surrounding structure is sound.
  • Insert plates with full penetration butt welds, sized and positioned so that seams do not fall in high-stress locations or coincide with existing welds.
  • Doubler plates are a temporary and conditional measure only, accepted at the society's discretion, usually with a time limit and a condition of class requiring permanent repair. A doubler conceals the parent material and traps moisture behind it, which is precisely why it is not a repair.
  • Weld repair and build-up for grooving corrosion along weld lines, within the limits the society allows — it is easy to do more harm than good by heat input into thin material.
  • Renewal of the corrosion prevention system: surface preparation, coating to the maker's specification, and renewal of sacrificial anodes.
  • Reinforcement or redesign where wastage is recurrent in the same location, because a repeated failure is a design or drainage problem rather than a maintenance one.

Two conditions govern all of it. First, a repair affecting classed structure requires the society's prior approval of the repair scheme and survey of the completed work. Second, the work must be recorded, because the next surveyor's scope depends on knowing what was done.

7. The role of the classification society

The society is present at every stage of what has just been described, and it is worth naming the roles separately because they are different.

  1. Rule-maker. It sets the scantlings, the corrosion additions and the renewal thicknesses against which everything is measured, and adopts the IACS Unified Requirements and the Common Structural Rules into its own rules.
  2. Survey planner. It agrees the survey programme in advance with the owner, and it determines the extent of close-up survey and thickness measurement — including the extension the finding requires.
  3. Attending surveyor. It carries out the overall and close-up surveys, witnesses the gauging and applies the assessment criteria in section 3.
  4. Approver of the repair. It approves the repair scheme, the materials, the welding procedures and the welders, and surveys the completed repair.
  5. Keeper of the record. It maintains the survey status, the thickness measurement records, the Condition Evaluation Report and the suspect-area register that determines future survey scope.
  6. Enforcer. Where compliance is impaired it imposes a condition of class with a due date; if that is not cleared, class is suspended. Because the Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate is normally issued by the same organisation acting as the flag State's Recognized Organization, the practical consequence reaches the ship's statutory certification too — but through the flag State's delegation, not through class itself.
  7. Certifier of the measuring company. The Code's own annex requires the thickness-measurement company to be certified, and that certification is a class function — a point almost no candidate makes and one that shows the whole chain has been understood.

Common mistakes

  • Giving only one of the two definitions in paragraph 1.2.9.
  • Saying substantial corrosion means 75 per cent of the plate thickness is lost, rather than 75 per cent of the allowable margin.
  • Treating substantial corrosion as a defect requiring immediate renewal, which contradicts 'but within acceptable limits'.
  • Describing the renewal survey generically and never saying what the finding changes — which is the actual question.
  • Omitting that extended measurements must be completed before the survey is credited.
  • Offering doubler plates as a repair method without saying they are temporary, conditional and normally attract a condition of class.
  • Attributing the ESP Code to SOLAS chapter II-1. It is made mandatory by SOLAS XI-1/2.

Examiner traps

  • 'Define' is the first word of the stem. An approximate paraphrase will not do; the two-limb definition is the cheapest three marks on the paper and the most commonly half-taken.

Understand this first

This question is really about one boundary: the line between what a classification society does for the owner under a private contract, and what it does for the flag State under a delegated statutory authority. Limb (a) is life on the owner's side of that line — how a ship becomes classed and stays classed, and what the society can actually do to an owner who lets her slip. Model written answer

(a) Requirements for classification, and the role of the society — 8 marks

1. What classification is, and the line between class and statutory work

Classification is a private, contractual certification of technical fitness against a society's own published rules. A classification society develops rules for the structure, machinery and electrical installations of a ship, approves the design against them, surveys the ship during construction and then surveys her periodically in service. If she complies, the society assigns and maintains class and issues a Certificate of Class.

Two consequences follow immediately, and a Class I answer states both.

  • Class is not law. It is a contract between the owner and the society. The society's remedy for non-compliance is to impose a condition, suspend or withdraw class — not to detain the ship.
  • Class is nevertheless commercially compulsory. Hull and machinery underwriters, P&I clubs, charterers, financiers and flag Administrations all require it, so a ship out of class is effectively unemployable and uninsurable. Withdrawal of class is the sharpest sanction in the industry precisely because it is not a legal one.

The statutory work — SOLAS, MARPOL, Load Line, Tonnage, ISM, ISPS, MLC — belongs to the flag Administration. It only reaches a classification society because the Administration delegates it. Keeping those two ideas apart is what limb (b) then tests.

2. The requirements a ship must satisfy to be assigned class

Classification is not a single event. It is a sequence of requirements running from the drawing board to the end of the ship's life, and the marks in this limb are in that sequence.

  1. Application and rule selection. The owner applies to the society; the applicable rule set is fixed by ship type, size, service and intended notations — and for bulk carriers and oil tankers by the IACS Common Structural Rules.
  2. Plan approval. Structural drawings, midship section, scantlings, shafting and propeller, main and auxiliary machinery, piping, electrical single-line diagrams and control systems are submitted and approved against the rules before construction.
  3. Approval and certification of materials and components. Steel, castings, forgings, welding consumables, main engine, boilers, pressure vessels, shafting, anchors and chain cables are made at approved works under survey and issued with certificates.
  4. Survey during construction. The society's surveyor attends the yard to verify that the ship is being built to the approved plans — alignment, welding and NDT, tank testing, sea trials and machinery trials.
  5. Assignment of class. On satisfactory completion the society assigns a class notation and issues the Certificate of Class. The notations record the basis on which she was built — hull and machinery character marks, service restriction, ice class, UMS, and any special notation.
  6. Maintenance of class in service. Class is retained only by a continuing survey regime: annual, intermediate and five-yearly renewal surveys, docking or in-water surveys, tailshaft and boiler surveys, and machinery surveyed either at renewal or on a rolling continuous-survey or approved planned-maintenance basis.
  7. Reporting of damage and of change. The owner must report damage, grounding, defects and repairs affecting class, and must not alter classed items without approval. Failure to do so is itself a breach.
  8. Conditions of Class. Where a defect impairs compliance, the surveyor imposes a time-bound requirement to rectify. Class continues meanwhile; if the condition is not cleared by its due date, class is suspended.

All of that is class practice and the IACS framework. It is not a SOLAS requirement, and stating it as one is the commonest error in this question.

3. Where class rules acquire legal force, and how the society ensures compliance

The society becomes legally relevant at two distinct points, and they are different mechanisms.

First, SOLAS adopts class rules as the construction standard. Regulation II-1/3-1 provides that, in addition to the requirements elsewhere in the regulations, ships shall be designed, constructed and maintained in compliance with the structural, mechanical and electrical requirements of a classification society which is recognized by the Administration in accordance with regulation XI-1/1, or with applicable national standards of the Administration which provide an equivalent level of safety. That single regulation is what converts a private rulebook into the operative construction standard for a Convention ship — and note the words designed, constructed AND MAINTAINED, which is why the survey cycle, not just the newbuilding survey, is a SOLAS matter.

Second, the Administration may delegate the statutory work itself. Regulation I/6 places inspection and survey on officers of the Administration, but permits the Administration to entrust them to nominated surveyors or to recognized organizations. Regulation XI-1/1 then requires that authorisation to be given in accordance with the RO Code (resolution MSC.349(92)), whose parts 1 and 2 are mandatory and part 3 recommendatory. The RO Code sets what an organisation must be and must do to be recognised, and how the Administration must oversee it.

So the society ensures compliance in two capacities that must never be confused: as classification society, enforcing its own rules by conditions of class and ultimately withdrawal; and as Recognized Organization, acting for the flag State under a specific delegation, issuing statutory certificates in the Administration's name. Under regulation I/6 the RO must at minimum be empowered to require repairs and to survey at a port State's request, and must notify the Administration where a ship's condition does not correspond substantially with her certificates.

(b) A ship classed by two societies — management and statutory responsibility — 8 marks

4. What dual class means, and why an owner takes it

Dual class — also called joint or double classification — is where a ship is entered with two classification societies simultaneously, and must comply with both societies' rules and hold a Certificate of Class from each. It is not the same as either of the two things candidates confuse it with:

  • It is not transfer of class, where the ship leaves one society and joins another under the IACS transfer-of-class procedure.
  • It is not the ordinary and universal split between a class society and a flag Administration.

Owners take dual class for commercial and regulatory access rather than for safety. Typical drivers: a national requirement that ships on a particular register or in a particular domestic trade be classed with the national society; charterer or oil-major preference for a specific society; financiers or the shipyard requiring a society the owner would not otherwise use; retaining an existing society's records on a second-hand purchase while adding the buyer's preferred society; and market access to a trade where one society is dominant.

The cost is real: two rule sets to satisfy, two sets of surveys to arrange and pay for, two sets of records, and the risk that the two societies take different views of the same defect.

5. How the arrangement is managed in practice

Management is contractual, and it is settled before the ship is entered, not after a dispute. The recurring elements are these.

  1. A joint-class agreement between the two societies and the owner. It records the scope each society takes, how surveys are shared, how findings are exchanged and how disagreement is resolved.
  2. The stricter rule governs. Where the two rule sets differ, the ship must satisfy both, so in practice she is built and maintained to the more demanding requirement in each area. Design approval is normally led by one society with the other reviewing and accepting.
  3. Division of the survey scope. The commonest shape is that each society surveys an agreed part of the ship — for example one taking hull and the other machinery — or that the societies alternate the survey cycle. Each then accepts the other's survey for its own class purposes.
  4. Mutual acceptance of records. Survey reports, thickness measurement reports, conditions of class and the survey status are exchanged so that both societies' records agree. IACS procedural requirements govern this exchange between IACS members; the arrangement between an IACS and a non-IACS society is purely a matter of the agreement.
  5. One survey status on board. The practical requirement is that the ship carries a single coherent picture of what is due and what is outstanding, whichever society raised it. Two divergent survey-status reports is the failure mode this whole arrangement exists to prevent.
  6. Conditions of class are honoured by both. A condition imposed by either society is a condition on the ship. Clearing it with one does not clear it with the other unless the agreement so provides.

6. Who conducts the statutory surveys and audits, and why this is a flag question

This is the part of the question that decides the mark, and the answer turns on a point of text.

Statutory surveys and audits are the flag Administration's, and only the flag Administration can say who performs them. Under regulation I/6 they are the Administration's own function; a society performs them only where that Administration has recognised and authorised it under regulation XI-1/1 and the RO Code. Dual class does not confer statutory authority on either society. A society may be the ship's classification society and have no delegation from her flag at all.

Regulation II-1/3-1 speaks of the requirements of a classification society — singular and indefinite. The construction standard is satisfied by compliance with one recognised society's requirements; nothing in the regulation contemplates or requires two.

The practical position is therefore:

  • The Administration nominates one RO to act for it in respect of a given ship — normally one of the two societies, and normally the one it has authorised. That society issues the statutory certificates in the Administration's name.
  • Statutory certification is not duplicated. A ship holds one Cargo Ship Safety Construction Certificate, not two. Two societies issuing the same statutory certificate would be a defect in the delegation, not a feature of dual class.
  • ISM and ISPS audits follow the same rule and are separately delegated. The Administration may authorise a different organisation for audits than for surveys, and the DOC and SMC belong to the company and ship respectively, not to a class society.
  • The Administration may reserve items to itself — commonly initial certification, exemptions, equivalents and detention decisions — and the delegation instrument says which.
  • Class survey and statutory survey may be carried out by the same surveyor on the same attendance, and usually are. That convenience is exactly what obscures the distinction, and the candidate must state it: the surveyor wears two hats and acts under two different authorities.

Conclusion for limb (b). Dual class doubles the classification obligation and leaves the statutory obligation single. The two societies share the class work by agreement between themselves; the statutory work is performed by whichever of them — or which other organisation, or the Administration's own officers — the flag State has authorised, and by nobody else.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question is two questions of eight marks and they are not the same kind of question. Limb (a) is a process question — it rewards a sequence, and the sequence is the ship's own life from plan approval to withdrawal of class. Limb (b) is a distinction question, and it rewards one idea held firmly: class is contractual and can be doubled; statutory authority is delegated and cannot.

Section 1 spends a short paragraph establishing that class is not law. That paragraph is what makes sections 3 and 6 scorable, because both turn on the boundary it draws. Candidates who skip it write eight marks of correct description in limb (a) and then cannot answer limb (b) at all.

The three capacities a classification society can occupy

Almost every error in this question comes from collapsing these into one. Hold them apart:

  • Classification society — acting under its own rules, under a private contract with the owner. Sanction: condition of class, suspension, withdrawal.
  • Recognized Organization — acting for a flag Administration under a delegation given per SOLAS XI-1/1 and the RO Code. Sanction: the Administration's, exercised through the RO.
  • Nominated surveyor — an individual, not an organisation, entrusted under regulation I/6.

A society is not automatically an RO, is not an RO for every flag, and is not an RO for every function of a flag that has authorised it. Each of those three is a separate examinable point.

Common mistakes

  • Writing that SOLAS requires a ship to be classed. It does not. It requires compliance with a recognised society's structural, mechanical and electrical requirements or with equivalent national standards of the Administration — and the alternative is in the text.
  • Treating class certificates as statutory certificates. The Certificate of Class is not a Convention certificate and a port State control officer does not detain on it, though he may well ask to see the survey status.
  • Answering limb (b) as though dual class meant transfer of class, or as though it meant the ordinary class-and-flag split.
  • Saying that under dual class the two societies each issue statutory certificates. That would mean two flag delegations issuing the same certificate for the same ship.
  • Listing the survey types in limb (a) and never reaching plan approval, material certification or survey during construction — which is where classification actually begins.
  • Inventing an IACS PR number for the mutual-recognition procedure. Name the mechanism; the number is not required and a wrong one costs more than an absent one.

  • 'Who is responsible?' in limb (b) invites the answer 'the classification society'. The correct answer names the flag Administration first and the society only as its delegate.

Model written answer

1. What has happened, and which State the examiner is asking about

Separate the incident into its legal components, because each triggers different machinery and different people:

  1. A collision — engaging the duties of both masters and the law of collision.
  2. Loss of life — which makes this a very serious marine casualty and, as section 6 shows, makes a safety investigation mandatory.
  3. Damage to the vessels — engaging seaworthiness, survey, detention and possibly salvage.
  4. An oil spill — engaging the pollution response machinery, the directions power and the liability conventions.

Now read the stem carefully, because it has been narrowed. It does not ask what steps should be taken generally. It asks what steps should be initiated by the coastal State. India is here wearing two hats at once — she is the flag State of the casualty vessel and she is the coastal State off whose coast the casualty occurred — and the examiner has asked about one of them. An answer built on flag-State machinery answers a different question.

The statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. It governs at this sitting and throughout the response to this casualty. Note the constant that runs through the whole answer: the Act is enabling as much as prohibitory. Most of what follows is the Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping being given power to direct, and the owner, master and salvor being obliged to comply.

2. The coastal State's jurisdiction - why it may act at all

Before listing steps, establish the basis for taking them, because that is what distinguishes coastal-State action from flag-State action.

  • The casualty is in India's maritime zones. Those zones are established by the Maritime Zones of India Act, 1976, and within them India exercises the jurisdiction a coastal State has over the protection of its marine environment and its coastal interests.
  • Coastal-State jurisdiction is territorially based, not flag-based. It reaches both ships involved in this collision — including the other vessel, whether she is Indian or foreign flagged. That is the single most important consequence of the narrowing, and it is what flag-State powers cannot do.
  • The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 supplies the domestic powers, principally the pollution provisions of Part XA and the unseaworthy-and-unsafe-ships power, exercised by the Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping.
  • Intervention is proportionate. The coastal State acts to protect its coastline and related interests from pollution or the threat of it, and its measures must be proportionate to the harm threatened.

3. What the ship must do, and what the coastal State requires of her

The first steps are the Master's, and they are taken before any authority is involved — but the coastal State's whole response depends on them, which is why they belong in this answer. The order is life, then ship, then environment, then evidence.

  1. Muster, account for all persons, and search for the missing. With loss of life reported, this is the first and overriding duty.
  2. Render assistance to the other vessel and to persons in danger, so far as he can without serious danger to his own ship and those on board, and stand by; exchange identifying particulars. This duty is in the Act's collision Part and is not discretionary.
  3. Sound tanks and compartments, assess damage and stability, and contain flooding before any decision to move.
  4. Stop the source of the spill — isolate the damaged tank, transfer internally where safe, and deploy the ship's SOPEP.
  5. Report. To the Indian Coast Guard and the nearest MRCC or coastal radio station for search and rescue and the pollution response; to the Directorate General of Shipping through the nearest Mercantile Marine Department; to the owner and DPA; to the port and coastal authorities. Alongside these sit a MARPOL Protocol I report and a SOLAS chapter V danger message.
  6. Preserve evidence. Secure the voyage data recorder, charts, logs, engine and alarm records. This is the step candidates omit and it decides both the investigation and the liability.

4. The coastal State's steps for the safety of the ships

  1. Take charge of the casualty response and coordinate it. Establish a command structure, obtain the picture from both ships, and decide the sequence of operations.
  2. Give directions to the master, owner or salvor. Under Part XA the Central Government and the Director-General may direct a ship to move, to take or refrain from specified action, or to be salved. This is the power that converts a commercial decision into a compellable one, and it is exercisable in respect of either ship.
  3. Detain an unsafe ship. The unseaworthy-and-unsafe-ships provisions — sections 334 to 336 — empower detention of a ship unsafe by reason of defective hull or machinery, undermanning, overloading or improper loading. A damaged ship after a collision is squarely within that power, and the initiator is the Director-General of Shipping or a surveyor acting for him.
  4. Decide on a place of refuge. If a casualty must be moved to sheltered water, the decision is the coastal State's — it is not the owner's and not the flag State's. The current guidance is resolution A.1184(33), Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, adopted by the 33rd Assembly on 6 December 2023 and revoking A.949(23). The decision is taken on an objective analysis of the risks of granting refuge against the risks of refusing it. This is the clearest coastal-State power on the paper and it should be named as such.
  5. Secure navigational safety. Mark the casualty, issue navigational warnings, and establish an exclusion or safety zone where necessary.
  6. Require survey and approve repairs before the ship proceeds, and oversee any salvage. Where the casualty produces wreck, the Act's wreck and salvage Part — sections 390 to 404 — and the Merchant Shipping (Wrecks and Salvage) Rules, 1974 provide the framework and the receiver of wreck's functions.

5. The coastal State's steps for the marine environment

This is the second object the stem names, and it is where coastal-State responsibility is at its clearest.

  1. The statutory directions power is the legal engine. Under Part XA — prevention and containment of pollution of the sea by oil, at sections 356J, 356K and 356L — the Central Government and the Director-General may give directions to the owner, master or salvor for the prevention or containment of pollution, may take the action themselves where directions are not complied with or the case requires it, and may recover the cost from the owner.
  2. Activate the national response. The National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan is the standing plan, and the Indian Coast Guard is responsible for the response at sea in India's maritime zones. The plan is tiered, escalating from a local response with local resources to national and, where necessary, international mobilisation.
  3. Mount response operations. Aerial and satellite surveillance to establish the extent and drift of the slick; containment with booms; mechanical recovery with skimmers; dispersant application where and only where authorised for those waters and that oil; protection of sensitive shorelines and intakes; shoreline clean-up; and waste reception and disposal.
  4. Protect coastal and related interests. Coordinate with State pollution control boards, fisheries and environment departments and port authorities; close or restrict affected areas; and protect water intakes, aquaculture and amenity shorelines.
  5. Recover the cost. The coastal State is not expected to carry the bill: the Part XA power to act and recover, backed by the owner's P&I cover and the compulsory insurance behind the CLC or Bunkers certificate, is what makes the response fundable — with the certificate supporting direct action against the insurer.

6. Investigation - which part is the coastal State's, and which is not

There are two parallel systems with different purposes, and the narrowing in the stem makes it important to say which belongs to whom.

The national inquiry under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 is administered by India as the Administration for her own ship. Its Part on investigations and inquiries into shipping casualties runs in stages: a report of the casualty; a preliminary inquiry by a person appointed for the purpose; and, where the circumstances warrant it, a formal investigation before a court sitting with assessors, which may find fault and may cancel or suspend the certificate of a master, mate or engineer. Its orientation is partly disciplinary.

The marine safety investigation under the Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84), mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/6, exists not to apportion blame but to prevent recurrence. It is mandatory here, because a very serious marine casualty includes one involving a death — and this scenario states loss of lives. Note that it is engaged by the death, not automatically by the spill: a spill engages it only if the environmental damage is severe.

Where the coastal State stands in all this. Both of those are, in their primary form, flag State functions, and here India happens to be the flag State too. But the coastal State has its own standing, and a July answer should say so: a State whose environment or coastal interests are seriously harmed is a substantially interested State under the Code and is entitled to participate in the investigation; and if the other vessel is foreign flagged, India's coastal-State interest is the basis on which she participates in that ship's investigation, since she is not that ship's flag State. India's coastal-State evidence — surveillance imagery, response records, samples and shoreline survey — is also what the investigation will largely rest on.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem is a scenario with two express demands — what steps, and who is responsible for initiating them — a two-part object, the safety of the ships and the marine environment, and one decisive qualifier: by the coastal state. Sections 4 and 5 answer the two objects in the stem's own order and every step in them is attributed. Section 7 answers 'who' as a table, because the examiner asked for it twice and a named allocation cannot be skimmed over.

Section 2 has no counterpart in a general version of this question and it is where a strong answer separates itself. Before listing what a coastal State does, say why it may do it — territorial jurisdiction in India's maritime zones, reaching both ships regardless of flag. That single paragraph is what shows the narrowing has been noticed.

The word in the stem that changes the answer

'By the coastal state.' India here is both the flag State of the casualty vessel and the coastal State off whose coast it happened, and it is very easy to write a complete, accurate, well-organised answer about the flag State — the casualty report, the preliminary inquiry, the formal investigation, the cancellation of certificates — and score poorly, because none of that is what was asked.

The test to apply to every sentence you write: would this power still exist if the ship were foreign flagged? If yes, it is coastal-State power and it belongs in the answer. If no, it is flag-State power and it belongs only in the boundary discussion in section 6.

The fact in the scenario that changes the law

'Resulting in the loss of lives.' Under the Casualty Investigation Code a marine safety investigation is mandatory for a very serious marine casualty — total loss of the ship, a death, or severe damage to the environment. A casualty short of that falls under recommended practice.

So the mandatory limb is engaged here, and it is engaged by the death, not automatically by the oil spill. A spill engages it only if the environmental damage is severe. Getting that reasoning the right way round is the most examinable point in the investigation limb — but keep it in proportion on this sitting, because the investigation is primarily a flag-State function and the stem has asked about the coastal State.

Common mistakes

  • Answering the flag State's question. The commonest and most expensive error on this sitting: a full account of the casualty report, preliminary inquiry, formal investigation and certificate cancellation, none of which is coastal-State action.
  • Citing the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. At this sitting it had not received assent. The governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.
  • Inventing section numbers of the 1958 Act. Name the Part; a wrong section costs more than an absent one.
  • Describing procedures without saying who owns each one, when the stem asks 'who is responsible' expressly.
  • Treating the place of refuge decision as the owner's or the salvor's. It is the coastal State's.
  • Saying the safety investigation exists to apportion blame. It exists to prevent recurrence and its findings are not to be used to apportion liability.
  • Forgetting the other vessel. Coastal-State jurisdiction reaches her too, whatever her flag.
  • Stating that CLC applies without qualification. It turns on whether she is a CLC ship and whether the oil is persistent; otherwise the Bunkers Convention is the relevant regime.

Examiner traps

  • 'By the coastal state' is the whole trap. The examiner rewrote this question from an earlier sitting precisely to insert it.
  • Asking which Act governs. At July 2025 it is the 1958 Act — the 2025 Act had not been enacted.
  • Asking whether the coastal State can act against the other ship. Yes: coastal-State jurisdiction is territorial, not flag-based.
  • Asking who decides on a place of refuge, and on what basis — the coastal State, on an objective analysis of the risks of granting against refusing, under A.1184(33).
  • Asking what makes this a very serious marine casualty. The death, not the spill.
  • Asking who pays for the response. The owner, through the Part XA cost-recovery power and the compulsory insurance behind the certificate — not the coastal State's public funds.

Uncertainty and jurisdiction notes

The statute that governs this question is the one the corpus does not hold. The True Source corpus holds the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 and holds no copy of the Act of 1958. Its own instrument log goes further and instructs that 1958-section-number answers be re-based onto the 2025 Act. The section-number discipline is correspondingly strict. Because the 1958 Act is not held, the only section numbers asserted are three groups already established in this corpus for exactly this machinery — ss.356J–356L in Part XA, ss.390–404 in the wreck and salvage Part, and ss.334–336 for unseaworthy and unsafe ships — and each is carried as authoritative secondary and flagged. Paragraph 1.2.9 carries both definitions; paragraph 1.1.3 makes the Code a minimum and requires the survey to be extended when substantial corrosion is found; paragraph 1.2.8 turns the location into a suspect area for every later survey.
Substantial corrosion: wastage in excess of 75% of allowable margins, but within acceptable limits		 Saying that substantial corrosion means the plate must be renewed, or that 75 per cent of the plate thickness has been lost. Neither is what the Code says. It is 75 per cent of the allowable margin — the corrosion addition, not the plate — and the Code adds 'but within acceptable limits', so the steel is still acceptable. The required response is to measure more widely, not to cut it out.
Q8 Classification Requirements and Dual Class
16 marks		 What classification is, and the line between class and statutory work → The requirements a ship must satisfy to be assigned class → Where class rules acquire legal force, and how the society ensures compliance → What dual class means, and why an owner takes it → How the arrangement is managed in practice → Who conducts the statutory surveys and audits, and why this is a flag question SOLAS II-1/3-1 read with XI-1/1 and I/6. II-1/3-1 adopts the requirements of a classification society recognised by the Administration as the construction standard, and requires the ship to be designed, constructed and maintained to them; I/6 makes inspection and survey the Administration's own function with a power to entrust it; XI-1/1 requires any such authorisation to be given in accordance with the RO Code.
RO Code MSC.349(92) - parts 1 and 2 MANDATORY, part 3 RECOMMENDATORY		 Answering limb (b) with 'the classification societies do the statutory surveys'. Statutory surveys and audits belong to the flag Administration, and a society performs them only where that Administration has recognised and authorised it. Dual class is an arrangement between the owner and two societies; the flag State is not a party to it and its delegation is unaffected by it.
Q9 Collision off the Indian Coast - Action by the Coastal State
16 marks		 What has happened, and which State the examiner is asking about → The coastal State's jurisdiction - why it may act at all → What the ship must do, and what the coastal State requires of her → The coastal State's steps for the safety of the ships → The coastal State's steps for the marine environment → Investigation - which part is the coastal State's, and which is not → Who is responsible for initiating each step Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, Part XA, sections 356J to 356L - the Central Government and the Director-General of Shipping may give directions to the owner, master or salvor of a ship for the prevention or containment of pollution, may take the action themselves where the directions are not complied with, and may recover the cost from the owner. That is the legal engine of the coastal State's whole environmental response and the direct answer to 'who is responsible'.
MS Act 1958 Part XA, sections 356J, 356K and 356L - directions, action in default, cost recovery (section numbers flagged)		 Answering the flag State's question. The stem asks what steps should be initiated by the coastal state, and because the vessel is Indian-flagged it is easy to write a full, accurate account of the casualty report, the preliminary inquiry, the formal investigation and the cancellation of certificates — none of which is coastal-State action. Test every power by asking whether it would still exist if the ship were foreign flagged. Second trap: citing the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025. It received assent on 18 August 2025 and commenced on 15 March 2026, so at this July 2025 sitting it had not been enacted at all — the governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. Third trap: inventing section numbers of the 1958 Act. Name the Part; a wrong section costs more than an absent one.
