## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `legal`. The route is the single canonical sequence; the model answer's
principal headings **are** these steps, and `validate_spec.py` enforces that
correspondence.

1. **The Convention and where it applies** *(limb (a))*
   - Nairobi International Convention on the Removal of Wrecks, 2007 - adopted 18 May 2007, in force 14 April 2015
   - The printed stem names only 'the Wreck Removal Convention', so supply the full title once
   - Applies in the Convention area - the exclusive economic zone of a State Party, Article 1(1)
   - A State Party may extend it to its own territorial sea by declaration under Article 3(2)
   - At this sitting the domestic Indian statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958
2. **What "wreck" means - the four categories** *(limb (a))*
   - Article 1(4): a wreck arises only "following upon a maritime casualty"
   - Category one - a sunken or stranded ship
   - Category two - any part of a sunken or stranded ship, including an object that is or has been on board
   - Category three - any object lost at sea from a ship, stranded, sunken or adrift
   - Category four - a ship about to sink or strand where effective assistance measures are not already being taken
3. **Hazard - the switch that turns the Convention on** *(limb (a))*
   - Article 1(5): a danger or impediment to navigation, or a threat of major harmful consequences to the marine environment or to coastal and related interests
   - Article 6 lists the criteria - depth, proximity to routes, traffic density, type and quantity of cargo and oil, ship type and condition, weather and tidal exposure
   - No hazard, no removal duty: the Convention does not require every wreck to be lifted
4. **Objectives and key provisions** *(limb (b))*
   - Objective one - a uniform international rule for prompt and effective removal beyond the territorial sea, which UNCLOS did not supply
   - Objective two - the cost falls on the owner and the owner's insurer, not on the coastal State's public purse
   - Objective three - legal certainty for owners, insurers and States about who does what, and by when
   - Machinery: report (Art 5), determine hazard (Art 6), locate (Art 7), mark (Art 8), remove (Art 9)
   - Financial security: strict liability (Art 10), compulsory insurance and direct action (Art 12), time bar (Art 13)
5. **The shipowner - strict liability and its limits** *(limb (c))*
   - Article 10 makes the registered owner strictly liable for the cost of locating, marking and removing the wreck
   - Narrow exceptions only - act of war or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; wholly the act of a third party done with intent to cause damage; wholly the negligence of an authority responsible for navigational aids
   - Article 11 stands the Convention down where CLC, HNS, Bunkers or the nuclear conventions govern the same damage
   - Article 12 - compulsory insurance for ships of 300 GT and above, a certificate to be carried, and direct action against the insurer
   - Article 10(2) preserves the owner's right to limit under the applicable limitation regime, and Article 13 bars claims after three years from determination of the hazard and in any case six years from the casualty
6. **The coastal State - judgement, notice and intervention** *(limb (c))*
   - Determines whether the wreck is a hazard, applying the Article 6 criteria
   - Informs the flag State and the registered owner, and warns mariners
   - Sets a reasonable deadline for the owner to remove, and may lay down conditions
   - May remove the wreck itself - immediately where the hazard is severe - and recover the cost from the owner
   - Its powers are not open-ended: measures must be proportionate to the hazard and exercised consistently with international law
   - Outside the Convention area, or in a territorial sea with no Article 3(2) declaration, the State acts under domestic law - in India, the wreck and salvage provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 through the Receiver of Wreck
7. **Environmental implications, and what the regime does not reach** *(limb (c))*
   - The wreck itself is a continuing source - bunkers and cargo leaking over years, cargo residues, antifouling and other hazardous materials in the structure
   - Physical damage to the seabed, reefs and fishing grounds, plus obstruction of ports and routes
   - An unmarked wreck causes the next casualty, which is why marking sits inside an environmental instrument
   - Strict liability plus compulsory insurance is what converts an environmental risk into a funded removal
   - Limits: only wrecks that are hazards; only in the Convention area unless extended; liability is capped by the applicable limitation regime, so a very expensive removal can still exceed the fund
