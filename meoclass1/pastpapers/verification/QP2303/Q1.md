The answer route is the single canonical spine; the decomposition, memory cue, quick revision, retrieval cards

**Answer route:**

1. **The Convention's definition of pollution, and what makes it unusually wide** _(A.)_ - Article 1(1)(4) - the introduction by man, directly or indirectly, of substances or energy into the marine environment, including estuaries; which results or is likely to result in deleterious effects; "by man" excludes a natural seep; "indirectly" catches a river carrying waste to the sea; "or energy" - heat, noise and radiation are pollution; "or is likely to result" - no damage need have occurred; Five effects: harm to living resources and marine life; hazards to human health; hindrance to marine activities including fishing; impairment of quality for use of sea water; reduction of amenities; The definition is source-neutral - nothing in it is about ships
2. **The general obligation, and why it is not confined to any zone** _(A.)_ - Article 192 - States have the obligation to protect and preserve the marine environment; Free-standing and not zone-limited - the zones allocate rights; this imposes a duty across all of them; Article 193 - the sovereign right to exploit resources is expressly subject to that duty; Article 194 - all measures necessary, from any source, by the best practicable means and in accordance with capabilities; Activities under a State's jurisdiction or control must not cause damage by pollution to other States; No transferring damage from one area to another, and no transforming one type of pollution into another
3. **The coastal State's obligations - rule-making** _(A.)_ - The Convention deals with pollution source by source; Article 207 land-based sources - adopt national law, taking into account international rules. Wide discretion; Article 210 dumping - national law, and express prior approval of the coastal State within its zones; Article 211 vessels - act through IMO; national law must have at least the same effect as the international standard; Article 212 from or through the atmosphere; The discrimination that scores: stricter for your own ships, yes; laxer, no; and no construction standards on foreign ships in innocent passage; The rules are to be re-examined from time to time
4. **The coastal State's obligations - enforcement, and its limits** _(A.)_ - Article 217 flag State - compliance by its own ships wherever the violation occurs. The primary duty; Article 218 port State - where the ship is voluntarily in port, proceedings for discharges outside its own waters; Article 220 coastal State - violations in the territorial sea or EEZ, powers graded by seriousness and evidence; Information, then physical inspection, then detention on clear objective evidence of major damage; Article 221 - measures beyond the territorial sea after a maritime casualty, proportionate to the damage threatened; Article 235 - responsibility, liability, and recourse for prompt and adequate compensation; Article 235(2) is the bridge to limb B; The Part XII safeguards constrain how the powers may be exercised
5. **Why the oil pollution compensation regime has three tiers** _(B.)_ - A major spill can cost more than any owner or insurer could reasonably carry; Unlimited liability would have made tankers uninsurable; leaving victims uncompensated was unacceptable; The compromise: the owner is strictly but limitedly liable; and the balance is placed on the cargo interest - those who receive the oil; The regime shares the cost between the ship and the cargo, which is its defining idea
6. **Tier one - the shipowner and his insurer** _(B.)_ - CLC 1992. The registered owner is strictly liable - no fault need be proved; Liability is channelled to him - not the master, crew, pilot, charterer, manager or salvor; Compulsory insurance for ships carrying persistent oil in bulk, evidenced by a certificate carried on board; Right of direct action against the insurer; Source of funds: the shipping side - the owner and his P&I club; He may limit by reference to tonnage. No figure is given - the limits are amended over time; The limit is lost for his personal act or omission, with intent or recklessly and with knowledge; Narrow exceptions: war, exceptional natural phenomenon, wholly a third party's intentional act, negligence of a navigational-aids authority
7. **Tiers two and three - the Funds, and who actually provides the money** _(B.)_ - Tier two - the 1992 IOPC Fund, where tier one gives nothing or is financially inadequate; Tier three - the Supplementary Fund under the 2003 Protocol, where tier two is insufficient; Source for both: contributions levied on RECEIVERS OF CONTRIBUTING OIL - crude and heavy fuel oil received by sea above a threshold quantity; The cargo interest pays, not the shipowner and not governments - the point most candidates miss; The tiers are cumulative and sequential, each with its own overall ceiling; Tier three is OPTIONAL and requires membership of the 1992 Fund first; So the compensation available for the same casualty depends on where it happens
8. **What the three tiers do NOT cover - and the trap** _(B.)_ - The regime is about persistent oil carried in bulk as cargo - the tanker case; Bunker oil from a non-tanker is the Bunkers Convention 2001 - a single tier with no fund behind it; Hazardous and noxious substances have their own convention, not in force at this sitting; Geography matters - the tiers respond only in States party, and the third only where opted into; Closing: the three tiers exist because UNCLOS article 235(2) requires recourse for prompt and adequate compensation
