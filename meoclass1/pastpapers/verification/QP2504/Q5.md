The controls are the statute-regression sweep and the rule that no proposition is stated without a section number from the held text.

**Technical ambiguities adjudicated.**

- Whether admiralty jurisdiction lies in all courts - it does not; section 3 vests it in the High Courts
- Whether the jurisdiction is unlimited at sea - it is not; it runs to and including territorial waters, extendable only by Central Government notification
- Whether every maritime claim is a maritime lien - it is not; only the five in section 9(1), and section 4(1)(w) lists maritime lien as merely one head of claim among twenty-three
- Whether a maritime lien survives a change of owner or flag - it does, by section 9(2), which is what makes it formidable
- Whether a maritime lien survives a judicial sale - it does not; section 8 vests the vessel free from all encumbrances and liens
- Whether a sister ship can always be arrested - it cannot; section 5(2)'s proviso bars it for an ownership or possession claim under section 4(1)(a)
- Whether arrest is risk-free for the claimant - it is not; section 11 exposes it to an undertaking or security for wrongful or unjustified arrest
- Whether assessors are witnesses - they are not; they assist the court, and section 13(2) preserves the parties' right to call expert witnesses
- Whether assessors are appointed case by case - the Central Government appoints a LIST by notification, from which the court draws

**Target answer shape.** Five numbered sections under two printed limb headings. Limb (a): one section on origin and purpose including the repeal of the colonial statutes, one on jurisdiction and application, one on the claims and the arrest machinery with the twenty-three heads GROUPED in a table and the priority ladder set out. Limb (b): one section on who assessors are and how they are appointed, taking section 13 close to the text, and one on qualifications, role and the mechanics of technical assistance - which is where a marine engineer candidate can genuinely outperform.

## 6. OPEN ITEMS AND EVIDENCE GAPS

- The RULES made under section 16(2)(a) - which are what actually prescribe assessors' qualifications, experience, duties and fees - are NOT held by the corpus, and no notification appointing a list of assessors is held. Section 13(1) and section 16(2)(a) create the power and name the subject matter; the answer states what the Act provides and describes the qualifications an assessor would be expected to hold as a matter of substance, expressly flagged as not being a quotation from any prescribed rule.
- Section 1(3) provides that the Act comes into force on a date the Central Government appoints by notification. The commencement notification is not held by the corpus. The Act is treated as in force at this sitting, which is uncontroversial and is corroborated by the corpus manifest recording the Act as complete-official held law, but the notification itself is not cited.
- No decided case is cited. Indian admiralty case law - including the Supreme Court authority that shaped the pre-2017 jurisdiction - is outside the corpus entirely, and attributing a proposition to a judgment that cannot be checked would be exactly the false precision the production protocol forbids. The answer states the statutory position, which is what the stem asks for.
- No fee scale for assessors is stated. Fees are prescribed by rules that are not held.
- The number of High Courts exercising admiralty jurisdiction is not stated as a figure. Section 3 vests it in 'the respective High Courts' without enumeration, and any count would depend on notifications not held.
