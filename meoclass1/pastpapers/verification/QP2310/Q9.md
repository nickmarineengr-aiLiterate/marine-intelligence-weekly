## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: `explain`. The principal headings of the model answer ARE this route.

1. **a) What root cause analysis is, and why it is required**
   - A structured method for finding the underlying organisational reasons an event occurred
   - Its purpose is the PREVENTION OF RECURRENCE, not the identification of the immediate failure
   - Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84) of 2008; Parts I and II mandatory through SOLAS XI-1/6 from 1 January 2010
   - A safety investigation is required into every VERY SERIOUS MARINE CASUALTY - total loss, death, or severe damage to the environment
   - The Code separates the safety investigation from blame and liability - and that separation is what MAKES root cause analysis possible
   - ISM Code regulation 9 - non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences reported, investigated and analysed, with corrective action procedures
   - So the obligation is both external, on the flag State, and internal, on the Company
2. **a) The principles of root cause analysis**
   - Seek causes, not culprits
   - Every accident has multiple causes - a single-cause finding is an incomplete investigation
   - Go beyond the immediate; keep asking why until you reach something the organisation controls
   - Base findings on evidence, and gather perishable evidence first
   - Establish the sequence before attempting the explanation
   - Test each causal factor by removal - would the outcome have changed without it?
   - A root cause must be ACTIONABLE. 'Human error' is not a root cause
   - Correct at the level the cause sits - a systemic cause cannot be fixed by retraining one person
   - Close the loop - verify implementation AND effectiveness, and share the lesson across the fleet
   - Just culture - honest reporting must be safe, or the raw material never arrives
3. **a) The steps of a root cause analysis**
   - Recognise and report - and make the situation safe first
   - Preserve and collect evidence - recorders, alarm and event logs, maintenance and defect records, work and rest hours, permits, risk assessments, drawings, and early witness accounts
   - Establish the sequence of events - a timeline, or an event and causal factor chart
   - Identify the causal factors, and test each by removal
   - Analyse to root cause - five whys, fishbone, fault tree or barrier analysis
   - Identify the failed defences - absent, inadequate, bypassed or unmaintained
   - Develop corrective AND preventive action, each with an owner and a date
   - Implement and verify - two checks, implementation AND effectiveness
   - Communicate fleet-wide and feed the finding back into risk assessment, procedures, training and management review
4. **a) Proximate cause versus root cause**
   - Proximate - the event directly preceding the outcome; at the sharp end, immediate in time, and usually obvious
   - Root - the underlying systemic reason; at the blunt end, often years earlier, and rarely obvious
   - Correcting the proximate restores the situation. Correcting the root prevents recurrence. That single sentence is the difference
   - Worked example - a seized lubricating oil pump bearing, traced back through an isolation never restored, to a permit close-out that was not enforced, to a form that required no signature, to a generic procedure never reviewed for this ship
   - Correcting the proximate replaces a bearing; correcting the root revises the permit system across the fleet
   - A test that keeps the two apart: if the fix is 'replace it and remind him', you have found the proximate cause
   - NOTE the different sense in marine insurance - there 'proximate cause' means the dominant or effective cause of a loss. Related idea, different test, and worth distinguishing
5. **b) The accident causation models**
   - Sequential models came first - the domino model, an accident as the last of a line of falling dominoes
   - Useful for the idea of a chain, but it encourages the search for one removable cause close to the accident
   - The organisational accident model replaced it - defences in depth, popularly the Swiss cheese model
   - Successive layers: design, engineered safeguards, procedures, supervision, training, alarms, and finally the crew
   - Each layer has holes - and THE HOLES MOVE, opening and closing with workload, manning, fatigue and maintenance state
   - An accident occurs only when the holes in EVERY layer momentarily line up
   - Failures then divide by two things - WHERE they originate and HOW FAST their effect appears. That division is exactly what the two printed terms name
6. **b) Active failures**
   - Unsafe acts by people in DIRECT CONTACT with the system, whose effects are felt almost immediately
   - Slips and lapses - skill-based; the intention was right and the execution or the memory was wrong
   - Example - opening the adjacent valve from habit, because it is identical and next to the right one
   - Mistakes - planning failures; the action was carried out as intended but the plan was wrong
   - Example - misdiagnosing a burning exhaust valve as an instrument fault and continuing at load
   - Violations - deliberate departures from a rule; routine, situational or exceptional
   - Example - a permit signed retrospectively because the job is always done that way
   - They are the most visible element, which is precisely why investigations stop at them
   - A ROUTINE VIOLATION IS DIAGNOSTIC OF A LATENT CONDITION - if everybody does it, the rule and the conditions are the problem, not the person
7. **b) Latent failures**
   - The delayed consequences of decisions by designers, builders, procedure writers, regulators and management
   - Present in the system BEFORE the accident, often for years, dormant until combined with a local trigger
   - They act in two ways - creating error-provoking conditions in the workplace, and creating long-lasting holes in the defences
   - Design - poor ergonomics; identical adjacent valves with unclear labelling
   - Procedure - generic, unusable or absent; a checklist never revised for this installation
   - Manning and resourcing - a manning level that makes the rest-hour regime unachievable; deferred maintenance
   - Training - familiarisation that certifies attendance rather than capability
   - Maintenance management - generic intervals never adjusted to the ship; a tolerated defect backlog
   - Culture - production pressure, discouraged reporting, near misses not investigated
   - They cause nothing on their own, which is exactly why they survive so long
8. **b) Why the distinction matters, and how the two combine**
   - Active failures are hard to prevent and easy to blame - the next person in the same conditions makes the same slip
   - Latent conditions are the manageable target, and they are findable PROACTIVELY - by audit, risk assessment, near-miss analysis and trend data
   - That is precisely the justification for the ISM Code's audit and review machinery
   - The accident needs BOTH - which is why trivial errors sometimes cause catastrophes and usually cause nothing at all
   - Chance is real, so OUTCOME IS A POOR MEASURE OF BEHAVIOUR
   - Hence near misses must be investigated with the same rigour - they are free evidence about the state of the defences
   - Blame destroys the data; a just culture separates honest error from recklessness and wilful violation
   - Closing line - root cause analysis is the technique that converts an active failure into information about latent conditions
