## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: `evaluate`. The principal headings of the model answer ARE this route.

1. **What port State control is, and the power it rests on**
   - Inspection of foreign ships in national ports to verify compliance with the international conventions
   - A safety net beneath the flag State - it supplements the flag State's duty and does not transfer it
   - Each convention carries its own control article: SOLAS I/19; MARPOL 5 and 6; Load Lines 21; STCW X and I/4; Tonnage 12; AFS 11; BWM 9; MLC Title 5.2
   - UNCLOS sits above them - article 94 flag State duties, articles 218 and 219 port State enforcement, article 226 limits on inspection
   - The consolidated IMO Procedures are recommendatory - Governments are invited to implement them
   - At this sitting those Procedures are A.1155(32) of December 2021
   - No more favourable treatment - a non-Party or below-size ship gains no advantage from her flag's non-ratification
2. **(i) Why the cooperation had to become regional**
   - A single State inspecting alone sees a snapshot of one call and knows nothing of the previous four ports
   - Ships trade in regional patterns, so a region is the natural unit over which a ship can actually be tracked
   - Without sharing, effort is duplicated on good ships and missed on bad ones
   - Uncoordinated national standards invite port shopping - the substandard ship simply calls elsewhere
   - A memorandum of understanding, not a treaty, is the chosen instrument: administrative, quick to agree, quick to amend, no ratification needed
   - It binds maritime authorities to each other rather than creating rights and duties between States
3. **(i) The regional agreements**
   - Paris MoU - Europe and the North Atlantic; the oldest and the model for the rest, established in the early 1980s after a major tanker casualty
   - Tokyo MoU - Asia and the Pacific; the largest by traffic
   - Indian Ocean MoU - India is a member, and Indian inspections are carried out by the Directorate General of Shipping
   - Vina del Mar Agreement - Latin America; Caribbean MoU; Mediterranean MoU
   - Abuja MoU - West and Central Africa; Black Sea MoU; Riyadh MoU - the Gulf
   - The United States Coast Guard operates its own national programme rather than joining a memorandum - a deliberate exception worth naming
   - IMO's role is coordination and technical cooperation, not command - it does not run any regime
   - No membership figure, inspection volume or detention rate is asserted - those move every year
4. **(i) How the cooperation actually works**
   - A shared regional database of inspections, deficiencies and detentions - the core of the whole arrangement
   - Harmonised procedures, checklists and deficiency codes, so that one authority's finding means the same as another's
   - A ship risk profile built from flag performance, recognized organization performance, ship type, age, and the ship's own inspection history - which decides how often and how hard she is inspected
   - Avoidance of duplicated inspection - a ship recently inspected is not normally re-inspected within the period, which frees effort for the ships that need it
   - Concentrated inspection campaigns - all members examine one topic in the same window, producing comparable data and a real deterrent
   - Published performance lists for flags and for recognized organizations - reputational pressure applied where regulation cannot reach
   - Detention review arrangements, so an owner has somewhere to go and the regime is disciplined by challenge
   - Training, harmonisation seminars and exchange of officers - because consistency between inspectors is the regime's weakest point
5. **(ii) The future of port State control**
   - Data-driven targeting. Richer risk profiles, and better use of reported data so that inspection effort follows evidence rather than rotation
   - Digitalisation. Electronic certificates and their verification; single-window reporting; less paper checking and more condition checking
   - Remote and hybrid inspection. Developed rapidly out of necessity and remained under active discussion - useful for documents, no substitute for walking the machinery space
   - A widening scope. The regime now reaches labour conditions under MLC, ballast water, anti-fouling with the cybutryne controls applying from 1 January 2023, and energy efficiency, with EEXI and the carbon intensity regime applying from the same date
   - Cyber. Maritime cyber risk management has to be addressed in the safety management system, so it is reachable on an ISM-related inspection
   - Alternative fuels. LNG in service and methanol and ammonia in prospect create a real competence gap for inspectors, and training is the constraint
   - The human element. Hours of rest, fatigue, familiarity and seafarer welfare are increasingly where the findings are, rather than in hardware
   - Quality over quantity - fewer, better-targeted, more consistent inspections; professionalisation and qualification of officers
   - Greater harmonisation between regimes, and the long-term aspiration of genuinely global data exchange rather than nine regional pools
6. **(iii) The case that it is effective**
   - It reaches ships the flag State does not - which is the whole point, and there is no other mechanism that does
   - The deterrent effect is larger than the inspection effect: ships are maintained to a standard because they expect to be inspected
   - No more favourable treatment removes the commercial reward for non-ratification, which is a structural achievement rather than an operational one
   - Performance lists for flags and recognized organizations create reputational and commercial consequences that regulation alone cannot
   - Detention is a commercially serious sanction - it costs charter, reputation and future targeting, so it changes owner behaviour
   - It generates the only large independent evidence base on the actual condition of the world fleet, which feeds back into rule-making
   - Concentrated inspection campaigns demonstrably move the fleet on a chosen topic within a season
   - Substandard operation has become materially harder and more expensive to sustain than it was before the regime existed
7. **(iii) The limits of the tool**
   - An inspection is a snapshot in port, not a survey, and not a voyage - it sees the ship at her most prepared
   - It is reactive: it finds deficiencies that already exist rather than preventing them
   - Consistency between inspectors and between regimes is the persistent weakness, and it is a fairness problem as well as a safety one
   - Ships can be, and are, prepared for inspection - a clean engine room on the day proves less than it appears to
   - Deficiencies are symptoms. The causes are ashore, in manning, budget and maintenance policy, and only the ISM route reaches them at all
   - Coverage is uneven - regimes differ in resources, and a ship trading only where enforcement is weakest is inspected least
   - It has no direct power over the flag State; it can publish, but it cannot compel
   - Detention numbers are a poor measure: falling detentions may mean a better fleet, or fewer inspections, or softer ones
   - There is a real risk of it being relied on as a substitute for flag State implementation and company safety management, which it can never be
8. **(iii) The verdict, and what it depends on**
   - Yes - but as a safety net and a deterrent, and it must be judged as one
   - It has been demonstrably effective at the bottom of the market, which is exactly where it was aimed
   - It is least effective at the top, where a well-run ship gains little from an inspection she was always going to pass
   - Its effectiveness is conditional: on inspector competence, on consistency, on honest data, and on regional coverage
   - It is a complement, not a replacement. Safety is built by the flag State's implementation and the company's safety management; port State control verifies and deters
   - The right measure is behaviour changed, not deficiencies recorded - which is why the deterrent effect matters more than the detention statistic
   - For the Chief Engineer the conclusion is practical: a ship kept ready for inspection every day is a ship that is genuinely safe, and one prepared the night before is neither
