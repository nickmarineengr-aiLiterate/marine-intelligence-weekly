## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE

**Question intent.** A two-part instruction on one subject. The first sentence asks for BACKGROUND and RELEVANCE, which is a historical and architectural question - why did this Convention have to be written at all. The rest asks for four DEFINITIONS and, in the same breath, for an explanation of HOW THEY DIFFER from CLC 92. Both halves of that instruction must be visibly obeyed on each item. At management level the question is testing whether the candidate understands the compensation architecture as a designed system - and in particular whether he notices that the biggest differences between the two regimes are NOT among the four printed items.

**Mark allocation.** The question prints a single (16) before the four items and no individual allocation, so nothing is inferred about the examiner's split. Route weighting: two framing steps, one step per printed item in the printed order, and a closing comparison step which carries the analysis. The closing step is not asked for by name but is required by the instruction 'explain how they differ', because the differences that matter are structural rather than definitional.

**Examiner expectation.** The gap correctly identified - CLC limited to persistent oil carried as cargo by a tanker, leaving a non-tanker's own bunkers uncovered - with the dates of adoption and entry into force. The three borrowed mechanisms: strict liability, compulsory insurance with a certificate, and direct action. All four definitions given accurately in substance, with bunker oil correctly stated to include lubricating oil and residues and not to be confined to persistent oil, and the time limits correctly stated to be the SAME as CLC. Exclusions correctly distinguished from defences. And a closing comparison covering who is liable, the absence of a free-standing limit, the absence of a fund, and the different insurance threshold.

## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: `compare`. The principal headings of the model answer ARE this route.

1. **The background - the gap the Convention was written to close**
   - The international compensation regime was built in response to tanker casualties
   - CLC therefore covered only persistent oil carried as CARGO in bulk by a tanker, together with the bunkers of such a ship
   - A non-tanker carries no oil cargo - but a large one carries thousands of tonnes of her own fuel
   - That fuel does the same damage to the same coastline, and fell outside the regime entirely
   - The claimant was left to national law: identify the defendant, prove fault, and then find assets to enforce against
   - Adopted 23 March 2001; in force 21 November 2008
2. **Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture**
   - It completes the pollution-liability map for the world fleet - tankers were covered, everything else now is
   - It borrows the machinery the tanker regime had proved: strict liability, compulsory insurance and direct action against the insurer
   - Strict liability means the claimant need not prove negligence, only the escape and the damage
   - Compulsory insurance with a certificate, and the certificate is checkable by port State control
   - Direct action means the claimant can sue the insurer, so an insolvent owner does not defeat the claim
   - But it borrows its limit from the LLMC and has no fund behind it - so it improves certainty of recovery rather than size of recovery
3. **(a) Pollution damage**
   - Loss or damage caused OUTSIDE the ship by contamination resulting from the escape or discharge of bunker oil from the ship, wherever the escape or discharge may occur
   - Environmental impairment other than loss of profit is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken
   - It includes the costs of preventive measures, and further loss or damage caused by those measures
   - Preventive measures are reasonable measures taken by any person after an incident to prevent or minimise pollution damage
   - Difference from CLC 92: the FORMULA IS THE SAME. This definition is deliberately modelled on it
   - The difference is not in the definition but in what oil it attaches to - and that is item (b)
4. **(b) Bunker oil**
   - Any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of such oil
   - It expressly includes LUBRICATING OIL - which many candidates miss
   - It includes RESIDUES, so sludge and tank washings are within it
   - It is NOT confined to PERSISTENT oil - a gas oil or a distillate bunker is still bunker oil
   - Difference from CLC 92 - and this is the sharpest of the four. CLC attaches to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil CARRIED AS CARGO in bulk, plus the bunkers of such a ship
   - So the two regimes are distinguished by the character and the role of the oil: cargo and persistent, against fuel and any hydrocarbon mineral oil
   - The 'ship' to which this Convention applies is correspondingly wide - any seagoing vessel and seaborne craft of any type whatsoever
5. **(c) Time limits for claims**
   - THREE YEARS from the date the damage occurred - rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within that period
   - SIX YEARS from the date of the incident which caused the damage - an absolute long stop, and in no case may an action be brought after it
   - Where the incident consists of a SERIES of occurrences, the six years run from the date of the FIRST of them
   - The two periods run from different events - damage, and incident - which is why they are not the same clock
   - The three-year period can therefore be cut short by the six-year long stop where damage emerges late
   - Difference from CLC 92: NONE OF SUBSTANCE. CLC 92 Article VIII imposes the same three-year and six-year structure, and the Bunker Convention deliberately mirrors it
   - Saying plainly that this one is the SAME is worth a mark - it shows the comparison was actually made rather than assumed
6. **(d) Exclusions**
   - First and most important - CLC damage is excluded. The Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable under it
   - That provision prevents overlap and makes the two regimes mutually exclusive rather than cumulative
   - Warships, naval auxiliaries, and ships owned or operated by a State and used on government non-commercial service are excluded
   - A State may nevertheless decide to apply the Convention to such ships, and may extend it to ships not otherwise covered
   - DISTINGUISH EXCLUSIONS FROM DEFENCES - the printed item invites them to be blurred and keeping them apart is worth marks
   - Defences under Article 3: act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection or an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible natural phenomenon; damage wholly caused by a third party's act or omission done with intent to cause damage; and damage wholly caused by the negligence of a government in maintaining navigational aids
   - Plus contributory negligence of the claimant, which reduces or defeats the claim
   - Difference from CLC 92: the exclusions and defences closely mirror CLC. The real divergence is elsewhere - in step 7
7. **The differences taken together - who, limit, fund, threshold**
   - WHO IS LIABLE - the largest difference. The Bunker Convention defines 'shipowner' widely: the owner, including the registered owner, the bareboat charterer, the manager and the operator, and they are JOINTLY AND SEVERALLY liable
   - CLC 92 does the opposite: it CHANNELS liability EXCLUSIVELY to the REGISTERED OWNER, and expressly bars claims against a listed group including the charterer, manager, salvor and crew
   - So the Bunker Convention gives the claimant more defendants; CLC gives him one certain defendant. Both solve the same problem in opposite directions
   - THE LIMIT. The Bunker Convention has NO free-standing limit - it expressly preserves the right to limit under any applicable regime, in practice the LLMC as amended. CLC 92 has its own limit calculated on tonnage
   - THE FUND - the most practically important difference. CLC 92 is backed by the 1992 Fund Convention and the 2003 Supplementary Fund Protocol: three tiers of compensation. The Bunker Convention has NO fund behind it at all - when the limitation fund is exhausted, that is the end
   - THE INSURANCE THRESHOLD. Bunker compulsory insurance bites by gross tonnage, above 1,000 GT; CLC by CARGO CARRIED - more than 2,000 tonnes of persistent oil in bulk as cargo
   - The honest summary: the Bunker Convention improves the claimant's chance of finding a solvent defendant, but not the total sum ultimately available

- MANAGEMENT JUDGEMENT: that the practical significance of the Bunker Convention is certainty of recovery rather than size of recovery - strict liability, a wide range of defendants, compulsory insurance and direct action give a claimant a defendant who can pay, while the absence of a fund means the total available is bounded by the limitation regime alone.
