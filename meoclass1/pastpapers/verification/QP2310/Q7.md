## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: `legal`. The principal headings of the model answer ARE this route.

1. **a) What the Convention is, and the bargain it strikes**
   - The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 - the LLMC
   - Adopted 19 November 1976; in force 1 December 1986. It replaced the 1957 Convention
   - The 1996 Protocol, in force 13 May 2004, raised the limits and introduced tacit acceptance
   - Amendments adopted under that Protocol in 2012 took effect on 8 June 2015
   - India gives effect to it through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958
   - It caps total liability arising from one occurrence, by reference to the ship's tonnage
   - The bargain: near-unbreakable, but near-automatic. Article 2 claims are limitable whatever the basis of liability
2. **a) Its purpose - why the law permits a cap at all**
   - Insurability. A liability with a calculable ceiling can be underwritten; one without a ceiling cannot
   - Certainty and finality - both sides know the worst case before the casualty, not after the trial
   - To encourage investment in a venture that is inherently hazardous and conducted far from the owner's supervision
   - To give claimants a fund that actually exists, rather than a judgment against a company with no assets
   - To provide a single forum and an orderly distribution instead of a race between claimants in several jurisdictions
   - The quid pro quo: because the right is so hard to break, it must be, and is, very hard to abuse
3. **a) The two heads of claims for which limitation is stated**
   - They are set out in Article 6
   - HEAD ONE - claims for LOSS OF LIFE OR PERSONAL INJURY
   - HEAD TWO - ANY OTHER CLAIMS, in practice property damage and consequential loss
   - Each head has its own separate fund, each calculated on the ship's tonnage
   - The life and personal injury fund is the larger of the two
   - If the life fund is exhausted, the unsatisfied balance of those claims ranks rateably with the property claims against the other fund
   - PASSENGER claims are separate again, under Article 7, and are NOT one of the two heads - the commonest error on this limb
4. **a) Who can limit liability, and what the Convention never covered**
   - Article 1 - shipowners and salvors
   - 'Shipowner' is defined widely: the owner, charterer, manager and operator of a seagoing ship
   - A salvor is any person rendering services in direct connexion with salvage operations
   - Any person for whose act, neglect or default the shipowner or salvor is responsible - so the master and crew are covered
   - The liability insurer, to the same extent as the assured - which is what makes the cap commercially real
   - Article 3 excepts certain claims from limitation altogether: salvage and general average contribution; oil pollution damage within CLC; nuclear damage; and certain claims by the owner's or salvor's own servants
   - So the cap is not universal - some liabilities were never inside it
5. **b) Plaintiff and Defendant**
   - Plaintiff - the party who brings the civil action and asserts the claim; also called the claimant in many jurisdictions
   - He bears the burden of proving his case - the loss, the breach and the causal link
   - Defendant - the party against whom the action is brought and who answers it
   - Here the defendant is typically the shipowner, charterer, manager, operator, salvor or liability insurer
   - In a limitation action the procedural roles can invert: the shipowner may commence proceedings to establish his right to limit and to constitute the fund
   - The burden of BREAKING the limit lies on the claimant - which is exactly why the limit so rarely breaks
6. **b) Privity - and why it is the OLD test**
   - Privity means knowledge and concurrence - the owner knew of the state of affairs or the fault, and assented to it
   - It is knowledge plus acquiescence, not mere knowledge, and it is personal to the owner or his alter ego
   - Under the 1957 Convention and earlier law the owner lost the right to limit where the loss occurred with his 'actual fault or privity'
   - Critically, that test placed the burden on the OWNER to prove the ABSENCE of fault or privity
   - Article 4 of the LLMC 1976 replaced it. The limit is now broken only where the loss resulted from the person's personal act or omission, committed with intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result
   - The burden moved to the claimant and the standard rose. That single change is why the modern right is described as near-unbreakable
   - Distinguish privity of CONTRACT - the separate doctrine that only a party to a contract may sue upon it. Same word, different subject
7. **b) Unlimited liability - and the three routes to it**
   - Unlimited liability means liability not capped by any fund - the defendant answers for the loss in full, as proved
   - Route one - the Article 4 conduct is proved. Intent, or recklessness with knowledge that such loss would probably result
   - Route two - the claim is an Article 3 excepted claim, and was therefore never inside the Convention at all
   - Route three - the Convention does not apply: the forum State is not a party, or the owner fails to constitute or is not entitled to the fund
   - The practical consequence is what matters at management level: the exposure becomes effectively uninsurable
   - So the loss of the right to limit is not merely expensive - it is the event the whole insurance structure of the industry is built to avoid

- INTERNAL REUSE (MIW verification record QP2301/Q5, read in full): the Convention structure, the two-heads distinction against the separate passenger limit, the categories of person entitled to limit, and the historical displacement of the 'actual fault or privity' test.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: the historical account of limitation - the pre-1976 'actual fault or privity' test under which the OWNER bore the burden of disproving fault, and its replacement by an intent-or-recklessness test under which the CLAIMANT bears the burden, which is what made the modern right near-unbreakable.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: the ordinary legal usage of plaintiff and defendant, and the distinct doctrine of privity of contract, named in order to be separated from privity in the limitation sense.
- MANAGEMENT JUDGEMENT: that the practical purpose of limitation is insurability - a liability with a calculable ceiling can be underwritten, and one without a ceiling cannot - and that this is why the loss of the right is commercially catastrophic rather than merely expensive.
