## 2. WHAT THE QUESTION ASKS

Two eight-mark limbs on a human-element topic set in an engineering management paper. Limb (a) wants a management argument for diversity plus a structured account of the barriers - it is a 'discuss', so it wants a position. Limb (b) is two distinct four-mark demands in one sentence: what the IMO does, and what India does. The hidden demand, and the one that separates answers, is whether the candidate can locate any HARD law in a field that is mostly aspiration.

**Mark allocation.** 16, printed as 8 + 8. Within limb (b) the marks divide again, roughly evenly, between the IMO and India - so three sections there rather than two, with the mandatory legal layer given its own section because it belongs to neither and explains both.

**Examiner expectation.** For (a): SDG 5 named and quickly left; a real argument for diversity resting on labour supply, selection quality, safety culture, retention and the shore pipeline; and the barriers organised rather than listed, with the shipboard ones - PPE fit, accommodation, isolation, harassment, ergonomics - treated as engineering and management problems. For (b): the IMO's Women in Maritime Programme, the regional associations, the agenda-setting acts and the survey, with an honest statement that this work is promotional rather than mandatory; and then India - the Directorate General of Shipping under the 1958 Act, open access to approved training and the Indian Maritime University, the cadet-berth bottleneck, and policy commitments. A strong answer also reaches the MLC.

**Required answer components:**

- Where gender equality sits in the SDG framework and why it reaches shipping at all
- Why gender diversity matters to the maritime industry - the manning, safety and performance arguments, stated as arguments and not as slogans
- The challenges women actually face, separated into structural, shipboard and career categories
- The IMO's role - the programme, the mechanism and its limits
- The hard legal layer, and what changed ten weeks before this sitting
- India's initiatives, stated at the level the evidence supports

**Ambiguities adjudicated:**

- 'The importance of gender diversity' could be answered ethically or commercially. In an Engineering Management paper the commercial, safety and manpower arguments carry more weight, so the answer leads with them and lets the ethical point close it.
- 'Challenges faced by women in this sector' spans shore and sea. Both are covered, but the shipboard ones are developed further because they are what a Chief Engineer can act on.
- 'The role of the IMO in promoting gender equality' invites a list of activities. The better answer also says what kind of instrument each is, and where the IMO's competence stops.
- The stem prints 'the international Maritime Organization' with a lower-case initial. Preserved in text_verbatim; not reproduced and not remarked on in the answer.
- 'Initiatives taken by the Indian government' could invite policy-document recitation. Without held sources, the answer states the institutional framework and the operative bottleneck instead, which is more useful and more defensible.

## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `explain`. The route is the single canonical sequence; the model answer's principal
headings **are** these steps, and `validate_spec.py` enforces that correspondence.

1. **Where gender equality sits, and why it reaches shipping**
   - SDG 5 - gender equality and the empowerment of all women and girls, 2030 Agenda, adopted 2015
   - it is a cross-cutting goal, not a sector programme
   - shipping is caught by it sharply - one of the most male-dominated occupations in the world of work
   - it is internationally regulated, so a global norm can be given effect through IMO and ILO instruments
   - and it faces a structural officer shortage while recruiting from half the population
2. **Why gender diversity matters to the industry**
   - labour supply - the largest untapped source of certificated officers, available without new training capacity
   - quality of selection - a wider pool raises the standard of entry at no cost
   - decision quality and safety culture - diverse teams resist the conformity behind a recognisable class of casualty
   - retention and the shore pipeline - today's sea-going population is tomorrow's shore management
   - commercial and ESG expectation from charterers, financiers and cargo interests
   - legitimacy of an industry that asks for public and regulatory trust
3. **The challenges women actually face**
   - STRUCTURAL - recruitment and cadet-berth allocation, social expectation, absence of role models and sponsors
   - SHIPBOARD - PPE and workwear sized for the average male body, which is a safety defect and not a comfort issue
   - SHIPBOARD - accommodation and sanitary arrangements on single-sex-designed ships; isolation; medical access
   - SHIPBOARD - harassment and bullying, aggravated by a closed community and a reporting line through the person complained of
   - SHIPBOARD - equipment and manual-handling design based on assumed male anthropometry
   - CAREER - interrupted sea time against revalidation, thin shore paths, perception of competence, attrition at first command
4. **The IMO's role - the programme, the mechanism, and its limits**
   - the Women in Maritime Programme within technical co-operation - fellowships, training places, institutional support
   - the regional Women in Maritime Associations as its most durable structure
   - agenda-setting - the 2019 World Maritime Day theme and the International Day for Women in Maritime on 18 May
   - measurement - the joint IMO and WISTA Women in Maritime Survey
   - gender mainstreaming in the Strategic Plan and technical co-operation
   - STCW is already gender-neutral, which locates the barrier in access, conditions and retention
   - THE LIMIT - this work is promotional and capacity-building, not mandatory; no IMO convention requires it
5. **The hard legal layer, and what changed ten weeks before this sitting**
   - the MLC is an ILO instrument, amended by the Special Tripartite Committee under Article XIII, not by IMO tacit acceptance
   - the 2022 amendments entered into force 23 December 2024
   - Standard A4.3(1)(b) now requires all necessary APPROPRIATELY-SIZED personal protective equipment
   - it is in Part A of the Code, therefore mandatory, and it is verifiable at inspection
   - the 2016 amendments on harassment and bullying, in force 8 January 2019, and the ICS/ITF guidance the Convention points to
   - the 2025 amendments are AFTER this sitting and form no part of the answer
6. **India's initiatives to reduce the gender gap**
   - regulatory - DG Shipping as competent authority under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, with MLC obligations given effect by rules made under it
   - training access - DG Shipping-approved institutes across deck, engine and electro-technical streams, and the Indian Maritime University Act, 2008
   - employment - the cadet berth is the real bottleneck, addressed by administrative measures on berths, shipboard conditions and redressal
   - policy and advocacy - national maritime policy commitments and support for the IMO's regional association
   - the closing judgement - the regulatory barrier has largely gone; berth allocation, conditions and retention are owner and senior-officer decisions
