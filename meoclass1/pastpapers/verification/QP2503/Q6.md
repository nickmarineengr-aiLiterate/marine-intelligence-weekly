## 2. WHAT THE QUESTION ASKS

A three-limb question on a convention that is adopted but not in force. Limb (a) tests whether the candidate knows the Convention defines its scope by REFERENCE to other instruments rather than by listing commodities. Limb (b) asks for the legal heads of damage, not a description of chemical spills. Limb (c) is the analytical limb: it asks why one ship can need three certificates, and the answer is that the three conventions are defined by three different things and a products tanker's trade moves across all of them.

**Mark allocation.** 16, printed as 6 + 5 + 5. Limb (a) carries the most and gets two sections - the categories, then the bulk and packaged split that limb (c) later needs. Limb (b) gets the heads of damage and the exclusions, because the exclusions are what set up limb (c). Limb (c) gets the three-convention analysis and then the certificate and the two tiers.

**Examiner expectation.** For (a): that article 1.5 defines by reference to seven other IMO instruments, the seven named with an example each, and the residues extension in 1.5(b). For (b): the four heads of article 1.6 - death and personal injury, property, environmental contamination with the reinstatement restriction, and preventive measures - with the recognition that the first head involves no pollution at all. For (c): that CLC is defined by the oil's persistence, Bunkers by the oil's use with a carve-out for CLC damage, and HNS by the cargo's category with a carve-out for CLC damage; that a products tanker changes cargo voyage by voyage; and the certificate under article 12 with the article 9 limits and the Fund's 250 million ceiling.

**Required answer components:**

- What the Convention is and what its status was at THIS sitting - the stem's premise adjudicated
- The substances - the seven categories of article 1.5, each defined by reference to another instrument
- The bulk and packaged split, and why it changes the answer
- The heads of damage under article 1.6
- What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it
- Why an oil products tanker sits across all three conventions
- The certificate itself, and the two tiers of compensation behind it

**Ambiguities adjudicated:**

- The stem's premise that the Convention 'is expected shortly to come into force' is a statement about time that was already doubtful when the task was first set. It is preserved and adjudicated rather than repeated or corrected away.
- 'Types of pollution and subsequent losses or damages' reads as a technical question and is a legal one. Answered from article 1.6, with the four chemical risk types named alongside so the technical reading is also satisfied.
- 'Probably require' in limb (c) is deliberately hedged by the examiner and the answer must be hedged the same way - the requirement depends on the cargo of the day, and explaining that dependency IS the mark.
- Whether category (vii) covers a solid bulk cargo depends on its being also subject to the IMDG Code in effect in 1996 when carried in packaged form, which is a narrower test than 'listed in the IMSBC Code'. Stated as the Convention states it.
- Units of account are Special Drawing Rights; no currency conversion is offered.

## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `legal`. The route is the single canonical sequence; the model answer's principal
headings **are** these steps, and `validate_spec.py` enforces that correspondence.

1. **The Convention, and its status at this sitting**
   - the 1996 Convention as amended by the 2010 Protocol, read together as one single instrument under Protocol article 18
   - at March 2025 it is ADOPTED and NOT IN FORCE, and its entry-into-force conditions are not met
   - the stem's 'expected shortly' premise is preserved, not adopted as a prediction and not contradicted
   - the substantive provisions were fixed by the 2010 Protocol, so they do not change while the instrument waits
   - the scheme: strict liability up to a limit, compulsory insurance and certificate, plus a second-tier Fund financed by cargo receivers
2. **(a) The substances covered - the seven categories of article 1.5**
   - the Convention lists no commodities - article 1.5(a) defines by reference to seven other IMO instruments, so the list updates automatically
   - (i) oils in bulk per MARPOL Annex I reg 1; (ii) noxious liquid substances per Annex II reg 1.10 and provisional X, Y, Z under 6.3
   - (iii) dangerous liquids in bulk in IBC Code ch.17 and products under para 1.1.6
   - (iv) dangerous, hazardous and harmful substances in PACKAGED form under the IMDG Code
   - (v) liquefied gases in IGC Code ch.19 and products under para 1.1.6
   - (vi) liquids in bulk with a flashpoint not exceeding 60 degrees C, closed cup
   - (vii) solid bulk materials with chemical hazards under the IMSBC Code, so far as also subject to the IMDG Code in effect in 1996 in packaged form
   - article 1.5(b) - residues from previous bulk carriage of (i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii)
3. **(a) Bulk HNS and packaged HNS - why the split matters**
   - article 1.5bis - bulk HNS is categories (i) to (iii) and (v) to (vii), plus residues
   - article 1.5ter - packaged HNS is category (iv) alone
   - the split fixes the owner's limit of liability under article 9
   - packaged cargo does not contribute to the Fund as bulk cargo does, and a mixed or indeterminate cargo takes the packaged figures
4. **(b) The heads of damage - article 1.6**
   - loss of life or personal injury, on board or outside the ship - NO pollution element required, which is the largest difference from the oil regime
   - loss of or damage to property outside the ship
   - loss or damage by contamination of the environment, with compensation for impairment other than loss of profit limited to reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken
   - the costs of preventive measures and further loss caused by them
   - article 1.6 also addresses damage that cannot reasonably be separated from other causes
   - the four chemical risks the stem's word 'pollution' reaches for: fire and explosion, toxicity, contamination, reactivity
5. **(b) What is excluded, and the mechanism that excludes it**
   - 4.1 - claims other than those under a contract for the carriage of goods and passengers
   - 4.2 - disapplied where incompatible with workers' compensation or social security schemes
   - 4.3(a) - CLC pollution damage excluded, WHETHER OR NOT compensation is payable under that Convention; the exclusion bites on the definition
   - 4.3(b) - class 7 radioactive material under the IMDG or IMSBC Codes
   - 4.4 - warships and State non-commercial ships
   - the conventions are drafted to tile rather than overlap
6. **(c) Why an oil products tanker needs certificates under all three**
   - CLC is defined by the oil being PERSISTENT, as cargo or in the bunkers of such a ship; certificate over 2,000 tons of oil in bulk as cargo
   - CLC applicability turns on two facts - is she a CLC ship, and is the oil persistent - and must never be stated absolutely
   - Bunkers is defined by the oil's USE for operation or propulsion; certificate over 1000 GT; but article 4.1 stands down where the damage is CLC pollution damage
   - HNS is defined by the CARGO's category - gasoline and naphtha in (vi), chemical and vegetable-oil parcels in (ii) or (iii)
   - the commercial reason all three are carried: her trade is not one cargo, and certificates cannot be obtained at the loading berth
   - residues under HNS 1.5(b) and CLC article I.1 mean the previous cargo can still reach her on the next voyage
7. **(c) The certificate itself, and the two tiers behind it**
   - article 12 - insurance or other financial security in the article 9.1 sums, for a ship registered in a State Party actually carrying HNS
   - certificate in the Annex I model form, carried on board, mutually recognised, with direct action against the insurer under 12.8
   - article 9.1(a) bulk - 10 million units of account to 2,000 tons, rising by 1,500 then 360, capped at 100 million
   - article 9.1(b) packaged, mixed or indeterminate - 11.5 million, rising by 1,725 then 414, capped at 115 million
   - article 14.5(a) - the HNS Fund, financed by receivers, with the whole scheme capped at 250 million units of account
   - article 7 - strict liability, the 7.2 defences including the shipper's failure to inform, and channelling under 7.5
   - article 37 - three years from knowledge, ten years absolute from the incident
