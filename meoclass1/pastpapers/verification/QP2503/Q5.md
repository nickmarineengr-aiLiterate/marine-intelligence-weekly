## 2. WHAT THE QUESTION ASKS

A 16-mark undivided marine insurance question carrying three express demands and one implied one. Express: define and identify the perils of the sea that a marine policy covers; explain with examples what an insured can do about a peril that is not an insured peril; and enumerate the perils that are covered subject to a due-diligence proviso whose burden falls on the underwriters. Implied, and necessary to both later demands: that a hull policy covers a good deal that is NOT a peril of the sea, because the term itself is narrow.

**Mark allocation.** 16, undivided. **Examiner expectation.** The statutory definition - fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas, excluding the ordinary action of wind and waves - with both tests drawn out and a list of what falls outside. The perils actually enumerated in a hull policy, with the recognition that most of them are not perils of the sea. Four routes to cover an uninsured peril, each with a concrete example, and the P&I route among them. Then the Inchmaree group named item by item, the due-diligence proviso stated with the persons it reaches, and the burden correctly allocated: the assured proves the peril, the insurer proves want of diligence. A strong answer states that the Master and crew are NOT in the diligence list, and closes on what that means for a Chief Engineer's records.

## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `legal`. The route is the single canonical sequence; the model answer's principal
headings **are** these steps, and `validate_spec.py` enforces that correspondence.

1. **What 'perils of the sea' actually means**
   - the Rules for Construction of Policy in the Schedule to the Marine Insurance Act, 1963
   - fortuitous accidents or casualties of the seas; NOT the ordinary action of the winds and waves
   - test one - fortuity: accidental, not inevitable, not the natural consequence of going to sea
   - test two - of the sea, not merely at sea: the sea must be the cause
   - covered: stranding, grounding, sinking, foundering, collision, striking a submerged object, heavy weather, accidental entry of seawater, capsizing
   - excluded: wear and tear, ordinary leakage and breakage, inherent vice, corrosion, vermin, machinery damage not proximately caused by a maritime peril
2. **The maritime perils that are covered but are NOT perils of the sea**
   - perils of the seas is only the FIRST item in the perils clause
   - fire and explosion - listed separately precisely because the sea did not cause it
   - violent theft by persons from outside the vessel; jettison; piracy
   - contact with land conveyance, dock or harbour equipment or installation
   - earthquake, volcanic eruption or lightning
   - accidents in loading, discharging or shifting cargo or fuel
3. **How an insured covers a peril that is not an insured peril - with examples**
   - extend the policy by an additional clause for extra premium - example: additional perils cover buying back the defective part itself
   - buy a separate policy - examples: war and strikes, loss of hire, increased value and hull interest, freight
   - cover it mutually through a P&I club - examples: pollution, cargo claims, crew injury and repatriation, wreck removal, damage to fixed and floating objects
   - adjust the terms rather than the perils - higher sum insured, lower deductible, removal of a restrictive warranty
   - the two limits: an inevitable loss is not insurable, and a known existing defect is a repair bill not a risk
4. **The perils whose cover is qualified by due diligence**
   - the Inchmaree clause, named from the case in which a donkey-engine casualty was held not to be a peril of the seas
   - bursting of boilers; breakage of shafts; latent defect in machinery or hull
   - negligence of Master, Officers, Crew or Pilots; negligence of repairers or charterers not assured under the policy; barratry
   - the proviso - provided the loss has not resulted from want of due diligence by the Assured, Owners, Managers or Superintendents or any of their onshore management
   - the Master, Officers and Crew are NOT in the diligence list - their negligence is the insured peril
   - latent defect cover pays the resulting damage, not the renewal of the defective part
   - barratry is a wilful wrongful act of Master or crew to the prejudice of the owner
5. **Where the onus of proof lies, and why it matters**
   - the assured proves that the loss was caused by a peril insured against
   - the insurer proves any exception, exclusion or proviso on which it relies
   - the due-diligence provision is a proviso limiting liability, not a condition precedent
   - therefore the ONUS OF PROVING WANT OF DUE DILIGENCE LIES ON THE UNDERWRITERS
   - in practice these claims are fought on documents - planned maintenance, defect reports, superintendent's reports, class conditions, SMS records
   - the direct answer to the stem: boilers, shafts, latent defect, negligence, repairers, barratry
6. **The Indian frame, and what it means on board**
   - the governing statute is the Marine Insurance Act, 1963 - not the UK Act of 1906
   - section 19 utmost good faith; section 20 disclosure of every material circumstance; section 66 general average loss
   - the perils are in the policy, not the statute - Indian hull policies are ordinarily written on London market forms
   - your negligence as an engineer is insured; shore management's failure to act is what puts the claim at risk
   - a defect reported and not repaired is the most dangerous document in a machinery casualty file
   - notify promptly, preserve the failed component, photograph, and do not dismantle beyond necessity before the surveyor attends
