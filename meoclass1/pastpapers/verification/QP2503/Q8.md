## 2. WHAT THE QUESTION ASKS

Three short notes at 6 + 5 + 5 on in-service energy efficiency, framed by the stem's own reference to the recent regulatory stress on efficiency. The examinable thread running through all three is MEASUREMENT of a surface that cannot be seen, and the decision-making that measurement supports. Limb (b) additionally tests whether the candidate understands what 'condition based' means as against calendar-based maintenance.

**Mark allocation.** 16, printed as 6 + 5 + 5. Limb (a) carries the most and gets two sections - the management cycle and the regulatory frame - because 'management' at six marks demands a management answer. Limb (b) gets one section built round the three groups of condition indicator and the trigger. Limb (c) is two topics in five marks and gets one section each, kept tight.

**Examiner expectation.** For (a): both kinds of roughness with physical roughness recognised as largely irrecoverable; average hull roughness and its measurement; in-service performance monitoring with normalisation; coating system selection matched to the trade; inspection including niche areas; cleaning and grooming with the coating-damage and port-permission constraints; and the SEEMP and carbon intensity drivers. For (b): that the interval is set by measured condition, the performance indicators from the machinery including heavy running, direct inspection findings including cavitation erosion, machinery condition indicators, the trigger threshold, and the limits of the method. For (c): coating purpose, type, application demands and its wear at leading edge and tip; polishing method through progressive grades with the leading edge emphasised; the boundary between polishing and repair; and why polishing pays out of proportion to its cost.

**Required answer components:**

- The physical basis common to all three limbs - where the resistance actually goes and why roughness matters so much
- Hull roughness management as a managed cycle: measure, predict, intervene, verify
- The regulatory and guidance frame that has made it a duty rather than a preference
- Condition-based propeller maintenance - what condition is monitored, how, and what triggers action
- Propeller coating - what it is for, and its real limits
- Propeller polishing - grades, method, and why it pays out of proportion to its cost

**Ambiguities adjudicated:**

- 'Hull Roughness Management' could be read as measurement only. Read as the full management cycle, because it is worth six marks and because 'management' is the printed word.
- 'Condition Based Propeller Maintenance' is a term of art. Answering it as periodic inspection would answer a different question, so the calendar-based contrast is stated explicitly.
- 'Propeller Coating & Polishing' is two topics in one five-mark limb. Both are covered, and the relationship between them is stated - coating lengthens the interval, it does not replace polishing.
- Whether to give percentage fuel penalties. Resolved against: the figures are hull-, coating- and trade-specific and originate largely in maker's material, and the mechanisms carry the marks without them.
- Whether propeller repair belongs in limb (c). Only as a boundary: polishing removes surface material, and reshaping or pitch correction is repair requiring approval. Saying where the boundary lies is worth more than describing repair.

**Sources consulted:**

- ENGINEERING FACT - the decomposition of a ship's resistance into frictional resistance, which dominates at normal merchant-ship speeds and is the component roughness acts on, and residuary resistance comprising wave-making and form effects. Roughness increases the momentum deficit in the turbulent boundary layer, thickening it and raising skin friction; the effect is a function of both the height and the texture and spacing of the roughness, which is why biological fouling is far more damaging than an equivalent height of physical roughness.
- ENGINEERING FACT - the two kinds of hull roughness: physical roughness, which is permanent or semi-permanent - plate waviness, weld seams, corrosion pitting, mechanical damage, coating breakdown, over-applied or badly applied coating, and the accumulated roughness of successive coating layers; and biological roughness, which is fouling and is recoverable - slime, weed, and hard shell fouling such as barnacles and tubeworm.
- ENGINEERING FACT - average hull roughness as the conventional measure, taken as a mean apparent amplitude over a defined sampling length with a hull roughness gauge at a defined pattern of stations, used to track the hull's condition dry-docking to dry-docking.
- ENGINEERING FACT - antifouling coating system types and their working principle: self-polishing copolymers, which hydrolyse at a controlled rate to expose fresh biocide and to maintain a smooth surface; controlled-depletion systems; and biocide-free foul-release silicone or fluoropolymer systems, which work by presenting a low-surface-energy, low-adhesion surface from which fouling is removed by the ship's own motion above a threshold speed, and which are therefore activity-dependent.
- ENGINEERING FACT - the propulsive efficiency chain: open-water efficiency, hull efficiency and relative rotative efficiency, and the effect of blade surface condition on the section's lift and drag, hence on torque absorbed, thrust delivered and the propeller's operating point relative to the engine. A roughened or fouled blade absorbs more torque for the same revolutions, so the engine is loaded earlier - a heavy running condition observable from the engine itself.
- ENGINEERING FACT - blade surface condition and its causes: biological fouling, calcareous deposits, cavitation erosion characteristically on the suction face near the leading edge and tip, impingement and corrosion pitting, edge damage from debris or ice, and the roughness left by grinding or by a previous poor polish.
- ENGINEERING FACT - the surface-finish grade convention used in propeller work, in which a defined roughness grade is specified for the blade surface and the leading-edge region is treated to a finer standard than the mid-chord, because the leading edge governs the section's inflow behaviour.

## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `explain`. The route is the single canonical sequence; the model answer's principal
headings **are** these steps, and `validate_spec.py` enforces that correspondence.

1. **The physical basis common to all three limbs**
   - resistance divides into frictional and residuary; friction dominates at merchant-ship speeds
   - roughness thickens the turbulent boundary layer and raises skin friction
   - the penalty depends on TEXTURE AND SPACING as well as height - which is why slime costs more than physical roughness of the same thickness
   - consequence: more shaft power for the same speed, or speed lost at constant power; and on the propeller, more torque, so the engine runs heavy
2. **What is being managed, and the cycle that manages it**
   - physical roughness - plate waviness, weld seams, pitting, damage, coating breakdown, accumulated layers - largely irrecoverable and it ratchets upward
   - biological roughness - slime, weed, hard fouling - recoverable
   - MEASURE: average hull roughness at dock, and in-service performance monitoring NORMALISED for draught, trim, weather and current
   - SPECIFY: surface preparation, film thickness and application quality, because a bad application is itself a roughness decision
   - CHOOSE: self-polishing copolymer, controlled depletion or foul-release, matched to speed, idle time and trade - foul-release is activity-dependent
   - INTERVENE AND VERIFY: inspection including NICHE AREAS, grooming in preference to reactive cleaning, and verification against the performance trend
   - two constraints: aggressive cleaning damages the coating, and in-water cleaning needs port permission which is not automatic
3. **Why it has become a duty rather than a preference**
   - the SEEMP under the revised MARPOL Annex VI, MEPC.328(76), in force 1 November 2022 - the work is documented and auditable
   - operational carbon intensity, which makes in-service condition commercially visible to charterers
   - the IMO Biofouling Guidelines - a biofouling management plan and record book, niche areas, in-water cleaning - GUIDANCE, NOT LAW
   - the AFS Convention - organotin prohibited, and the cybutryne control effective 1 January 2023, operative at this sitting
4. **Monitoring the condition, and letting it set the interval**
   - definition: the propeller is worked on when measured condition says so, not when the calendar says so
   - THE SHIP SAYS: power-speed-revolution relationship, heavy running, slip, torsion meter - all normalised
   - THE DIVER SEES: fouling and calcareous deposit, cavitation erosion on the suction face near leading edge and tip, impingement and pitting, edge damage, poor previous polish
   - THE MACHINERY TELLS: bearing wear-down, stern tube oil condition, seal performance, blade-rate vibration signature
   - the output is a TRIGGER, not a date - intervention when the trend or the inspection grade crosses an agreed threshold
   - contrast with calendar-based polishing, which either wastes money or leaves a fouled propeller running
   - limits: needs normalised data and a baseline, an agreed threshold, and access - port permission, water clarity, current, diver safety
5. **Propeller coating - purpose and real limits**
   - purpose: resist fouling and corrosion and hold a smooth surface for longer, lengthening the polish interval
   - typically biocide-free foul-release silicone or fluoropolymer, presenting a low-adhesion surface
   - application demands full cleaning, blasting and profiling, controlled conditions and correct film thickness - a bad application is worse than none
   - benefits: slower fouling, lower average roughness across the cycle, easier cleaning, some corrosion protection
   - LIMITS: coatings wear preferentially at leading edge and tip, exactly where it matters most, and cavitation removes them locally
   - a coating is a life-extender between polishes, not a substitute for polishing
6. **Propeller polishing - method, grades and why it pays**
   - controlled removal of fouling, calcareous deposit and roughness to a specified finish, in water by divers or in dock to a higher standard
   - remove deposit from both faces plus boss and rope guard - the areas skipped in a hurried job
   - work through progressively finer grades; never leave coarse directional scratching
   - particular attention to the leading edge and outer radii, which are specified finer because they do the work
   - DO NOT RESHAPE - dressing edge damage, pitch correction and cavitation repair are propeller REPAIR, needing approved procedures and class involvement
   - record the grade and verify against the performance trend, closing the loop from limb (b)
   - why it pays: a small area at high relative velocity setting the whole chain's efficiency; cheap, quick and done in water; and it unloads the engine
   - closing judgement: the cheapest energy-efficiency measure available in service, and it is really about measurement
