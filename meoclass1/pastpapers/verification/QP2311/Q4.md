## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE - COMPLETED BEFORE RESEARCH

**Question intent.** Three limbs at 6, 6 and 4. Limb a) wants identity, objectives and the key provisions, and prints the word 'preventing' against a Convention that is expressly not about prevention - so the boundary must be stated and then answered generously rather than ignored. Limb b) prints 'analyse' and asks about MEMBER STATES, not flag States, which brings the article 6 national system into scope as the centre of the limb; and it asks for implementation challenges, which the donor does not set at all. Limb c) prints 'enlist' and names TWO holders - Member States and ships - so it needs two lists, and the shipboard list is answered out of MARPOL rather than OPRC. The discriminating feature is whether the candidate knows that OPRC binds States and MARPOL Annex I regulation 37 binds the ship.

**Mark allocation.** 6 + 6 + 4, all printed. Limb c) at four marks gets two tight lists and one closing paragraph on why the shipboard list comes out of MARPOL.

**Examiner expectation.** That the candidate can state the three nouns of the Convention's own title as its objectives; can give the article architecture in order; can distinguish what a State does as flag State from what it does as coastal State AND know that a Member State question includes both; can analyse rather than list; can name the SOPEP correctly with its two thresholds and four contents; and can say why the ship's document obligation is specified in MARPOL although created in OPRC. **Freshness risk.** LOW - the Convention is unamended and thirty-three years old at this sitting, and the regulation carrying the document duty is unamended. The live edge runs the OTHER way from the donor: MEPC.384(81) is recorded in the corpus and in the donor as adopted-but-not-in-force, and at THIS sitting it does not exist at all, so an inherited note would assert the existence of a future instrument. That is the reversal, and it is a deletion rather than a re-statement.

**Jurisdiction risk.** MEDIUM. The question is set on an Indian syllabus and limb b) is where the answer becomes national, because article 6 national systems are exactly what a Member State builds. India's system - a national oil spill contingency plan with a designated response authority - is described in outline as the SHAPE an article 6 system takes, without citing an instrument, because none is held. The implementing statute at this sitting is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

**Technical ambiguities adjudicated.**

- Whether OPRC prevents pollution - not directly; prevention is MARPOL's work, but preparedness prevents escalation, article 4 covers a PROBABLE discharge, and drills discipline the operations where spills begin
- Whether OPRC deals with liability or compensation - it does not; that is CLC, FUND and Bunkers
- Whether the SOPEP requirement comes from OPRC or MARPOL - OPRC obliges the Party to require it; MARPOL Annex I regulation 37 imposes it on the ship
- Whether a Member State question includes the article 6 national system - it does, and that is the difference between this question and a flag State question
- Whether 'Member State' and 'Party' are the same thing - they are not; a State is a Member of IMO and a Party to OPRC, and the sets are not identical
- Whether the thresholds are the same for tankers and other ships - 150 GT for oil tankers, 400 GT for other ships
- Whether the four SOPEP contents are exhaustive - regulation 37.2 says the plan shall consist AT LEAST of them
- Whether a combined plan is still a SOPEP - combined with the Annex II plan its title becomes 'Shipboard marine pollution emergency plan'
- Whether the Member State document list is printed in the Convention - it is not; it is derived from what articles 3, 5, 6, 7, 10 and 12 require a Party to establish and communicate

**Target answer shape.** Three printed limb headings. Under (a), a short identity-and-objectives section, a seven-row article table, and a significance section that states the prevention boundary and then gives three defensible senses in which OPRC is preventive. Under (b), a four-row capacity table separating the flag, coastal, international and domestic roles with a note that Member State framing puts article 6 in scope, then nine numbered challenges each carrying its reason, closing on one sentence identifying what they have in common. Under (c), two lists - what a Member State maintains, stated as derived rather than printed, and what the ship carries built on regulation 37 - and a closing paragraph on OPRC binding States while regulation 37 binds the ship.

**Sources consulted.**

- PRIMARY: MARPOL Annex I regulation 37.1, from the held official text of resolution MEPC.117(52) - every oil tanker of 150 gross tonnage and above and every ship other than an oil tanker of 400 gross tonnage and above shall carry on board a shipboard oil pollution emergency plan approved by the Administration.
- PRIMARY: MARPOL Annex I regulation 37.2 - the plan shall be prepared based on guidelines developed by the Organization and written in the working language of the master and officers, and shall consist AT LEAST of four items.
- PRIMARY: MARPOL Annex I regulation 37.2.1 to 37.2.4 - the reporting procedure required by article 8 and Protocol I; the list of authorities or persons to be contacted; the detailed description of immediate action by persons on board; and the procedures and point of contact for co-ordinating shipboard action with national and local authorities.
- PRIMARY: MARPOL Annex I regulation 37.3 - where Annex II regulation 17 also applies the plan may be combined, and the combined plan is titled 'Shipboard marine pollution emergency plan'.
- PRIMARY: MARPOL Annex I regulation 37.4 - all oil tankers of 5,000 tons deadweight or more shall have prompt access to computerized, shore-based damage stability and residual structural strength calculation programs.
- PRIMARY (identity and status): the corpus amendment register records only two MARPOL Annex I amendments beyond the held base - MEPC.359(79), in force 1 May 2024, and MEPC.384(81), an MEPC 81 output of 2024. BOTH ARE FUTURE AT THIS SITTING and neither touches regulation 37.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: the International Convention on Oil Pollution Preparedness, Response and Co-operation was adopted on 30 November 1990 and entered into force on 13 May 1995, following the Exxon Valdez grounding of 1989 which exposed the absence of any international framework for co-ordinated response.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: OPRC article 3 requires each Party to require ships flying its flag to have on board an oil pollution emergency plan as required by and in accordance with provisions adopted by the Organization, with parallel requirements for offshore units, sea ports and oil handling facilities under its jurisdiction.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: OPRC article 4 requires reporting without delay of any event involving a discharge or probable discharge of oil, to the nearest coastal State; article 5 governs assessment and notification of States whose interests are affected, together with the Organization.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: OPRC article 6 requires each Party to establish a national system for responding promptly and effectively, including a national contingency plan and a designated competent national authority and operational contact point, and within its capabilities a minimum level of pre-positioned response equipment, a programme of exercises and training, and detailed plans and communication capabilities.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: OPRC articles 7 to 10 provide for co-operation in response on request, research and development, technical co-operation with developing countries, and the promotion of bilateral and multilateral agreements; article 12 places the institutional and information-clearing role with IMO.
- AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: the OPRC-HNS Protocol, 2000 extends the same preparedness, response and co-operation architecture to hazardous and noxious substances.
- CONTEXT: UNCLOS article 199 obliges States to develop and promote contingency plans for responding to pollution incidents; article 194 is the general obligation on which the response architecture rests.
- ENGINEERING AND POLICY JUDGEMENT: the implementation-challenges analysis in limb b) - resource asymmetry, the gap between a plan on paper and a rehearsed capability, equipment obsolescence and stockpile geography, customs and immigration friction on cross-border assistance, inter-agency co-ordination, funding and cost recovery, training and turnover, and the difficulty of exercising at realistic scale.
