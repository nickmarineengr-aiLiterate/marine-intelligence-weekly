**Technical ambiguities identified before drafting:**

- Whether 'the main function' in the singular means the examiner wants ONE function named. It cannot sensibly mean that a bill has only one function, and an answer that names one and stops will not survive the word 'in detail' in the same sentence. The reading taken is that the candidate must give the three classical functions in detail AND then answer the singular question honestly by saying which is principal and why. The answer states which reading it takes and defends it: the receipt function is the historical origin and the evidential function is the everyday one, but the DOCUMENT OF TITLE function is what makes the instrument commercially unique, and it is the only one the sea waybill does not share.
- Whether 'made out to order' means to the order of the shipper or to the order of a named consignee. It covers both, and the distinction matters commercially: 'to order' simpliciter or 'to order of shipper' leaves control with the seller until he endorses, while 'to order of X' passes the document into X's hands but still requires X's endorsement to go further. Both are explained, because the reasons for choosing each differ.
- Whether a straight bill is a document of title at all. The answer treats it as a bill that is NOT NEGOTIABLE - it cannot be transferred by endorsement to give a transferee the right to demand delivery - which is precisely why it does not serve the financing purpose. Whether the carrier may deliver to the named consignee WITHOUT production of the straight bill is a genuinely contested question in practice and is NOT resolved here; the answer records that a prudent carrier requires production in any event, and states the point as a practical rule rather than a legal one.
- Whether limb (b) is asking a trick question. It is not. The proposition it recites - that the bill is evidence of the contract - is correct and is the general rule, and the question is asking for the exceptions to it. The answer therefore explains the general rule first, and only then the circumstances that displace it. Treating the printed proposition as a false statement to be attacked is the wrong reading.
- Whether the charterparty bill belongs in limb (b). It does, and it is the strongest single illustration available: in the charterer's own hands the bill operates as a receipt only, because the contract of carriage between owner and charterer is the charterparty; in the hands of an indorsee it constitutes the contract between the owner and that holder. The same document, two legal characters, decided by whose hands it is in.
- Whether the printed 'a Bill of lading' with a lower-case l is a different term from 'Bill of Lading' used twice in the same limb. It is not; it is a typographic inconsistency, it is preserved in text_verbatim, and it is recorded at anchor section 1.4.

## 7. ANSWER CONSTRUCTION

**Answer route** - the principal headings of the model answer ARE this route:

1. **Limb (a), first demand - the functions of a bill of lading, and which is the main one** *(limb (a))*
   - Receipt for the goods - quantity, marks, and apparent order and condition
   - Evidence of the contract of carriage - not, normally, the contract itself
   - Document of title - transferable, and the key to delivery of the goods
   - The main function is the document of title function: it is the only one a sea waybill does not share
2. **Limb (a), second demand - what an order bill is, and how it operates** *(limb (a))*
   - Consigned 'to order', or 'to order of' the shipper or a named party - never to a named consignee simply
   - Transferred by endorsement and delivery; a bearer bill by delivery alone
   - The carrier delivers against presentation of one original, whereupon the others stand void
3. **Limb (a), second demand - the reasons for making a bill out 'to order'** *(limb (a))*
   - To sell the cargo afloat - string sales, and a buyer who is not known at the time of shipment
   - To finance the shipment - the bank takes the bill as security; a straight bill gives it none
   - To retain the right of disposal until payment - the seller endorses only when paid
   - To keep delivery under control - no delivery without an original
   - Costs: mis-delivery exposure, letters of indemnity, fraud, and the stale bill
4. **Limb (b) - why the bill is normally only EVIDENCE of the contract** *(limb (b))*
   - The contract is concluded before the bill is issued - at booking, on the fixture, or on shipment
   - The bill is signed by one party only and is issued after the bargain
   - So between carrier and shipper it is strong but not conclusive evidence of terms already agreed
5. **Limb (b) - the circumstances in which the bill BECOMES the actual contract** *(limb (b))*
   - THE PRINCIPAL CASE - once transferred to a consignee or endorsee. The third party was not party to the antecedent bargain and can look only at the document, so the bill's terms ARE the contract between him and the carrier
   - The charterparty bill in a third party's hands - a mere receipt for the charterer, the contract for an indorsee
   - Where there was no antecedent agreement at all - the bill issued on shipment is itself the contract
   - Where the parties so agree - a booking note providing that the carrier's bill terms shall govern
   - By statute - the Indian Bills of Lading Act 1856 vests the rights of suit in the transferee as if the contract had been made with him
6. **The statutory frame operative at this sitting** *(limb framing)*
