## 9. ANSWER ROUTE AS BUILT

Archetype `legal`. The principal headings of the model answer ARE this route - `validate_spec.py` enforces the correspondence, so the candidate cannot be taught one order and shown another.

**1. Two doctrines, two questions**

- Salvage: what does the outsider who saved the property get paid?
- General average: how do the owners of the saved property share the cost between themselves?
- Reward to a stranger against apportionment among the adventure
- Convention and law against contract

**2. Limb A - the principles of modern salvage law** - limb **A**

- Salvage Convention 1989, in force 14 July 1996, replacing the Brussels Convention 1910
- Voluntariness - no pre-existing duty; a tug under a towage contract or a crew member is not a salvor
- Real danger - need not be immediate, but must be real not fanciful
- Maritime property - life salvage attracts no reward of itself, but a life salvor shares in the property reward
- Article 12 - no cure, no pay: a useful result gives a right to a reward, no result gives nothing
- Article 13 - ten criteria (a) to (j): salved value, environmental skill and efforts, measure of success, danger, skill in salving, time and expenses, risk of liability, promptness, availability of equipment, readiness and value of equipment. Capped at the salved value
- Article 14 - special compensation where the environment was threatened: expenses, plus up to 30 per cent, and up to 100 per cent at a tribunal's discretion
- Article 8 - duties both ways: salvor's due care and environmental care; owner and master to co-operate and accept redelivery
- Freedom of contract, subject to annulment for undue influence or inequitable terms
- In practice: Lloyd's Open Form, and SCOPIC as the tariff-based contractual alternative to arguing Article 14

**3. Limb B - what general average is** - limb **B**

- Sacrifice or expenditure made intentionally and reasonably for the common safety, shared rateably by all interests saved
- Why it exists: it stops the master hesitating, and stops one shipper bearing a loss taken for everyone
- Rule A - five elements: extraordinary, intentional, reasonable, for the common safety, and property preserved
- Give the failure case for each - washed overboard is not intentional; jettisoned is
- Examples: jettison, water damage fighting a fire, deliberate beaching, engaging salvors, machinery damage refloating, port of refuge expenses
- The Rules are NOT law - CMI rules incorporated by the bill of lading or charterparty; the edition depends on the contract
- In India, recognised by section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act, 1963

**4. Limb C(i) - Entitlement** - limb **C(i)**

- The party who sacrificed or spent is entitled to a contribution from the interests saved
- Contributors: ship, cargo and freight at risk, on contributory values at the termination of the adventure
- The sacrificing party contributes too - it recovers all but its own share
- The loss must be the direct consequence; demurrage and market loss are excluded
- Actionable fault defeats the claim - the party whose fault caused the peril cannot recover from those who could sue him
- Security: lien on cargo, discharged by an average bond from the receiver plus an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter, or a cash deposit

**5. Limb C(ii) - Artificial general average** - limb **C(ii)**

- Say first that it is practitioner terminology, NOT defined in the Rules
- It describes allowances the numbered Rules permit on a common-benefit basis although they fail Rule A's strict common-safety test
- Standard examples all arise at a port of refuge - the peril has passed, the adventure is interrupted not endangered
- Discharging, storing and reloading cargo to permit repairs; port charges; crew wages and maintenance during detention; temporary repairs; substituted expenses
- The practical point: this is where most of the money usually is - the jettison is cheap, four months in a port of refuge is not
- And where the edition of the Rules matters most, because editions have moved these allowances in and out

**6. Limb C(iii) - Adjustment** - limb **C(iii)**

- The average adjuster - appointed by the shipowner but acting impartially between all interests
- He does NOT declare general average - the shipowner does
- He does NOT decide liability - fault and contract are for courts or arbitrators
- He determines allowances under the applicable edition and their valuation
- He ascertains contributory values at the termination of the adventure
- He calculates the rate and apportions, collects bonds, guarantees and deposits
- He issues the general average statement - allowances, values, apportionment, balances
- It is slow: values cannot be fixed until cargo is delivered and its arrived value known

**7. Limb C(iv) - Contestation** - limb **C(iv)**

- The statement is evidence, not a judgment - the adjuster has no judicial authority
- Ground 1 - no general average act: not extraordinary, intentional, reasonable, or for the common safety
- Ground 2 - the allowance is not permitted, or the wrong edition applied
- Ground 3 - values or apportionment wrong - misstated contributory values, wrong basis, arithmetic
- Ground 4 - actionable fault: the peril was the claimant's own fault. Can defeat the claim entirely
- Ground 5 - time bar, or security given under protest whose terms are not met
- Resolution: negotiation with underwriters first, then arbitration or litigation per the contract
- Cargo underwriters, not receivers, are usually the real contestants

**8. Where the two doctrines meet**

- The salvage award is itself a general average expenditure - extraordinary, intentional, reasonable, for the common safety
- So one casualty produces a salvage arbitration and a GA adjustment in parallel
- And the P&I club and the cargo underwriters provide security in both - link to Q7
- Indian anchor: section 66 Marine Insurance Act, 1963; domestic statute the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958
