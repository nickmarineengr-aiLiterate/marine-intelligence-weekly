**Technical ambiguities identified before drafting:**

- 'Optimization of ship dimensions' is the stem's frame, but limb (a) is about SIZE AND CAPACITY and limb (b) is about SPEED, neither of which is a dimension. The stem is really about the principal design parameters, of which dimensions are one. The answer takes the three limbs as printed and does not quarrel with the framing, but it does say in the preamble that speed and capacity are chosen alongside dimensions rather than being dimensions themselves - which is what makes section 14 on the order of decisions possible.
- 'Ships size and capacity' printed as one limb conflates two related but separable things: the physical size of the hull and the cargo capacity it provides. A ship can be large and carry little - a car carrier is voluminous and light; a small tanker is dense and deep. The answer separates volume-limited from weight-limited trades where it matters, because the economy of scale behaves differently in each.
- 'Service speed' could be read as the operational speed on a given voyage or as the design service speed. The impact on energy efficiency differs: the first is an operational decision, the second is a design decision that fixes installed power for the ship's life. The answer takes it primarily as the DESIGN service speed, since the stem is about optimisation of the ship, and deals with operational speed reduction as the consequence.
- The cubic power-speed relationship is an approximation. Its exponent varies with hull form, loading, Froude number and the onset of significant wave-making, and it is not reliable at very low load. It is presented as an approximation with those qualifications, and is not used to compute a numerical saving.

**Answer route:**

1. **The unit the question is really about** _intro_ - The unit is energy per unit of transport work - per tonne-mile - not per hour; This is why a bigger, slower ship can be efficient while being a large consumer; EEDI and CII both measure in this form
2. **Why dimensions are the largest lever, and when it can be pulled** _intro_ - They fix resistance, installed power and transport work for the whole life of the ship; They can only be chosen once, at design; everything later is mitigation; Hence the regulatory focus on new-ship design
3. **Where the economy of scale comes from** _(a)_ - Displacement grows with volume, wetted surface with area; Machinery, hotel and auxiliary loads do not scale with cargo; Crew, stores and overheads are near-constant per ship
4. **The limits, and the condition the whole saving depends on** _(a)_ - Canal, port, berth, draught and terminal constraints; Cargo availability, utilisation and ballast legs; A large ship part-loaded is worse per tonne-mile than a smaller full one
5. **The regulatory consequence of scale** _(a)_ - EEDI's capacity term rewards larger ships on the same reference line; Larger ships also carry more absolute emissions, which is what the CII rating exposes; Scale improves the index; it does not by itself reduce total emissions
6. **The power-speed relationship and what follows from it** _(b)_ - Resistance rises steeply with speed and power rises faster still; Approximately with the cube of speed over the normal range; Fuel per mile therefore rises approximately with the square of speed
7. **What the design service speed fixes permanently** _(b)_ - Installed power, engine selection, propeller design and the hull lines; The bulbous bow and lines are tuned to a design speed and draught; Off-design operation carries a penalty the ship cannot design away
8. **The operational and commercial trade-off** _(b)_ - Slower means less fuel per mile but longer voyages and more ships for the same annual capacity; Cargo value, freight rate and fuel price set the economic speed; Slow steaming has real machinery consequences
9. **The resistance components the dimensions act on** _(c)_ - Frictional resistance follows wetted surface; Residuary resistance follows form and speed-length ratio; Air and appendage resistance are smaller but not negligible
10. **Length** _(c)_ - Greater length for a given displacement lowers the speed-length ratio and wave-making; But increases wetted surface, structural weight and cost; Longer parallel middle body is cheap to build and carries cargo well
11. **Beam, draught and fullness** _(c)_ - Beam gives stability and deck area but adds wave-making and demands power; Draught is limited by ports and by the load line; deeper is generally more efficient; Fullness carries cargo but adds residuary resistance and complicates the wake
12. **The afterbody, the aperture and the propeller** _(c)_ - Afterbody form governs wake quality and hull efficiency; The aperture must allow the largest, slowest-turning propeller that will fit and stay immersed; Rudder, bilge keels, bossings and openings all cost something
13. **What stops optimisation being a free gain** _all_ - Stability, subdivision and damage stability; strength and structural weight; Seakeeping, added resistance in waves, and manoeuvrability; Tonnage measurement, port dues and canal tolls; building cost and yard capability
14. **How the three interact, and the order in which they are decided** _all_ - Speed is decided first because it sizes the powering problem; Capacity is decided by the trade and the route constraints; Dimensions are then optimised within those two, which is why the order matters
