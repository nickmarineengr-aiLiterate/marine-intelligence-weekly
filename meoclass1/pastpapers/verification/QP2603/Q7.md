## Printed question, as transcribed

> You as Chief Engineer are required to inform your Company with respect to inspection of propellers in dry dock during which you noticed a surface crack on one of the blades. State the steps taken by you as Chief Engineer for successful handling of the situation.

*Marks note:* Set as a single undivided question with no lettered limbs and no printed marks. The whole question carries the paper's equal-marks value of 16.

## Findings

**This is a procedural answer and its provenance is honestly weighted towards
industry practice and engineering judgement rather than primary instruments.** The
provenance summary records that: P1 is low, P3 and ENGINEERING_JUDGEMENT are high. That is
the correct shape for a "state the steps you would take" question and is not a defect, but
it is stated rather than disguised.

**The one hard technical fact is load-bearing and is settled.** Marine propellers are cast
in **non-ferromagnetic** alloys - nickel-aluminium bronze and manganese bronze - so
**magnetic particle inspection cannot be used**. Liquid dye penetrant is the standard
surface method, with eddy current or ultrasonic where depth must be established. A
candidate who proposes MPI has revealed they have not done this job.

**Two outcomes are left deliberately open.** The question supplies **no cause** for the
crack. Whether the occurrence is a reportable casualty to the Administration turns on cause
and on whether seaworthiness was affected; whether a hull and machinery claim lies turns on
cause and on the policy wording. Both are therefore stated as **conditional tests** rather
than answered. Asserting either would be inventing facts the examiner did not give.

**Repair zoning is described in principle, not quoted.** MIW holds no licensed copy of any
society's rules or of ISO 484. The principle that the highly stressed region of the blade
is the most restricted for repair and the tip region the least is common across societies;
the zone boundaries and the extent of welding permitted in each are not, and are not
reproduced.

**Merchant Shipping Act 2025 check - CLEAR, and the answer was drafted to stay clear.** The
flag-State reporting limb is expressed as a conditional test tied to cause and
seaworthiness, and is **not** tied to a named national instrument. That keeps it correct
either side of 15 March 2026 without the sitting date having to be established.

## Verification status

The class relationship and the owner's duty to report damage affecting classification taken from the verified QP2607 Q3 treatment of the RO Code and classification; the non-applicability of magnetic particle inspection to non-ferromagnetic propeller alloys is settled materials engineering; ISO 484 identified as the propeller manufacturing tolerance standard, with repair zoning left to class rules rather than asserted in detail

## Sources actually used

- P2 AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: classification-society rules and guidance on propeller damage, repair and approval - the owner's duty to report to the society any damage or defect which affects or may affect classification; attendance and assessment by the surveyor; repair only by an approved repairer to an approved procedure; and re-examination, geometry check and balancing after repair.
- P2 AUTHORITATIVE SECONDARY: ISO 484 (parts 1 and 2) as the standard governing shipbuilding propeller manufacturing tolerances and blade zoning by radius, against which repair acceptance is judged. The detailed zone boundaries and the extent of welding permitted in each are set by the individual society's rules and are not reproduced here.
- P3 INDUSTRY GUIDANCE: materials and NDT practice - marine propellers are cast in non-ferromagnetic alloys, principally nickel-aluminium bronze and manganese bronze, so MAGNETIC PARTICLE INSPECTION IS NOT APPLICABLE; liquid dye penetrant testing is the standard surface method, with eddy current or ultrasonic techniques used where depth must be established.
- P3 INDUSTRY GUIDANCE: propeller failure mechanisms - cavitation erosion, fatigue initiating at cavitation pitting or at a casting defect acting as a stress raiser, impact with submerged objects or ice, dezincification of manganese bronze, and galvanic attack where the impressed current system or anodes are defective.
- INTERNAL REUSE VERIFIED: QP2607 Q2 for the incident reporting and evidence discipline; QP2607 Q3 for the role of the classification society and the recognized organization; QP2601 Q8 for the casualty reporting threshold.
- ENGINEERING JUDGEMENT: the ordering of the six steps, and the emphasis on not dressing the crack before assessment, reflect standard superintendency practice rather than any single published procedure.

## Open / unresolved

- MIW holds no licensed copy of any classification society's rules or of ISO 484. Repair acceptance criteria, blade zone boundaries and the extent of welding permitted in each zone differ between societies and are deliberately described in principle rather than quoted.
- Whether a propeller blade crack found in dry dock is a reportable casualty to the Administration depends on its cause and on whether seaworthiness was affected. The answer states the test rather than asserting a single answer, because the question gives no cause.
- Whether a hull and machinery claim lies depends on the policy wording and on the cause established. The answer sets out the distinction between an insured peril and ordinary wear or cavitation erosion without asserting an outcome.
