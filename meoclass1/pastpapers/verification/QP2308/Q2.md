## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE

**Question intent.** Five questions in one paragraph, moving from a definition to a proprietary characteristic to two procedural terms to an instrument and finally to a ranked list. The examiner is testing whether the candidate understands that shipping law solves the assetless-debtor problem by making the ship itself the defendant, and whether he can then produce the priority order accurately. The discriminator is the last sentence: most candidates can define a lien and most can say what in rem means, and comparatively few can give the order of settlement correctly with the costs of arrest at the top of it.

**Mark allocation.** Sixteen marks with no printed split on this copy, and none on the April 2023 sitting of the same stem either; the April 2024, September 2025 and December 2025 sittings print (16) on the stem alone, so no limb split exists anywhere. The five questions are not equal: the definition and the travelling characteristic carry the conceptual marks, the two Latin terms are short and definitional, and the convention plus the order of settlement carry the factual marks that separate candidates. The answer allots sections 1 and 2 to the first two, one short section each to in rem and in personam, and four sections to the convention, the claims, the order and the Indian statute.

**Examiner expectation.** That the lien is described as attaching to the ship and not to a person. That its secrecy and its survival of a change of ownership are stated, because they are what 'travels with property' means. That in rem and in personam are distinguished with the practical reason for bringing both. That the 1993 Convention is named with its dates. That the five claims are given in the RIGHT order with wages first. That the costs of arrest and sale are placed ahead of the liens. And that India's non-party status is stated rather than glossed over.

**Jurisdiction risk.** HIGH, and it is the real risk on this question rather than currency. An answer that presents the Convention as binding India, or that treats every arrestable claim as a lien, has the orders confused.

## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: **legal**. The principal headings of the model answer ARE these steps.

1. **What a maritime lien is**
   - A privileged claim against the vessel herself, arising by operation of law
   - Feature one - it attaches to the res, not to a person; the ship is the debtor
   - Feature two - it arises the moment the claim arises, unregistered and undiscoverable: a SECRET lien
   - Feature three - it is inchoate until enforced, then crystallised by arrest and forced sale
   - Feature four - it ranks ahead of registered mortgages
   - The justification: the claimants protected are those who could not realistically pursue the owner
2. **How the lien travels with the property**
   - It continues notwithstanding change of ownership, registration or flag
   - It binds a purchaser in good faith without notice, because it is secret
   - Extinguished after ONE year, but TWO years for crew claims
   - Time runs CONTINUOUSLY without suspension, excluding any period under arrest or seizure
   - From discharge for wages; from accrual of the claim for the rest
   - Defeated only by a forced sale, which passes clean title and moves the liens to the proceeds
3. **In rem - the action against the ship**
   - The vessel is named as defendant, served physically, and may be arrested
   - Every maritime lien gives a right in rem, but not every right in rem is a maritime lien
   - The statutory right in rem covers many more claims but does not survive a change of ownership and does not outrank a mortgage
   - Arrest exists to compel SECURITY - a club letter, bank guarantee or payment into court
   - If no security is given, forced sale produces the fund
4. **In personam - the action against the person**
   - The ordinary action against owner, demise charterer, manager or operator
   - Worthless against a single-ship company with no other asset
   - The action in rem reaches a physical asset that must eventually enter a port
   - The two are commonly pursued together - in rem to secure, in personam to establish liability
   - In India both are exercised by the High Courts, and the statute keeps them in separate provisions
5. **Which convention deals with maritime lien**
   - The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993
   - Adopted at Geneva 6 May 1993; in force 5 September 2004
   - Successor to the Brussels Conventions of 1926 and 1967, neither widely accepted
   - Its solution is a short closed list of five, with national liens ranking after the five AND after mortgages
   - INDIA IS NOT A PARTY - the Admiralty Act, 2017, in force 1 April 2018, gives effect to the scheme
6. **The five secured claims, and how they rank among themselves**
   - (a) crew wages, repatriation costs and social insurance contributions
   - (b) loss of life or personal injury in direct connection with the operation of the vessel
   - (c) salvage reward including special compensation
   - (d) port, canal and other waterway dues and pilotage dues
   - (e) tort for loss or damage by the operation of the vessel, EXCLUDING cargo and containers
   - Liens outrank registered mortgages; salvage jumps liens attached before it and salvages rank in inverse order of time; within a category, equal and rateable
   - Excluded altogether: oil and HNS damage compensable elsewhere, and nuclear
7. **The order of settlement after legal action**
   - First, the costs and expenses of arrest, seizure and sale, and the upkeep of vessel and crew
   - Then wreck removal costs where national law gives them that priority - optional, not automatic
   - Then the five maritime liens in their order, with the salvage rules applied
   - Then a shipbuilder's or shiprepairer's right of retention where national law recognises one
   - Then registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges
   - Then all other claims, including national liens and ordinary maritime claims
   - Residue to the owner; the purchaser takes free of everything
8. **The Indian position, stated separately**
   - The Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, in force 1 April 2018
   - Section 9(1) lists the same five liens in the same order - wages first
   - Section 9(2) - travels with the vessel; one year, two years for crew claims
   - Section 9(3) - commences on discharge or on accrual, and runs continuously excluding time under arrest
   - Section 10 - liens, then registered mortgages, then all other claims; equal within a category; salvages in inverse order of time
   - Section 8 - a court sale vests the vessel in the purchaser free of everything
   - Arrest in rem and jurisdiction in personam are separate provisions, both exercised by the High Courts
