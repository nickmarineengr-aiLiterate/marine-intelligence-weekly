## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE

**Question intent.** A scenario question set on the candidate's own ship, printed as one paragraph with three embedded tasks and no limb labels. The examiner is testing whether a Chief Engineer can turn an instrumentation system into a management regime: select from hundreds of points, design a document that could actually be issued, and present and interpret the result. The discriminator is the chart - most candidates can discuss condition monitoring and comparatively few produce a tabulated chart with reference conditions, baselines and separate alert and alarm columns. The second discriminator is whether the answer is UMS-specific at all, or a generic condition monitoring essay that would read the same on a manned ship.

**Mark allocation.** Sixteen marks with no printed split and no limb labels, so the split is adjudicated rather than read. The stem sets three tasks of visibly different size - 'explain the special attention you will pay to the specific data' is the largest, 'formulate a chart' is a single deliverable, and 'highlight the tools in computer application' covers both graphical representation and trend analysis. The answer allots sections 2 to 4 to the data and the disciplines, one section to the chart, and sections 6 and 7 to the tools, with a management close. The absence of any printed mark is the printed copy's and is not repaired.

**Examiner expectation.** That the answer is specific to an unattended machinery space and says what has been given up as well as gained. That the data is SELECTED with reasons rather than listed. That exhaust deviation, differential pressures and wear-metal rates appear, because they are the parameters an experienced engineer reaches for. That an actual chart is drawn, with reference condition and baseline in it. That tools are given as graphical forms answering questions rather than as software names. And that the answer ends in a work order and a review, because data that changes no decision is worth nothing.

**Freshness risk.** LOW - and the reason is that the question has no donor and no date. SOLAS chapter II-1 part E and the ISM maintenance element are stable across the whole corpus. **Jurisdiction risk.** LOW to MEDIUM. An answer that attributes the UMS arrangements to ISM, or that treats a planned maintenance scheme as automatically accepted, has the levels wrong.

**Technical ambiguities.** 'Formulate a chart' is genuinely ambiguous - it could mean a graph or a tabulated schedule. ## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: **procedure**. The principal headings of the model answer ARE these steps.

1. **The position you are in, and what the question is asking**
   - More measurements than any engineer has had, and fewer people watching the machinery
   - Three tasks: which data deserves special attention, a chart to formulate, and the computer tools
   - All three are markable - an answer without the chart has abandoned a third of the marks
2. **What a UMS ship gives you, and what it takes away**
   - It takes away the watchkeeper's senses - the knock, the smell, the weep, the change in note
   - It gives a continuous, time-stamped, objective record free of transcription error
   - The value of the record has to be actively extracted; the job is to interrogate the log, not read the printout
   - An alarm is a FAILURE of monitoring, not a success
   - The unattended-space arrangements under SOLAS II-1 part E are themselves equipment whose availability must be monitored
3. **The specific data that deserves special attention**
   - Group A, slow condition data - exhaust deviation per unit, Pmax and Pcomp, turbocharger and cooler DP, normalised shaft power, oil condition and wear metals, liner wear, bearing temperatures, vibration, filter differentials, boiler chemistry, A/E load sharing
   - The diagnostic quantity for cylinders is the DEVIATION from the mean of all units, not the absolute temperature
   - Group B, fast safety data - oil mist detector, scavenge temperature, bilge levels, fire detection, steering gear, safety system and overrides, electrical availability, starting air, engineers' and dead-man alarms
   - Group C, system health - inhibited and faulty channels, alarm frequency and repeats, calibration status, data gaps
   - Group C is the group almost nobody mentions and the one that matters most on a UMS ship
4. **Telling a trend from noise**
   - Fix the operating condition, or normalise to a reference condition before plotting
   - Fix the method - same instrument, same point, same mounting, same procedure
   - Establish the ship's OWN baseline after commissioning or overhaul, not the shop-test figure
   - Distinguish three limits: baseline, alert from the parameter's own scatter, alarm from the maker
5. **Formulating the condition monitoring chart**
   - One row per monitored parameter, carrying ten fields, each answering a question somebody will ask
   - Parameter, point and method, frequency, reference condition, baseline, alert, alarm, trend rule, action, record
   - Reference condition and baseline are what make a reading interpretable
   - Alert and alarm are separate columns - one creates work, the other stops the engine
   - A trend rule makes acting on a trend a defined event and not an opinion
   - Action and record connect the chart to the PMS and to the ISM maintenance element
6. **The computer tools for graphical representation**
   - Answer by what graphical form answers what question, not by product name
   - Time-series line chart with baseline, alert and alarm as reference lines - the workhorse
   - Trend line and moving average, and the slope extrapolated to give time to intervention
   - Multi-series comparison of like units; scatter with regression for normalisation
   - Control chart, which flags a RUN of points a limit-based alarm cannot detect
   - Radar for a machine profile; bar and Pareto for alarm counts; histogram for widening scatter
   - Vibration spectrum and waterfall - the only presentation that says WHICH component
   - Conditional formatting and exception reporting; automate the export; pivot tables do the reduction
7. **Trend analysis proper**
   - Normalise first - the omitted step that invalidates everything after it
   - Smooth with a moving average whose window matches the deterioration rate
   - Slope and rate of change - the RATE matters more than the value
   - Extrapolate to the limit for remaining useful life, and state the assumption it rests on
   - Correlate across parameters - it is also how a sensor fault is told from a plant fault
   - Compare against own history, sister ship and maker's expectation
   - Never move the baseline to accommodate deterioration; never raise a setpoint to silence an alarm
8. **What you do with what the trend tells you**
   - Convert the alert into a planned job with spare, port and manpower identified
   - A minuted weekly engineers' review of the exception list and alarm history
   - A monthly report in a fixed format so ships and superintendents can compare
   - Feed back what was actually found on opening up, or the regime becomes superstition
   - Recorded data can support a planned maintenance scheme in place of an opened-up survey
   - Maintain the instrumentation as machinery - a neglected sensor is believed
   - Keep the manual competence alive as fallback and as check

**Memory cue.** POSITION - TRADE-OFF - DATA - DISCIPLINE - CHART - GRAPHS - TREND - ACTION (1 to 8). Inside step 3 hold SLOW CONDITION / FAST SAFETY / SYSTEM HEALTH. Inside step 4 hold CONDITION - METHOD - BASELINE - LIMITS. Inside step 7 hold NORMALISE - SMOOTH - SLOPE - EXTRAPOLATE - CORRELATE - COMPARE.
