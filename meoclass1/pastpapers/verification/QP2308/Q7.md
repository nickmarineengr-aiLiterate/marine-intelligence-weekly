## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE

**Question intent.** Three tasks in three printed sentences, moving from recall to explanation to evaluation. The first is standard and every candidate has it. The second requires dates and is where most answers become vague - 'it takes a long time' is not an answer to a question that says 'with examples'. The third is evaluative and is the discriminator: the entry-into-force family is solved five times in the corpus and NOT ONE of those questions asks what could be done about the delay, so no prepared answer exists for it and the candidate must actually think. The examiner is testing whether he understands that the delay is a consequence of consent-based law rather than of bureaucracy.

**Mark allocation.** Sixteen marks with no printed split. The three tasks are visibly unequal in difficulty though not in length of stem: the four stages are recall, the delay with examples requires evidence, and the remedies limb requires analysis. The answer allots five sections to the stages - one naming them and four explaining them, since they are the framework everything else hangs on - one to causes, one to worked examples and one to remedies. Because there is no printed split the proportions are adjudicated, and the deliberate choice is to give the last two sections about a third of the answer between them, since that is where marks are won and lost.

**Examiner expectation.** That exactly four stages are given, because the stem says four. That adoption is correctly described as creating no obligation. That the four modes of consent are distinguished and the deposit rule stated. That the entry-into-force conditions are given with BOTH elements and the reason for each. That examples carry dates. That the Hong Kong Convention is correctly placed as not yet in force. That tacit acceptance appears in the remedies. And that the answer recognises the delay as structural rather than as inefficiency.

**Freshness risk.** HIGH - and it is concentrated entirely in the examples. Every donor in this family sits at least twenty-two months later and uses the Hong Kong Convention as a completed example, in force 26 June 2025. At this sitting it is not in force, and its conditions had been satisfied only two months earlier, which makes it the live example rather than the finished one. No treaty status figure is asserted, which removes the remaining currency risk.

**Jurisdiction risk.** MEDIUM. Three levels must be kept apart: the international treaty, which binds only States that have consented; the national legislation by which a State gives it domestic effect and without which no ship can actually be prosecuted; and regional measures such as EU legislation, which are not IMO law at all and which the answer identifies as commercial pressure rather than as part of the treaty mechanism. An answer that says IMO 'passes' a regulation, or that treats a regional measure as international law, has the levels wrong.

**Technical ambiguities.** 'The four main stages' is not a term of art and is divided differently in different sources - some texts give proposal, adoption, ratification and entry into force; others fold proposal and drafting together and add implementation as a fourth. The answer takes the first division, gives exactly four as the stem requires, and names implementation expressly as what follows, so the choice is visible rather than asserted. 'Coming into force date' is read as entry into force generally, not as the date a particular State becomes bound, which is a separate and later date for a State ratifying after entry into force; the distinction is stated so it is not lost.

## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: **explain**. The principal headings of the model answer ARE these steps.

1. **What an IMO convention is, and the four stages**
   - The Organization has no power to make law - binding force comes from States' consent and their national legislation
   - Stage 1 proposal and development; stage 2 adoption; stage 3 acceptance; stage 4 entry into force
   - Implementation and enforcement FOLLOW the fourth stage and are not a fifth
   - Much of what a State must do to implement has to be done BEFORE it can responsibly ratify
2. **Stage one - proposal and development**
   - A Member State or group proposes to MSC, MEPC, the Legal Committee or the Facilitation Committee
   - Proposals commonly follow a casualty, a scientific finding or a commercial development
   - Technical drafting is done in a sub-committee over several sessions, with consultative organisations contributing
   - Slow legitimately: a text pushed through against unresolved objections is one States will not ratify
3. **Stage two - adoption**
   - A new convention is normally adopted by a diplomatic conference open to all States, not only IMO Members
   - Amendments and codes are adopted by the relevant committee or by the Assembly, by resolution
   - Adoption creates NO obligation - the text exists and nobody is bound
   - Adoption is the date from which the delay is measured
   - Signature at the conference is usually subject to ratification and is not consent to be bound
4. **Stage three - acceptance, and the four ways of giving it**
   - Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval - a two-step route; the gap is where national legislation is done
   - Definitive signature, where permitted - consent in one step; rare for technical conventions
   - Accession - a single act by a State that did not sign, with identical legal effect to ratification
   - In every case the instrument is DEPOSITED with the Secretary-General as depositary
   - It is the date of deposit, not the date of the national law, that counts
   - Ratification is the END of a long domestic process, not a formality
5. **Stage four - entry into force**
   - Every convention contains its own entry-into-force article - that article decides, not the Organization
   - For an important technical convention: a minimum number of Contracting States, commonly 15 to 30
   - Plus a minimum percentage of world merchant shipping by gross tonnage, commonly 50 per cent
   - Occasionally a third subject-specific criterion - the Hong Kong Convention's recycling capacity
   - Then a prescribed period, commonly 12 or 24 months, before it takes effect
   - The numerical condition secures legitimacy; the tonnage condition secures practical effect - and causes the wait
6. **Why the delay happens**
   - STRUCTURAL: demanding thresholds by design; conflicting national interests; the Organization cannot compel
   - NATIONAL: parliamentary time for enabling legislation; administrative capacity to survey, certify and prosecute; legal and linguistic work; political change
   - INDUSTRIAL: the technology may not exist or be affordable at adoption - the BWM case
   - INDUSTRIAL: cost, and the first mover bearing it before his competitors
   - INDUSTRIAL: the free-rider problem - each State gains slightly by going last, which is the economic root
   - INDUSTRIAL: uncertainty about interpretation where guidelines are still being written
7. **The delay, in worked examples**
   - SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, in force 25 May 1980: about five and a half years
   - MARPOL - the 1973 Convention never entered into force alone; absorbed into the 1978 Protocol, Annexes I and II in force 2 October 1983
   - Bunkers 2001 - adopted 23 March 2001, in force 21 November 2008: nearly eight years, with no technology requirement
   - Maritime Liens and Mortgages 1993 - adopted 6 May 1993, in force 5 September 2004: eleven years
   - Nairobi Wreck Removal 2007 - adopted 18 May 2007, in force 14 April 2015: nearly eight years
   - Ballast Water Management 2004 - adopted 13 February 2004, in force 8 September 2017: thirteen years, technology and cost
   - Hong Kong Convention 2009 - adopted 15 May 2009, conditions satisfied June 2023, TWO MONTHS BEFORE THIS SITTING; twenty-four-month clock running; NOT IN FORCE today
   - And some never arrive - the HNS regime and the Cape Town Agreement
   - Typical range five to fifteen years, driven by cost to industry and by how concentrated tonnage is
8. **What can be done to reduce the delay**
   - TACIT ACCEPTANCE - the burden reversed, silence means yes; MARPOL article 16 and the SOLAS equivalent. The single most effective reform
   - Make a standard binding through a convention already in force - the ISM, ISPS, RO and III Codes all arrived this way
   - Amend by protocol where an instrument has stalled - the 1978 Protocol rescued MARPOL; the 1988 Protocols modernised SOLAS and Load Lines
   - Technical co-operation, model national legislation and regional workshops, attacking the national-capacity cause
   - The Member State Audit Scheme and the III Code, raising the capability ratification depends on
   - Publish guidelines WITH the instrument - States will not ratify into interpretive uncertainty
   - Propositions: realistic thresholds at drafting; phasing and grandfathering; encourage early voluntary application; do not adopt requirements depending on undemonstrated technology; targeted diplomacy with major flags; accept regional pressure as part of the mechanism, at the cost of fragmentation

**Memory cue.** STAGES - PROPOSE - ADOPT - ACCEPT - ENTER INTO FORCE - CAUSES - EXAMPLES - REMEDIES (1 to 8). Inside step 6 hold STRUCTURAL / NATIONAL / INDUSTRIAL. Inside step 8 hold TACIT ACCEPTANCE - CODE THROUGH CONVENTION - PROTOCOL - CAPACITY - AUDIT - GUIDELINES WITH THE TEXT.
