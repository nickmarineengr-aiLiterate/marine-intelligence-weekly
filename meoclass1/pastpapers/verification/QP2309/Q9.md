## 2. DECOMPOSITION GATE

**Question intent.** Three named parts of the port State control regime, deliberately chosen and deliberately not the usual ones. The examiner has avoided clear grounds, detention and appeal entirely and asked instead about the edges of the system - the ship whose flag is outside the treaty and the ship that is too small for it. What is really being tested is whether the candidate understands that a convention binds only its Parties and covers only what it says, and that port State control is the mechanism by which that limitation is prevented from destroying the system. A candidate who does not see the free-rider problem answers three unconnected questions.

**Mark allocation.** Sixteen marks printed against the stem with the three parts run inline and no mark against any of them. The three are treated as of comparable weight, with limb (iii) given a little more room because it is the principle the other two implement and because it carries the two-class point that is the most commonly dropped mark. Framing is kept short - two brief sections - so that the three named limbs take the great majority of the answer.

**Examiner expectation.** That a non-Party's certificate is described as lacking treaty standing rather than as being simply rejected, and that the consequence for the inspection is stated. That a below-size ship is recognised as properly certificate-free but still inspectable on condition. That the principle is quoted in the conventions' own language and attributed to at least two instruments. And, above all, that no more favourable treatment is recognised as reaching below-size ships as well as non-Party ships.

**Freshness risk.** MEDIUM - The treaty layer is completely stable - the control articles and the no-more-favourable-treatment provisions are decades old and unchanged - but the consolidated procedural guidance was revised three months after this sitting and revised again two years later, so there are two later editions to avoid and the corpus holds one of them and not the operative one. The mitigation is structural: the answer rests on the treaty provisions, which did not move, and names the operative Procedures without quoting any part of them.

**Jurisdiction risk.** HIGH by the standards of this paper, and it is the subject rather than a hazard. The whole question is about the boundary of treaty obligation: who is bound, who is covered, and what a State may do about a ship that is neither. Three errors must be avoided - describing port State control as replacing flag State responsibility; suggesting that a port State can confer convention status on a non-Party's ship; and suggesting that a port State may require of a below-size ship a document the convention does not require. For an Indian port the power is exercised under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958 at this sitting, with India in the Indian Ocean MOU.

**Technical ambiguities.** 'Provisions are made under Port State Control' is loose - port State control is a practice rather than an instrument, and the provisions are in the conventions and in the consolidated Procedures. The answer reads it as asking what the regime does about each of the three matters and says where each rule lives. 'Below convention size' is not defined and differs by convention, which is why the answer gives several thresholds rather than one. The stem prints 'favorable' in American spelling and inline sub-limbs without marks; both are preserved. Note also that limb (i) and limb (iii) could be read as the same question - they are not, and keeping them apart is part of the answer.

**Target answer shape.** Eight numbered sections following the printed limb order, with two short framing sections first and a synthesis section before the close. Prose for the treaty-standing argument in limb (i), which is a piece of reasoning rather than a list, and bulleted lists everywhere else so that each provision and each consequence is separately countable. The three limbs are drawn together explicitly in section 7 rather than left as three parallel answers.

## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: **legal**. The principal headings of the model answer ARE these steps.

1. **Where the port State's power comes from, and what it is not**
   - Inspection of foreign ships in national ports against the conventions
   - A safety net beneath the flag State - it does not transfer flag State duty and does not certify the ship
   - Each convention carries its own control article - SOLAS I/19, MARPOL 5 and 6, Load Lines 21, STCW X, TONNAGE 12, AFS 11, BWM 9
   - UNCLOS above them - jurisdiction over a vessel voluntarily within the port
   - The consolidated IMO Procedures, and regional memoranda; India in the Indian Ocean MOU under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958
2. **The problem all three limbs exist to solve**
   - A treaty binds only its Parties and applies only to the ships it covers
   - A State could decline to ratify and its ships would trade free of the standards
   - A ship could sit below a size threshold and carry no certificate
   - In both cases the ship is advantaged by not being covered - the free-rider problem
   - The three limbs are the three answers to it
3. **(i) Certificates issued by non-party States to their ships**
   - A convention certificate has treaty standing only because the issuing State is bound by the treaty
   - A non-Party has no authority under the Convention to issue anything under it
   - So the document is not a convention certificate whatever it is headed
   - It is NOT accepted as prima facie evidence of the ship's condition
   - It is not rejected outright either - it may be examined for what it says, but it carries no entitlement
4. **(i) What the officer actually does with such a ship**
   - Proceeds to a more detailed inspection without needing clear grounds
   - Satisfies himself the ship, equipment and manning are substantially equivalent to the convention standard
   - The burden shifts - the ship must show she is up to standard
   - Detention where deficiencies are clearly hazardous, as for any ship
   - The practical effect - fuller inspection, more often, and the paperwork buys nothing
5. **(ii) Inspection of ships below convention size**
   - Thresholds - SOLAS certification from 500 GT, Load Lines from 24 m, MARPOL Annex I from 150 GT for oil tankers and 400 GT otherwise, TONNAGE from 24 m
   - The port State cannot demand a certificate the Convention does not require
   - But it may satisfy itself that the ship does not present a hazard - a condition judgement, not a documentary one
   - The reference standard becomes the flag State's national requirements, regional MoU procedures and the port State's own law
   - No more favourable treatment reaches her - small is a different documentary regime, never a lower safety standard
   - The power to stop an unsafe ship does not depend on which certificate she holds
6. **(iii) No more favourable treatment**
   - Parties shall apply the requirements as may be necessary to ensure no more favourable treatment is given to ships of non-Parties
   - Not one provision but a repeated one - MARPOL 5(4), 1988 SOLAS Protocol I(3), STCW X(5), MLC Article V(7)
   - Reflected in the III Code's port State provisions and in the IMO Procedures
   - It reaches TWO classes - non-Party ships AND below-size ships
   - It is an obligation on the port State, not a right
   - Its purpose is to remove the commercial advantage of staying outside - a ship cannot escape a convention by choosing a flag
7. **How the three interlock**
   - The principle says the standard applies regardless of flag or size
   - Limb (i) is how that is done where the paperwork has no legal standing - discount the paper, inspect the substance
   - Limb (ii) is how it is done where there is no paperwork to have - inspect against national and regional requirements
   - In both, the port State stops relying on documents and looks at the ship
8. **The practical limits, and the position at this sitting**
   - The port State applies the standard; it cannot confer status or issue a convention certificate
   - Jurisdiction is territorial and occasion-based - while the ship is voluntarily in port
   - No more favourable does not mean less favourable - the convention standard, no higher, and no harassment
   - Undue delay or detention carries a right to compensation
   - The procedures in force are A.1155(32) of the 32nd Assembly; the 33rd Assembly revision is three months future
