**Question intent.** A three-limb question moving from definition to function to defect, and the marks rise as it goes. Naming the less obvious fittings - garbage chutes, spurling pipes, air pipe closing devices, drain channel non-return valves - is what marks out a shipboard answer.

## 5. ANSWER ROUTE

Archetype: **explain**. The principal headings of the model answer ARE these steps.

1. **Where the term comes from - what a freeboard assumes**
   - Freeboard measures reserve buoyancy - enclosed intact volume above the waterline
   - The reserve is only real while the enclosed volume stays enclosed
   - So freeboard cannot be assigned on hull form alone
   - It is assigned on the assumption that every opening can be closed and kept tight
2. **A. The definition**
   - The structural and closing-appliance requirements of Annex I chapter II of the Load Line Convention as modified by the 1988 Protocol
   - The chapter is itself headed 'Conditions of assignment of freeboard'
   - Conditions, not recommendations - the freeboard is assigned subject to them
   - Continuing, not a one-off entrance test
   - Ship-specific and recorded in the Form of Record, fitting by fitting
   - The price of the freeboard - less freeboard buys stricter conditions
3. **A. What the conditions consist of**
   - Regulations 10 to 26 of Annex I chapter II
   - Information to the master; superstructure end bulkheads; doors; position of openings; hatchways and covers
   - Machinery space openings; miscellaneous openings; ventilators; air pipes
   - Cargo ports; scuppers, inlets and discharges; garbage chutes; spurling pipes
   - Side scuttles, windows and skylights; freeing ports; protection of the crew; type A ships
   - Regulation 10 is a condition too - approved stability and loading information carried on board with evidence of approval
4. **B. Protecting the openings - position and sill height**
   - Position 1 and position 2 grade the exposure and the protection follows
   - Machinery access door sills 600 mm position 1, 380 mm position 2
   - Companionway sills 600 mm and 380 mm; deckhouse doors 600 mm without access from above, 380 mm with
   - Ventilator coamings 900 mm and 760 mm, specially supported above 900 mm
   - Air pipes 760 mm freeboard deck, 450 mm superstructure deck
   - Side scuttle sills 2.5 per cent of breadth or 500 mm above the summer load line, whichever greater; no windows below the freeboard deck
5. **B. Closures, coamings and the valves that stop back-flooding**
   - Machinery openings efficiently enclosed by steel casings of ample strength
   - Closures permanently attached - a cover that can be left ashore is not a closing appliance
   - Weathertight hatch covers with gaskets and clamping, located against horizontal loads in any sea
   - Air pipes with automatic closing devices; PV valves acceptable on tankers
   - Cargo ports matching the shell for watertightness, opening outwards, minimum in number, 230 mm above the uppermost load line
   - Discharges graded by non-return valve arrangement at 0.01L and 0.02L, controls accessible with open/closed indicators
   - Scuppers from cargo spaces only where the deck edge is not immersed at 5 degrees heel
   - Garbage chutes interlocked and marked; spurling pipes permanently closed
6. **B. Draining the deck, and protecting the crew who must close things**
   - Freeing ports - ample provision to free the decks rapidly; A = 0.7 + 0.035l up to 20 m, A = 0.07l beyond
   - Half the area for superstructure deck wells; adjustment for bulwark height
   - Gutter bars over 300 mm on tanker decks treated as bulwarks, closures unable to jam at sea
   - Trapped water is weight and free surface in the worst place - a ship can be lost without anything having leaked
   - Protection of the crew is a condition in its own right - every closure must be shut by a person, in the weather that requires it
   - Regulation 10 information keeps the loaded condition inside what all of the above was designed for
7. **C. Why they need periodic inspection**
   - They are the only part of the freeboard that can deteriorate - hull dimensions do not change, gaskets and valves do
   - They are working machinery handled every day in port
   - They corrode where they are hardest to maintain - coamings, compression bars, drain channels
   - They are struck by cargo gear
   - They are defeated for operational convenience
   - They are lost - hence the Convention's insistence on permanently attached
   - Ships are modified, and the arrangement no longer matches the record
   - The Form of Record exists precisely so inspection is item-by-item verification, not impression
8. **C. Specific instances where they are found less than fully effective**
   - Hatch covers - hardened or painted gaskets, wasted compression bars, slack cleats, drain channel non-return valves missing or seized
   - Weathertight doors - painted gaskets, seized or missing dogs, worn hinges, set-in door leaf
   - Ventilators - closing appliances absent, mushroom heads seized, coamings wasted at the deck
   - Air pipes - float seized, ball missing, gauze corroded solid, head distorted by cargo gear
   - Freeing ports - shutters seized, jammed by dunnage, or deliberately lashed shut
   - Discharges - non-return valves seized open, controls buried behind later pipework, indicators wrong, pipework wasted
   - Side scuttles - deadlights missing or painted open
   - Garbage chutes - covers open, interlocks defeated, markings painted over
   - Crew protection - slack rails, parted wires, bent stanchions
   - Regulation 10 information superseded by conversion or evidence of approval no longer aboard
