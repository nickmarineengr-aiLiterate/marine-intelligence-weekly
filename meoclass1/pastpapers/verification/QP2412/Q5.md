## 3. The finding that mattered

The finding is the regulatory footing that most treatments of this subject omit. Root cause analysis is usually presented as pure management theory; it has a hard anchor. The Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84) of 2008, was made mandatory as to Parts I and II by SOLAS regulation XI-1/6 with effect from 1 January 2010, and requires a marine safety investigation into every VERY SERIOUS MARINE CASUALTY - one involving the total loss of the ship, a death, or severe damage to the environment. The provision that matters is the Code's statement that such an investigation is conducted TO PREVENT FUTURE CASUALTIES and is SEPARATE AND DISTINCT FROM, AND MUST NOT SEEK TO APPORTION, BLAME OR DETERMINE LIABILITY. That separation is not incidental - it is what makes root cause analysis possible at all, because an investigation that can attribute liability meets defensive witnesses and stops at the individual. The second finding is a provenance one, recorded rather than glossed: the active/latent distinction and the defences-in-depth model are attributed to James Reason and to the human-factors literature. They are reproduced in IMO and flag State guidance but are NOT the text of an IMO instrument, and the answer does not present them as one.

## 9. Answer route as built

**1. What root cause analysis is, and why it is required** - limb `a)`

- structured method to find the underlying organisational reasons an event occurred
- purpose is PREVENTION of recurrence, not identification of the immediate failure
- Casualty Investigation Code, MSC.255(84) of 2008; Parts I and II mandatory via SOLAS XI-1/6 from 1 January 2010
- safety investigation required into every VERY SERIOUS MARINE CASUALTY - total loss, death, or severe damage to the environment
- the Code separates the investigation from blame and liability - and that separation is what MAKES root cause analysis possible
- ISM Code regulation 9 - non-conformities, accidents and hazardous occurrences reported, investigated, analysed; corrective action procedures

**2. The principles of root cause analysis** - limb `a)`

- seek causes, not culprits
- every accident has multiple causes - a single-cause finding is an incomplete investigation
- go beyond the immediate; keep asking why until you reach something the organisation controls
- base findings on evidence; gather perishable evidence first
- establish the sequence before attempting the explanation
- test each causal factor by removal - would the outcome have changed?
- a root cause must be ACTIONABLE - 'human error' is not one
- correct at the level the cause sits; a systemic cause cannot be fixed by retraining one person
- close the loop - verify implementation AND effectiveness, and share across the fleet
- just culture - honest reporting must be safe, or the raw material never arrives

**3. The steps of a root cause analysis** - limb `a)`

- recognise and report; make the situation safe first
- preserve and collect evidence - VDR, alarms, logs, PMS and defect records, work and rest hours, permits, risk assessments, drawings, early witness accounts
- establish the sequence of events - timeline or event and causal factor chart
- identify causal factors and test each by removal
- analyse to root cause using five whys, fishbone, fault tree or barrier analysis
- identify the failed defences - absent, inadequate, bypassed or unmaintained
- develop corrective AND preventive action, each with an owner and a date
- implement and verify - two checks, implementation and EFFECTIVENESS
- communicate the lesson fleet-wide and feed it into risk assessment, procedures, training and management review

**4. Proximate cause versus root cause** - limb `a)`

- proximate - the event directly preceding the outcome, at the sharp end, immediate in time
- root - the underlying systemic reason, at the blunt end, often years earlier
- correcting the proximate restores the situation; correcting the root prevents recurrence
- worked example - seized LO pump bearing back through an unrestored isolation, a permit close-out that was not enforced, a form that required no signature, to a generic procedure never reviewed ship-specifically
- correcting the proximate replaces a bearing; correcting the root revises the permit system fleet-wide
- NOTE the different sense of 'proximate cause' in marine insurance - the dominant or effective cause of a loss; related but not the same test

**5. The accident causation models** - limb `b)`

- sequential models first - the domino model, an accident as the last of a line of falling dominoes
- useful for the idea of a chain, but encourages a search for one removable cause close to the accident
- the organisational accident model - James Reason - defences in depth, the 'Swiss cheese' model
- successive layers: design, engineered safeguards, procedures, supervision, training, alarms, the crew
- each layer has holes, and THE HOLES MOVE
- an accident occurs only when the holes in EVERY layer momentarily line up
- failures divide by WHERE they originate and HOW FAST their effect appears

**6. Active failures** - limb `b)`

- unsafe acts by people in DIRECT CONTACT with the system, effects felt ALMOST IMMEDIATELY
- slips and lapses - skill-based; intention right, execution or memory wrong; e.g. opening the adjacent valve from habit
- mistakes - planning failures; rule-based misapplication or knowledge-based in a novel situation; e.g. misdiagnosing a burning exhaust valve as an instrument fault
- violations - deliberate departures; routine, situational, exceptional; e.g. a permit signed retrospectively
- they are the MOST VISIBLE element, which is why investigations stop at them
- a ROUTINE VIOLATION IS DIAGNOSTIC of a latent condition

**7. Latent failures** - limb `b)`

- delayed consequences of decisions by designers, builders, procedure writers, regulators and management
- present in the system BEFORE the accident, often for years, dormant until combined with local triggers
- Reason's term - RESIDENT PATHOGENS
- two effects - error-provoking conditions in the workplace, and long-lasting holes in the defences
- design - poor ergonomics; identical adjacent valves with unclear labelling
- procedure - generic, unusable or absent; a checklist never revised for this installation
- manning and resourcing - a manning level that makes the rest-hour regime unachievable; deferred maintenance
- training - familiarisation that certifies attendance rather than capability
- maintenance management - generic PMS intervals, tolerated defect backlog
- culture - production pressure, discouraged reporting, near misses not investigated

**8. Why the distinction matters, and how the two combine** - limb `b)`

- active failures are hard to prevent and easy to blame; the next person in the same conditions makes the same slip
- latent conditions are the manageable target - findable PROACTIVELY by audit, risk assessment, near-miss analysis and trend data
- that is the justification for the ISM Code's audit and review machinery
- the accident needs BOTH - which is why trivial errors sometimes cause catastrophes and usually cause nothing
- chance is real, so OUTCOME IS A POOR MEASURE OF BEHAVIOUR
- hence near misses must be investigated with the same rigour - free evidence about the defences
- blame destroys the data; just culture separates honest error from recklessness and wilful violation
- CLOSING LINE - RCA is the technique that converts an active failure into information about latent conditions
