## 9. Answer route as built

**1. Why the duration of responsibility matters** - limb `a)`

- the period decides WHICH REGIME applies, and therefore who bears the loss
- outside the period the carrier may lawfully exclude liability by contract
- inside the period Article III rule 8 makes any derogating clause NULL AND VOID
- so identical damage produces different outcomes depending on WHEN it occurred
- each successive convention extends the period because the previous one left a gap

**2. Hague and Hague-Visby - tackle to tackle** - limb `a)`

- Article I(e) - from the time the goods are LOADED ON to the time they are DISCHARGED FROM the ship
- covers only the sea carriage stage
- before loading and after discharge fall OUTSIDE the compulsory regime
- carriers contract out of the gaps by 'before and after' clauses, lawfully
- Article I(b) - applies only where a bill of lading or similar document of title is issued
- Article I(c) - 'goods' excludes live animals and deck cargo stated and carried as such
- EXAMPLE - rain damage on the quay before loading falls outside; the same damage an hour later does not

**3. Hamburg - port to port** - limb `a)`

- Article 4 - the whole time the carrier is IN CHARGE of the goods at the port of loading, during carriage, and at the port of discharge
- deemed in charge from taking over from the shipper or a mandatory authority
- until delivered to the consignee, placed at its disposal, or handed to a mandatory authority
- Article 5 - PRESUMED FAULT: liable unless it proves all measures reasonably required were taken
- NO nautical fault exception and no long catalogue of excepted perils
- Article 20 - TWO-year time bar
- EXAMPLE - the same quay damage now falls INSIDE the period

**4. Rotterdam - door to door, and not in force** - limb `a)`

- Article 12 - from RECEIPT of the goods for carriage to DELIVERY
- not tied to ports; can cover inland road, rail or inland waterway legs
- applies to contracts wholly or partly by sea
- Article 13(2) - permits FIOS agreements for loading, handling, stowing or discharging
- ADOPTED 2008, opened for signature 2009 - NOT IN FORCE
- Article 94 - requires twenty ratifications; threshold not reached at this sitting
- significance is the direction of travel, not applicable law

**5. The comparison, and the Indian position** - limb `a)`

- tackle to tackle / port to port / door to door; Art I(e) / Art 4 / Art 12
- one year / two years / two years
- Hague-Visby dominant; Hamburg in force but marginal; Rotterdam NOT in force
- INDIA at this sitting - Carriage of Goods by Sea Act 1925 as amended 1993, giving effect to Hague-Visby
- alongside the Indian Bills of Lading Act 1856 for transfer of rights of suit
- India is NOT a party to the Hamburg or Rotterdam Rules - so the Indian period is TACKLE TO TACKLE
- WORKED COMPARISON - custody day 1, loaded day 3, discharged day 20, collected day 25; damage day 2 and day 22
- Hague-Visby - neither inside; Hamburg - both inside; Rotterdam - both, plus inland, but hypothetical

**6. The grain claim - how it is framed** - limb `b)`

- consignee must prove GOOD IN, BAD OUT
- a CLEAN bill of lading is prima facie evidence of shipment in apparent good order - Article III rule 3
- and CONCLUSIVE evidence in the hands of a good-faith third party transferee, under the Visby amendment
- the burden then shifts to the carrier to bring itself within a defence
- so the owner must name a SPECIFIC defence, not assert general care

**7. The excepted perils that apply to grain** - limb `b)`

- Article IV rule 2(m) - INHERENT DEFECT, QUALITY OR VICE, and wastage in bulk or weight - the principal defence
- grain is hygroscopic and organic; loaded above safe moisture it self-heats, sweats, cakes, moulds and discolours
- rule 2(i) - act or omission of the shipper or owner of the goods
- rule 2(n) - insufficiency of packing
- rule 2(c) - perils, dangers and accidents of the sea, requiring real evidence of exceptional conditions
- rule 2(a) - neglect in the NAVIGATION or MANAGEMENT of the ship, but NOT care of cargo; ventilation is the battleground
- rule 2(p) - latent defects not discoverable by due diligence
- rule 2(b) - fire without actual fault or privity of the carrier
- rule 2(q) - the catch-all, with the burden of proof expressly on the carrier

**8. The overriding obligations that can defeat every defence** - limb `b)`

- Article III rule 1 - due diligence BEFORE AND AT THE BEGINNING of the voyage
- including making the holds fit and safe for the reception, carriage and preservation of the goods - CARGOWORTHINESS
- clean, dry holds free of residues and infestation; weathertight hatch covers
- Article IV rule 1 - where loss arises from unseaworthiness, the BURDEN OF PROVING DUE DILIGENCE IS ON THE CARRIER
- Article III rule 2 - properly and carefully load, handle, stow, carry, keep, care for and discharge
- for grain that means the VENTILATION regime, temperature monitoring, bilge soundings, protection from rain
- CONCLUSION - inherent vice succeeds ONLY if the ship was cargoworthy and the cargo properly cared for

**9. Procedural and quantum defences** - limb `b)`

- Article III rule 6 - notice in writing before or at removal, or within THREE DAYS if damage is not apparent
- absence of notice is prima facie evidence of delivery as per the bill, though not a bar in itself
- Article III rule 6 - discharged from all liability unless suit is brought within ONE YEAR
- Article IV rule 5 - package or unit limit OR weight-based limit, whichever is higher
- FOR BULK GRAIN THERE ARE NO PACKAGES, so the WEIGHT-BASED limit applies
- the right to limit is lost only on intent, or recklessness with knowledge that damage would probably result
- Article 4 bis - Himalaya protection for servants and agents, not independent contractors
- title to sue - Indian Bills of Lading Act 1856 transfers rights of suit to the consignee or endorsee

**10. The evidence that makes the defence work** - limb `b)`

- pre-loading hold inspection and cleanliness certificate
- hatch cover weathertightness test - ultrasonic or hose test
- pre-shipment cargo survey, and above all MOISTURE CONTENT AND TEMPERATURE AT LOADING
- mate's receipts and any clausing - a clean bill over a claused mate's receipt is a serious problem
- complete ventilation records with dew point readings, hold temperature logs, bilge soundings
- weather records and deck log for any heavy weather relied on; draught surveys for shortage
- prompt discharge-port survey, jointly where possible, and preservation of samples
- immediate notification of the P&I Club
- CONCLUSION - inherent vice under 2(m), supported by 2(i), backed by the one-year bar and the weight limit
