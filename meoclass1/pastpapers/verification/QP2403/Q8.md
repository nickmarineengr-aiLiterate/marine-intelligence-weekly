## 2. The judgement this question is really testing

Route step 4 exists because the scenario admits **three genuinely different failures**, and they
have **three different remedies**:

| Failure | What happened | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Loss of remote (bridge) control** | The engine *could* have run astern but never received or executed the order | Transfer to ECR or local station — which is why they exist — then repair the control system |
| **Mechanical inability to produce astern thrust** | The engine could not start or run astern: air, reversing gear, fuel | Mechanical repair. **Transferring control would have achieved nothing** |
| **An adequate response ordered too late** | The engine *did* respond; the headway could not be absorbed | **Navigational and procedural** — notice of engines, approach speed, tugs |

**The engine movement and telegraph recorder settles which it was** — what was ordered, at what
time, whether the engine received it, and whether the shaft reversed. Until that record is read the
report must **attribute cause to neither bridge nor engine room**, and the Model Answer says so
explicitly. A first report that blames one side before the record is read is a report that will have
to be withdrawn.

---

## 3. The safety line, stated in the answer

> **No protective trip is to be reset, and no interlock bypassed, before the cause is established.**

A trip that operated may have been doing its job; an interlock that blocked the reversal may have
been protecting the engine; and resetting destroys the fault-log evidence. Where an isolation or
bypass is unavoidable to move the ship, the answer requires it to be **risk-assessed, documented,
time-limited and agreed with class**. This is stated in the Model Answer, in the Study Guide and in
a retrieval card, because it is the point at which a plausible-sounding answer becomes an unsafe
one.

---

## 4. The fault tree, and why it is a tree

The stem states **neither the engine type nor the control architecture** — camshaft or electronic,
pneumatic or electronic control, fixed or controllable pitch. A single confident diagnosis would
therefore be unsupportable. The answer walks the chain:

**bridge lever and telegraph → control transmitter → remote control system → safety and interlock
logic → control air → starting air distributor and manoeuvring valves → reversing arrangement →
fuel**

with candidate faults at each stage: control signal (encoder, cable, I/O card, power/UPS); control
logic (transfer state, loss of shaft speed/direction feedback); **control air** (pressure, moisture,
oil, ice, sticking pilot valve, blocked orifice, undrained receiver); starting air (receiver
pressure after repeated manoeuvres, distributor timing, stuck starting valve); reversing (servo oil
pressure, camshaft shift, worn linkage, interlock never satisfied; ECU/HCU or angle-encoder fault on
electronic engines); fuel (sticking rack or index); interlocks and overrides; and — stated honestly
— **human and organisational** causes including a late astern order, insufficient astern power, and
no proven astern trial before arrival.

**Presenting a fault tree and saying why is the correct treatment of an under-specified scenario,
not a gap in research.** In the examination, saying that the diagnosis depends on the installation
is itself a mark of competence.

The working hypothesis advanced — clearly labelled as such — is a **latent defect in the remote
control or control air system that only manifested under the rapid, repeated manoeuvring of a dock
approach**, on a system whose astern function had not recently been proven under load.
