## Primary maker documentation read in full this session

**MAN Energy Solutions, *Basic Principles of Ship Propulsion***, obtained from `man-es.com` and read
directly. The PDF was not machine-readable through the fetch layer, so the text was extracted locally.
**Every quantity in this answer comes from this one document**, and each is attributed:

| Established | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Light running margin** | `LRM = (nL − nH) / nH × 100 %`. **MAN recommends 4 to 7 %**, depending on the specific ship and operational profile, **up to 10 % in special cases**. |
| **The sensitivity** | *"As a rule of thumb, 20 % increase in required power from added resistance corresponds to 1 % heavy running, i.e. 1 % lower rpm for the same engine power."* |
| **Load diagram limits** | **Line 4 is the torque/speed limit of the engine, limited mainly by the thermal load on the engine.** Line 5 is the maximum mep for continuous operation and is *"only a limit at high loads and speeds. At lower speeds, line 4 is a stricter limit."* Line 6 is the light propeller curve, *for clean hull and calm weather*. Line 7 is maximum continuous power. **Line 8 is the overload limit** — overload running *"possible only for limited periods, 1 hour per 12 hours or when required in an emergency situation, as the resulting thermal load on the engine is high."* Line 9 is maximum trial rpm, 110 % of SMCR rpm. |
| **Class speed limit** | *"class rules do not allow an engine to run faster than 115 % of SMCR-rpm."* |
| **Fouling** | Frictional resistance *"increases with the fouling of the hull, i.e. by the growth of algae, sea grass and barnacles."* |
| **Shallow water** | *"In general, shallow water will have no influence when the water depth is more than 10 times the ship draught."* |
| **Bollard pull** | A heavy running factor of **15–20 %** relative to the light propeller curve has been experienced — an rpm reduction of 15–20 % for the same power, *"equivalent to approx. a 60-100 % increase in power for the same rpm."* |
| **Margins** | Sea margin typically **10–30 %**, historically **15 %**; engine margin historically **10 %**, typically **10–30 %**. |

## Reasoning recorded

- **The definition is anchored to the curve, because the stem says so.** *"Referring to propeller
  Curve"* is the instruction, not scene-setting: the condition is only definable *relative to* the
  light propeller curve. The study notes tell the candidate to **sketch the load diagram**, because
  every later limb can then be pointed at rather than described.
- **`Torque rich` is identified as the examination's idiom for `heavy running`.** Every maker manual,
  load diagram and service letter the candidate will consult uses the second term, so the equivalence
  is worth a line.
- **The `20 % / 1 %` relationship is made the centrepiece of limb (c).** The rpm change is invisible
  and the power penalty behind it is enormous — which is *why* the indications must be **comparisons
  against reference data** rather than absolute readings, and is the argument for systematic
  performance monitoring.
- **Line 4 being a THERMAL limit is why the condition is dangerous rather than merely uneconomic.**
  At reduced rpm the turbocharger delivers less air while fuel per cycle rises; the air-fuel ratio
  falls exactly when more heat is being released. Exhaust temperature, smoke, fouling and thermal
  stress all follow from that one imbalance.
- **Limb (d) is sequenced `confirm → exclude → protect → remedy → report`, and the sequence IS the
  answer.** The instinctive response — *clean the hull and polish the propeller* — is the right ending
  and the wrong beginning. Several engine-side faults (fouled turbocharger or air cooler, injection
  timing or VIT, leaking exhaust valves, poor fuel quality) mimic heavy running on the instruments,
  and so does a drifting torsionmeter or tachometer. **A candidate who sends the ship for an
  underwater clean because a thermocouple has drifted has answered badly even though the words look
  right.**
- **The boundary of the second engineer's authority is stated.** Persistent heavy running with a clean
  hull and sound propeller is a **matching** problem, and re-pitching or replacing a propeller is the
  owner's decision with the propeller designer, engine builder and class. He **establishes and
  preserves the evidence; he does not prescribe the cure.** That is what *"what action will you ask
  your second engineer to take"* is really probing — and it is the same principle Q6 of this paper
  tests in the dry dock.
- **`Chasing the rpm with more fuel` is named as the single most damaging response**, because it
  drives the engine straight into the thermal limit the diagram exists to keep it away from.

## What is deliberately NOT asserted

- **No fuel penalty or speed-loss figure for a stated degree of fouling.** Published values vary
  enormously with coating, trade, water temperature and lay-up history; none could be verified. The
  mechanism is explained instead.
- **The margins are presented as recommendations and typical ranges, not as rules.** The light running
  margin actually built into a particular ship is in **her own project documentation and engine load
  diagram**, and the answer says so — twice, including in the final bullet of limb (d).
- **The numbered limit lines are MAN's numbering.** Other builders show the same physical limits
  differently, so the answer **describes what line 4 *is*** as well as naming it, and the point
  survives a different maker's diagram.

## Family benefit

The corpus's first propulsion-matching question. The MAN figures are now verified and reusable for any
future question on hull and propeller performance, sea and engine margins, load diagram limits or
fouling. The cross-link to **Q4** of this paper is reciprocal and mechanistic: on a gas-fuelled engine

temperature and smoke.
