Understand this first

Strip the policy language away and there are two different things in this question that people constantly run together. One is a proportion - how many of the people on ships are women - and that is a target, something an industry can aim at and may miss for years without anyone breaking a rule. The other is a right - that nobody is kept out of a job, a training place or a promotion because of their sex, and that everybody gets a workplace and a home on board that is safe and decent. The second is already binding on the flag State and therefore on the ship, whatever programmes exist. Once you see that, the last part of the question stops being about attitudes and becomes concrete: the boiler suit that fits, the lockable cabin, the overhaul she is actually trusted with, and what happens the first time somebody says something out of place. The programmes can only get a person to the gangway. What decides whether she is still in the profession in three years happens in the engine room.

Model written answer

1. What gender equality means at sea, and what the goal actually says

The United Nations 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development sets seventeen goals, of which the one the question quotes is to achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls. Applied to shipping, gender equality does not mean pretending that the ship is a neutral place. It means that a seafarer's access to the job, to training, to promotion and to a safe working and living environment does not depend on her sex - and, because a ship is also a home, that the accommodation, the sanitary arrangements, the medical provision and the behavioural standards on board are fit for a mixed crew rather than merely tolerable to a minority within it.

Two distinctions are worth making at the outset, because the rest of the answer depends on them. First, equality of opportunity is not the same as equality of outcome: the obligation is to remove barriers, not to guarantee proportions. Second - and this is the point the examiner is really testing - gender balance is an aspiration, but non-discrimination is already law. Under the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 each Member must satisfy itself that its law respects the fundamental right to the elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation, and every seafarer has the right to a safe and secure workplace and to decent working and living conditions on board. Those are binding obligations that reach the ship whether or not any programme exists.

2. Why gender balance is required

  • It is a matter of right, not of favour. Excluding half the population from a profession is discrimination in respect of employment and occupation, which the Convention's own fundamental rights article requires each Member to eliminate. That is the first and sufficient reason, and it does not depend on any business case.
  • The industry needs the people. Shipping recruits officers from a shrinking pool and competes with shore employment that offers a normal domestic life. A profession that draws from half its potential workforce is choosing to make its own shortage worse.
  • Selection from a wider pool raises quality. If the best candidate for a fourth engineer's berth is excluded before she applies, the berth is filled by someone less good. This is a straightforward consequence of restricting the field and it holds regardless of any argument about aptitude.
  • Mixed teams manage risk differently. A crew whose members share one background tends also to share its blind spots, and the human-element failures behind most casualties - unchallenged authority, poor communication, reluctance to speak up - are exactly the failures a more varied team is less prone to. This is an argument about diversity of perspective, not about the qualities of either sex, and it should be put that way.
  • Retention. Recruiting women and then losing them within a contract or two is more expensive than not recruiting them. Retention depends on the conditions on board, which is precisely what limb (c) is about.
  • It is now a commercial expectation. Charterers, financiers, insurers and terminal operators increasingly ask about the social element of an operator's practice, and a policy that cannot be evidenced is a commercial exposure as well as an ethical one.
  • It changes the profession's own standards. Accommodation, sanitary arrangements, personal protective equipment sizing, medical provision and shipboard behaviour all improve for everyone when they are designed for a mixed crew instead of assumed for a single one.

One caution belongs here, and it is worth a mark in itself: the case does not need to be exaggerated. Figures for the proportion of women in the world seafaring workforce are widely quoted and vary considerably between sources. This answer quotes none, because an unsourced statistic in an examination script is a liability, and the argument above stands without one.

3. What the International Maritime Organization does

The Organization's work on this is programme work rather than regulation. It has no convention on gender, and it does not certify a ship for it. What it does, through its technical cooperation function, is build the supply of qualified women and make the profession visible to them:

  • a long-standing women in maritime programme under the Integrated Technical Cooperation Programme, aimed at access to maritime training and to positions in maritime administrations;
  • support for regional women in maritime associations, which give a professional network across national administrations and industry in each region;
  • fellowships and reserved places for women at the Organization's own academic institutions, including the World Maritime University and the International Maritime Law Institute;
  • a World Maritime Day theme devoted to women in the maritime community, used to put the subject in front of Member States and industry for a full year;
  • gender-parity mechanisms written into ordinary technical programmes, which is the most durable form because it does not depend on a separate initiative. A worked example can be given from an instrument that has nothing to do with gender at all: the Organization's GHG-SMART capacity-building programme, described in the appendix to the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy, provides for two trainees - one female and one male - to go on to a World Maritime University scholarship.
  • the same Strategy's insistence that the human element and the impact on seafarers be addressed in the energy transition, which is where the next generation of shipboard jobs will be created.

What is deliberately not said: no resolution number is given for any of this. The Organization has adopted instruments touching the subject, but no licensed text of one is held for this answer, and inventing a number to look authoritative is precisely the error that costs marks in the oral.

4. What the Government of India does

India's maritime administration is the Directorate General of Shipping, acting under the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, which is the statute in force at this sitting. Its instruments are the ordinary administrative ones - training and examination rules, certification requirements, approval of maritime training institutes, and notices to the industry. The measures relevant here operate at that level:

  • Admission to training on the same terms. Women are admitted to approved Indian maritime training institutes and to the pre-sea and post-sea courses that lead to certificates of competency, in the engineering as well as the nautical stream.
  • Certification and service in all departments and ranks. Eligibility for a certificate of competency and for shipboard service is not restricted by sex, so there is no legal barrier to a woman holding the Chief Engineer's certificate this examination leads to.
  • Conditions attached to institute approval. The administration's approval regime for training institutes is the practical lever - separate accommodation and sanitary facilities, safeguarding requirements and complaint mechanisms are conditions of running a course, not aspirations.
  • Berth and cadet-training availability. The recurring practical obstacle for an Indian cadet is not admission but obtaining a training berth, and administration and industry pressure on shipowners to provide them is where policy meets reality.
  • Implementation of the Maritime Labour Convention through national law. India's ratification carries the Article III and Article IV obligations into the domestic regime, which is what gives a woman seafarer on an Indian ship an enforceable position rather than a policy statement.
  • Promotion and visibility - recognition of women officers, participation in the regional women in maritime association, and encouragement of shipowners to recruit and retain women.

Again, no circular, rule, notice or scheme number is asserted here. The administration issues numbered instruments and a candidate who has one in front of him should cite it; one who does not should describe the measure and say what it does, which scores, rather than invent a number, which does not.

6. As Chief Engineer - the physical ship

  • Accommodation and sanitary arrangements. Confirm before the crew joins that the individual cabin required by the Convention is in fact allocated and lockable, and that private, secure sanitary facilities are available. If the arrangement depends on sharing, it is my problem to solve before arrival, not hers to raise afterwards.
  • Personal protective equipment that fits. Boiler suits, gloves, safety shoes, harnesses, breathing apparatus face masks and immersion suits stocked in the full range of sizes. Ill-fitting PPE is a direct safety failure and it is the most common, most visible and most easily corrected signal that a ship was not expecting her.
  • Workshop and machinery-space practicalities. Lifting aids and correct tooling used as routine rather than as a concession, so that no job in the engine room is done by main force alone. This is good practice for every engineer and it removes the argument before it is made.
  • Medical provision. Ensure the medical locker and the ship's medical arrangements match the crew actually carried, and that the confidentiality of medical matters is real.
  • Safety equipment sizing checked in drills, not assumed from the inventory.

7. As Chief Engineer - work, watches and training

  • Allocate work by rank and competence, and by nothing else. The single most damaging thing a Chief Engineer can do is protect a woman engineer from the dirty or heavy jobs. It looks like consideration; it is exclusion, it destroys her credibility with the ratings, and it leaves her under-trained for her next certificate.
  • Give the same watch and project responsibility - the same overhauls, the same faults to diagnose, the same responsibility for a survey item - and hold it to the same standard.
  • Appraise honestly. Feedback withheld for fear of appearing harsh is the quiet way careers are ended. She is entitled to the same correction as anyone else and to the same record of her competence.
  • Mentor, and be seen to. A cadet or junior engineer who is the only woman on board has no peer to ask. Deliberate, planned time on machinery and on certificate preparation costs little and is what converts recruitment into retention.
  • Include her in the department's decisions - planned maintenance, the repair list, the pre-arrival plan - so that authority follows the rank rather than the individual.
  • Watch the workload distribution in the hours of rest records: a junior who is given every awkward job, or excluded from every desirable one, will show up there before she says anything.

8. As Chief Engineer - behaviour, complaints and accountability

  • Set the standard explicitly and early. At the departmental meeting on joining, state what is expected and what will not be tolerated - not as a policy reading, but as an instruction from the head of department. The standard on a ship is whatever the senior officers visibly accept.
  • Deal with the first incident, however small. A remark left uncorrected is a permission. Correcting it once, immediately and privately, prevents almost everything that would otherwise follow.
  • Make the complaint route real. Ensure every member of the department has the on-board complaint procedure, knows who the impartial adviser is, and knows that the right to go to the master or to an external authority is not affected by raising it with me first.
  • Protect the complainant. Victimization is prohibited and penalized under the Convention. If a complaint is raised, my job is to ensure that nothing changes in her work allocation, her appraisal or her treatment because of it - and to be seen to ensure it.
  • Treat harassment as an OSH matter, which is what the Convention's own guideline makes it: risk-assess it, address it in the safety committee, and record it like any other hazard rather than handling it privately as a personality problem.
  • Use the safety management system. Report and record under the SMS so that the Company and the DPA see the pattern, and so that the response does not depend on which Chief Engineer happens to be aboard.
  • Brief the reliefs. Standards that survive one contract and lapse at the next are not standards. Hand over the department's expectations as deliberately as the machinery condition.
  • Be careful with visibility. Singling someone out as an example is as isolating as ignoring her. The objective is that within a few weeks she is simply the fourth engineer.

The honest summary is that the initiatives in limb (b) can only get a woman to the gangway. Whether she stays in the profession is decided almost entirely by what happens in the six months after she walks up it, and in the engine room that is decided by the Chief Engineer.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

This looks like an easy question and is not. It is easy to fill a page with sentiment and score four marks out of sixteen. The marks are in three quite different places: an argued case in limb (a), specific initiatives in limb (b), and a practical, ship-level programme in limb (c). Limb (c) is the one addressed to the candidate personally and it is where an examiner looks for original thought, so it should get at least a third of the writing.

The prize move on this question is to anchor it in the Maritime Labour Convention. Almost every script treats gender equality as a matter of policy and goodwill; a script that says non-discrimination is a fundamental right under Article III, that harassment is an occupational safety and health matter under Guideline B4.3.1, and that victimization of a complainant is prohibited under Standard A5.1.5, has moved the whole answer from opinion to obligation. That is a management-level answer and it is what the marks are for.

The idea that makes limb (c) write itself

Initiatives recruit; conditions retain. Everything in limb (b) is about getting a woman to the gangway - training places, fellowships, certification access, visibility. None of it has any effect on whether she is still at sea in three years. That is decided by the cabin, the boiler suit that fits, the overhaul she is trusted with, and whether the first inappropriate remark was corrected or laughed at. Say that once and the whole of limb (c) follows from it in the right order: the ship, the work, the behaviour.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a percentage. Figures for women as a share of the seafaring workforce are widely repeated and vary between sources. An unsourced number in a script invites the one oral question you cannot answer. The argument does not need it.
  • Inventing a resolution or circular number to look authoritative. If you have one, cite it; if not, describe the measure and what it does. A named measure with no number scores; a wrong number costs.
  • Writing limb (c) as a list of good intentions - 'I will treat everyone equally'. The marks are in specific, checkable actions: PPE sizing, cabin allocation, work allocation by rank, the named impartial adviser, the SMS report.
  • Protective allocation. Writing that you would keep her off the heavy or dirty jobs. It reads as considerate and is in fact the discrimination the question is about.
  • Treating harassment as a matter of shipboard manners. Under the Convention's own guideline it belongs in the occupational safety and health regime.
  • Answering limb (b) for only one of the two bodies. The stem asks about the Government of India and the Organization, and half an answer is half the marks.
  • Ignoring the introduction the stem asks for. It says 'with brief introduction on gender equality' - one short paragraph, not a page.

Examiner traps

  • The printed marks. This question prints no per-limb marks - a single (16) sits at the end of limb c. Do not invent a split and do not assume the limbs are equal in effort; give limb (c) the most writing because it is the one that asks for your own judgement.
  • The Indian statute. At this sitting the governing Act is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 commenced 15 March 2026, twenty months after this paper, and must not appear.
  • The SDG number. The goal the stem quotes is the gender equality goal; do not confuse it with the climate goal the IMO cites in its GHG work.
  • Being asked in the oral 'what does MLC actually require you to provide?' The answer is the individual sleeping room on a cargo ship under Standard A3.1, the safe and secure workplace under Article IV, and the complaint procedure with its impartial adviser under Standard A5.1.5.
  • Being asked 'what would you do if a complaint was made against a senior officer?' The procedure resolves at the lowest level possible, but the seafarer's right to go directly to the master and to external authorities is preserved, and victimization is prohibited and penalized.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which Convention makes non-discrimination a fundamental right for seafarers? MLC 2006, Article III(d) - the elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation.
  • Where does harassment sit in the Convention? In occupational safety and health, through Guideline B4.3.1, which also directs attention to the jointly published industry guidance on eliminating shipboard harassment and bullying.
  • What does the Convention say about a seafarer who complains? Victimization is prohibited and penalized, and every seafarer is given the procedure, the flag State and home-country contacts, and the name of an impartial adviser on board.
  • Name one concrete thing you would change before a woman engineer joins. PPE in the correct sizes, and confirmation that the individual cabin and private sanitary facilities are in fact allocated.
  • Why is protecting her from heavy work wrong? It leaves her under-trained for her next certificate, destroys her authority with the ratings, and is itself discrimination in employment.
  • Which Indian statute governs at present? The Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, administered by the Directorate General of Shipping.

Uncertainty and evidence limits

This is the question on the paper with the weakest available primary evidence, and the answer is built around that rather than pretending otherwise. What is carried at primary grade is the part that matters most - the binding obligations in limb (c) - because MLC 2006 as amended through 2018 was read at source and supplies Article III(d), Article IV, Standard A3.1, Guideline B4.3.1 and Standard A5.1.5. One worked example of an IMO programme with an explicit gender-parity mechanism is also read at source, in the appendix to resolution MEPC.377(80). Everything else in limb (b) is authoritative secondary and is stated at programme and policy level. No IMO resolution number, no Assembly resolution, no DG Shipping circular, rule, notice or scheme number is asserted anywhere in this answer, because MIW holds no licensed text of any such instrument - and a number invented to look authoritative is exactly what fails under an oral follow-up. No statistic is quoted either: no share of the seafaring workforce, no participation figure, no officer-shortfall projection. None can be sourced, and the case in limb (a) is complete without one. Both limits are recorded as open items rather than written around.

Understand this first

On the older engine a single long shaft with lumps on it decided everything. The shape of each lump decided when fuel went in, how long for, and when the exhaust valve opened - and once the shaft was made, those decisions were fixed for the life of the engine. That was fine at the load the engine was designed for and poor everywhere else, which is why the old engine smokes when you slow her down: the pump is driven by the shaft, so as the engine turns more slowly the fuel is squeezed more slowly and sprayed more coarsely. The newer engine keeps all the muscle and throws away the shaft. Oil at very high pressure is kept ready in a common supply, and at each cylinder a small fast electrically-commanded valve decides the instant it is let through and how quickly. That oil pushes a large piston which pushes a small plunger, and because the small end is much smaller than the big end the fuel comes out at very high pressure - regardless of how slowly the engine happens to be turning. The engine can now be re-timed by changing numbers instead of changing metal.

Model written answer

1. The essential difference - what was removed, and what replaced it

The conventional MC engine is mechanically controlled. A chain- or gear-driven camshaft runs the length of the engine and does three jobs: it drives the jerk-type fuel injection pump on each cylinder, it drives the exhaust valve actuator, and it operates the starting air distributor. Every event in the cycle - when injection begins, how long it lasts, when the exhaust valve opens and closes - is written into the geometry of the cams. It is decided at the drawing board and it does not change afterwards. Variable injection timing gives a limited, mechanically produced variation, and reversing is achieved by physically displacing or switching the cam arrangement.

The ME engine is electronically controlled. The camshaft and its drive are removed. In their place there are two things: a supply of high-pressure hydraulic oil, and a computer control system that decides, cycle by cycle and cylinder by cylinder, when and how that oil is released to do the work the cams used to do. The muscle is still hydraulic and mechanical; only the decision has moved from the shape of a cam into software.

That single change is the answer to the whole question, and it is worth stating before the detail. Timing is no longer a property of the engine's geometry; it is a set of values in a control system. Everything else - what the HPS is for, why there is an HCU on each cylinder, what the ELFI or FIVA valve does, and why the fuel pump has become a pressure booster - follows from it.

3. The Hydraulic Power Supply - what it is for

Purpose. The HPS produces and maintains the high-pressure hydraulic oil that is the working medium of the whole engine. It is the muscle that replaced the camshaft. Everything the cams used to drive is now driven by this oil: the fuel pressure boosters, the exhaust valve actuators and, through the control units, the associated functions. It must deliver that pressure steadily, at all loads, and available before the engine is started.

What it consists of. In the usual arrangement:

  • Engine-driven main hydraulic pumps - variable-displacement axial piston pumps driven from the crankshaft through a step-up gear, which supply the engine once it is running;
  • Electrically driven start-up and standby pumps, which build up pressure before starting and provide redundancy;
  • Suction and delivery filtration to a high standard, usually with automatic back-flushing, because the servo valves have very fine clearances and dirt is the principal cause of failure;
  • Safety and relief valves limiting the maximum pressure, and non-return valves preventing back-flow;
  • Accumulators smoothing the pressure and providing the instantaneous flow each injection demands;
  • the high-pressure manifold or rail running along the engine to feed each hydraulic cylinder unit;
  • pressure, temperature and level instrumentation reporting to the control system, with alarms and slowdown functions.

The working medium is normally the engine's own system lubricating oil, drawn from the main system and given its own additional fine filtration. That is a considerable operational advantage - one oil, one purifier, no separate hydraulic charge - and a considerable operational obligation, because the cleanliness and water content of the system oil now determine whether the engine will run at all.

4. The Hydraulic Cylinder Unit, and what it contains

What it is. The HCU is the local hydraulic block mounted at each cylinder, at platform level beside the unit. If the HPS is the power station, the HCU is the substation: it takes high-pressure oil from the common supply and distributes it, under the command of that cylinder's own control unit, to the two things that have to be actuated on that cylinder. There is one HCU per cylinder, which is what makes cylinder-by-cylinder control possible.

The components it includes are typically:

  • the HCU block or base, machined with the internal galleries that distribute the oil - it is the manifold as well as the mounting;
  • the fuel oil pressure booster, the hydraulically driven intensifier that develops injection pressure - limb (d);
  • the fuel injection control valve, the ELFI - or, where the combined valve is fitted, the FIVA - limb (c);
  • the exhaust valve actuator, the hydraulic cylinder that opens the exhaust valve against its air spring;
  • the exhaust valve control valve, where the exhaust valve is controlled separately from the fuel;
  • accumulators - one supporting the injection event and one supporting the exhaust valve actuation - which supply the very high instantaneous flow each event needs without collapsing the rail pressure;
  • filters, non-return valves and safety valves local to the unit;
  • the high-pressure pipework from the booster to the cylinder cover fuel valves, and the connection to the exhaust valve actuator;
  • position and pressure sensors reporting booster plunger position, injection pressure and exhaust valve movement back to the cylinder control unit, which is how the system closes the loop;
  • in many arrangements, the supply to the electronically controlled cylinder lubricator for that unit.

5. The ELFI and the FIVA valve

Both are fast-acting, electronically controlled proportional servo valves, mounted on the HCU and driven directly by that cylinder's control unit. They are the point at which the electronic decision becomes a hydraulic action - the true replacement for the cam.

  • The practical vulnerability is contamination. These valves have very fine clearances and operate at high pressure many times a second. Dirty or wet servo oil sticks them, and a sticking valve shows as an injection timing deviation alarm long before it shows as a broken component - which is why oil cleanliness is the single most important routine on this engine.

6. The fuel pressure booster compared with the conventional fuel pump

The conventional jerk pump. A cam lifts a plunger inside a barrel. The plunger closes the suction and spill ports and compresses the trapped fuel until it lifts the injector needle. The pressure is generated by the direct mechanical thrust of the cam. Delivery quantity is set by rotating the plunger relative to its helix - by the fuel rack - and timing is set by the cam geometry, with variable injection timing giving limited adjustment. Shutdown is by a puncture valve or by racking to zero. Two consequences follow, and they are the whole of the comparison:

  • Injection pressure depends on plunger velocity, and plunger velocity depends on engine speed. At low load and low revolutions the pressure falls, atomisation deteriorates, combustion worsens and the engine smokes. That is why a conventional engine has a practical lower limit to clean running.
  • Timing cannot be changed while running beyond the range the mechanical arrangement allows.

The ME fuel pressure booster. It is a hydraulically driven pressure intensifier, not a pump in the jerk sense. Low-pressure fuel from the ship's fuel supply system fills the fuel side of the booster. On command, the ELFI or FIVA valve admits high-pressure servo oil to a hydraulic piston of large area, rigidly connected to a fuel plunger of much smaller area. The pressure on the fuel side is multiplied in proportion to the ratio of the two areas, and rises to injection pressure. When the control valve vents the servo oil, the pressure collapses, injection ends cleanly, and a spring returns the assembly ready for the next cycle.

What the change buys, in operating terms. Full injection pressure at low load means clean combustion far down the load range, which is what makes genuine slow steaming possible without smoke, fouled turbochargers and unburnt fuel in the exhaust. Variable timing and rate shaping allow the engine to be tuned across the load range rather than at one design point, improving consumption at part load and giving a lever on NOx formation. Cylinder-by-cylinder control allows automatic balancing and much simpler management of a defective unit.

What it costs. A dependence on hydraulic oil cleanliness and dryness that has no equivalent on an MC engine; the need for electrical power and a working control system before the engine will start at all, with the redundancy that implies; a department that must be competent in electronics, sensors and software version control as well as in mechanics; and the discipline that the injection settings are NOx-critical parameters and may only be varied within the range recorded in the engine's NOx Technical File.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four short notes at four marks each. The written-answer discipline is equal time, and the risk here is different from the usual one: limb (a) invites a very long list, and a candidate who writes fifteen differences has spent eight marks' worth of time earning four. Six to eight well-chosen differences in a table is enough; the marks in limbs (b), (c) and (d) are waiting.

Limb (b) is really two questions - the purpose of the HPS, and what the HCU is and contains - so split it deliberately. Limb (d) says 'describe the differences', which means a comparison, not a description of the booster alone. A table earns more than prose in both.

The one sentence that carries the whole question

The camshaft did not become unnecessary; it became software. The work is still done hydraulically and mechanically - oil at high pressure still pushes a piston that still pushes a plunger - but the decision about when and how fast has moved out of the shape of a cam and into a control system. Say that once and every limb follows: the HPS is the muscle that replaced the cam, the HCU is where that muscle is applied at each cylinder, the ELFI or FIVA is the point at which the electronic decision becomes a hydraulic action, and the booster is the actuator that used to be a cam-driven pump.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the ME engine's fuel pump a jerk pump. It is a hydraulically driven pressure intensifier; the pressure comes from an area ratio, not from a cam.
  • Saying the ME engine is 'electric'. Only the control is electronic; the power is hydraulic and mechanical throughout.
  • Forgetting that the servo oil is normally the engine's own system oil, and that its cleanliness therefore becomes a running requirement rather than a housekeeping preference.
  • Confusing ELFI and FIVA. ELFI is fuel only; FIVA does fuel and exhaust valve in one valve. If you can only remember one thing about limb (c), remember that.
  • Saying the camshaft is eliminated on every variant. On some, the exhaust valve actuation remains mechanically driven and only the injection is electronic - say that variants differ rather than overstating.
  • Listing advantages without the operational costs. Oil cleanliness, electrical dependence, control-system redundancy and the new competencies are half the answer.
  • Quoting a hydraulic pressure, injection pressure or consumption gain. These are builder- and variant-specific; an unsourced figure invites the one question you cannot answer.

Examiner traps

  • 'Where does the hydraulic oil come from?' The engine's own system lubricating oil in the usual arrangement, given additional fine filtration - which is why water in the system oil is an engine-stopping problem, not just a lubrication problem.
  • 'Why are there accumulators on the HCU?' Because an injection event demands a very large flow for a very short time, and without local storage the rail pressure would collapse.
  • 'Why can an ME engine slow steam cleanly?' Because injection pressure is independent of engine speed - the booster is driven by servo oil at full pressure whatever the revolutions.
  • 'What must you check before changing an engine running mode?' That the settings remain within the ranges recorded in the engine's NOx Technical File. Modes and timings are NOx-critical parameters. This is the answer that links limb (c) to a regulatory obligation and it is worth a mark on its own.
  • 'How do you start an ME engine after a blackout?' The electrically driven start-up hydraulic pumps must build up pressure and the control system must be alive before the engine can be started - it is not simply a matter of air on the turning gear side.
  • 'What is the commonest cause of servo valve trouble?' Contamination - dirt or water in the servo oil - showing first as a timing deviation alarm.

Model written answer

(a) The structure of IMO

1. What IMO is, and the organs that do the work

The International Maritime Organization is the specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and for the prevention of pollution from ships. It works through a two-tier governing structure - an Assembly and a Council - together with five main committees supported by seven sub-committees, all served by a permanent Secretariat headed by the Secretary-General. The governing organs decide policy, budget and membership questions; the technical output that actually reaches a ship comes almost entirely from the committees.

2. The Assembly - the governing body

  • The highest governing body. All Member States sit in it, each with one vote, and Associate Members participate without the full voting rights of Members.
  • Meets in regular session once every two years. An extraordinary session may be convened between regular sessions where the business requires it.
  • Approves the work programme and votes the budget, and determines the financial arrangements of the Organization. Nothing the committees plan can proceed unless the Assembly has funded and programmed it.
  • Elects the Council for the following two-year term.
  • Receives and considers the reports of the Council and of the committees, and decides what action to take on their recommendations.
  • Adopts Assembly resolutions - recommendations to Governments, guidelines, and codes - under Article 15(j) of the Convention on the IMO. This is the function the Assembly reserves to itself and does not delegate to the Council.
  • Worked example read at source: the 2011 ESP Code was adopted by the Assembly as resolution A.1049(27) on 30 November 2011, expressly under Article 15(j) and on the recommendation of the Maritime Safety Committee at its 89th session. That single recital shows the whole machine working - technical body proposes, governing body adopts.

3. The Council - the executive organ

  • 40 Members, elected by the Assembly for a two-year term in three categories: (a) ten States with the largest interest in providing international shipping services; (b) ten States with the largest interest in international seaborne trade; (c) twenty States elected to secure representation of all major geographic areas. The three categories exist so that the organ cannot be captured by tonnage alone.
  • Between Assembly sessions it performs all the functions of the Assembly, except the function of making recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention, which the Assembly keeps to itself. That single exception is what stops the executive organ from legislating in the governing organ's absence.
  • Supervises the work of the Organization and co-ordinates the activities of its organs, and receives the committees' reports between Assembly sessions.
  • Considers the draft work programme and budget prepared by the Secretary-General and submits them to the Assembly with its own comments and recommendations.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General, subject to Assembly approval, and takes responsibility for the arrangements for the Secretariat.
  • Concludes agreements and arrangements with other organizations, subject to Assembly approval.
  • Position at this sitting: the serving Council was elected at the 33rd Assembly in December 2023 for the 2024-2025 biennium and is in the first year of that term. The amendment that would enlarge the Council to 52 Members was adopted in December 2021 but requires acceptance by two thirds of Members and is not in force; the correct figure is therefore 40.

4. The five main committees and what each owns

CommitteeWhat it is responsible for

All five are open to all Member States and each reports to the Assembly through the Council. The point that matters for a ship is that MSC and MEPC are the two that adopt amendments to the mandatory instruments - SOLAS, MARPOL, Load Lines, STCW, the mandatory codes - and they do so by resolution, under the amendment article of the convention concerned. The Assembly does not adopt SOLAS or MARPOL amendments; the relevant committee does. Adoption is followed by the tacit acceptance procedure: the amendment is deemed accepted on a stated date unless a stated proportion of Parties objects, and enters into force on a further stated date. That is why a ship can be bound by a change no national parliament ever debated.

5. The seven sub-committees and the role they perform

The sub-committees are HTW (Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping), III (Implementation of IMO Instruments), NCSR (Navigation, Communications and Search and Rescue), PPR (Pollution Prevention and Response), SDC (Ship Design and Construction), SSE (Ship Systems and Equipment) and CCC (Carriage of Cargoes and Containers).

Their role is preparatory and technical. A committee sits for about a week and cannot itself draft detailed regulation, so it remits the work to the sub-committee holding the relevant expertise. The sub-committee develops the draft text, guidelines or unified interpretation, settles the technical argument, and reports back to its parent committee - MSC, MEPC, or both, since several sub-committees serve two parents. The typical life of a work item is therefore: a Member State or organization submits a proposal; the committee accepts it into the work programme; the sub-committee develops the text over one or more sessions, often through a correspondence group between sessions; the committee approves the draft and circulates it; and the committee then adopts it by resolution at a later session.

A sub-committee adopts nothing. It prepares, the parent committee approves, and the committee or the Assembly adopts. That division is what keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands, and it is also why the sub-committee stage is where a shipowner's or a class society's technical argument is actually won or lost - by the time the committee adopts, the engineering has been settled.

(b) Conventions and the other instruments

6. What a convention is

A convention is a formal, legally binding treaty between States. It is negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference convened for the purpose, or by the Assembly, and is then opened for signature, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession. Signature alone does not bind: the State becomes a Contracting Government only when it deposits its instrument with the Secretary-General as depositary.

A convention enters into force only when the conditions written into the convention itself are satisfied - typically a stated number of Contracting States whose combined merchant fleets represent a stated percentage of world gross tonnage, followed by a stated waiting period. It then binds only those States that have become party to it, and it reaches a ship through the flag State's own national law. Its detailed content is normally kept current not by re-negotiation but by amendment under its own amendment article, using tacit acceptance. Example: the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974, whose amendments are adopted by MSC under article VIII.

The three examples in the last row make the point that no single label settles the question. Two resolutions of the same committee, adopted eight months apart, do entirely different legal work: MEPC.377(80) carries a strategy and obliges no ship to do anything, while MEPC.385(81) adopts amendments to MARPOL Annex VI which, once in force, bind every Party. The label 'MEPC resolution' tells you nothing; the route does.

Adoption is not entry into force. MEPC.385(81) was adopted on 22 March 2024, four months before this sitting, and is still not binding on anyone: it is deemed accepted on 1 February 2025 and enters into force on 1 August 2025. The same distinction runs through the whole hierarchy, and it is the distinction the examiner is most likely to probe in the oral. Alongside these four instruments sit the non-binding working papers of the system - circulars, guidelines and unified interpretations (MSC.1/Circ., MEPC.1/Circ., MSC-MEPC. joint circulars). They explain how a requirement is to be applied and are heavily used at survey, but they create no obligation of their own unless a convention pulls them in by reference.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks in limb (a) sit in the function column, not in the names. Anyone can write 'MSC, MEPC, Legal, TC, FAL'; the mark comes from saying what each one is for. The examiner has also asked one narrow question inside limb (a) - what role the sub-committees play in supporting the main committees - and it is the part most candidates leave out. Step 5 answers it explicitly and states the negative as well as the positive: a sub-committee has no adoption power.

Limb (b) carries the same eight marks and is far quicker to write, so write it properly. A three-column table with a worked example in every row banks the marks in about a third of the time limb (a) takes.

The one idea that makes limb (b) easy

Candidates try to memorise which instruments are 'mandatory'. That model does not survive a follow-up question. The right model is: obligation comes from the treaty route, not from the label. The RO Code is the cleanest proof available - SOLAS regulation XI-1/1 makes parts 1 and 2 of that single Code mandatory and part 3 recommendatory. Same document, same title, two different legal statuses, decided entirely by what the Convention says about each part.

How a change actually reaches your ship

This is the thread that ties both limbs together and it is worth being able to state in the oral. A Member State submits a proposal to a committee. The committee accepts it into the work programme and remits the technical work to the sub-committee that holds the expertise. The sub-committee drafts, argues and reports back. The committee approves the draft and circulates it to Parties. At a later session the committee adopts it by resolution under the convention's amendment article. Tacit acceptance then runs: the amendment is deemed accepted on a stated date unless enough Parties object, and enters into force on a further stated date. The flag State gives it domestic effect, the certificate changes, and a surveyor arrives to verify it.

MEPC.385(81) is that sequence caught in the middle. It has been adopted; it has not been deemed accepted; it is not in force. Anyone who can walk an examiner along that chain has both limbs of this question, because limb (a) is the machinery and limb (b) is the instruments the machinery produces.

Common mistakes

  • Naming only MSC and MEPC as the 'main committees'. There are five, and the question says 'various main committees'.
  • Saying the Assembly meets annually. It meets in regular session every two years.
  • Describing the sub-committees as if they adopt regulation. They do not.
  • Writing that a Code is 'always mandatory' or 'always recommendatory'. Neither is true.
  • Confusing a Protocol with an amendment. An ordinary amendment is adopted under the convention's own amendment article; a Protocol is a separate treaty.

Understand this first

Start from what kind of document this is, because everything else follows. It is not a rule. It is the Organization writing down where it intends shipping to be by 2030, 2040 and 2050, and what kind of measures it will build to get there. So the answer has three layers that must not be blurred: how much reduction is promised, what mechanisms are meant to deliver it, and when each mechanism arrives. The mechanisms that already exist work by measuring a ship's design efficiency and her operational carbon per unit of work, and they are law today. The mechanisms still being designed work differently - one would cap how much greenhouse gas each unit of energy she burns may carry, counted all the way back to how that fuel was made, and the other would put a price on what she emits. Those two are a plan with dates, not an obligation. And the reason the whole thing is slow is a genuine tension: every ship must be treated alike whatever her flag, yet the States least able to absorb higher freight costs are the ones the measures would hit hardest.

Model written answer

1. Which instrument is 'the revised strategy', and what it replaced

The revised strategy is the 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships, adopted by the Marine Environment Protection Committee as resolution MEPC.377(80) on 7 July 2023 at MEPC 80. It replaces the Initial IMO GHG Strategy of 2018, resolution MEPC.304(72), which the 2023 resolution expressly revokes as from the date of adoption. The Committee agreed at the same time to keep the Strategy under review with a view to adopting a further revised strategy in 2028.

The Strategy is a policy instrument, not a regulation. It binds no ship directly. Obligation arrives only when a measure it calls for is adopted as an amendment to MARPOL Annex VI and enters into force. Saying that clearly is the first mark on this question, because it explains why the short-term measures already bite and the mid-term measures do not.

The Strategy sets out the scale of the problem in its own words: the Third IMO GHG Study 2014 estimated international shipping at some 2.2% of anthropogenic CO2 in 2012, and the Fourth IMO GHG Study 2020 at some 2.89% of global anthropogenic GHG in 2018, with emissions potentially reaching 90% to 130% of 2008 levels by 2050 if nothing is done.

2. The vision and the objectives

Vision. IMO remains committed to reducing GHG emissions from international shipping and, as a matter of urgency, aims to phase them out as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition.

Objectives. The Strategy states three:

  1. to enhance IMO's contribution to global efforts by addressing GHG emissions from international shipping - expressly linked to the Paris Agreement and to the UN 2030 Agenda and its SDG 13, urgent action to combat climate change;
  2. to identify actions to be implemented by the shipping sector, while addressing impacts on States and recognising shipping's role in global trade;
  3. to identify actions and measures to help achieve those objectives, including incentives for research and development and the monitoring of emissions.

3. The levels of ambition

The Strategy sets four levels of ambition, and they are the heart of the answer:

Level of ambitionWhat the Strategy commits to
Carbon intensity of the shipTo decline through further improvement of the energy efficiency of new ships - the Strategy commits to a review with the aim of strengthening the energy efficiency design requirements
Carbon intensity of international shippingTo decline: reduce CO2 emissions per transport work, as an average across international shipping, by at least 40% by 2030 compared to 2008
Uptake of zero or near-zero GHG emission technologies, fuels and/or energy sourcesTo increase, to represent at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030
GHG emissions from international shipping to reach net zeroTo peak emissions as soon as possible and reach net-zero GHG emissions by or around, i.e. close to, 2050, taking into account different national circumstances, while pursuing efforts towards phasing them out

Two points about how these are written repay attention. The 2050 ambition is net-zero GHG, not carbon-neutral CO2, and it is expressed as by or around 2050 rather than as a hard date - the qualification was the price of consensus and it is part of the text. And the whole set is to be read on a well-to-wake basis, taking into account the life cycle GHG intensity of marine fuels under the LCA guidelines (MEPC.376(80)), so that emissions are not simply shifted out of the ship's funnel and into the fuel's production chain.

4. The indicative checkpoints

Distinct from the levels of ambition, and often confused with them, are the two indicative checkpoints on the way to net zero. They are expressed as total annual GHG emissions, not as intensity:

  • reduce total annual GHG emissions from international shipping by at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, compared to 2008; and
  • reduce total annual GHG emissions from international shipping by at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040, compared to 2008.

The distinction is a real examination point. An intensity target can be met while total emissions rise, if the fleet and the trade grow; an absolute checkpoint cannot. The 2023 Strategy is the first IMO instrument to set absolute reduction figures at all, and that - more than any single percentage - is what makes it a step change from the 2018 Initial Strategy.

5. The guiding principles

  • The principles already enshrined in existing instruments: non-discrimination and no more favourable treatment, from MARPOL and the other IMO conventions; and common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities, in the light of different national circumstances, from the UNFCCC, its Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement.
  • The requirement that all ships give full and complete effect, regardless of flag, to mandatory measures implementing the Strategy.
  • The need to consider the impacts of measures on States, particularly least developed countries and small island developing States.
  • The need for evidence-based decision-making balanced with the precautionary approach.

The first two principles pull against each other, and knowing that is worth a mark. No more favourable treatment means a measure must apply to every ship on the same terms whatever her flag; common but differentiated responsibilities means States are not equally placed to bear the cost. Reconciling the two is the reason the mid-term measures carry a comprehensive impact assessment and a revenue-disbursement argument, rather than being simply drafted and adopted.

6. Short-term measures - and where they stand at this sitting

The Strategy defines short-term measures as those finalized and agreed by the Committee between 2018 and 2023. They are already in MARPOL Annex VI and they are the measures a ship is actually complying with today:

  • the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI) and the Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan (SEEMP), introduced by MEPC.203(62);
  • the IMO Data Collection System (DCS) for fuel oil consumption, introduced by MEPC.278(70) for ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above;
  • the Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index (EEXI), the operational Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) with its A to E rating, and the enhanced SEEMP including Part III, all introduced by MEPC.328(76), the 2021 revised Annex VI.

The Strategy also fixes a deadline for looking at them again: under regulations 25.3 and 28.11 of MARPOL Annex VI, the review of the mandatory goal-based technical and operational measures shall be completed by 1 January 2026. At this sitting that review is running and has not reported.

7. The basket of candidate mid-term measures

The Strategy requires a basket of candidate mid-term measures to be finalized and agreed by the Committee by 2025, comprising both of the following, together:

ElementWhat the Strategy specifies
Technical elementA goal-based marine fuel standard regulating the phased reduction of the marine fuel's GHG intensity - a limit on how much GHG each unit of energy used on board may carry, tightening over time, measured well-to-wake
Economic elementA measure on the basis of a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism - putting a price on the emissions, with the candidate designs to be assessed against specific criteria in the comprehensive impact assessment

Beyond the basket, the Strategy lists other candidate mid-term measures that may be finalized between 2023 and 2030: annual IMO GHG emission and carbon intensity estimates from the DCS; a feedback mechanism on implementation; further development of the LCA guidelines; a regulatory assessment of the safety aspects of reducing GHG emissions and a road map to support safe delivery; measures addressing methane and nitrous oxide and further work on volatile organic compounds; incentives for first movers; and measures encouraging port developments including onshore power supply from renewable sources. Potential synergies with the existing CII are expressly to be considered.

Where this stands at this sitting is the point on which the answer must be exact. The Strategy's own follow-up timetable sets approval of the measures at MEPC 83 in spring 2025, adoption at an extraordinary MEPC session about six months after MEPC 83, and entry into force sixteen months after adoption, in 2027. In July 2024 the basket has therefore been neither approved nor adopted, and no mid-term measure is in force. It is a programme of work with dates attached, and it must be described as one.

8. Long-term measures, the timetable and the review

  • Long-term measures are defined as measures finalized and agreed by the Committee beyond 2030, to be developed as part of the 2028 review of the Strategy. The Strategy deliberately does not specify them; that is the honest answer to the 'long-term goals' part of the question, and inventing content for it is a way to lose marks.
  • The follow-up timetable runs: MEPC 80 (2023) initiation of the comprehensive impact assessment and Phase III of the work plan; MEPC 81 (spring 2024) interim report and finalization of the basket; MEPC 82 (autumn 2024) finalized impact-assessment report; MEPC 83 (spring 2025) approval of measures; an extraordinary session about six months later for adoption; entry into force sixteen months after that.
  • The impacts of any measure on States are to be assessed and taken into account before adoption, against eight criteria: geographic remoteness and connectivity to main markets, cargo value and type, transport dependency, transport costs, food security, disaster response, cost-effectiveness, and socio-economic progress and development.
  • The Strategy is subject to a five-yearly review, the first due in 2028, taking account of updated emission estimates, reduction options, IPCC reports and data on the impacts of measures already applied.

The honest summary for a Chief Engineer is this. The short-term measures are law and are being enforced now. The mid-term measures are a defined programme of work with dates, not obligations - approval in 2025, adoption thereafter, entry into force around 2027. Study guide

Why this structure scores

This is a 16-mark question with no printed limbs, which means the candidate supplies the structure and gets no help. The stem contains four separate asks - key objectives, key measures, the short-, mid- and long-term goals, and the impact on industry - and each is worth real marks. The commonest failure is to write four hundred words about net zero by 2050 and stop. The route here spends roughly a quarter of the answer on ambitions and checkpoints, a quarter on the measures by timeline, and a quarter on impact, which matches where the marks are.

Numbers are the cheapest marks on this question and they are all quotable: 40% intensity by 2030, 5% striving for 10% uptake by 2030, 20% striving for 30% absolute by 2030, 70% striving for 80% by 2040, net zero by or around 2050. Learn those five lines and half the question is already banked.

The distinction that carries the most marks

Levels of ambition are not the same as indicative checkpoints. The 40% figure is carbon intensity - emissions per transport work. The 20 / 30 and 70 / 80 figures are total annual emissions. An intensity target can be met while total emissions rise, if the fleet grows; an absolute checkpoint cannot. The 2023 Strategy is the first IMO instrument to set absolute figures at all, and a candidate who explains that difference has demonstrated understanding rather than recall.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the Strategy as a regulation. It is a resolution carrying a policy. Obligation comes only when a measure is adopted as a MARPOL Annex VI amendment and enters into force.
  • Saying 'carbon neutral by 2050'. The Strategy says net-zero GHG emissions by or around, i.e. close to, 2050. GHG, not CO2; and 'by or around', not a hard date.
  • Quoting the 2018 Initial Strategy's 50% by 2050 figure. That Strategy was revoked by MEPC.377(80) as from 7 July 2023.
  • Muddling intensity and absolute targets.
  • Describing the mid-term measures as though they were in force.
  • Inventing content for the long-term measures. The Strategy defines them as measures agreed beyond 2030, to be developed in the 2028 review, and says no more.
  • Forgetting the well-to-wake basis, which is what makes the fuel standard a life-cycle instrument rather than a funnel measurement.

Examiner traps

  • The single biggest trap on this paper is answering this question with today's knowledge. At July 2024 the answer is MEPC.377(80) and nothing later. The IMO Net-Zero Framework, the Global Fuel Intensity mechanism and the approved basket all belong to 2025. Naming any of them dates the script instantly and, worse, misstates the law at the sitting.
  • MEPC 82 had not met. It sat 30 September to 4 October 2024, three months after this paper. Nothing may be attributed to it - including the 2024 SEEMP guidelines it adopted.
  • MEPC 81 had met, in March 2024, four months before. But the safe position is to describe the Strategy's own timetable rather than assert a session outcome: the basket was to be finalized at MEPC 81, approved at MEPC 83 in 2025 and adopted at an extraordinary session thereafter.
  • The review deadline is 1 January 2026, for the short-term measures under regulations 25.3 and 28.11. It is running at this sitting and has not reported.
  • FuelEU Maritime applies from 1 January 2025 and is future here. The EU ETS, by contrast, brought shipping into scope from 1 January 2024 and is operative - the two are constantly confused.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which resolution, and when? MEPC.377(80), adopted 7 July 2023 at MEPC 80.

  • What are the two elements of the basket? A goal-based marine fuel standard on GHG intensity, and an economic element on the basis of a GHG emissions pricing mechanism.
  • What does well-to-wake mean and why does it matter? Life-cycle GHG intensity from production to use, so emissions are not shifted from the ship to the fuel's supply chain.
  • Is your ship complying with the Strategy today? No - the ship complies with EEXI, CII, SEEMP and the DCS under MARPOL Annex VI. Those are the short-term measures the Strategy refers to.
  • What is the difference between the 40% target and the 20% checkpoint? Intensity per transport work against total annual emissions.

Understand this first

Think about what happens to a bad ship when she is sold. New name, new owner, new flag, new class - and, without this chapter, no way for anyone to know what she was before. So the Convention does two things. It gives the hull a number that never changes and marks it where paint cannot hide it, and it gives the ship a running history book that stays on board when the owner leaves, with earlier pages never removed and earlier books attached to the new one. The second limb is the same idea applied to steel rather than paperwork: for the two ship types that used to break up and sink, the survey is not left to a walk-round but is prescribed in advance - which spaces, how close, how many thickness readings - and the results are written into a file that follows the hull. Identity, paperwork and structure are all made to outlive the owner.

Model written answer

(a) Ship identification number and the Continuous Synopsis Record

1. Where these requirements sit, and what they were written to solve

SOLAS chapter XI-1, Special measures to enhance maritime safety, is the chapter that attacks a particular problem: a ship whose identity can be changed. A substandard ship that is sold, renamed, re-flagged and re-classed can shed its record and start again clean, and both the port State and the next buyer lose the history that would have condemned her. Chapter XI-1 answers this by giving every ship a number that never changes and a document that travels with the hull rather than with the owner. At this sitting the operative text is the Consolidated Edition 2024, which carries all amendments in effect on 1 July 2024.

The chapter also carries regulation 1 (authorization of recognized organizations under the RO Code), regulation 2 (enhanced surveys, which is limb (b)), regulation 4 (port State control on operational requirements), regulation 6 (casualty investigation under the Casualty Investigation Code) and regulation 7 (portable atmosphere testing instruments).

2. The ship identification number - regulation XI-1/3

  • Application: all passenger ships of 100 gross tonnage and upwards and all cargo ships of 300 gross tonnage and upwards.
  • Every such ship is provided with an identification number conforming to the IMO ship identification number scheme adopted by the Organization. The number is allocated to the hull and does not change on sale, change of name or change of flag.
  • The number is inserted on the certificates, and on certified copies, issued under regulation I/12 or I/13.
  • It must be permanently marked in two places - one visible from outside, one accessible from inside.

Every ship to which chapter I applies is issued with a Continuous Synopsis Record, issued by the Administration - not by class, not by the Company - to each ship entitled to fly its flag. For ships built before 1 July 2004 the history runs from 1 July 2004.

4. Keeping the CSR - the rules that give it its value

  • Changes to entries 3.4 to 3.12 are recorded so as to give the current position together with the history of the changes. The Administration issues a revised CSR or amendments not later than three months from the date of change, and, pending that, requires the Company or the master to amend the CSR, the Company then informing the Administration without delay.
  • The CSR is in English, French or Spanish, with a translation into the Administration's own language if desired, and in the format developed by the Organization.
  • Previous entries may never be modified, deleted, erased or defaced. This is the provision that makes it a record rather than a status sheet.
  • On transfer of flag, sale, change of bareboat charterer or change of Company, the CSR is left on board.
  • Before a transfer of flag the Company notifies its Administration of the incoming flag, the outgoing Contracting Government transmits to the new Administration a copy covering its own period together with any earlier CSRs, and the new Administration appends the previous records to the one it issues.
  • The CSR is kept on board and available for inspection at all times.

5. Why the two together enhance maritime safety

  • They defeat identity laundering. A permanent number plus an unbroken, appended history means a ship cannot escape her record by changing name, owner, flag or class.
  • They give the port State control officer an immediate picture before he inspects: how often the ship has changed flag or class, whether she has passed through several owners quickly, and who audited her management system. Those are classic targeting indicators, and they are on the bridge in one document.
  • They make flag-hopping and class-hopping visible rather than invisible, which deters both.
  • They tie the ship to a Company and to a registered owner by number, so responsibility under the ISM Code can be traced to a real entity even through a chain of single-ship companies.
  • They support casualty investigation and detention follow-up, since the history is on board and does not depend on the cooperation of a former owner.
  • Because the number is on the certificates as well as on the hull, they make certificate fraud harder: the paper and the steel have to agree.

(b) The Enhanced Survey Programme

6. What the ESP is, and which ships are subject to it

The Enhanced Survey Programme is a mandatory, prescribed programme of hull inspections that goes far beyond a general survey: it fixes, ship type by ship type and age band by age band, exactly which spaces must be entered, how much of the structure must be examined within arm's reach, how many thickness readings must be taken and where, and which tanks must be pressure tested. It exists because bulk carriers and oil tankers had the worst structural loss record of any ship types, and because those losses were caused by wastage and cracking that a walk-round survey does not find.

  • SOLAS regulation XI-1/2 requires bulk carriers as defined in regulation IX/1.6 and oil tankers as defined in regulation II-1/2.22 to be subject to an enhanced programme of inspections in accordance with the 2011 ESP Code, adopted by the Assembly as resolution A.1049(27) on 30 November 2011, as may be amended.
  • That is how the Code becomes mandatory: not because it is called a Code, but because regulation XI-1/2 says so - and its amendments must be adopted and brought into force under SOLAS article VIII.
  • The Code is organised as Annex A for bulk carriers - Part A for single-side skin construction, Part B for double-side skin - and Annex B for oil tankers, with a part for double-hull tankers.
  • Annex A Part A applies to self-propelled bulk carriers of 500 gross tonnage and above other than double-side skin bulk carriers. For hybrid arrangements, the double-side skin part governs the double-side skin holds and their wing spaces.
  • The surveys are carried out during the surveys prescribed by SOLAS regulation I/10 - they are not an extra survey regime bolted on, but a defined depth for the annual, intermediate and renewal surveys.
  • Regulation XI-1/2-1 allows cargo ships not subject to the ESP Code to harmonize their intermediate and renewal survey periods with the ESP Code periods.

Four definitions carry most of the technical weight and are worth knowing exactly. A transverse section is the cross section of the hull perpendicular to the centreline, including all longitudinal members. Suspect areas are locations showing substantial corrosion or considered by the surveyor to be prone to rapid wastage. Substantial corrosion is wastage in excess of 75% of the allowable margins but still within acceptable limits - and for ships built to the IACS Common Structural Rules it is a measured thickness between tnet + 0.5 mm and tnet. Coating condition is graded GOOD (only minor spot rusting), FAIR (local breakdown at edges of stiffeners and weld connections, or light rusting, over 20% or more of the area under consideration), or POOR (general breakdown of coating over 20% or more of the area, or hard scale at 10% or more). The coating grade is not cosmetic: a ballast tank whose coating falls to POOR moves into annual internal examination, so coating condition drives survey frequency.

8. The objectives of the ESP

  • To detect structural deterioration - wastage, cracking, coating breakdown and buckling - before it becomes a casualty, in the two ship types whose structural failures have historically been sudden and catastrophic.
  • To define a minimum extent of examination that is the same for every ship of a given type and age, so that a survey means the same thing whoever performs it and wherever it is performed.
  • To make condition measurable rather than impressionistic, through thickness measurement by a certified company and through graded coating condition.
  • To escalate automatically: where substantial corrosion or structural defect is found, the survey is extended and further close-up survey and measurement are required. The programme adapts to what the ship is actually telling the surveyor.
  • To force planning and owner participation rather than turn-up-and-look, through the survey programme, the planning meeting and the owner's own inspection reports.
  • To build a continuous structural history in the survey report file and the Condition Evaluation Report, so successive surveys measure a trend rather than a snapshot - which, like the CSR in limb (a), is the theme of this whole chapter: the ship's record must survive the change of owner.

Why this structure scores

A CSR that could be replaced on change of flag would be worthless, and the examiner is testing whether you know why.

The idea underneath both limbs

Chapter XI-1 is about continuity of identity and continuity of record. Limb (a) makes the ship's identity and her documentary history survive a change of owner. Limb (b) makes her structural history survive it too - the survey report file, the thickness measurement records and the Condition Evaluation Report follow the hull, not the company. Say that once and both limbs become one argument instead of two lists.

Common mistakes

  • Giving one marking location. There are two - externally visible and internally accessible - and the two heights differ: 200 mm and 100 mm.
  • Saying the CSR is issued by class or by the Company. It is issued by the Administration.
  • Saying the CSR is surrendered or replaced on change of flag. It is left on board, and the new Administration appends the previous records.
  • Writing that the ESP applies to 'all ships'. It applies to bulk carriers and oil tankers, by SOLAS regulation XI-1/2.
  • Treating the ESP as a separate survey. It is the prescribed depth of the surveys already required by regulation I/10.
  • Describing close-up survey as 'a detailed look'. It has a definition - within reach of hand - and quoting it costs one line and earns a mark.
  • Guessing a percentage for substantial corrosion. It is wastage in excess of 75% of the allowable margins, still within acceptable limits.

Understand this first

Two States are looking at the same ship for the same purpose, but from different positions. The State whose flag she flies has to build a working system - inspect her, certify her, and stand behind that certificate even when it has paid a class society to do the visiting. The State whose port she enters starts from trust: it must take the certificate at face value and look no further, because if every port re-ran the whole inspection the certificate would be worthless and the ship would never trade. What makes the arrangement safe rather than naive is that trust ends the moment there is a reason to doubt the paper - it is missing or false, what the officer can see contradicts it, the ship has switched flag to escape it, or someone living on board says it is not true. Only then does the port State dig, and only if it finds people in danger, or a breach that is serious or keeps recurring, may it stop the ship sailing.

Model written answer

1. Where these obligations live, and the one rule that makes Title 5 different

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 is built in three layers: Articles, Regulations, and a Code in two parts - Part A, mandatory Standards, and Part B, non-mandatory Guidelines. Compliance and enforcement is Title 5, and it divides exactly as this question does: Regulation 5.1 flag State responsibilities, Regulation 5.2 port State responsibilities, and Regulation 5.3 labour-supplying responsibilities.

One rule in the introduction to Title 5 is worth stating first because it is the mark most candidates miss. Elsewhere in the Convention, Article VI paragraphs 3 and 4 allow a Member to implement Part A of the Code through substantially equivalent provisions. That permission does not apply to Part A of the Code in Title 5. Enforcement machinery must be implemented as written - a State may run its labour standards its own way, but it may not run its enforcement its own way. Title 5 also states that seafarers and shipowners are equal before the law and entitled to its equal protection, and that Title 5 does not itself determine legal jurisdiction or venue.

2. Flag State - the general responsibility and the system it must build

  • Each Member is responsible for ensuring implementation of its obligations on ships that fly its flag (Regulation 5.1.1).
  • It must establish an effective system for the inspection and certification of maritime labour conditions, so that working and living conditions meet, and continue to meet, the standards of the Convention.
  • It must set clear objectives and standards for the administration of that system and adequate procedures for assessing whether they are being attained (Standard A5.1.1).
  • It must require every ship flying its flag to carry a copy of the Convention on board.
  • It reports on the system, including the method used to assess its effectiveness, to the International Labour Office under article 22 of the ILO Constitution.

3. Recognized organizations - what may be delegated, and what may not

  • The Member may authorize public institutions or other organizations - in practice classification societies - to carry out inspections, to issue certificates, or both. In all cases the Member remains fully responsible for the inspection and certification of the seafarers' conditions. Delegation of work is permitted; delegation of responsibility is not.
  • An organization is recognized only after the competent authority has reviewed its competency and independence and satisfied itself that it has the necessary expertise in the relevant aspects of the Convention and knowledge of ship operations; the ability to maintain and update that expertise; knowledge of the Convention, national law and relevant international instruments; and appropriate size, structure, experience and capability for the authorization given.
  • Any inspection authorization must, as a minimum, empower the organization to require the rectification of deficiencies it identifies and to carry out inspections at the request of a port State.
  • The Member must establish a system to ensure the adequacy of the organization's work and procedures for communication with and oversight of it, and must give the International Labour Office a current list of its recognized organizations and the functions each is authorized to perform, which the Office makes publicly available.

4. The Maritime Labour Certificate and the DMLC

Certification is compulsory for ships of 500 gross tonnage or over engaged on international voyages, and for ships of 500 gross tonnage or over flying a Member's flag and operating from a port, or between ports, in another country. Any other ship may be certified at the shipowner's request. Together the two documents are prima facie evidence that the ship has been duly inspected and meets the requirements to the extent certified.

5. Inspection and enforcement

  • The Member verifies through an effective and coordinated system of regular inspections, monitoring and other control measures that ships flying its flag comply.
  • It appoints a sufficient number of qualified inspectors, with the training, competence, terms of reference, powers, status and independence to do the work - independent of changes of government and of improper external influences.
  • Inspections take place at the intervals required for certification, and the interval shall in no case exceed three years.
  • On a complaint that is not manifestly unfounded, or evidence of non-conformity or of serious deficiencies in the measures set out in the DMLC, the Member investigates and ensures remedial action.
  • Inspectors may board a ship flying the Member's flag, carry out any examination, test or inquiry they consider necessary, require rectification, and - where deficiencies constitute a serious breach or a significant danger to seafarers' safety, health or security - prohibit the ship from leaving port. Any such action carries a right of appeal to a judicial or administrative authority.
  • Inspectors have discretion to advise instead of prosecuting where there is no clear breach endangering seafarers and no history of similar breaches; must treat the source of a complaint as confidential and give no intimation that an inspection followed from one; and must have no direct or indirect interest in anything they inspect and reveal no commercial secrets.
  • Every inspection is reported to the competent authority, with a copy in English or the ship's working language to the master and a copy posted on the notice board for the seafarers. Records are kept, an annual report is published within six months of the year end, and a report following a major incident goes to the authority within one month of the conclusion of the investigation.
  • All reasonable efforts are made to avoid a ship being unreasonably detained or delayed; compensation is payable for wrongful exercise of the inspectors' powers, the burden of proof being on the complainant; and adequate penalties must exist and be enforced for breaches and for obstructing inspectors.

6. On-board complaint procedures and marine casualties

  • Every ship must have on-board procedures for the fair, effective and expeditious handling of seafarer complaints (Regulation 5.1.5), and the Member must prohibit and penalize any kind of victimization of a seafarer for filing one.
  • The procedures seek resolution at the lowest level possible, but in all cases the seafarer has the right to complain directly to the master and, where he considers it necessary, to appropriate external authorities.
  • The seafarer has the right to be accompanied or represented, and every seafarer receives, with the employment agreement, a copy of the procedures - including the contact details of the competent authority in the flag State and in the seafarer's country of residence, and the name of a person on board who can give confidential, impartial advice.
  • Under Regulation 5.1.6 the flag State must hold an official inquiry into any serious marine casualty leading to injury or loss of life involving a ship flying its flag, and the final report is normally made public.

7. Port State - the starting position

  • Every foreign ship calling in the normal course of its business or for operational reasons in the port of a Member may be inspected, under Article V paragraph 4, to review compliance with the Convention including seafarers' rights (Regulation 5.2.1).
  • But the Member shall accept the Maritime Labour Certificate and the DMLC as prima facie evidence of compliance, and accordingly the inspection shall be limited to a review of the certificate and the declaration except in the circumstances specified in the Code. That is the default, and it is a real constraint on the officer.
  • Inspections are carried out by authorized officers, in accordance with the Code and other applicable international arrangements governing port State control.
  • Any inspection is limited to verifying conformity with the Articles, the Regulations and Part A only of the Code. Part B is guidance and cannot found a deficiency.
  • The Member must operate an effective port State inspection and monitoring system and report on it to the International Labour Office.

8. The more detailed inspection, and detention

A more detailed inspection may be carried out where the authorized officer finds that:

  1. the required documents are not produced or maintained, are falsely maintained, do not contain the required information, or are otherwise invalid;
  2. there are clear grounds for believing that the working and living conditions do not conform to the Convention;
  3. there are reasonable grounds to believe the ship has changed flag to avoid compliance; or
  4. there is a complaint alleging that specific conditions do not conform.

Such an inspection shall in any case be carried out where the conditions believed or alleged to be defective could constitute a clear hazard to the safety, health or security of seafarers, or where the officer has grounds to believe the deficiencies are a serious breach. Under grounds 1 to 3 the detailed inspection covers in principle the matters in Appendix A5-III; on a complaint it is generally limited to the scope of the complaint, though the complaint may itself provide clear grounds for a wider inspection.

Deficiencies are brought forthwith to the master with deadlines for rectification. Where they are significant, or relate to a complaint, the officer also brings them to the appropriate seafarers' and shipowners' organizations in the port State, and may notify a representative of the flag State and provide the next port of call with the information. A copy of the report, with any flag State reply, may be transmitted to the Director-General of the International Labour Office.

Detention has two grounds and only two. Where a more detailed inspection shows non-conformity and either (a) the conditions on board are clearly hazardous to the safety, health or security of seafarers, or (b) the non-conformity constitutes a serious or repeated breach of the Convention including seafarers' rights, the officer shall take steps to ensure the ship does not proceed to sea until the non-conformities are rectified or until he has accepted a plan of action to rectify them and is satisfied it will be implemented expeditiously. If the ship is prevented from sailing he must forthwith notify the flag State, invite its representative to be present if possible, request a reply within a prescribed deadline, and inform the shipowners' and seafarers' organizations in the port State. Every possible effort must be made to avoid undue detention or delay, and compensation is payable for loss or damage from undue detention, the burden of proof lying on the complainant.

9. Onshore complaint handling, and the third leg of Title 5

  • Under Regulation 5.2.2 a seafarer on a ship calling at a port may report an alleged breach to an authorized officer in that port, who undertakes an initial investigation.
  • The officer considers, where appropriate, whether the on-board complaint procedures have been explored, may conduct a more detailed inspection, and seeks to promote resolution at ship-board level.
  • If the investigation reveals a non-conformity within the detention grounds, those grounds apply. Otherwise, if the complaint is not resolved on board, the officer forthwith notifies the flag State, seeking advice and a corrective plan of action within a prescribed deadline.
  • If it is still unresolved, the port State transmits the report to the Director-General of the International Labour Office, with the flag State's reply, and informs the shipowners' and seafarers' organizations. Statistics on resolved complaints are submitted regularly, and confidentiality is safeguarded.
  • The third leg, Regulation 5.3, completes the scheme: a Member also has labour-supplying responsibilities for recruitment and placement services on its territory and for the social security protection of seafarers who are its nationals or residents, enforced through its own system of inspection, monitoring and legal proceedings. Flag, port and labour supply together are what make the Convention effective against a ship whose flag State will not act.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem gives you the two headings and nothing else, which is a trap disguised as a gift: candidates write two general essays about 'the flag State is responsible for its ships' and score badly. The marks are in the machinery - the certificate and its periods, the DMLC's two parts, the three-year inspection interval, the four triggers for a more detailed inspection, the two grounds for detention. Write the machinery and the discussion writes itself.

Balance matters. Limb (a) has more material than limb (b) and it is easy to spend twelve marks' worth of writing on the flag State. Sections 2 to 6 are limb (a); sections 7 to 9 are limb (b); stop limb (a) when you reach the complaint procedures.

The idea that organises the whole answer

The flag State certifies; the port State checks the certificate. Everything else follows from that division. It is why the certificate and the DMLC are prima facie evidence, why the port State inspection is limited to reviewing them unless something specific goes wrong, and why the four triggers are all reasons to stop believing the paperwork - it is missing or false, the conditions visibly contradict it, the ship has changed flag to escape it, or a seafarer says it is untrue. Once you see the triggers as 'reasons to doubt the certificate', they are impossible to forget.

Common mistakes

  • Treating MLC port State control as ordinary port State control. Here the officer must accept the certificate as prima facie evidence and is limited to reviewing it unless a trigger fires.
  • Founding a deficiency on Part B. A port State inspection is limited to the Articles, the Regulations and Part A only of the Code.
  • Saying the DMLC is issued by the flag State. Part I is; Part II is drawn up by the shipowner and then certified.
  • Forgetting that the certificate ceases to be valid on change of flag and when the shipowner ceases to be responsible.
  • Writing that the flag State may delegate to a recognized organization and is then discharged. It remains fully responsible.
  • Giving detention grounds vaguely. There are two: clearly hazardous conditions, or a serious or repeated breach - and an accepted plan of action is an alternative to continued detention.
  • Omitting Regulation 5.3 entirely. It is not asked, but one sentence showing you know Title 5 has three legs is cheap and reads well.

Examiner traps

  • The edition. At this sitting the operative text is MLC 2006 as amended through the 2018 amendments. The 2022 amendments do not enter into force until 23 December 2024, five months after this paper. Anything from the 2022 set - and there is a consolidated text carrying it in circulation - is a post-sitting import.
  • Substantial equivalence does not reach Title 5. Article VI paragraphs 3 and 4 are expressly disapplied to Part A of the Code in this Title. This is a real mark and almost nobody writes it.
  • Three years is a ceiling, not a period. The inspection interval shall in no case exceed three years; certification drives the actual pattern.
  • Six months, five years, five months, three anniversary dates. Interim certificate six months; full certificate five years; extension five months; intermediate inspection between the second and third anniversary dates. These four numbers are the most examinable facts in limb (a).
  • Being asked in the oral 'can the port State inspect hours of rest?' Only if a trigger has fired - otherwise the inspection is limited to reviewing the certificate and declaration.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Who draws up each part of the DMLC? Part I the competent authority, Part II the shipowner, certified by the authority or the RO.
  • Your ship changes flag next week - what happens to the Maritime Labour Certificate? It ceases to be valid; an interim certificate may be issued for up to six months on the change of flag.
  • What must a port State officer do before detaining? Establish non-conformity on a more detailed inspection and either clearly hazardous conditions or a serious or repeated breach; then either rectification or an accepted plan of action; then notify the flag State forthwith.
  • A seafarer complains to you as Chief Engineer. What does the Convention require? Handling under the on-board procedures, resolution at the lowest level, no victimization, and his right to go directly to the master or to external authorities preserved.
  • Where would you find the list of matters inspected before certification? Appendix A5-I - sixteen matters, from minimum age to financial security.

Understand this first

Two machines have to work together that behave nothing like each other. The propeller can only take power in one fixed relationship between how fast it turns and how hard it is being driven, and that relationship changes as the hull gets dirty or the weather gets bad. The engine has boundaries of its own - most importantly a limit on how much torque it can produce at low speed without getting too hot, because at low speed the turbocharger is not delivering much air. So the designer draws both on one picture and then deliberately builds in slack. He sizes the propeller for a perfect day - clean hull, flat water - and then rates the engine well above that, and arranges for the engine to reach its rating at slightly higher revolutions than that perfect day would ask for. The slack is there to be used up: as the hull fouls, the ship eats into it. When it is gone she is not just slow, she is being driven towards the limit that exists to stop her cooking her own engine.

Model written answer

(a) Propeller curves and the safety margins

1. The propeller law, and what a propeller demand curve is

For a fixed pitch propeller working in a given hull and weather condition, the power the propeller absorbs varies approximately as the cube of the rate of revolution, and the torque approximately as the square. Plotted as power against revolutions on the same axes the engine builder uses for the engine, that relation is the propeller demand curve - the locus of every operating point the engine can occupy while driving that propeller in that condition.

The essential point, and it governs the whole question, is that there is not one curve but a family of them. Each hull and weather condition has its own. The reference curve is the light propeller curve, corresponding to a clean hull, calm water and trial conditions - a newly docked ship on a still day. Every deterioration - fouling, roughness, heavy weather, a following sea, shallow water, a damaged or over-pitched propeller, ice, a deep or trimmed-by-the-head condition - moves the curve to the left: the same power is now absorbed at fewer revolutions, or the same revolutions demand more power.

2. The significance of the propeller curve

  • It is the matching tool. The engine and the propeller are two machines with entirely different characteristics, and the curve is where they are made to agree. Nothing about propulsion design is settled until the propeller demand curve and the engine's own limits are drawn on one diagram.
  • It defines the engine's actual working line. An engine driving a fixed pitch propeller cannot operate anywhere on its diagram; it can only operate along the propeller curve. Power and revolutions are not independent variables.
  • It shows whether the operating point is safe. Drawn against the engine's torque, mean effective pressure and speed limits, it shows immediately whether the engine is working in the region it was designed for or is being driven towards a protective limit.
  • It is the diagnostic instrument for hull and propeller condition. Because a change in hull or propeller condition moves the curve, comparing the ship's actual power-and-revolutions record against the design curve is how fouling, propeller damage and increased resistance are detected - and how the case for docking or a hull clean is made in figures rather than in impressions.
  • It is the basis of the margins. The engine is deliberately not laid out on the light curve; the distances between the curves and the layout point are the margins, which is limb A's second half.
  • It underlies performance monitoring and, now, regulatory reporting. Shaft power, revolutions, fuel index and speed logged against the design curve are what turn a fuel bill into an engineering diagnosis, and the same data feeds carbon-intensity reporting.
  • For a controllable pitch propeller it becomes a family of curves, one per pitch setting, and the combinator curve is the chosen path across them - which is why a CPP ship can be driven off the intended relation by a combinator setting as easily as by a foul hull.

3. The engine layout and load diagram - the limits the curve must live inside

The engine builder plots the engine's own boundaries on the same power-against-speed axes. The layout diagram is the area within which the specified maximum continuous rating may be chosen at the design stage, bounded by the maximum and minimum permissible mean effective pressures and the maximum and minimum speeds. Once the rating is chosen, the load diagram gives the boundaries within which the engine may actually be operated:

  • the maximum speed limit, on the right;
  • the torque / speed limit, which slopes down to the left and is the binding limit at reduced speed. It exists principally to protect the engine against thermal overload - too much fuel per cycle for the air the turbocharger can deliver at that speed;
  • the maximum mean effective pressure limit for continuous operation, which binds at high load and speed but is not the limiting boundary at low speed;
  • the overload limit, available only for a restricted period at sea trials or as the builder permits;
  • and, at the bottom left, the minimum speed below which the engine will not run steadily.

A heavy-running ship moves up and to the left on this diagram - more torque for fewer revolutions - which drives it towards the torque limit. That is why heavy running is a mechanical problem and not merely an economic one.

6. The relation itself

Stated in one sentence: the maximum rated output of the propulsion plant is deliberately and substantially greater than the power for which the propeller is designed, and it is displaced to higher revolutions as well as to higher power.

  • Vertically, SMCR exceeds the propeller design power by the sea margin and then by the engine margin. The propeller design power is the ideal-condition requirement; the plant rating is that requirement plus what a real hull in a real sea needs, plus a reserve.
  • Horizontally, SMCR is displaced from the light propeller curve by the light running margin, so that as the hull fouls and the curve moves left, the operating point moves back towards the engine's layout curve rather than past the torque limit.
  • The propeller is therefore matched to a condition the ship almost never occupies - clean hull, calm water, trial draught - and the engine is matched to the condition she will actually spend her life in. That deliberate mismatch is the design, not a defect.
  • The consequence in service. A new ship on trials is light running: she reaches her contract speed at fewer revolutions and less power than the layout point, and the fuel index is low. As the hull fouls the curve migrates left and the ship consumes the light running margin. When the margin is gone she is heavy running, or torque rich: full power now requires the engine to be driven towards its torque limit, the engine is thermally overloaded before it is speed-limited, and the ship can no longer make her speed.
  • The reverse case is real too. An over-generous light running margin, or a propeller that turns out to be under-pitched, leaves the engine unable to reach its rated power without exceeding its speed limit - the ship 'runs away' on revolutions and never develops full power. Both directions are matching failures.
  • For a controllable pitch propeller the relation is managed by the combinator: pitch and revolutions are chosen together so the operating point follows a path across the family of curves that keeps the engine inside its load diagram. The same margins are still designed in; they are simply defended by the combinator programme instead of by fixed geometry.
  • Two limits sit above all of this. The engine cannot be operated above the SMCR except within the overload allowance and for the stated period; and where the engine's power has been limited for regulatory purposes, the effective ceiling is the limited power and the whole matching relation must be read against it.

7. When the relation breaks down - and what the Chief Engineer does

  • Recognise it from the diagram, not from the fuel bill. Plot the ship's actual power and revolutions against the design curve. Heavy running shows as an operating point above and left of where it should be: lower revolutions for the same power, higher fuel index, higher mean effective pressure and Pmax, rising exhaust temperatures, falling scavenge air pressure margin, turbocharger working harder and, in the worst case, surging and visible smoke.
  • Confirm before acting. Check the instrumentation first - a shaft power meter or torsionmeter reading wrongly has sent more than one ship to a hull clean it did not need. Then exclude the environmental causes: draught and trim, shallow water and squat, current, wind and sea state.
  • Then separate hull from propeller. A gradual, progressive change since the last docking points to hull fouling and roughness; a step change points to propeller damage, a rope or net around the boss, or a changed pitch. Underwater inspection settles it.
  • Protect the engine while the cause is being found. Reduce power to bring the operating point back inside the load diagram, keep within the torque limit rather than chasing revolutions, watch exhaust temperature spread and turbocharger condition, and do not use the overload allowance to hold a schedule.
  • Remedy. Propeller polishing and hull cleaning restore the curve; a de-pitched or over-pitched propeller requires a class-approved pitch correction; and persistent mismatch is a technical-management matter, not an engine-room one.
  • Report and record. Performance data, the diagnosis and the action taken go to the Company through the safety management system and the technical department. The Chief Engineer's authority is to protect the machinery and to establish the facts; the decision to dock, clean or re-pitch belongs ashore, and the engineering case is what gets it made.

Why this structure scores

Two limbs at eight marks each, and the second is a drawing question in disguise. Limb B says 'using typical engine power versus engine speed diagram and propeller demand curves' - so draw it. A clear sketch with the light propeller curve, the load diagram boundaries and the four points marked earns marks that three paragraphs of prose cannot, and it takes a fraction of the time.

Limb A contains two separate commands. 'Explain the significance' is a reasoning task and 'enumerate the safety margins' is a list task, and both must be done. A script that explains the curve beautifully and then names one margin loses half of limb A.

The one idea behind the whole question

The propeller is designed for a condition the ship almost never occupies, and the engine is rated for the condition she actually lives in. That is the relation limb B asks for, in one sentence. The propeller design point is clean hull, calm water, trial draught. The engine's rating is that power plus the sea margin plus the engine margin, and shifted to the right by the light running margin. Everything else - why a new ship is light running, why fouling makes an engine thermally overloaded rather than merely slow, why the margins are not interchangeable - falls out of it.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the propeller curve as a single curve. It is a family, one per hull and weather condition, and the light curve is only the reference.
  • Confusing the three margins. Sea margin buys power; engine margin buys headroom below the rating; light running margin buys revolutions. They do different jobs and are not interchangeable.
  • Saying the engine is laid out on the light propeller curve. It is deliberately laid out to the right of it.
  • Describing heavy running as an efficiency problem only. It drives the operating point towards the torque limit, which is a thermal protection.
  • Saying the mean effective pressure limit is what stops a heavy-running engine. At reduced speed the torque/speed limit is the stricter one.
  • Not drawing the diagram when limb B expressly asks for one.
  • Quoting a fouling penalty or efficiency-gain percentage that cannot be sourced. The margin ranges given here are attributed engine-builder guidance; a fouling percentage is not, so do not invent one.

Examiner traps

  • 'Which comes first, the propeller or the engine?' The propeller design point is derived from the required ship speed in the trial condition; the engine rating is then built up from it by the margins. Getting the direction of the derivation right is the mark.
  • The light running margin is designed in, not a fault. A new ship that is light running on trials is correct. Saying she is 'over-propelled' is wrong.
  • The over-generous case. Too much light running margin, or an under-pitched propeller, leaves the engine unable to reach rated power without exceeding its speed limit. Examiners like this because most candidates only know the fouling direction.
  • The 20 per cent to 1 per cent sensitivity. A very large power penalty shows up as a very small revolution change - which is why the condition is missed and why performance monitoring, not the watchkeeper's impression, finds it.
  • Instrumentation. Be ready to say that you would confirm the shaft power meter or torsionmeter before condemning the hull.
  • Where authority stops. The Chief Engineer protects the machinery and establishes the facts; docking, hull cleaning and re-pitching are technical-management decisions ashore.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which limit does a heavy-running engine approach, and why does that limit exist? The torque/speed limit, which protects against thermal overload.
  • Name the three design margins and say what each buys. Sea margin - power; engine margin - headroom below the rating; light running margin - revolutions.
  • Where does the propeller design point sit? On the light propeller curve, at the power for the design speed in the trial condition.
  • Your ship makes her revolutions but not her speed. What do you suspect? Instrument error, draught or trim, current or shallow water, or a propeller problem - check the instrumentation and the environment before condemning the hull.
  • What would you do before using the overload allowance to keep a schedule? Not use it - reduce power, bring the point back inside the load diagram, and report.

Too generous a light running margin, or an under-pitched propeller, leaves the engine unable to reach its rated power without exceeding its speed limit - the ship over-revs and never develops full power. It is a matching failure in the other direction, and examiners use it because most candidates only know the fouling case.

Knowing both directions demonstrates the relation is understood rather than memorised.

Confirm the instrumentation before condemning the hull. Exclude draught and trim, shallow water, current and sea state. Separate hull from propeller: a gradual change since docking suggests fouling, a step change suggests propeller damage or a fouled boss. Protect the engine by reducing power to stay inside the load diagram, without using the overload allowance to hold a schedule. Then remedy, and report through the SMS to the technical department, which owns the docking decision.

Confirm, exclude, separate, protect, remedy, report - and knowing where your authority stops.

Understand this first

Both halves of this question are about who ends up paying, and both turn on a deliberate gap. In the first, the law had got itself into a corner: a shipowner was excused from paying for damage his crew's carelessness caused, but was also barred from asking anyone else to share the cost of the rescue that carelessness made necessary. So he paid for the rescue of everybody's property out of his own pocket. The trade fixed that by writing a term into the carriage contract - if I am not answerable for what went wrong, you still share the rescue cost with me - and the courts upheld it. Note what it does not do: it only works while he can show he had done his part in making the ship fit to sail. In the second half the gap is on purpose. The hull insurer pays only three quarters of what the owner owes another ship after a collision, and nothing at all for wrecks, oil, injuries or his own cargo, precisely so that the owner is never indifferent to whether a collision happens. That is why a clause in an insurance policy ends up shaping how the engine room maintains the steering gear.

Model written answer

1. Where these two clauses live, and why that matters

The two halves of this question belong to different contracts, and the marks begin with saying so. The New Jason Clause is a clause of the contract of carriage - it appears in the bill of lading or the charterparty and it governs the relationship between the shipowner and the cargo interests. The 3/4ths Collision Clause is a clause of the hull and machinery policy of insurance - it governs the relationship between the shipowner and his hull underwriter. One decides who bears a loss; the other decides who pays for a liability the shipowner has already incurred.

In the Indian jurisdiction the contract of marine insurance is governed by the Marine Insurance Act, 1963. General average adjustment is not governed by statute at all: it is settled by the York-Antwerp Rules, which are a contractual code and take effect only because the bill of lading or charterparty incorporates them - which is why those documents name the edition. The 2016 edition, adopted by the Comité Maritime International at Beijing, is the current one, and the 1994 edition is still met in older contracts. They are not a convention: they have no entry into force of their own and bind only through incorporation, so the edition named in the contract is the edition that governs.

2. General average, in outline

Under Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure. Where there is such an act, the sacrifice or expenditure is shared rateably between all the interests saved - ship, cargo, bunkers and freight at risk - in proportion to their values.

Every element must be present, and the absence of any one defeats the claim: a real peril to the common adventure; an act that is extraordinary, not something the ship was already bound to do; intentional and voluntary, not accidental; reasonable; for the common safety rather than for one interest alone; and there must be a saving of property, because if nothing is saved there is nothing to contribute. Typical acts are jettison of cargo, voluntary stranding, water damage from firefighting, machinery damage sustained in refloating, and salvage, towage and port of refuge expenses.

Separate general average from particular average at once, because that distinction is itself a mark. Particular average is accidental partial loss or damage, and it is borne by the owner of the property damaged alone. General average is a deliberate sacrifice or expenditure for the common safety, and it is shared rateably by ship, cargo and freight. Not everything that goes wrong in a casualty is general average.

Two limits follow, and both are commonly missed. Under Rule C, only such losses, damages or expenses which are the direct consequence of the general average act shall be allowed as general average; loss or damage sustained as a consequence of delay, such as market loss, is not allowed, however real the commercial injury. And under Rule VI, salvage payments are not allowed in general average - special compensation payable to the salvor under Article 14 of the International Convention on Salvage, 1989, or under any similar provision such as SCOPIC, is treated as particular charges and falls outside the adjustment.

Rule D is the rule this question turns on, and it is the one candidates leave out. It provides that rights to contribution in general average shall not be affected, though the event which gave rise to the sacrifice or expenditure may have been due to the fault of one of the parties to the adventure; but this shall not prejudice any remedies or defences which may be open against or to that party in respect of such fault. Read it carefully, because it does two things at once. The adjustment still proceeds - fault does not stop the general average being drawn up and apportioned. But the fault is not forgiven: it is preserved as a matter of remedies and defences under the law applicable to the contract. So whether the shipowner actually keeps what the adjustment awards him depends on whether cargo has a defence arising out of his fault - and that is precisely the opening the New Jason Clause was written to close.

3. The problem the New Jason Clause was written to solve

The problem is a gap between two rules that were made at different times and did not fit together.

  • By the end of the nineteenth century, carriage legislation - and later the international carriage regime - relieved the carrier of liability for loss caused by the negligence of the master or crew in the navigation or management of the ship, provided he had exercised due diligence to make the vessel seaworthy.
  • But the courts held separately that a shipowner cannot claim contribution in general average where the peril arose from a fault of his own side. The reasoning was that a party may not profit from the consequences of its own default.
  • The result was an absurdity. The shipowner was not liable to cargo for the navigational negligence that caused the stranding, yet he could not recover from cargo any part of the extraordinary expenditure he then incurred to save cargo from the consequences of it. He bore the whole of a general average sacrifice made for everybody's benefit.
  • Shipowners answered this by writing a clause into the bill of lading. An early form was upheld by the courts in the litigation from which the clause takes its name, and the modern form is called the New Jason Clause because it is the successor to that first version.

4. What the clause does - and what it demands in return

  • It provides that, in the event of accident, danger, damage or disaster before or after commencement of the voyage resulting from any cause whatsoever, whether due to negligence or not, for which the carrier is not responsible by statute, contract or otherwise, the cargo and the other interests shall contribute in general average with the carrier.
  • So the operative condition is not that nobody was negligent, but that the carrier is not answerable for the negligence. Where the carriage regime exempts him from liability for negligent navigation, the clause restores his right to contribution.
  • The protection is conditional and the condition is the shipowner's. The exemption on which the clause depends is itself conditional on the carrier having exercised due diligence to make the vessel seaworthy. A shipowner whose ship was unseaworthy through want of due diligence loses the exemption and with it the clause - so the clause protects the diligent shipowner and does nothing for the negligent one. This is the single most important thing to say about it.
  • It also secures the shipowner's position on salvage and special charges incurred in the same circumstances.
  • It is a contractual device and takes effect only if it is in the contract of carriage. It is standard in modern bill of lading and charterparty forms.

That is the figure the security taken before release is designed to protect.

6. The 3/4ths Collision Clause - what the hull policy covers

The 3/4ths Collision Clause, historically also called the Running Down Clause, is the provision of a hull and machinery policy by which the hull underwriters agree to indemnify the assured for three-fourths of any sum the assured becomes legally liable to pay, and pays, to another party by reason of the insured vessel coming into collision with another vessel. The heads of liability it responds to are, in the standard market form:

  • loss of or damage to the other vessel, and to property on board her;
  • delay to, or loss of use of, the other vessel or the property on board her;
  • general average, salvage, or salvage under contract of the other vessel or the property on board her.
  • The cover is three-fourths of the liability, and it is limited to three-fourths of the sum insured under the hull policy in respect of any one collision.
  • Legal costs of defending liability or of taking proceedings to limit it are also borne on the same three-fourths basis, normally subject to the underwriters' prior written consent.
  • It responds to collision with another vessel only. Striking a berth, a crane, a buoy, a bridge, a lock gate or an offshore installation is not a collision for this purpose - contact with fixed and floating objects falls outside it.
  • Where both vessels are to blame, liabilities are ordinarily settled on the cross-liability principle: each vessel is treated as paying its proportion of the other's damages, rather than a single net balance being struck. Which basis applies materially affects what the hull underwriter pays, and is one reason the clause is worth understanding rather than memorising.

7. What it excludes, and where the rest goes

The clause is defined as much by what it leaves out. In the standard market form the collision liability cover does not extend to:

  • removal or disposal of obstructions, wrecks or cargoes, or anything whatever;
  • real or personal property other than another vessel and property on board her - which is the fixed and floating object exclusion again;
  • the cargo or other property on, or the engagements of, the insured vessel herself;
  • loss of life, personal injury or illness;
  • pollution or contamination of any real or personal property, other than another vessel with which the insured vessel is in collision and property on board her.

Those excluded heads, and the remaining one-fourth of the collision liability, are the province of protection and indemnity cover - the mutual clubs. So a single collision typically produces two claims from one incident: three-fourths of the ship-to-ship damage from the hull underwriter, and the remaining quarter plus wreck removal, pollution, personal injury and the insured ship's own cargo liabilities from the club. Coordinating those two, and notifying both promptly, is a real operational obligation on the shipowner.

Why three-fourths and not all of it? The uninsured quarter was originally deliberate: leaving the shipowner exposed to a share of every collision liability gave him a direct financial interest in navigating carefully and in defending claims properly, rather than being indifferent to an outcome his underwriter would pay for in full. That is a classic answer to moral hazard. In modern practice the quarter is itself usually insured by the P&I club, so the deterrent largely operates through the deductible, the claims record and the premium instead. Some modern hull forms provide four-fourths cover, and a candidate should say that the fraction is a feature of the policy form rather than a rule of law.

Every one of those six asks carries marks, and the commonest failure on this question is to define both clauses well and answer none of the four 'implications' or 'behaviour' asks. The idea that makes limb (a) make sense

Two rules were made independently and left a hole between them. Rule one: the carrier is not liable to cargo for the crew's negligent navigation, provided the ship was made seaworthy with due diligence. Rule two: a party cannot claim general average contribution for a peril its own side caused. Put together, the shipowner is not liable for the stranding but must nevertheless pay the whole cost of getting everyone off it. The New Jason Clause closes the hole by contract: if the carrier is not responsible for the cause, cargo still contributes. Say it that way and both the mechanism and the condition follow.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the New Jason Clause an insurance clause. It is a clause of the contract of carriage - bill of lading or charterparty.
  • Saying it protects the shipowner against his own negligence generally. It operates only where the carrier is not responsible for the cause, which depends on due diligence to make the vessel seaworthy.
  • Treating the York-Antwerp Rules as law. They are a contractual code, effective only when incorporated, which is why the contract names the edition.
  • Saying the 3/4ths Collision Clause covers all collision liability. It covers three-fourths, of a defined set of heads, arising from collision with another vessel.
  • Forgetting the fixed and floating object point. Striking a berth, buoy or bridge is not a collision for this clause.
  • Omitting the exclusions - wreck removal, pollution, personal injury, the insured ship's own cargo - and where they go, which is P&I.
  • Answering 'how does it influence behaviour' with one line. It is expressly asked and it is where the Chief Engineer's own material belongs.

Examiner traps

  • Quoting clause wording. Do not. Name the clause and state its effect. A misquoted clause is worse than an accurate description, and no examiner expects a recital.
  • The seaworthiness condition. Expect 'so does the New Jason Clause help a shipowner whose ship was unseaworthy?' No - it fails with the exemption it depends on.
  • Cross-liabilities. Where both ships are to blame, liabilities are ordinarily settled on the cross-liability principle rather than as a single net balance. Knowing the term is a mark.
  • Why three-fourths? The uninsured quarter was a deliberate moral-hazard device. Today it is usually covered by the club, and some forms give four-fourths - so the fraction is a feature of the policy, not a rule of law.
  • The two-insurer point. One collision, two notifications, and a duty not to admit liability. It is the practical answer an examiner is listening for.
  • The engineering link. Blackout, steering gear failure and astern failure are collision causes. Being able to connect the clause to critical-equipment maintenance is what makes this a Chief Engineer's answer rather than a lawyer's.

  • Who pays for wreck removal after a collision? Not the hull collision clause - it is expressly excluded. The club.

One collision, two insurers - the exclusions are as examinable as the cover.

The uninsured quarter was deliberate: leaving the owner exposed to part of every collision liability gave him a direct financial interest in avoiding collisions and defending claims. Today the club usually covers the quarter, so the deterrent works mainly through the deductible, the claims record and renewal terms. No. Under Rule D of the York-Antwerp Rules, rights to contribution are not affected even though the event was due to the fault of one of the parties - but this does not prejudice any remedies or defences open against or to that party in respect of that fault. Understand this first

Almost all the nitrogen oxide a diesel engine makes is not coming from the fuel. It is made out of the air itself, in the hottest part of the flame, and the amount made climbs very steeply as that peak temperature rises. Everything in this question is a different response to that single fact. The rules set a ceiling that gets lower in steps, and which step you sit on depends on when the ship was built and, for the strictest step, where she is at the time. One method attacks the cause by carrying water into the flame so it burns cooler. Another gives up on preventing it and instead sprays a chemical into the exhaust that turns the gas back into nitrogen and water over a catalyst. And because all of this is proved once on a test bed and then trusted for twenty-five years, the last part of the question is the document that records exactly which components and settings the engine was certified with - so that nobody can quietly undo the compliance with a spare part.

Model written answer

1. Regulation 13 - which engines are caught, and which are not

NOx from ships is controlled by MARPOL Annex VI regulation 13. It applies to each marine diesel engine with a power output of more than 130 kW installed on a ship, and to each such engine that undergoes a major conversion on or after 1 January 2000 unless it is an identical replacement. A major conversion means the engine is replaced or an additional engine installed, or a substantial modification is made as defined in the NOx Technical Code, or the maximum continuous rating is increased by more than 10% of the original certification.

It does not apply to an engine used solely for emergencies, to an engine powering equipment used solely for emergencies, or to a lifeboat engine; nor to an engine on a ship trading solely within the waters of her own flag State where that State has imposed an alternative NOx control measure. The Administration may also exclude engines on ships constructed, or converted, before 19 May 2005 trading solely to ports within the flag State.

2. Tier I, Tier II and Tier III - the limits, and what fixes which one applies

The limits are expressed as the total weighted emission of NO2 in grams per kilowatt-hour, and they depend on n, the rated engine speed in crankshaft revolutions per minute. The tier is fixed by the ship's date of construction, not by the engine's design.

Tier IIon or after 1 Jan 2011 - the global standard14.4 g/kWh44 · n-0.237.7 g/kWh
Tier IIIas below, and only while inside a NOx Tier III ECA3.4 g/kWh9 · n-0.22.0 g/kWh

The two that matter to a Chief Engineer are the last two. Tier II is the global standard and applies everywhere, all the time, to a ship built on or after 1 January 2011. It is achievable by engine design alone - injection timing and rate shaping, injector and nozzle geometry, compression ratio, turbocharging and Miller timing - and needs no aftertreatment. Tier III is roughly a 75% reduction on Tier II at slow-speed rating and cannot be met by engine tuning; it needs selective catalytic reduction or exhaust gas recirculation, or operation on a fuel such as gas in a low-NOx combustion process.

3. Where Tier III applies - the areas, and the logbook

  • Tier III bites only inside a NOx Tier III emission control area, and only for ships constructed on or after the relevant date: 1 January 2016 for the North American and United States Caribbean Sea areas, and 1 January 2021 for the Baltic Sea and North Sea areas. For any later-designated area, the trigger is construction on or after the date of adoption of that designation, or a later date specified.
  • Outside those areas the same ship reverts to Tier II. That is why a Tier III engine is certified to both tiers, and why the aftertreatment can be shut down at sea.
  • The recording requirement is the one that reaches the engine room. Where an engine is certified to both Tier II and Tier III, or to Tier II only, the tier and the on/off status must be recorded in the logbook or electronic record book prescribed by the Administration on entry into and exit from a NOx Tier III emission control area, and whenever the status changes within one, together with the date, time and position of the ship.
  • There are narrow exceptions - small recreational craft, and ships whose combined nameplate propulsion power is under 750 kW where compliance is shown to be impossible for design or construction reasons - and a temporary exemption for a ship on delivery, sea trials, or proceeding directly to or from a shipyard inside such an area, provided the engine meets the Tier II limits and the ship does not work cargo.
  • Do not confuse these areas with the SOx emission control areas, which are a different and longer list. The NOx Tier III areas at this sitting are the four above.

4. Homogenizers for water emulsion

What it is. A homogenizer is a mechanical device fitted in the fuel supply line - typically a high-shear rotor and stator, or a valve through which the mixture is forced at high pressure - which disperses a controlled quantity of fresh water into the heavy fuel oil as very fine droplets, producing a stable water-in-oil emulsion. The water is metered in ahead of the homogenizer; the homogenizer's job is to break it into droplets small enough that the emulsion does not separate before the fuel reaches the injector.

Why it reduces NOx. Almost all NOx from a diesel engine is thermal NO, formed from atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen in the hottest zone of the flame, and its formation rate rises very steeply with local peak temperature. The water carried into that zone absorbs its latent heat of vaporisation and its own sensible heat, and it increases the mass being heated. The result is a lower and flatter peak combustion temperature and a shorter time spent at that temperature, and therefore less thermal NO. The mechanism is temperature, not chemistry - nothing is being converted, the flame is simply being cooled.

Secondary benefits. Each water droplet flashes to steam inside the surrounding oil droplet and bursts it - micro-explosion - which improves atomisation and mixing. That can reduce soot and unburnt hydrocarbon, and it is one reason homogenizers are also fitted purely as fuel-treatment devices, where they additionally disperse the small amounts of water and sludge already present in bunkers and reduce separator losses.

The operational price, which is what the examiner wants. Water in the fuel means lower calorific value per unit mass, so consumption in mass terms rises for the same power. It means higher risk of corrosion in the fuel system and the exhaust path, and of injector and pump wear if the emulsion is unstable or the water is not fresh and treated. It means ignition delay increases, so the engine may need re-timing and may run rougher, and there are limits on how much water an engine builder will accept. Emulsified fuel cannot be stored, so the plant must run on-line and be capable of being bypassed instantly - a homogenizer failure must not stop the engine. And because the emulsion changes the fuel actually burned, its use has to be consistent with the engine's certified condition.

5. Selective Catalytic Reduction

What it is. SCR is an exhaust aftertreatment system: a reactor containing a catalyst, usually vanadium- or zeolite-based on a ceramic or metallic honeycomb, into which a reducing agent is injected upstream. The agent is normally a urea solution, which hydrolyses in the hot gas to ammonia, or ammonia directly. Over the catalyst the ammonia reduces the oxides of nitrogen to nitrogen and water vapour. It is the only aftertreatment that achieves Tier III on a conventional oil-fuelled engine across the full load range.

Arrangement. On a two-stroke engine the reactor is normally fitted before the turbocharger, on the high-pressure side, because the exhaust leaving a slow-speed engine's turbine is too cold for the catalyst to work; on four-stroke and auxiliary engines it is usually fitted after the turbocharger, where the temperature is already high enough. The plant comprises a urea storage tank and heated supply and dosing system, an injection and mixing section, the reactor with its catalyst layers, soot blowers or a compressed-air cleaning arrangement, and a control system taking engine load, exhaust temperature and NOx or ammonia feedback.

  • Temperature window. Too cold and the reaction does not proceed and, on residual fuel, ammonium bisulphate forms and fouls and corrodes the catalyst; too hot and the ammonia oxidises and the catalyst degrades. Holding the reactor within its window at low load - manoeuvring, port arrival - is the main operational difficulty, and is met by bypassing the reactor, by an exhaust bypass around the turbine, or by heating.
  • Sulphur. Residual fuel makes the low-temperature limit worse, because more SO3 is available to form bisulphate. Low-sulphur fuel widens the usable window.
  • Dosing accuracy. Under-dose and the NOx limit is missed; over-dose and unreacted ammonia slips past the catalyst, which is itself a pollutant and produces a smell and deposits.
  • Consumables and space. Urea must be bunkered and stored heated above its freezing point and is consumed continuously while the system runs. Reactor volume and urea tank capacity are significant design penalties.
  • Maintenance. Catalyst elements foul, poison and age and are replaced in layers; soot blowing, differential-pressure monitoring and periodic activity testing are the routine tasks.

The practical comparison is with exhaust gas recirculation, which suppresses NOx at source by recirculating cooled, scrubbed exhaust to lower the oxygen concentration and the peak temperature. EGR needs no consumable and no reactor, but it needs a scrubber and water treatment and it affects combustion directly; SCR leaves combustion alone but needs urea, space and a temperature window. Both are ways of reaching Tier III; neither is needed for Tier II.

6. The NOx Technical File

What it is. Regulation 13 sets the limits but does not say how compliance is proved. Paragraph 8 does that: the revised NOx Technical Code 2008 shall be applied in the certification, testing and measurement procedures for the standards in the regulation. The NOx Technical File is the document required under that Code for each engine. It is prepared by the engine builder, approved by the Administration at the time the engine is certified, and it accompanies the engine throughout its life on board, moving with it if the engine is transferred to another ship.

What it is for. An engine is tested on a test bed under an agreed test cycle, and the certificate says that that engine, in that condition, met the limit. The Technical File is what defines 'that condition'. It identifies the engine and the components, settings and operating values that affect its NOx emission, and it records the permitted range within which each of them may be adjusted or replaced. Stay inside the file and the engine is still the certified engine; go outside it and it is not.

What it broadly contains. The engine's identification - builder, type, serial number, rated power and speed. The identification of the NOx-critical components by their approved part numbers, typically the injection system - nozzles, pumps, cams and injection timing - the turbocharger and charge-air cooling arrangement, the cylinder head and valve timing, and the compression ratio. The allowable adjustment and operating ranges for the settings that affect NOx. The test-bed results and the parent-engine or engine-group data on which certification rested. And the on-board verification procedure to be used at survey.

  • Why it matters in the engine room. Fitting a nozzle of a different specification, altering injection timing beyond the recorded range, or changing a turbocharger for a non-approved unit takes the engine outside its certified condition. The engine's certificate then no longer reflects the engine, and the position must be regularised by the Administration or the recognized organization rather than by an entry in the maintenance system.
  • How a surveyor uses it. At survey the engine is verified against the file - typically by a parameter check, confirming that components and settings match those recorded, supported by the engine's own records of adjustments and component changes - rather than by measuring emissions on board.
  • Regulation 13 backs this with paragraph 9: the measurement procedures are intended to be representative of normal operation, and defeat devices and irrational emission control strategies are not allowed. Auxiliary control devices that protect the engine or facilitate starting remain permitted.
  • Where an Approved Method is fitted to a large pre-2000 engine under regulation 13.7, that method has its own approved method file with its own verification procedure, and its presence is noted on the ship's IAPP Certificate.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four short notes at four marks each. The discipline that wins this question is equal time: each limb is worth exactly as much as the others, so a candidate who writes a page on Tier II and III and three lines on the Technical File throws away six marks he already knew. Roughly four to six scoring propositions per limb, in about fifteen minutes each, is the shape.

Limb A is the only one with hard numbers, and the numbers are the marks - the three tiers, the construction dates, the four areas. Limbs B and C are mechanism questions: say what the device does, why that reduces NOx, and what it costs you to run, and all four marks are there. Limb D is the one candidates fear, and it is actually the easiest, because it has a single idea: the file defines the certified condition of the engine.

The idea that ties all four limbs together

Nearly all NOx from a diesel engine is thermal NO, formed from the nitrogen and oxygen already in the air, in the hottest part of the flame, at a rate that climbs very steeply with peak temperature. Every technique in this question is a different answer to that one fact. Tier II lowers the peak by engine design. Emulsion lowers it by putting water where the flame is hottest. SCR gives up on prevention and destroys the NO after it has formed. And the Technical File is what stops the crew quietly undoing all of it by fitting the wrong nozzle.

Common mistakes

  • Saying Tier III applies worldwide. It applies only inside a NOx Tier III emission control area, and only to ships constructed on or after the relevant date.
  • Confusing NOx areas with SOx areas. The lists are different, and at this sitting there are four NOx Tier III areas.
  • Saying the tier depends on the engine's build date. It depends on the ship's date of construction.
  • Writing that water emulsion works chemically. It works thermally - latent heat and added mass lower the peak temperature.
  • Forgetting that emulsified fuel cannot be stored and that the plant must be bypassable at once.
  • Placing the SCR reactor after the turbocharger on a slow-speed two-stroke. The gas there is too cold; on a two-stroke the reactor goes on the high-pressure side, before the turbine.
  • Ignoring ammonia slip and the low-temperature ammonium bisulphate problem - both are exactly what an examiner probes.
  • Describing the Technical File as 'a file with the engine's papers'. It is the document that defines the engine's certified condition and the permitted adjustment ranges.
  • Quoting a percentage NOx reduction for emulsion or SCR. Unless you can source it, do not - the marks are in the mechanism.

Examiner traps

  • The logbook entry. Tier and on/off status must be recorded on entry into and exit from a Tier III area and whenever the status changes inside one, with date, time and position. It is the part of regulation 13 that actually lands on the engine-room watch and it is very commonly missed.
  • 130 kW is the application threshold for the regulation; 750 kW is the combined propulsion power below which a Tier III exception may apply; 5,000 kW and 90 litres per cylinder is the Approved Method regime for pre-2000 engines. Three different numbers with three different jobs.
  • Defeat devices are prohibited under regulation 13.9, but auxiliary control devices that protect the engine or help starting are permitted. Knowing the difference is a mark.
  • Being asked 'can you switch the SCR off at sea?' Yes, outside a Tier III area, because the engine is certified to both tiers - and the switch must be logged.
  • Being asked what happens if you fit a non-approved injector nozzle. The engine leaves the condition recorded in its Technical File and the certificate no longer reflects it; the position is regularised with the Administration or the RO.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What are the Tier II limits for your main engine at, say, 80 rpm? Below 130 rpm the limit is 14.4 g/kWh.
  • Which four areas are Tier III areas? North American, United States Caribbean Sea, Baltic Sea and North Sea.
  • How do you meet Tier III? SCR or EGR, or a low-NOx gas combustion process. Not by tuning.
  • Why does water reduce NOx but not CO2? Because it lowers peak flame temperature and thermal NO formation; it does not change the carbon in the fuel burnt.
  • What is ammonia slip and why does it matter? Unreacted ammonia passing the catalyst, from over-dosing or a cold reactor - a pollutant in itself, and it forms deposits.
  • Who approves the NOx Technical File, and where is it kept? The Administration, at engine certification; it stays with the engine on board for its life.
