Understand this first

Strip away the marketing and the Internet of Things is one simple idea: put sensors on things that were previously silent, connect them, and let the data be acted upon somewhere other than beside the machine. A ship has always been full of instruments - the difference is that their readings used to die in a logbook. IoT means the bearing temperature that used to be written down at 0400 is now trended ashore against ten sister ships, and somebody predicts the failure before it happens. That single shift explains everything the question asks. Note also what this question is NOT: no IMO instrument requires IoT or prescribes how it is done. That means the answer is legitimately an engineering and management answer - but the moment you connect a ship you engage cyber risk, and THAT is regulated, so the regulatory content in this answer sits in the risks, not in the technology.

Model written answer

1. The principles of IoT

The Internet of Things is the connection of physical equipment - machinery, sensors, instruments - to a network, so that it can report its own condition and, in some cases, be acted upon remotely. The architecture is a chain of four stages:

  • Sense. Equipment is fitted with sensors and given a network address - temperature, pressure, vibration, flow, torque, position.
  • Connect. A shipboard network gathers the data, with edge processing on board to filter, compress and act on time-critical items locally; the rest goes ashore over the satellite link.
  • Analyse. A shore platform stores and aggregates the data, comparing a machine against its own history and against sister ships across the fleet.
  • Act. The analysis produces a decision - clean the hull, change the bearing, alter the trim - and that decision is fed back to the ship.

The essential change is not the sensor. Ships have always been instrumented. It is that the reading no longer dies where it is taken.

2. a) Advantages in the maritime industry

  • Continuous condition monitoring in place of periodic manual readings, so a trend is visible long before a limit is reached.
  • Measured energy performance. Fuel is the largest operating cost, and waste that cannot be measured cannot be managed.
  • Early warning of developing faults, reducing unplanned breakdown, off-hire and the cost of consequential damage.
  • Remote diagnostics and shore support - specialist and maker assistance available to the ship without waiting for attendance.
  • Automated reporting for statutory and commercial purposes, removing manual transcription and its errors.
  • Cargo monitoring, particularly reefer containers and temperature- or atmosphere-sensitive cargo, with alarms before the cargo is lost.
  • Better commercial and technical decisions - spares holding, drydock scope, chartering and voyage planning all improve when they rest on measured data.

3. a) Disadvantages and risks

  • Cyber risk is the dominant disadvantage. Every connection enlarges the attack surface, and connecting operational technology - engine controls, steering, ballast, cargo systems - to information technology networks exposes control systems that were previously safe because they were isolated.
  • Bandwidth dependence. Satellite communication is limited, costly and can be lost, and a system designed around constant connectivity degrades when it is not there.
  • Cost and integration. High capital outlay, and real difficulty retrofitting sensors and data buses to legacy machinery never designed to report anything.
  • Data quality. An uncalibrated or drifting sensor produces confident nonsense, and a prediction built on it is worse than no prediction at all.
  • Data overload and alarm fatigue where outputs are not filtered to what actually needs a decision.
  • Skills. A real competence gap both on board and ashore, and the opposite danger of de-skilling - an engineer who trusts the display and stops using his senses.
  • Data ownership and vendor lock-in. Who owns the ship's data - owner, charterer or the platform supplier - and proprietary formats that make changing supplier expensive.
  • Liability. Responsibility when a predictive system fails to predict, or advises a wrong intervention, is not settled.

4. b) How IoT is changing the maritime industry

The change is structural rather than merely technical:

  • Maintenance philosophy. The industry is moving from calendar-based overhaul to condition-based and predictive maintenance - work done because the machine needs it, not because the date arrived.
  • Where technical management happens. Fleet operations centres ashore now monitor machinery continuously, so decisions that were the chief engineer's alone are increasingly shared with a shore team.
  • Commercial transparency. Ship performance is measurable, so speed and consumption warranties, performance claims and charter-party disputes rest on data rather than on assertion.
  • Survey and class. Remote survey and remote support reduce the need for physical attendance, and classification societies accept approved condition-monitoring arrangements.
  • Digital twins. A modelled counterpart of the real machinery allows an intervention to be simulated before it is made.
  • The wider supply chain. Voyage and port-call optimisation, including just-in-time arrival, depends on ships and ports exchanging data.
  • Autonomy. IoT is the enabling layer beneath autonomous and smart-ship development - there is no remote or autonomous operation without it.
  • Regulatory administrability. The energy-efficiency regime that came into force in January 2023 requires continuous data collection, and digitalisation is what makes that practical.

5. c) Energy efficiency optimisation

This limb has become commercially compulsory rather than optional. EEXI and the operational carbon intensity indicator (CII) have applied since 1 January 2023, and the EU Emissions Trading System was extended to maritime transport from 1 January 2024 - so at the date of this paper, fuel burned has a direct regulatory and financial consequence. IoT is how that is managed:

  • Hull and propeller performance is monitored as a trend against a baseline, so fouling is quantified. Hull cleaning and propeller polishing are then done on evidence rather than on a fixed interval - too early wastes money, too late wastes fuel.
  • Trim optimisation - measuring actual power required at various trims and draughts and running at the best one.
  • Engine performance monitoring - shaft power and torque meters, cylinder pressures and exhaust temperatures compared with the shop trial baseline, so deterioration and maladjustment are found early.
  • Weather routing and speed optimisation using live and forecast data, including slow steaming and just-in-time arrival instead of steaming hard to wait at anchor.
  • Auxiliary and boiler load management, cutting unnecessary running machinery, and using shore power where it is available.
  • Automated data collection for the mandatory fuel-consumption reporting and rating regimes, removing manual error from a calculation that now has consequences.

6. c) Predictive maintenance

Maintenance can be placed on a ladder: breakdown, then planned or calendar, then condition-based, then predictive. IoT is what makes the last two possible at scale.

  • What is measured - vibration signature, bearing and winding temperature, pressures and differential pressures, thermography, lubricating-oil condition and wear debris, and overall performance efficiency.
  • How it works - each parameter is trended against an established baseline; the analysis identifies a developing fault by its signature, and the model estimates the remaining useful life so the work can be planned into a convenient port or drydock.
  • Why it matters - components are changed when they need changing, so there is less unnecessary opening-up of healthy machinery, fewer unplanned failures, lower spares holding, less off-hire, and better use of the crew's time.
  • Class recognition - classification societies already permit approved condition-monitoring and planned-maintenance schemes to stand in place of opening up machinery for survey, which converts the technical benefit into a survey and cost benefit.

7. What has to be in place for it to work

Three conditions are worth stating because they are where these projects fail. First, cyber risk management is mandatory, not optional: resolution MSC.428(98) requires cyber risks to be appropriately addressed in the safety management system, and the IMO's guidelines on maritime cyber risk management, in the revision current at this date, set out the approach - identify, protect, detect, respond, recover. Second, the data must be trustworthy: calibrated sensors, an established baseline, and enough bandwidth to move what matters. Third, people - trained crew and shore staff, a clear agreement on who owns the data and who acts on it, and an engineer who retains the judgement and the confidence to overrule the system when his own senses tell him something different.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are 6 + 5 + 5 with an unmarked introduction, and the answer is built to that. The introduction is genuinely brief - four bullets - because it earns nothing directly and every line spent there is taken from a marked limb.

Limb (a) is split into two separate steps, advantages and disadvantages, and that is deliberate. It is the six-mark limb, it explicitly asks for both, and the commonest failure is an enthusiastic page on benefits followed by two thin lines of drawbacks. Separating them forces the balance and makes it visible to the examiner.

Limb (b) is not the same question as limb (a). 'How IoT is changing the industry' asks about a structural shift - where maintenance decisions are taken, where technical management sits, how ships are surveyed and chartered - not about what one sensor does on one ship. Answering (b) with more advantages is the most common way to waste five marks here.

This is legitimately an engineering answer - but one limb is regulated

No IMO instrument requires IoT or prescribes how it is implemented. So most of this answer properly rests on engineering and management reasoning rather than on regulation, and that is not a weakness. What it does mean is that you should not invent a rule to make the answer look authoritative.

But the moment you connect the ship you engage something that IS regulated. Resolution MSC.428(98) requires cyber risks to be appropriately addressed in the safety management system, and the IMO guidelines on maritime cyber risk management give the five-function approach. Similarly the energy-efficiency limb has hard anchors - EEXI and CII in force from 1 January 2023, and EU ETS applying to shipping from 1 January 2024. Put the regulation where it genuinely belongs, in the risks and in the energy limb, and the answer reads like an engineer's rather than a brochure's.

The date of this paper

Digitalisation is the fastest-moving topic on any of these papers, so anything you read about it describes a much later state of the art. Two boundaries matter for a paper of this date.

First, the IMO's guidelines on maritime cyber risk management were revised again after this sitting. If you cite a revision number, cite the one current at the paper's date - safest of all, cite MSC.428(98), which is the mandatory hook and has not moved. Second, do not describe later developments in autonomous shipping or artificial intelligence as though they already existed. The energy-efficiency anchors used here - EEXI and CII from 1 January 2023, EU ETS from 1 January 2024 - were all genuinely in force at this sitting, and EU ETS by only three months, which makes it a strong and current point to raise.

Common mistakes

  • Unbalanced limb (a). Six marks explicitly asks for advantages AND disadvantages.
  • Answering (b) with more of (a). (b) is about the structural change to the industry, not about benefits to one ship.
  • Omitting cyber risk. It is the single most important disadvantage and it is the only part of this topic that is mandatory.
  • Quoting fuel savings or downtime reductions as percentages. Those figures are vendor-sourced and installation-specific. State the mechanism, not a number you cannot defend.
  • Treating condition-based and predictive maintenance as the same thing. Condition-based acts on the measured present state; predictive models the future state and estimates remaining useful life.
  • Describing IoT as new instrumentation. Ships were always instrumented; what changed is connectivity and analysis.
  • Forgetting that bandwidth is finite. A design that assumes constant high-rate connectivity at sea is not a marine design.

Examiner traps

  • OT versus IT. Expect to be pressed on why connecting engine control systems to the ship's business network is different from connecting an office computer. The answer is that operational technology was previously safe by isolation.
  • 'What if the sensor is wrong?' A favourite. Calibration, baseline validity and sanity-checking against independent readings are the answer - not more data.
  • De-skilling. An examiner may ask whether you would still walk the engine room. The expected answer is yes, and why.
  • Class and survey. Be ready for the link to approved condition-monitoring schemes standing in place of opening up machinery.
  • Who owns the data? Owner, charterer or platform provider - there is no single answer, and saying so with the reasons is stronger than picking one.
  • The energy link. Expect a follow-up connecting monitoring to CII, because that is what turns a nice-to-have into a commercial necessity.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the Internet of Things in your own words?
  • What is the biggest risk of connecting your engine room to shore?
  • What is the difference between operational technology and information technology?
  • Which resolution requires cyber risk to be addressed, and where must it be addressed?

  • Distinguish planned, condition-based and predictive maintenance.
  • What data would you trend to predict a bearing failure?
  • Your monitoring system says the bearing is healthy but it sounds wrong. What do you do?
  • How does condition monitoring affect machinery survey?

Model written answer

1. What ammonia is, and why it is proposed

Ammonia is NH3. The molecule contains no carbon, so its combustion produces no carbon dioxide at the point of use, and because it contains no sulphur it produces no SOx and no fuel-borne particulates. That single property is the whole reason it is under discussion as a marine fuel.

It has two further practical advantages over hydrogen, which is the other carbon-free candidate. It liquefies readily - either by modest refrigeration or by modest pressure - so it does not demand cryogenic storage near absolute zero. And it is already manufactured, stored, shipped and handled worldwide in very large quantities as a fertiliser feedstock, so an industrial base, a seaborne trade and a body of handling experience already exist. Ammonia may itself be regarded as a practical hydrogen carrier.

2. Technical viability

The central technical difficulty is energy density. Ammonia carries less than half the energy of fuel oil per tonne, and because liquid ammonia is also less dense in energy terms per unit volume, a ship needs roughly two and a half to three times the tank volume to carry the same energy. On a fixed hull that volume is taken from cargo, ballast or accommodation.

It is stored as a liquid, either fully refrigerated at about minus 33 degrees Celsius at atmospheric pressure, or pressurised at ambient temperature, or by a semi-refrigerated combination - the same three options familiar from gas carrier practice.

Ammonia is also a poor fuel to burn. It has a narrow flammability range, a high ignition energy, a high autoignition temperature and a very slow flame speed. It will not reliably compression-ignite on its own, so a marine two-stroke burns it in dual-fuel mode with a pilot fuel - which means a conventional fuel system, and a residual carbon emission, remain on board. Materials must also be selected with care: ammonia attacks copper, brass, zinc and some alloys, so conventional pipework, gauges and instrument fittings cannot simply be reused.

3. Safety, and the regulatory route at this sitting

Ammonia reverses the usual hazard priority. With LNG the dominant hazard is flammability; with ammonia it is acute toxicity. It is harmful, and then rapidly fatal, at concentrations far below those at which it would burn, so a release that presents no fire risk at all may still be lethal. It is corrosive to the eyes and the respiratory tract, and although it has a strong warning odour, that odour cannot be relied upon at dangerous concentrations.

Design therefore concentrates on containment and separation: double-walled fuel piping with the annular space monitored, fuel-preparation machinery in gas-tight enclosures with dedicated extraction, extensive gas detection, water deluge and absorption arrangements, controlled release and venting, and protected escape routes and safe-refuge arrangements for the crew.

The regulatory position at the date of this paper is the key point and it is one of absence. There was no IMO instrument in force governing the use of ammonia as a marine fuel. The IGF Code is the mandatory code for low-flashpoint fuels, but its detailed prescriptive provisions covered natural gas only. Where a fuel is not prescribed by the Code, the ship is approved through the alternative design and arrangements route of SOLAS regulation II-1/55, in which equivalence to the Code's goal and functional requirements is demonstrated by risk assessment and approved by the Administration or a recognized organization acting for it. In practice the technical detail was carried by classification-society rules. IMO work was under way but unfinished: the sub-committee on carriage of cargoes and containers had agreed fundamental principles to account for the toxicity of the fuel at its session of 20 to 29 September 2023, with the draft interim guidelines still to be finalised. Do not write that interim guidelines were in place.

4. Operational viability

There is no established bunkering infrastructure for ammonia as a fuel. Ammonia terminals exist for the chemical trade, but bunkering a ship is a different operation and is not widely available. It cannot be an open transfer: it requires closed, vapour-returned, leak-monitored connections with emergency release couplings, and a controlled exclusion zone.

Crew competence is a genuine gap. The STCW training regime for low-flashpoint fuels was framed around gas, and toxic-fuel operation demands additional training, different personal protective equipment, respiratory protection, dedicated medical first-aid and antidote arrangements, and rehearsed rescue from an enclosed space filled with a toxic vapour rather than an asphyxiant one. Purging, inerting and gas-freeing become routine engineering operations rather than occasional ones.

5. Commercial viability

Two costs dominate. First, the fuel. Almost all ammonia produced today is grey - made from natural gas by steam reforming and the Haber-Bosch process - so it is carbon-intensive upstream even though it is carbon-free at the funnel. Green ammonia, made from renewable hydrogen, is what the sustainability claim actually depends on, and it is scarce and considerably more expensive. Second, the ship: larger tanks, double-walled systems, gas-tight enclosures and safety systems raise the capital cost, and the space they occupy is a permanent loss of earning capacity.

The commercial case therefore does not rest on fuel price - on that basis ammonia loses. It rests on regulation putting a price on carbon and on charterers valuing a low-carbon voyage. At the date of this paper that pressure was real and increasing: the EU Emissions Trading System was extended to maritime transport from 1 January 2024, and the 2023 IMO GHG Strategy, adopted in July 2023, set the direction for the sector. Ammonia is best judged as a long-term candidate whose economics are created by regulation.

6. The environmental balance

Carbon-free at the funnel is not the same as sustainable overall, and a good answer says so. Three qualifications matter. The production pathway decides whether any real carbon saving occurs at all. Combustion produces nitrous oxide (N2O), a greenhouse gas hundreds of times more potent than CO2, so even a small quantity can erode the benefit; and NOx control is still required. And unburnt ammonia slip is both a toxic hazard and an environmental one, requiring after-treatment. The environmental gain is real, but it is conditional on green production and on effective exhaust after-treatment, not automatic.

7. Reasoned conclusion

Ammonia is technically feasible - the engines and the containment can be built, and orders had been placed by the date of this paper - but it is not yet mature. The binding constraints are not the engine; they are safety engineering, crew competence, bunkering supply and the availability of green ammonia, with the regulatory framework still being written at this sitting. It is best described as a credible long-term candidate for deep-sea decarbonisation whose viability is conditional - on green production, on infrastructure, and on a carbon price - rather than as a fuel ready for general adoption.

Common mistakes

  • Writing the IMO interim guidelines for ammonia as fuel into this sitting. They did not exist in April 2024. This is the trap the question is built on.

Understand this first

Ordinary law says that if you are owed money you sue the person who owes it. Shipping cannot work that way: the debtor may be a single-ship company in a jurisdiction you will never reach, and the only asset is a ship that will sail tomorrow. So maritime law does something unusual - it lets a small class of claims attach to the SHIP itself, silently and automatically, and follow her wherever she goes and whoever buys her. That is a maritime lien, and the action in rem is simply the procedure for enforcing it: you sue the ship. Everything else in this question - the priority list, the arrest, the forced sale - is the machinery for turning a ship into money and then deciding who gets paid out of it, in what order.

Model written answer

1. What a maritime lien is

A maritime lien is a privileged claim against the vessel herself rather than against any person. It is not created by agreement and it is not registered: it attaches to the ship by operation of law at the moment the claim arises, and it does so without notice to anyone. Until it is enforced it is inchoate - a right in the ship that lies dormant; it is crystallised by arrest and realised by forced sale. Its practical significance is its rank: a maritime lien takes priority over a registered mortgage, even one registered earlier and for a far larger sum.

That combination - secret, unregistered, automatic and senior - is what distinguishes it from an ordinary possessory lien, which depends on holding the goods and is lost when possession is given up.

2. How the lien travels with the property

This is the defining characteristic, and the Convention states it in one sentence. Article 8: the maritime liens follow the vessel, notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag.

  • The lien is attached to the res - the ship - and not to the owner, so selling the ship does not shed it.
  • It binds a bona fide purchaser for value without notice. A buyer may acquire a ship with unpaid crew wages or a salvage award still charged against her and have no means of discovering it from any register. This is why it is called a secret lien and why prudent buyers take indemnities and searches.
  • Reflagging and re-registration make no difference: the lien travels with the hull, not with the certificate.
  • It is defeated in only two ways - by a forced sale complying with article 12, which sweeps the ship clean of liens and encumbrances, or by lapse of time under article 9.

Under article 9 the article 4 liens are extinguished after one year unless, before then, the vessel has been arrested or seized and that arrest or seizure leads to a forced sale. Time runs from the claimant's discharge from the vessel for wage claims and from the moment the claim arises for the others, and it runs continuously, without suspension or interruption.

3. In rem and in personam

  • An action in personam is brought against a person - the owner, charterer, manager or operator. The claimant must find that person, establish jurisdiction over them, and then enforce against whatever assets they have. If the defendant is a single-ship company with no other assets, a judgment in personam may be worth nothing.
  • An action in rem is brought against the ship herself, which is named as defendant. The court's jurisdiction is founded on the presence of the res within the jurisdiction, and the ship may be arrested.

The connection between the two limbs of this question is the point the examiner is looking for: the maritime lien is what gives the claimant a right in rem against the ship. Because the lien attaches to the vessel and follows her, the claimant can proceed against the vessel wherever she is found and whoever now owns her.

Arrest is not usually an end in itself. It is a coercive step: it either compels the provision of security - a P&I club letter of undertaking or a bank guarantee - so that the ship may trade on, or, if no security is forthcoming, it produces a fund through forced sale out of which the claims are paid. The two forms of action are not alternatives and may be pursued together.

4. Which convention deals with maritime liens

The International Convention on Maritime Liens and Mortgages, 1993, adopted at Geneva on 6 May 1993 and in force from 5 September 2004. It is the successor to the earlier Brussels Conventions of 1926 and 1967 on the same subject, neither of which achieved wide acceptance. Its stated purpose is to improve the conditions for ship financing and the development of national merchant fleets - which explains its structure: it protects mortgagees by making mortgages recognisable and enforceable across States, and it does so by defining exhaustively the small class of claims that outrank them.

5. What the convention secures - article 4

Article 4(1) secures five claims, against the owner, demise charterer, manager or operator, by a maritime lien on the vessel:

  • (a) claims for wages and other sums due to the master, officers and other members of the vessel's complement in respect of their employment on the vessel, including costs of repatriation and social insurance contributions payable on their behalf;
  • (b) claims in respect of loss of life or personal injury, whether on land or on water, in direct connection with the operation of the vessel;
  • (c) claims for reward for the salvage of the vessel;
  • (d) claims for port, canal and other waterway dues and pilotage dues;
  • (e) claims based on tort arising out of physical loss or damage caused by the operation of the vessel, other than loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects carried on the vessel.

Article 4(2) removes two categories from limbs (b) and (e): damage from the carriage of oil or other hazardous or noxious substances for which compensation is payable under regimes providing strict liability and compulsory insurance, and damage from the radioactive properties of nuclear fuel, products or waste. The reasoning is that those claimants already have a dedicated fund, and there is no need to give them a lien as well.

Note what is not a maritime lien: cargo claims, and the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers and agents. They may be maritime claims founding an action in rem, but they carry no lien under article 4.

6. How the liens rank among themselves - article 5

  • The article 4 liens take priority over registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges, and no other claim takes priority over either, subject to article 12(3) and (4).
  • They rank in the order listed in article 4 - so wages before personal injury, before salvage, before dues, before tort.
  • But salvage liens jump the queue: a lien securing a claim for salvage reward takes priority over all other maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The logic is obvious - the salvor preserved the very fund the others are claiming against.
  • Liens within each of (a), (b), (d) and (e) rank pari passu among themselves.
  • Salvage liens rank in the inverse order of time in which the claims accrued - the later salvage ranks first - each claim being deemed to accrue on the date the salvage operation terminated.

7. The order of settlement after forced sale - article 12

A forced sale in a State Party sweeps the vessel clean: all registered mortgages, hypotheques and charges, except those assumed by the purchaser with the holders' consent, and all liens and other encumbrances of whatsoever nature, cease to attach to the vessel, provided the ship was within that State's jurisdiction and the sale complied with its law and with articles 11 and 12. The proceeds are then distributed in this order:

  • 1. The costs and expenses of the arrest or seizure and of the subsequent sale, paid first out of the proceeds. These expressly include the costs of upkeep of the vessel and the crew, and the wages and other sums under article 4(1)(a) incurred from the time of the arrest or seizure.
  • 2. Wreck-removal costs, where a State Party has so provided. Article 12(3) permits a State to provide that, where a stranded or sunken vessel has been removed by a public authority in the interest of safe navigation or protection of the marine environment, those removal costs are paid before all other claims secured by a maritime lien. This is optional, not automatic.
  • 3. The article 4 maritime liens, in their article 5 order and subject to the salvage rules.
  • 4. The right of retention of a shipbuilder or shiprepairer under article 7. Article 12(4) requires him to surrender possession to the purchaser, but entitles him to be satisfied out of the proceeds after the article 4 lienholders.
  • 5. Registered mortgages, "hypotheques" and charges complying with article 1.
  • 6. Other maritime liens granted under national law under article 6, which rank after both the article 4 liens and the registered mortgages.
  • 7. The residue, if any, to the owner, and States Parties must ensure the proceeds are actually available and freely transferable.

The purchaser is then entitled to a certificate that the vessel is sold free of encumbrances, on production of which the registrar must delete the registered mortgages and charges and either register the vessel in the purchaser's name or issue a certificate of deregistration.

8. The Indian statutory position, kept separate

In India the subject is governed domestically by the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017 (No. 22 of 2017, assented 9 August 2017), which consolidated admiralty jurisdiction and vested it in the High Courts. Its scheme closely follows the Convention, and the differences are worth knowing:

  • Section 9(1) lists the same five maritime liens in the same order, with two widenings: limb (c) covers salvage including special compensation, and limb (d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel.
  • Section 9(2) reproduces the travelling rule - the lien continues notwithstanding any change of ownership or of registration or of flag - and the one-year extinction, but with a proviso giving TWO years for wage claims under limb (a), running from the date the wage, repatriation cost or social insurance contribution fell due. That is more generous to seafarers than the Convention.
  • Section 10 sets the order of priority of maritime claims in an admiralty proceeding as: (a) claims where there is a maritime lien; (b) registered mortgages and charges of the same nature; (c) all other claims - with claims in a single category ranking equally, and salvage claims in inverse order of time.

(The stem asks about the convention, and the answer above is given at convention level. The Indian Act is set out separately because it is the law an Indian court would apply, and it should not be conflated with the Convention.)

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains five separate demands in sixteen marks - define the lien, explain how it travels, explain in rem and in personam, name the convention, and give the order of settlement. That is roughly three marks each, so none of them deserves an essay and none may be skipped. The two that candidates most often lose are the last: they name the convention vaguely, or give a half-remembered priority list. The order of settlement is the single most concrete thing in the question and it is the easiest to prepare, so it should be written out as a numbered list, not narrated.

The idea that links the whole question

The lien and the action in rem are two halves of one mechanism. The maritime lien attaches to the ship rather than to a person; that is precisely what allows the claimant to sue the ship rather than a person. If you state that link explicitly - the lien is what gives the right in rem - the middle three demands of the question join up instead of reading as three unrelated definitions.

Why the lien is called secret, and why that matters commercially

There is no register of maritime liens. A buyer inspecting the ship's registry sees mortgages and charges but cannot see unpaid crew wages, an outstanding salvage award or a personal injury claim - and under article 8 all of those follow the vessel through a sale, a change of registry and a change of flag. So a purchaser can take delivery of a ship already encumbered by claims ranking ahead of the mortgage financing the purchase. This is why sale contracts carry indemnities and why a forced sale under article 12 is commercially valuable: it is the one transaction that delivers a genuinely clean ship, which is exactly why an arresting creditor's remedy has teeth.

The two salvage rules - state both

Salvage is the exception to almost every ranking rule in the Convention and both exceptions are examinable.

  • Salvage jumps ahead of earlier liens. A salvage lien takes priority over all maritime liens which attached before the salvage operations were performed. The salvor preserved the fund everyone else is claiming against, so he is paid first out of it.
  • Salvage liens rank in inverse order of time. Where there is more than one salvage, the later ranks ahead of the earlier - each claim accruing on the date its salvage operation terminated. Every other category ranks pari passu.

What is NOT a maritime lien

A quick way to show command of article 4 is to say what it leaves out. Cargo claims are not secured by a maritime lien - article 4(1)(e) expressly excludes loss of or damage to cargo, containers and passengers' effects. Nor are the claims of necessaries men - bunker suppliers, repairers, agents and chandlers. These may still be maritime claims supporting an action in rem and an arrest, but they carry no lien and therefore rank behind registered mortgages. The distinction between a maritime claim and a maritime lien is exactly what the priority list is about.

The Indian dimension - and the statement to avoid

The examiner asks about the convention, so answer at convention level. The Indian statute is the Admiralty (Jurisdiction and Settlement of Maritime Claims) Act, 2017, and giving it separately is a strength: its section 9 reproduces the five liens and their order, and its section 10 gives the three-tier priority - liens, then registered mortgages, then all other claims.

Two genuine Indian divergences are worth knowing. Section 9(1)(c) covers salvage including special compensation, and section 9(1)(d) adds any other statutory dues related to the vessel. Most importantly, section 9(2) gives seafarers two years for wage claims where the Convention gives one - more generous than the Convention.

What to avoid: do not assert whether or not India is a party to the 1993 Convention. It is not necessary to answer any part of this question, and a confident statement either way is a risk for no reward. Say what the Convention provides, say what the Indian Act provides, and let the two stand side by side.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a maritime claim with a maritime lien. Every lien is a claim; very few claims are liens.
  • Saying a maritime lien is lost on sale of the ship. It is not - article 8 is the whole point of the question's second limb.
  • Treating in rem and in personam as alternatives. They may be pursued together.
  • Putting mortgages ahead of maritime liens. Article 5 puts the liens first, which is the commercially surprising and therefore examinable point.
  • Forgetting that arrest and sale costs and post-arrest crew wages are paid before everything, including the liens.
  • Omitting the salvage inverse-order rule, or applying inverse order to all categories rather than only to salvage.
  • Giving a mortgage a rank ahead of the shipbuilder's or shiprepairer's right of retention. Article 12(4) puts retention after the article 4 liens but the Convention leaves mortgages behind both.

Examiner traps

"You buy a ship with a clean registry search. Can you still be caught?" Yes. Maritime liens are unregistered and follow the vessel through a change of ownership, registration and flag. Only a forced sale under article 12, or the lapse of time under article 9, clears them.

"Crew wages or the bank - who is paid first?" Crew. Article 4(1)(a) is the first maritime lien and article 5(1) puts all article 4 liens ahead of registered mortgages. And the wages incurred after arrest are paid even before that, as part of the article 12(2) costs.

"Two salvage services, one in January and one in June. Who ranks first?" June - salvage liens rank in inverse order of time.

"Is a bunker supplier's claim a maritime lien under the Convention?" No. It may be a maritime claim founding an action in rem, but article 4 does not secure it, so it ranks behind registered mortgages.

Understand this first

A propeller does not convert all the power it is given into useful thrust. Some of what it leaves behind is axial - water thrown astern faster than it needs to be - and some is rotational: the race leaves the disc spinning, and that spin is wasted energy. Behind the propeller of a single-screw ship sits a large flat object in exactly that spinning race: the rudder. Every device in this question does one of two things with it - it either recovers some of the rotational energy and turns it into forward thrust, or it stops wasting more, by removing a vortex or a separation the rudder itself creates. Read the four limbs that way and they stop being four unrelated gadgets.

Model written answer

1. Where the energy to be recovered is

A screw propeller working behind a hull typically converts only about 65 to 72 per cent of delivered power into useful thrust. The losses are of three kinds: axial, in the sternward momentum of the race; rotational, in the swirl the blades impart to it; and frictional and vortex losses, principally the hub vortex shed from the boss. The rotational component alone is commonly of the order of 8 to 12 per cent of delivered power on a single-screw merchant ship.

The rudder is the only large body standing in that race. It is therefore both an opportunity - the swirl arrives at it already rotating, so a correctly oriented surface there sees an angle of attack for nothing - and a liability, because a plain rudder behind a bare boss adds drag and lets the hub vortex run on unchecked. The four devices below exploit the first and reduce the second.

2. (a) Rudder thrust fins

A rudder thrust fin is a horizontal foil, usually fitted as a pair port and starboard, mounted on the rudder at or near the level of the propeller shaft, inside the slipstream. Because the race is rotating, the flow arrives at the fin with a tangential component: upward on one side of the shaft and downward on the other. Each fin is set at the angle that turns that oncoming flow into lift, and because the flow is inclined, the lift vector is tilted forward. Its horizontal component is thrust, generated with no additional power.

  • The two fins are set at different angles, or in mirror image, because the swirl direction differs above and below the shaft.
  • Gains quoted in industry literature are typically of the order of 2 to 5 per cent in delivered power, and are strongly dependent on span, chord and angle of attack.
  • The angle is fixed at build. The device is optimised for one draught and speed and gives less, or in extreme off-design conditions nothing, elsewhere.
  • It is a source of added appendage drag when not loaded, and a candidate for cavitation erosion if it sits too close to the blade tips.

3. (b) Asymmetric and twisted rudders

Here the recovery is built into the rudder itself rather than added to it. On a single-screw ship the propeller turns one way, so the race strikes the upper half of the rudder from one side and the lower half from the other. A conventional symmetrical rudder, aligned with the centreline, therefore meets the flow at an angle of attack of the wrong sign over half its span, producing side force, drag and early cavitation.

An asymmetric rudder cures this by making the two halves different. In the developed form - the twisted leading edge rudder - the leading edge is twisted in opposite directions above and below the shaft line, so that each half is aligned with the swirl it actually meets.

  • The rudder becomes a swirl-recovery surface: the side force on the two halves is turned into a net forward component.
  • It reduces drag and cavitation, and with it rudder erosion and propeller-excited vibration.
  • It is handed - built for one direction of rotation - so it is a design decision, not a universal retrofit.
  • Because it changes the rudder body rather than adding to it, it carries no added appendage and its benefit degrades more gracefully off design than a fixed fin.

4. (c) Rudder bulbs

The propeller boss sheds a strong hub vortex, and behind a bare boss the flow separates, leaving a low-pressure wake that pulls the ship astern. A rudder bulb is a streamlined body of revolution fitted to the rudder leading edge, coaxial with the shaft and sized and positioned so that it forms an almost continuous body with the propeller hub cap, closing the gap between them.

  • It suppresses or displaces the hub vortex, removing a pure loss.
  • It restores pressure behind the boss by giving the flow a body to close around instead of separating, which is a genuine reduction in resistance rather than a recovery of energy.
  • The diameter and the axial gap govern the result; too large or too far aft and the bulb adds more drag than it saves.
  • It is most often fitted together with fins, the bulb conditioning the flow the fins work in, so the two are designed as one installation and their savings are not simply additive.

5. (d) Grim vane wheels

The vane wheel, patented by Otto Grim in 1966, is the most ambitious of the four and the only one that moves. It is a freely rotating bladed wheel mounted on the shaft line immediately aft of the propeller, of larger diameter than the propeller, with no mechanical connection to it or to the shaft. It runs at its own speed, typically around a third to a half of propeller revolutions, driven entirely by the water.

Its cleverness is that one blade does two jobs at different radii:

  1. The inner portion, standing inside the propeller race, works as a turbine. It extracts energy from the swirl and axial velocity of the slipstream, and that is what drives the wheel round.
  2. The outer portion, standing outside the race in undisturbed water, works as a propeller. Driven by the torque the inner part has extracted, it produces additional thrust over a larger disc area and therefore at higher efficiency.

The wheel thus takes wasted energy from a small, fast, disturbed stream and returns it as thrust in a large, slow, clean one. Gains of the order of 5 to 10 per cent were claimed and some were demonstrated. In service the device proved difficult: the long, thin, unsupported blades of the outer portion are fatigue-loaded and blade loss and shedding occurred; performance falls away in a seaway and off design, when the wheel can windmill unhelpfully; and it adds a rotating component aft of the propeller with its own bearing and clearance problems. It is now of more importance as a principle - and as an examination question - than as a device in general service.

6. Whether a claimed gain is real on this ship

All four claims are hull-specific. A device recovers only what the propeller behind that hull actually wastes, so the gain depends on the wake field, block coefficient, propeller loading, draught and service speed. Three consequences follow, and they are where the marks are at this level. First, a quoted percentage means nothing without the condition it was measured in, and model-scale results require a scale-effect correction before they can be believed at full scale. Second, devices interact: a bulb changes the flow the fins see, so savings must be assessed for the installation as a whole. Third, the real case is the operating profile - a ship that spends its life slow-steaming at part draught will not realise a gain optimised for design condition, and the payback calculation must be built on the actual profile, on class approval of the attachment and its structure, and on the effect on manoeuvring, which a rudder-mounted device necessarily touches.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has done the structuring for you. Four devices are named and each carries 4 marks, so the answer is four equal blocks and nothing else can be substituted for them. The single most common way to lose marks here is to write a good general essay on energy-saving devices - pre-swirl stators, ducts, propeller boss cap fins, air lubrication - none of which is asked for. Answer the four printed limbs.

The one idea that unlocks all four

Rotational loss. The propeller leaves the race spinning, and that spin is energy already paid for and thrown away. Two of these devices recover it - the fin and the twisted rudder turn swirl into forward force, and the vane wheel takes it mechanically. Two stop further waste - the bulb removes the hub vortex and the separation behind the boss. If you can say which of the two categories a device is in, you can reconstruct its explanation from first principles.

Keep recovery and drag reduction apart

  • Recovery - thrust fins, asymmetric and twisted rudders, vane wheels. They convert energy already in the water into thrust.
  • Drag and vortex reduction - rudder bulbs. They do not recover anything; they stop a loss from happening.
  • The bulb is often described loosely as recovering hub-vortex energy. The defensible statement is that it suppresses the vortex and restores pressure behind the boss. Say that and the point is safe either way.

Common mistakes

  • Describing an asymmetric rudder as a rudder offset to one side of the centreline. The asymmetry is in the section and the twist about the shaft line, matching the opposite swirl above and below.
  • Saying the Grim vane wheel is geared to or driven by the shaft. It is entirely free-running; there is no mechanical connection, and that is the whole point.
  • Making the vane wheel the same diameter as the propeller. It is larger, and it must be, because the outer portion has to stand outside the race to act as a propeller.
  • Quoting a saving as a hard number. Every figure is conditional on the hull and the operating condition.
  • Adding the percentages of several devices together.
  • Ignoring manoeuvring. Anything bolted to a rudder changes the way she steers, and class will want to see the structure.

Examiner traps

  • "Currently in use" in the stem. The Grim vane wheel is the odd one out - historically important, largely not in service. Say so rather than pretending it is common; an answer that quietly treats all four as current practice is weaker, not stronger.
  • Limb (d) is worth the same 4 marks as the others even though it is the least used. Do not short it.
  • The stem says the devices contribute to propulsion effectiveness, not to steering. Manoeuvring belongs in the closing judgement, not in place of the hydrodynamics.

Model written answer

1. a) The Convention - what it does and why

The International Convention on the Control of Harmful Anti-fouling Systems on Ships, 2001 - the AFS Convention - entered into force on 17 September 2008. Its purpose is to prohibit or restrict anti-fouling systems that are harmful to the marine environment while allowing ships to continue to control fouling.

It exists because of organotin compounds, principally tributyl tin. TBT was an outstandingly effective antifouling biocide and an outstandingly damaging pollutant: it persists, it accumulates in sediment and in the food chain, and it causes imposex and shell deformity in shellfish at concentrations of a few parts per trillion, with severe damage recorded around ports and shellfish grounds.

The Convention applies to ships entitled to fly the flag of a Party and to ships operating under a Party's authority. The substances actually controlled are listed in Annex 1, and that list can be added to by amendment - which is exactly what has since happened. Compliance is enforced through survey and certification and through inspection by port State officers, who may examine the certificate or declaration and may take a sample of the coating.

2. The controls - organotins and cybutryne

Organotin compounds acting as biocides in anti-fouling systems:

  • no ship may apply or re-apply them - effective 1 January 2003;
  • from 1 January 2008 a ship must either not bear such a system, or must carry a coating that forms a barrier to it leaching from the underlying non-compliant system.

Cybutryne was added to Annex 1 by amendment adopted on 17 June 2021, which entered into force on 1 January 2023. It was therefore fully in force at the date of this paper, and an answer that describes only the tin position is incomplete.

  • All ships: no application or re-application of an anti-fouling system containing cybutryne, from 1 January 2023.
  • Ships already bearing cybutryne in the external coating layer on that date must either remove the system or apply a coating forming a barrier to it leaching - at the next scheduled renewal of the anti-fouling system after 1 January 2023, and no later than 60 months after the last application of a cybutryne-containing system.
  • Limited exclusions apply, including fixed and floating platforms, FSUs and FPSOs constructed before that date which have not since been in dry dock, ships not engaged on international voyages, and ships under 400 GT on international voyages where the coastal State accepts it.

The practical lesson is that 'tin-free' does not mean 'unregulated'. Cybutryne was a common booster biocide in tin-free paints, so the second wave of control fell on the very coatings that replaced TBT.

3. a) Documents and records to demonstrate compliance

What a surveyor or port State control officer will expect to be produced:

  • International Anti-Fouling System Certificate (IAFS Certificate) - for ships of 400 gross tonnage and above engaged in international voyages, issued by or on behalf of the Administration after the required survey.
  • The Record of Anti-Fouling Systems - attached to and forming part of the certificate. It records the system applied, the facility that applied it and the date, and which compliance option applies to each controlled substance: never applied, applied and since removed, applied and covered with a sealer coat, or not in the external coating layer.
  • Declaration on Anti-Fouling System - for ships of 24 metres or more in length but less than 400 gross tonnage engaged in international voyages, signed by the owner or the owner's authorized agent.
  • Supporting documentation for that Declaration - a paint receipt or contractor's invoice, or equivalent evidence of what was applied.
  • Manufacturer's technical documentation - product data sheets and declarations confirming the composition of the system and the absence of controlled substances, together with application records: product, batch, area, dry film thickness and dates.
  • Survey records and reports - of the initial survey, and of any survey carried out when the anti-fouling system is changed or replaced, with the certificate endorsed or reissued accordingly.
  • Record of any sealer or barrier coat applied over a non-compliant system, naming the facility and the date, since that is what the certificate itself must state.
  • Dry-docking and planned maintenance records evidencing the last application and the next scheduled renewal - these establish the 60-month position for cybutryne.

One point specific to this period: since the cybutryne amendment the certificate must be in the amended model form, which carries cybutryne as well as organotins. Affected ships were given a transitional period to obtain a certificate in that form, and that transition was still running at the date of this paper.

4. b) Controlled depletion polymer (CDP)

A soluble matrix coating, traditionally rosin-based. Seawater slowly dissolves the binder, and as it dissolves it releases the biocide dispersed within it - essentially a refinement of the old soluble-matrix paints.

Because release depends on physical dissolution rather than a controlled chemical reaction, the release rate is not well regulated and tails off with time, and a thick, uneven leached layer builds up on the hull. That layer increases roughness and drag, so fuel performance degrades over the docking cycle. It is the cheapest of the four, easy to apply and easy to overcoat, and is generally suited to shorter drydock intervals and lower-activity ships.

5. Self-polishing copolymer (SPC)

The important difference is chemical. The biocide is chemically bonded into an acrylic copolymer backbone rather than merely mixed into a soluble binder. In seawater the surface layer hydrolyses, releasing the biocide at a controlled, near-linear rate, and the spent binder then becomes soluble and erodes away - so the coating polishes itself smooth as it works.

The consequences are a thin and stable leached layer, a predictable release rate that can be engineered to the ship's speed and activity, longer service life - commonly to a full five-year docking cycle - and better sustained hydrodynamic performance because the surface stays smooth. It costs more than CDP. Modern tin-free SPCs use copper, zinc or silyl acrylate chemistries in place of the original tin ones.

6. Hybrid TBT-free paints

A blend of the two technologies above - rosin and copolymer chemistry combined in one binder - intended to capture some of the controlled polishing behaviour of SPC at closer to CDP cost.

The result is intermediate on every axis: more predictable release and less leached-layer build-up than pure CDP, but less precisely controlled than a true SPC; intermediate service life; intermediate price. It is a sensible compromise for moderate trading patterns and medium docking intervals. Note that 'hybrid' is a commercial label rather than a defined term, and formulations sold under it vary considerably.

7. Fouling release coatings

These are fundamentally different: they contain no biocide at all and work by physics rather than by poisoning. A silicone or fluoropolymer elastomer gives a surface of very low surface energy and very high smoothness, to which fouling organisms cannot attach firmly. Such growth as does settle is sheared off by the water flow once the ship is moving.

Strengths: the lowest drag of any of the four, hence the best fuel performance, and no biocide released into the sea, which is the strongest environmental position. Limitations are real: performance depends on regular activity and adequate speed, so the ship fouls during prolonged idle periods or slow steaming; the film is mechanically soft and easily damaged and is difficult to repair; it requires a full tie-coat system and careful surface preparation; and it carries the highest initial cost.

8. Choosing between them

The choice follows the ship's trading pattern, service speed, proportion of idle time and intended drydock interval. A fast, regularly operating ship on a five-year cycle rewards a fouling release or a good SPC; a slower ship with irregular employment and long port stays is better served by a biocidal SPC or hybrid; CDP remains defensible where the docking interval is short and first cost dominates. Whatever is selected must be free of the substances controlled under Annex 1 - now including cybutryne - and must be documented so that compliance can be demonstrated.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) contains a trap of drafting. It says 'briefly discuss' the Convention and then 'state ALL documents and records'. The word all converts the second half into a checklist, and a checklist is marked by counting items. So the discussion is kept genuinely brief and the documents are written as an explicit list, one line each. A flowing paragraph that mentions the certificate in passing will lose marks that a list would have collected.

Limb (b) names four technologies at two marks each. Each is given mechanism, performance and limitation in that order. Step 8 is not asked for explicitly, but 'discuss various alternatives' invites a comparison, and a short selection paragraph is what distinguishes an engineer's answer from a paint catalogue.

The date of this paper - the trap runs BACKWARDS

The intake flag for this question said STABLE / LOW and it was wrong. The usual temporal danger is importing something that came later. Here the danger is the opposite: leaving out something that had already happened.

Cybutryne controls entered into force on 1 January 2023 - fifteen months before this sitting. Much of the study material on this topic was written when the AFS Convention meant TBT and nothing else, and an answer built from that material describes only the tin position and is incomplete for April 2024. This is worth internalising as a general point: an old paper's correct answer is not always the old answer.

Two further details are specific to this sitting. The 60-month rule was live - ships bearing cybutryne had to remove it or seal it over at the next scheduled renewal after 1 January 2023 and no later than 60 months after the last application. And the transitional period for obtaining a certificate in the amended form was still running, so at this date a ship could legitimately still hold a certificate in the older form.

The one idea that organises limb (b)

There are only two ways to keep a hull clean, and the four named products are variations on them. Either you release a biocide, or you make the surface impossible to grip.

  • Release a biocide - and then the only question is how well you control the release. CDP dissolves and controls it poorly. SPC hydrolyses and controls it well. Hybrid sits between them. That single axis explains cost, service life, leached-layer thickness and drag for all three.
  • Make the surface impossible to grip - fouling release. No biocide, lowest drag, but it depends on the ship moving, and the film is soft and expensive.

If you can state that framing in one sentence at the start of limb (b), the four descriptions write themselves and you will not confuse CDP with SPC, which is the commonest error in this topic.

Common mistakes

  • Answering the Convention as a TBT-only instrument. Cybutryne was controlled from 1 January 2023 and was in force at this sitting.
  • Giving the certificate but forgetting the Record of Anti-Fouling Systems, which is attached to and forms part of it, and the Declaration for the ship between 24 m and 400 GT.
  • Confusing CDP and SPC. CDP dissolves; SPC hydrolyses and polishes. The biocide is mixed into a CDP binder and chemically bound into an SPC backbone.
  • Calling fouling release paint an antifouling in the biocidal sense. It contains no biocide; that is its defining feature.
  • Presenting fouling release as simply the best option. It needs the ship to move, and it is soft, costly and hard to repair.
  • Writing the organotin dates the wrong way round. 1 January 2003 is when application stopped; 1 January 2008 is when ships had to stop bearing it or seal it over.
  • Treating 'briefly discuss' as permission to write half the answer on the history of TBT.

Examiner traps

  • 'All documents and records'. The examiner is counting. Include the Record, the Declaration, the supporting paint documentation and the survey records - not just the certificate.
  • Remove OR seal. For both organotins and cybutryne the requirement is a choice: remove the system, or apply a coating forming a barrier over it. Candidates routinely give only removal.
  • The tonnage and length thresholds. 400 GT and above gets a certificate; 24 m or more but under 400 GT gets a declaration. Expect to be asked what a 30 m, 300 GT ship carries.
  • Who signs the Declaration. The owner or the owner's authorized agent - not the master, and not a surveyor.
  • The 60-month rule. It is not a flat deadline: it is the next scheduled renewal after 1 January 2023, subject to an outer limit of 60 months from the last application.
  • Port State sampling. An officer may do more than read the certificate - a sample of the coating may be taken.

Uncertainty and source limits

MEPC.331(76) was read at source from the IMO's own published copy, so the cybutryne controls, the 60-month rule, the exclusions, the replaced Annex 4 regulation 2(3) and the amended certificate form are primary. The AFS Convention's own text was not read this session: the organotin dates, the 400 GT and 24 m thresholds and the Declaration requirement rest on authoritative restatements of the Convention, including national implementing legislation. MIW holds no licensed copy of the Convention.

The four coating types are commercial technology categories, not defined regulatory terms, and 'hybrid' in particular is used inconsistently across manufacturers. They are therefore described by mechanism, which is stable, rather than by specification. Service lives are given as typical ranges. No manufacturer, product name, price, biocide release rate or fuel-saving percentage is quoted anywhere - those figures are installation-specific and vendor-sourced, and false precision would be worse than the qualitative statement.

Understand this first

General average is not insurance and not liability - it is a rule of contribution, and it is one of the oldest ideas in maritime law. The problem it solves is a problem of incentives at the worst moment: if the master must sacrifice one interest to save the whole adventure, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, the owner of that interest would be ruined for everyone else's benefit and would fight the decision. Making the loss shared removes the conflict. Everything in limb (a) follows from that purpose - the sacrifice must be deliberate (or there was no decision to protect), for the common safety (or there is nothing to share), and reasonable (or the decision does not deserve protection). And limb (b) is simply that same test applied to an engine: damage suffered because the master chose to risk the machinery to save the ship is a sacrifice; damage suffered in the ordinary course is not.

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

The statement in the question is correct, and the features are not a matter of opinion: they are drawn from the definition itself. Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules provides that there is a general average act 'when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure'.

Note first how the Rules apply. They are not law: they take effect by incorporation into the contract of carriage - the bill of lading or charterparty - and the Rule of Interpretation then makes them apply 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. In India the concept is also recognised in the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

2. The essential features

Every phrase of Rule A is doing work, and each yields one essential feature:

  1. Extraordinary. The sacrifice or expenditure must be outside the ordinary performance of the contract of carriage. Fuel burned on a normal passage, or wear on machinery in ordinary service, is not extraordinary however costly.
  2. Intentional and voluntary. The loss must be deliberately incurred. Cargo washed overboard by a sea is accidental and lies where it falls; cargo jettisoned by decision is a sacrifice. This is the single feature that most sharply separates general from particular average.
  3. Reasonable. Reinforced by the Rule Paramount, which overrides everything else: 'In no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.'
  4. Peril. There must be real danger - not necessarily immediate, but genuine and substantial, and more than the ordinary risk of the sea.
  5. Common safety and common adventure. The act must be for the benefit of the whole adventure, not one interest alone, and there must be more than one interest at risk. A ship in ballast with no cargo on board has no common adventure and can have no general average.
  6. Preservation of property. The purpose must be to preserve the property from that peril. Success matters: there must be something saved to contribute.

3. The lettered rules that qualify the definition

  • Rule C - only losses which are the direct consequence of the general average act are allowed. It expressly excludes demurrage, loss of market, loss caused by delay and any indirect loss whatsoever, and excludes environmental damage and the consequences of escaping pollutants.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected even though the event was due to the fault of one of the parties, without prejudice to any remedies or defences in respect of that fault. So the adjustment proceeds first, and blame is fought separately.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming, who must give notice in writing to the average adjuster and supply particulars, generally within twelve months.

4. How the loss is shared, and what general average is not

Under Rule A.2 the sacrifice is borne by the different contributing interests - ship, cargo and freight at risk - rateably in proportion to their values at the termination of the adventure. The adjustment is prepared by an average adjuster, and security is taken before cargo is released, normally an average bond from the receiver and an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter.

Two distinctions complete the picture. General average is not particular average: particular average is accidental damage, falls on the interest that suffers it and is not shared. And general average is not insurance: it is a rule of contribution between the parties to the adventure, which predates marine insurance entirely, though the contribution is in practice recoverable under the policy.

5. b) The refloating scenario - the rule that governs it

This situation is dealt with expressly, and by a rule written for exactly these facts. Rule VII, 'Damage to Machinery and Boilers', provides:

'Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.'

6. Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not

Yes, the burnt bearings can be allowed in general average - provided four things are shown.

  1. The ship was ashore. She was aground, so the first condition is met on the facts given.
  2. She was in a position of peril. This must be established, not assumed. A vessel lying safely aground on soft mud in fine weather, in no danger and awaiting the tide, is not in peril; one working on a hard bottom, or exposed to weather, tide or the risk of breaking her back, is. This is the condition on which the claim will actually turn.
  3. The machinery was worked with an actual intention to refloat for the common safety. Running the engine ahead and astern to shake her off is precisely that - a deliberate act directed at the safety of the whole adventure, not a routine manoeuvre.
  4. The damage arose at the risk of such damage. The master must have accepted the risk of damaging the engine as the price of refloating. Working an engine hard, astern and ahead, against a grounded hull is known to overload the bearings, and it is that knowing acceptance that makes the loss a sacrifice rather than an accident.

The damage then satisfies Rule A as well: it is extraordinary (not ordinary service), intentional (the engine was deliberately worked in a way likely to damage it), reasonable if refloating was a sensible response, and directed at the common safety. It is also the direct consequence of the act, as Rule C requires. Under Rule D it makes no difference to the right of contribution that the grounding may have been somebody's fault.

The converse must be stated, because it is the trap. Rule VII closes with an absolute prohibition: where the ship is afloat, damage from working the propelling machinery is 'in no circumstances' allowed. So if she had already come off and the bearings burnt while manoeuvring clear, or if she were merely touching and not in peril, the same physical damage would be particular average - the owner's own loss, recoverable if at all under the hull policy and not shared with cargo. The engine damage is identical; the legal answer is opposite; and what decides it is the ship's condition and the master's purpose at the moment the engine was worked.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) is a 'discuss the statement' question, and the highest-scoring way to discuss it is to show that the essential features are not a list somebody invented but the clauses of the Rule A definition read one at a time. That converts a memory answer into an analytical one. Limb (b) is worth the same 8 marks and is decided by a single rule, so the answer must quote Rule VII, apply its four conditions to the facts, and then state the converse. An answer that says 'yes, it is a sacrifice' without naming the rule or testing peril has taken about half the marks available.

Why general average exists at all

It is far older than marine insurance and older than most maritime law - the principle is found in the Rhodian sea law and passed through Roman law into every modern maritime system. The problem it solves is one of incentives at the worst possible moment. If a master must jettison cargo to save the ship, and the loss lay entirely where it fell, then whoever owned that particular cargo would bear everything while the rest of the adventure was saved at his expense. Nobody would consent, and the master's decision would be contested at the moment when hesitation is most dangerous. Making the loss a shared loss removes the conflict: every interest knows that a sacrifice made for the common safety will be contributed to by all. Seen that way, general average is a rule about decision-making under peril, not really a rule about money.

Sacrifice versus accident - the line that decides everything

Almost every general average problem in an examination is a disguised question about whether the loss was chosen. Cargo swept overboard by a sea is an accident; cargo jettisoned is a sacrifice. An engine that fails on passage is an accident; an engine deliberately worked to destruction to refloat the ship is a sacrifice. Water damage from a leak is an accident; water damage from firefighting is a sacrifice. Rule VII is simply that same line drawn for one specific case - and the drafters drew it so firmly that they added an absolute prohibition for the afloat case, because otherwise every hard manoeuvre would generate a claim.

The four conditions of Rule VII, and which one is really in issue

Ashore; in a position of peril; an actual intention to float her for the common safety; at the risk of such damage. In practice the first and third are usually obvious on the facts and the fourth follows from them. The condition that is actually litigated is peril. A ship aground is not automatically in peril - the question is whether she was in real danger, having regard to the bottom, the weather, the tide, her condition and the exposure of the position. That is why a careful answer says the claim 'turns on establishing peril' rather than simply asserting it, and why the master's log, weather records and any surveyor's evidence matter so much afterwards.

Which version of the Rules applies

The York-Antwerp Rules are contractual, not statutory. They apply because the bill of lading or charterparty says they do, which is why the Rule of Interpretation has to declare that they operate 'to the exclusion of any law and practice inconsistent therewith'. The consequence is that the version in force between the parties may be the 1994, 2004 or 2016 text, depending on what the contract incorporates. On this question it does not matter: Rule VII is materially identical in all three. On other questions it can matter a great deal, so the habit of asking which version is incorporated is worth forming.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the features without tying them to Rule A, which is where they come from.
  • Omitting the requirement of a common adventure - with no cargo on board there is nothing to contribute and no general average.
  • Forgetting the Rule Paramount. Even a textbook sacrifice is disallowed if unreasonably made.
  • Answering limb (b) 'yes' without naming Rule VII or testing whether the ship was in peril.
  • Missing the afloat prohibition, which is the other half of the rule and the source of the marks for a complete answer.
  • Saying the owner's fault in grounding the ship defeats the claim. Rule D says it does not, without prejudice to separate remedies.
  • Confusing general average with particular average, or treating general average as a form of insurance rather than of contribution.

Examiner traps

  • 'General average is based on certain essential features' - the word essential invites a definitional answer. Give the features as conditions, each of which must be satisfied.
  • 'Discuss the statement' - agreement plus explanation. Do not merely define general average.
  • 'Give justification to your answer' in limb (b) - the justification is the marks. A bare yes or no scores almost nothing.
  • The facts say 'ahead and astern directions ... to shake the vessel loose'. That wording is doing deliberate work: it establishes both the intention to refloat and the acceptance of risk, so quote it back.

Understand this first

The phrase human element is not a polite word for human error. In STCW it means the whole set of conditions that decide whether a competent person actually performs competently on the day - whether the standard he was assessed against was published and objective, whether he has slept, whether he is medically fit, whether he can work as part of a team, and whether his company has made any of that possible. That is why limb A cannot be answered with rest hours alone: rest is one of four fronts. Model written answer

1. A) What the human element means in STCW

STCW refuses to treat competence as something that time served automatically confers. It replaces experience with demonstrated competence, and then addresses the conditions - fitness, rest, teamwork and company responsibility - under which competence can actually be exercised. The instrument is the STCW Convention and Code, 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force from 1 January 2012.

2. Competence-based standards

  • The Part A tables of competence specify, for every function and level, the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods for demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard, not an examiner's impression.
  • Part A is mandatory; Part B is guidance - a distinction worth stating explicitly.
  • The Manila amendments added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking at operational level and leadership and managerial skill at management level - where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention. It is the explicit recognition that technically competent individuals still fail as a team.
  • Regulation I/6 governs training and assessment, and regulation I/8 requires a quality standards system, so the training system itself is audited rather than assumed.
  • Regulation I/11 requires periodic revalidation, and chapter VI mandatory safety familiarisation and basic training before a seafarer is assigned duties.

3. Fitness for duty - the physical human being

  • Regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require that watch systems are arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The Manila-amended A-VIII/1 minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Authorised exceptions are tightly conditioned: weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours in any 7-day period, exceptions from the 77-hour norm may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • The Manila amendments also introduced alcohol limits for masters, officers and other seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards, and regulation VIII/2 with section A-VIII/2 governs watchkeeping arrangements and principles.

4. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring that seafarers hold valid certificates, are familiarised with their duties and with the ship's arrangements, and that documentation is maintained - so the human element is not left as an individual's problem. Administrations operate quality standards systems and are subject to independent evaluation and reporting to IMO.

5. B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The guidance is MSC.1/Circ.1598, 'Guidelines on fatigue', of 24 January 2019, approved at the 100th session of the Maritime Safety Committee and finalized by the HTW Sub-Committee. It supersedes MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, not mandatory - the binding rest-hour requirements remain in STCW chapter VIII and in MLC 2006. The Guidelines are arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party:

  • Module 1 - Fatigue: what it is and its causes, grouped as sleep and rest, the biological clock and circadian rhythm, health and stress, environmental factors such as noise, vibration and motion, sleep disorders and operational factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods.
  • Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company: assessing, mitigating and managing fatigue risk in the safety management system, in manning decisions and in scheduling.
  • Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer: practical guidance for the master, officers and ratings on sleep quality, diet and the limits of countermeasures - notably that caffeine is not a substitute for sleep.
  • Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training.
  • Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design: mitigation designed in - accommodation location, noise, vibration, lighting and habitability.
  • Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities: how regulators and inspectors should approach it.

6. How the guidance and the rules fit together

The Guidelines are explicit that hours of rest are set by a prescriptive formula in STCW chapter VIII, and that complying with the formula is not the same as being rested: managers must allow for circadian rhythm and sleep debt, and - in the Guidelines' own words - rest means rest, not the substitution of a different form of work. The same theme runs through MLC 2006 Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7. A management-level engineer should therefore treat recorded compliance as the floor, and a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant as a warning rather than a reassurance.

Why this structure scores

The organising idea for limb A

STCW attacks the human element on three fronts, and saying so gives the limb a spine: can they do it (competence standards, assessed against published criteria), are they fit to do it now (fitness for duty - rest, medical, alcohol), and who is responsible for making that possible (regulation I/14, the company). The Manila amendments strengthened all three and added the fourth dimension the older Convention lacked - can they work as a team, through mandatory leadership and teamworking training.

The numbers you must get right

10 hours in any 24, and 77 hours in any 7 days. Divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods. Exceptions may not go below 70 hours in any 7 days and may not run more than two consecutive weeks. The wrong-edition trap, stated plainly

This is worth a paragraph of its own because it caught this project. A freely available IMO circular copy of section A-VIII/1 - STCW.6/Circ.1 - reproduces the 1995 Code: five paragraphs, 70 hours weekly, and no 14-hour interval provision at all. Model written answer

1. a) Audit and survey distinguished

A survey is an examination of the ship herself - her structure, machinery, equipment and arrangements - against prescriptive technical requirements, to establish that her physical condition satisfies the relevant convention. A safety management audit is a systematic, independent and documented examination of the management system, using objective evidence to establish whether the procedures required by the ISM Code exist, are understood, are implemented and are effective.

Put shortly: a survey looks at the ship; an audit looks at the system and the people who run it. A survey verifies condition and reports deficiencies; an audit verifies management and reports non-conformities and observations.

2. b) The position of the recognized organization

The four scenarios below all turn on one point. Under the 2023 Guidelines on implementation of the ISM Code by Administrations the Administration is responsible for verifying compliance with the Code and for issuing the Document of Compliance to companies and the Safety Management Certificate to ships. A recognized organization acts only under a written authorization from that Administration, and where an Administration authorizes an organization to issue these certificates on its behalf, the RO Code (mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/1) and the III Code apply.

So the RO makes the technical judgements that verification requires - is this a non-conformity, is it major, has corrective action been taken. It does not have authority to alter the terms on which the flag State certified the ship. That distinction decides limbs (iii) and (iv).

3. Where there is evidence of a major non-conformity

A major non-conformity is an identifiable deviation posing a serious threat to the safety of personnel or the ship, or a serious risk to the environment, requiring immediate corrective action, and it includes the lack of effective and systematic implementation of a requirement of the Code.

  • A certificate must not be issued, and an existing certificate must not be endorsed for annual or intermediate verification.
  • The finding must be downgraded to a non-conformity by immediate corrective action before the certificate can be acted on; otherwise the certificate is withdrawn.
  • The RO reports to the Administration and acts under the documented procedures its certification scheme is required to hold for actions in cases of major non-conformity.
  • After a withdrawal, an interim certificate must not be issued in its place, and a new DOC or SMC is issued only after an initial verification, or an additional verification of the extent and scope of an initial verification.
  • Failure to take adequate corrective action, including measures to prevent recurrence, may itself be considered a major non-conformity.

4. Where non-conformities are found

An ordinary non-conformity is an observed situation where objective evidence shows a specified requirement has not been fulfilled, but without the serious threat that makes it major. The consequences are materially different.

  • The certificate may still be issued or endorsed.
  • The company - not the RO - is responsible for determining and initiating the corrective action needed to correct the non-conformity or its cause.
  • Corrective actions and any subsequent audits are completed within the time period agreed; for corrective actions this should not normally exceed three months.
  • The company applies for the follow-up audits as agreed.
  • The findings are recorded in the audit report, a copy of which goes to the company.
  • Failure to correct non-conformities with specific requirements of the Code may affect the validity of the Document of Compliance and the related Safety Management Certificates.

5. Where an extension of the Safety Management Certificate is requested

Neither the ISM Code nor the 2023 Guidelines contains a general power to extend a Safety Management Certificate. The Code provides for renewal verification before expiry, and fixes the validity of the new certificate where renewal is completed within three months before expiry - but that is a renewal, not an extension.

It follows that the RO has nothing of its own to grant. The request lies outside the delegated verification function, and the RO's action is to refer it to the Administration, which alone may decide what to do with a certificate it issued, and to act only on the Administration's specific instruction. The RO advises the company that the proper course is to apply for renewal verification before the certificate expires, and records the request and the referral.

6. Where revision of an entry on a certificate is requested

The entries on a DOC or SMC record what the flag State certified - the company and its address, the ship, the ship types covered. The RO issues on the Administration's behalf and so may not amend that record on its own authority. Its first action is to establish which kind of change is being asked for.

  • Administrative - a change of company name or address, or of ship name, where the underlying facts of certification are unchanged. The RO verifies the change is genuine and supported, refers it to the Administration, and on its authority the certificate is reissued with the corrected entry and the original expiry date retained.
  • Substantive - a change of the company actually responsible for operating the ship, a change of flag, or the addition of a ship type not covered by the DOC. These are not revisions at all. They require fresh verification and a new certificate, with an interim certificate issued to cover the intervening period where the criteria for one are met.

In both cases the RO records the change and reports it to the Administration; it does not simply amend the certificate in hand.

7. c) When the DOC and the SMC may be invalidated

The Document of Compliance may be withdrawn or cease to be valid where:

  • the annual verification is not requested - it must be carried out within three months before or after each anniversary date;
  • there is evidence of major non-conformity with the Code;
  • it expires without renewal verification having been completed;
  • the company changes - the DOC is issued to a company, not to a ship;
  • the company is found to be operating ship types not stated on it, or has stopped operating a type, so that appropriate action is required.

The Safety Management Certificate may be withdrawn or cease to be valid where:

  • the Document of Compliance is withdrawn - all associated Safety Management Certificates and interim certificates are withdrawn with it, and this is the trigger candidates most often miss;
  • the intermediate verification is not requested - at least one is required between the second and third anniversary dates;
  • there is evidence of major non-conformity with the Code;
  • it expires without renewal verification;
  • the ship changes flag, or the company responsible for her operation changes, so that the certificate no longer records the true position.

Underlying all of these, failure to correct non-conformities may affect the validity of the Document of Compliance and the related Safety Management Certificates, and failure to take adequate corrective action may itself be treated as a major non-conformity - which then engages the withdrawal triggers above.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks are 2 + 8 + 6 and the answer is shaped to them. Limb (a) is two marks: one clean distinction, no padding. Limb (b) is four scenarios at two marks each, so each gets a short, specific list of actions rather than an essay. Limb (c) is six marks and is written as two separate lists, DOC and SMC, because the triggers differ and a single merged list loses the one point the examiner is most likely to be looking for.

Step 2 is not in the printed question and is the reason the answer holds together. Establishing that the RO acts only under delegated authority before you answer the four scenarios turns limbs (iii) and (iv) from guesswork into a deduction.

The edition point - and why it matters at this sitting

The IMO guidelines on implementation of the ISM Code by Administrations were replaced by resolution A.1188(33), the 2023 Guidelines, adopted on 6 December 2023. Its operative paragraph 5 revokes resolution A.1118(30) - the 2017 revision, which had stood for six years and which almost all circulating study material cites.

At the date of this paper the 2023 Guidelines had been the current edition for four months. Citing A.1118(30) here is a wrong-edition error. It is a dangerous one because the substance of the paragraphs used in this answer is largely unchanged between the two, so the answer still reads correctly - only the authority is wrong. The 33rd Assembly of December 2023 is a standing edition boundary for every 2024 sitting: check any Assembly-adopted guideline you are about to cite in a 2024 paper against it.

The idea that makes limb (b) easy

Ask who owns the decision. The Administration is responsible for verifying compliance and issuing the certificates; the RO only acts under a written authorization. So split the four scenarios in two:

  • (i) and (ii) are technical findings. Deciding whether something is a non-conformity, whether it is major, and whether corrective action is adequate is exactly what the RO was authorized to do. The RO acts.
  • (iii) and (iv) are changes to the terms of certification. Extending a certificate or altering an entry on it changes what the flag State certified. The RO has no authority of its own and refers to the Administration.

If you can state that split, you can construct both halves of limb (b) even under pressure, and you will avoid the common error of inventing a rule that lets the RO extend a certificate on its own.

Common mistakes

  • Citing A.1118(30). It was revoked on 6 December 2023, four months before this paper.
  • Treating a major non-conformity as merely a worse ordinary one. The consequence is categorical: the certificate cannot be issued or endorsed, where an ordinary non-conformity does not stop it.
  • Saying the RO corrects the non-conformity. The company determines and initiates corrective action; the RO verifies it.
  • Forgetting that withdrawing the DOC withdraws all the associated SMCs. This is the single highest-value point in limb (c).
  • Inventing an extension provision for the SMC. There is none in the Code or the Guidelines. Saying so, and referring the request to the Administration, is the correct answer and a stronger one than a fabricated rule.
  • Confusing revision of an entry with a change of company or flag. The first is a correction to the record; the second needs fresh verification and a new certificate.
  • Writing limb (a) at length. It is worth two marks.

Examiner traps

  • The three-month figure appears twice and means different things. Corrective action should normally not exceed three months; annual verification of the DOC is carried out within three months before or after the anniversary date. Do not merge them.
  • Anniversary versus expiry. Annual verification runs off the anniversary date; the intermediate verification of the SMC falls between the second and third anniversary dates. Work backwards from the expiry date when you check one.
  • 'Invalidated' is broader than 'withdrawn'. A certificate can cease to be effective by expiry, or by the facts on it ceasing to be true, without anyone withdrawing it. Answer both.
  • Who issues, who verifies. An examiner may press on whether the RO or the Administration withdraws a certificate. The Administration withdraws it, or the Contracting Government which issued it does so at the Administration's request.
  • Interim certificates after a withdrawal. Expect the follow-up: no, an interim certificate is not the way back after a withdrawal for a major non-conformity.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Define a major non-conformity. How does it differ from a non-conformity?
  • You find a major non-conformity during an SMC audit. What happens to the ship?
  • Can the major non-conformity be downgraded, and by what?
  • Who withdraws a Document of Compliance, and on what grounds?
  • If the DOC is withdrawn, what happens to the ships' certificates?
  • When is the intermediate verification of the SMC carried out?
  • A company asks you to extend the SMC by a month. What do you do?
  • The company has changed its registered address. Is a new certificate needed?
  • Which resolution carries the current guidelines on implementation of the ISM Code by Administrations?

Memory framework

For limb (b), remember DECIDE or REFER. Findings - major and ordinary - the RO decides. Certificate terms - extension and entries - the RO refers. For limb (c), remember that the DOC is the parent: kill the DOC and every SMC dies with it. Then each certificate has the same two triggers of its own - verification not requested, and evidence of a major non-conformity - plus expiry and a change in the facts recorded on it.

Understand this first

UNCLOS is often called the constitution of the oceans, and the description is exact: it does not regulate the detail of anything, it allocates AUTHORITY. Limb (b) is the heart of it - as you move seaward from the coast, the coastal State's power weakens by stages, from full sovereignty in the territorial sea, through specific enforcement powers in the contiguous zone, to resource rights only in the EEZ and on the shelf, to no sovereignty at all on the high seas. Learn it as one continuous gradient and the five zones stop being five separate definitions to memorise. Limb (a) then follows the same logic: UNCLOS states the duty to protect the marine environment and says who must set the rules and who may enforce them - but for ships it hands the actual technical standard-setting to the IMO. That is precisely why MARPOL exists and why UNCLOS itself contains no discharge limits.

Model written answer

1. a) The general obligation

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, 1982, entered into force on 16 November 1994 and is properly described as the constitution of the oceans. Part XII is devoted to the protection and preservation of the marine environment, and its highlights are these:

  • A general obligation on all States to protect and preserve the marine environment. It is a free-standing duty, owed generally and not only to neighbours.
  • The sovereign right to exploit natural resources is expressly qualified by it. States may exploit their resources pursuant to their own environmental policies, but in accordance with the duty to protect and preserve.
  • A duty to take all measures necessary to prevent, reduce and control pollution of the marine environment from any source, using the best practicable means at their disposal and in accordance with their capabilities.
  • A duty not to transfer damage or hazards from one area to another, and not to transform one type of pollution into another. This is a sophisticated provision and a good one to quote - it prevents a State from solving its own problem by exporting it.
  • A duty to prevent the intentional or accidental introduction of alien or new species which may cause significant and harmful changes - the provision that underlies the whole ballast water regime.

2. a) Every source of pollution is covered

A frequently missed highlight is the breadth of Part XII. It is not a shipping instrument. It requires States to adopt laws and take measures addressing pollution:

  • from land-based sources, including rivers, estuaries, pipelines and outfalls;
  • from seabed activities subject to national jurisdiction, and from activities in the Area - the deep seabed beyond national jurisdiction;
  • by dumping, which requires the express prior approval of the coastal State;
  • from vessels;
  • from or through the atmosphere.

Land-based sources contribute the greater part of marine pollution, and an answer which treats UNCLOS as being about ships alone has missed the point of the Part.

3. a) Standards, and the role of the IMO

For pollution from vessels UNCLOS does something structurally important: it does not set technical standards itself. States are to act through the competent international organization - in practice the IMO - to establish international rules and standards, and national laws must have at least the same effect as those international rules.

This is the hinge of the whole system. It is why UNCLOS contains no discharge limits and no construction standards, and why MARPOL exists. UNCLOS allocates the authority; the IMO instruments supply the content.

4. a) Enforcement, cooperation and liability

UNCLOS then distributes enforcement three ways, which is one of its most practical features:

  • Flag State - must ensure compliance by ships flying its flag wherever the violation occurs, and must provide for effective enforcement irrespective of where it happened.
  • Port State - may investigate and, where the evidence warrants, institute proceedings in respect of a discharge, including one occurring outside its own waters. This is the basis of the port State control system.
  • Coastal State - may act in respect of violations within its territorial sea or exclusive economic zone, with the powers graduated according to the seriousness of the harm, ranging from requiring information to inspection and detention.

Beyond enforcement, Part XII provides for global and regional cooperation, immediate notification to States likely to be affected by imminent or actual damage, contingency plans, monitoring and environmental impact assessment, and scientific and technical assistance to developing States. It preserves the right of a State to intervene beyond the territorial sea to protect its coastline after a maritime casualty; it establishes responsibility and liability for damage caused by breach of these obligations; it gives coastal States special powers in ice-covered areas; and it preserves sovereign immunity for warships and other State ships on non-commercial service, while requiring States to ensure such ships act so far as reasonable in a manner consistent with the Convention.

5. b) Territorial sea

All the zones are measured from the baseline - normally the low-water line along the coast, with straight baselines permitted where the coast is deeply indented or fringed with islands. Waters landward of the baseline are internal waters.

The territorial sea extends up to 12 nautical miles from the baseline. The coastal State has full sovereignty over it, extending to the airspace above and to the seabed and subsoil below. That sovereignty is subject to one major qualification: the right of innocent passage enjoyed by ships of all States. Passage must be continuous and expeditious and must not be prejudicial to the peace, good order or security of the coastal State.

6. b) Contiguous zone

A zone contiguous to the territorial sea, extending up to 24 nautical miles from the baseline - that is, a further 12 miles beyond the territorial sea.

It is not an area of sovereignty. The coastal State may exercise only the control necessary to prevent infringement of its customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws and regulations within its territory or territorial sea, and to punish such infringement where it has already been committed there. The four subjects are the whole of it - it confers no general policing power.

7. b) Exclusive economic zone

An area beyond and adjacent to the territorial sea extending up to 200 nautical miles from the baseline. The distinction that carries the marks is between sovereignty and sovereign rights: in the EEZ the coastal State has sovereign rights, not sovereignty, and they are resource rights.

  • Sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, living and non-living, of the waters superjacent to the seabed and of the seabed and its subsoil, and for other economic exploitation such as the production of energy from water, currents and winds.
  • Jurisdiction over artificial islands, installations and structures; marine scientific research; and the protection and preservation of the marine environment.
  • Other States retain the freedoms of navigation and overflight and of the laying of submarine cables and pipelines. A merchant ship transits an EEZ as of right.

8. b) Continental shelf

The continental shelf is the seabed and subsoil of the submarine areas beyond the territorial sea - not the water column above it, which is the distinction from the EEZ. Its extent is defined two ways, and the coastal State takes the greater:

  • throughout the natural prolongation of its land territory to the outer edge of the continental margin; or
  • to a distance of 200 nautical miles from the baseline where the outer edge of the margin does not extend that far.

Where the margin extends beyond 200 miles the outer limit is constrained - it may not exceed 350 nautical miles from the baselines, or 100 nautical miles from the 2,500 metre isobath. The coastal State has sovereign rights for the purpose of exploring it and exploiting its natural resources. Two points distinguish a good answer: these rights are exclusive and exist automatically, without any need for occupation or express proclamation; and they do not affect the legal status of the waters above or of the airspace.

9. b) High seas

All parts of the sea not included in the exclusive economic zone, the territorial sea, the internal waters of a State, or archipelagic waters. They are open to all States, coastal and land-locked alike, and no State may validly purport to subject any part of them to its sovereignty. They are reserved for peaceful purposes.

The freedoms of the high seas include navigation, overflight, the laying of submarine cables and pipelines, the construction of artificial islands, fishing, and scientific research, each exercised with due regard for the interests of other States.

Jurisdiction rests with the flag State, which must exercise effective administrative, technical and social control over its ships. The recognised exceptions are important: piracy, which any State may act against; the right of visit where there is reasonable ground to suspect piracy, slave trading, unauthorised broadcasting or statelessness; and hot pursuit from a coastal State's waters, which must be continuous and ends the moment the pursued ship enters the territorial sea of its own or a third State. There is also a general duty to render assistance to any person in danger of being lost at sea.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (b) names five zones for eight marks, so each is worth about a mark and a half and each gets three things and no more: the limit, the legal character, and the principal right. Writing a page on the territorial sea and one line on the continental shelf is how this limb is normally lost.

Limb (a) says 'important highlights', which is an invitation to structure. Four structured highlights - the general duty, the breadth of sources, the deferral to the IMO, and the three-way enforcement - score better than one continuous paragraph on the duty to protect, however well written. The word highlights is asking you to select and organise, and that selection is itself being marked.

The one idea that carries limb (b)

Coastal State power decreases with distance, in stages. Do not learn five definitions; learn one gradient:

  • Internal waters - full sovereignty, no right of passage at all.
  • Territorial sea, 12 miles - full sovereignty, but qualified by innocent passage.
  • Contiguous zone, 24 miles - no sovereignty; only control, and only over four named subjects.
  • EEZ, 200 miles - sovereign rights over resources, plus jurisdiction over three named matters; navigation stays free.
  • Continental shelf - sovereign rights over the seabed only; the water above is unaffected.
  • High seas - no sovereignty at all; flag State jurisdiction only.

Said that way the answer is one idea rather than five, and the distances 12, 24 and 200 sit on it naturally. Note also that every one of them is measured from the baseline, not from the shoreline - the contiguous zone is 24 miles from the baseline, not 24 beyond the territorial sea.

The date of this paper

UNCLOS itself is stable: in force since 16 November 1994, and neither the zone provisions nor Part XII has changed. There is, however, one live boundary.

The BBNJ Agreement - the agreement under UNCLOS on the conservation and sustainable use of marine biological diversity of areas beyond national jurisdiction, sometimes called the High Seas Treaty - was adopted on 19 June 2023, which is before this sitting, and opened for signature in September 2023. It did not enter into force until well after this paper. So at this sitting it may properly be mentioned as adopted and awaiting entry into force, and it is a good closing sentence for limb (a) if you have time - but it must never be written as applying. Adopted, in force and applying are three different states, and collapsing them is one of the commonest and most costly errors in a law answer.

Common mistakes

  • Measuring the contiguous zone from the outer edge of the territorial sea. It is 24 miles from the baseline, which is 12 beyond the territorial sea.
  • Saying the coastal State has sovereignty in the EEZ. It has sovereign rights over resources - a materially different thing, and the examiner is watching for the word.
  • Confusing the continental shelf with the EEZ. The shelf is the seabed and subsoil; it says nothing about the water above.
  • Treating Part XII as being about ships. It covers land-based sources, seabed activities, the Area, dumping, vessels and the atmosphere.
  • Expecting to find discharge standards in UNCLOS. It sets none - it directs States to act through the competent international organization, which is why MARPOL exists.
  • Giving only flag State enforcement. Port State and coastal State enforcement are both express, and the port State power over discharges outside its own waters is the striking one.
  • Writing the BBNJ Agreement as being in force at this sitting. It was adopted before the paper and in force long after it.

Examiner traps

  • Sovereignty versus sovereign rights. The single highest-value distinction in the whole question. Territorial sea - sovereignty. EEZ and shelf - sovereign rights.
  • The four contiguous-zone subjects. Customs, fiscal, immigration, sanitary. Expect to be asked to name them, and expect no credit for a general 'law enforcement' answer.
  • 'Do the shelf rights need to be claimed?' No - they exist automatically and exclusively, without occupation or proclamation. A favourite follow-up.
  • Innocent passage. What makes passage not innocent, and does it apply in the EEZ? In the EEZ the freedom of navigation applies, which is wider than innocent passage.
  • Hot pursuit. It must be continuous and it ends when the ship enters the territorial sea of its own or a third State. Candidates routinely omit the ending condition.
  • The transfer provision. Not transferring damage from one area to another, or transforming one type of pollution into another, is distinctive and quotable, and few candidates give it.
