Model written answer

(a) The structure of IMO

1. What IMO is, and the organs that do the work

The International Maritime Organization is the specialised agency of the United Nations responsible for the safety and security of shipping and for the prevention of pollution from ships. It works through a two-tier governing structure - an Assembly and a Council - together with five main committees supported by seven sub-committees, all served by a permanent Secretariat headed by the Secretary-General. The governing organs decide policy, budget and membership questions; the technical output that reaches a ship comes almost entirely from the committees.

2. The Assembly - the governing body

  • The highest governing body. All Member States sit in it.
  • Meets in regular session once every two years.
  • Approves the work programme and votes the budget, and determines the financial arrangements.
  • Elects the Council.
  • Receives the reports of the Council and the committees, and adopts Assembly resolutions - recommendations, guidelines and codes.
  • Example of its output at this sitting: resolution A.1186(33), the HSSC Survey Guidelines 2023, adopted 6 December 2023.

3. The Council - the executive organ

  • 40 Members, elected by the Assembly for a two-year term, in three categories: (a) ten States with the largest interest in providing international shipping services; (b) ten States with the largest interest in international seaborne trade; (c) twenty States elected to secure geographical representation of all major areas.
  • Between Assembly sessions it performs all the functions of the Assembly, except the function of making recommendations to Governments on maritime safety and pollution prevention, which the Assembly reserves to itself.
  • Supervises the work of the Organization and co-ordinates the activities of the organs.
  • Considers the draft work programme and budget and submits them to the Assembly with its comments.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General, subject to Assembly approval.

4. The five main committees and what each owns

CommitteeWhat it is responsible for
Maritime Safety Committee (MSC)The highest technical body. All matters of navigational safety, construction and equipment, manning from a safety standpoint, casualty investigation, salvage and rescue
Marine Environment Protection Committee (MEPC)Prevention and control of pollution from ships - MARPOL and its amendments, ballast water, anti-fouling, and greenhouse-gas measures
Legal CommitteeAny legal matter within the scope of the Organization - liability and compensation conventions in particular
Technical Cooperation CommitteeImplementation of the Organization's technical assistance work, particularly for developing States
Facilitation CommitteeElimination of unnecessary formalities and red tape in international shipping - the FAL Convention

All five are open to all Member States. MSC and MEPC are the two that adopt amendments to the mandatory instruments.

5. The seven sub-committees, and the role they perform

The sub-committees are HTW (Human Element, Training and Watchkeeping), III (Implementation of IMO Instruments), NCSR (Navigation, Communications and Search and Rescue), PPR (Pollution Prevention and Response), SDC (Ship Design and Construction), SSE (Ship Systems and Equipment) and CCC (Carriage of Cargoes and Containers).

Their role in supporting the main committees is preparatory and technical. A committee meets for a week or so and cannot itself draft detailed regulation, so it remits the work to the sub-committee with the relevant expertise. The sub-committee develops the draft text, guidelines or unified interpretation, resolves the technical argument, and reports back to its parent committee - MSC, MEPC, or both. A sub-committee adopts nothing. It prepares; the parent committee approves; the committee or the Assembly adopts. That division is what keeps technical depth and treaty-making authority in different hands.

(b) Conventions and the other instruments

6. What a convention is

A convention is a formal, legally binding treaty between States. It is negotiated and adopted at a diplomatic conference or by the Assembly, is then opened for signature, ratification, acceptance, approval or accession, and enters into force only when the conditions written into the convention itself are satisfied - typically a stated number of Contracting States representing a stated share of world tonnage. It binds only those States that have become party to it. Example: the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea, 1974.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The marks in limb (a) are in the function columns, not the names. Anyone can write 'MSC, MEPC, Legal, TC, FAL'; the mark comes from saying what each one is for. The examiner has also asked one narrow question inside limb (a) - what role the sub-committees play in supporting the main committees - and it is the part most candidates leave out. Step 5 answers it explicitly and states the negative as well as the positive: a sub-committee has no adoption power.

Limb (b) is worth the same eight marks and is far quicker to write, so write it properly. A three-column table with an example in each row banks the marks in about a third of the time limb (a) takes.

The one idea that makes limb (b) easy

Candidates try to memorise which instruments are 'mandatory'. That is the wrong model and it does not survive a follow-up question. The right model is: obligation comes from the treaty route, not from the label. A Code is a container of technical text. It becomes binding when a convention to which a State is party says it is binding. That is why the ISM Code binds - SOLAS chapter IX says so - and why an identically-titled 'Code' with no such hook does not.

Common mistakes

  • Naming only MSC and MEPC as the 'main committees'. There are five, and the question says 'various main committees'.
  • Saying the Assembly meets annually. It meets in regular session every two years.
  • Describing the sub-committees as if they adopt regulation. They do not.
  • Writing that a Code is 'always mandatory' or 'always recommendatory'. Neither is true.
  • Confusing a Protocol with an amendment. An ordinary amendment is adopted under the convention's own amendment article; a Protocol is a separate treaty.
  • Giving no examples in limb (b) when the question expressly asks for at least one of each - that is a straight loss of marks for something the candidate already knows.

Examiner traps

  • The Council's size. An amendment to enlarge the Council from 40 to 52 was adopted in December 2021 but requires acceptance by two thirds of Members. It was not in force at this sitting. Write 40, and if the enlargement is mentioned, mention it as an amendment not yet in force.
  • The Secretary-General. Arsenio Dominguez took office on 1 January 2024, seven weeks before this paper. Naming his predecessor dates the script badly.
  • Being asked in the oral 'so is the ISM Code a convention?' The answer is no - it is a Code given mandatory force by SOLAS chapter IX.
  • Being asked which organ adopts MARPOL amendments. MEPC does, by resolution, under the MARPOL amendment article - not the Assembly.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which committee would deal with a new requirement for lifeboat release gear? MSC, remitted to SSE.
  • Who adopted the revised MARPOL Annex VI? MEPC, by resolution MEPC.328(76).
  • What is the difference between adoption and entry into force? Adoption is the decision to make the text; entry into force is when it starts to bind, on the conditions the instrument itself sets.
  • Name a Code that is not mandatory. Part B of the Intact Stability Code.
  • Who appoints the Secretary-General? The Council, subject to Assembly approval.

Model written answer

1. The background - the gap the Convention was written to close

By the late 1990s the international regime for oil pollution was substantial but narrow. The 1992 CLC, backed by the 1992 Fund, applied only to persistent oil carried as cargo in bulk by a tanker. It said nothing about the far more common case of a bulk carrier, container ship or car carrier spilling its own fuel. A large modern non-tanker may carry several thousand tonnes of heavy fuel oil - more than the entire cargo of a small tanker - and a claimant faced with such a spill had no dedicated convention, no guaranteed insurer to sue and no certainty of recovery. The International Convention on Civil Liability for Bunker Oil Pollution Damage was adopted on 23 March 2001 and entered into force on 21 November 2008 to close exactly that gap.

2. Its relevance - where it sits in the liability architecture

Its relevance is that it completes the map. Taken with CLC and the Fund for tanker cargo, and the HNS Convention for hazardous and noxious substances, the Bunkers Convention brings the fuel of essentially the whole world fleet inside a regime of strict liability, compulsory insurance and direct action against the insurer. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and exclusive economic zone of a State Party, and to preventive measures wherever taken. Its structural weakness, dealt with at step 7, is that it stops there: it creates no limitation fund of its own and no second tier of compensation.

3. (a) Pollution damage

Bunkers article 1.9 defines pollution damage as loss or damage caused outside the ship by contamination resulting from the escape or discharge of bunker oil, provided that compensation for impairment of the environment other than loss of profit from that impairment is limited to the costs of reasonable measures of reinstatement actually undertaken or to be undertaken; and it includes the costs of preventive measures and further loss or damage caused by preventive measures.

Against CLC 92: the definition is deliberately the same in substance. The drafters copied the CLC formula so that the two regimes would be interpreted consistently and no claimant would be better or worse off according to which convention applied. The difference lies not in what pollution damage means but in which oil, from which ship, triggers it.

4. (b) Bunker oil

Bunkers article 1.5: any hydrocarbon mineral oil, including lubricating oil, used or intended to be used for the operation or propulsion of the ship, and any residues of such oil. Three features matter. It expressly catches lubricating oil, which candidates routinely forget. It catches residues, so sludge and tank washings are within it. And unlike CLC it is not limited to persistent oil - a marine gas oil spill is bunker oil for these purposes.

Against CLC 92: CLC applies to persistent hydrocarbon mineral oil - crude, fuel oil, heavy diesel, lubricating oil - carried as cargo, and to such oil in the bunkers of a ship carrying that cargo. So the same drum of lubricating oil is CLC oil in a laden tanker's bunkers and Bunkers Convention oil in a bulk carrier's sump.

5. (c) Time limits for claims

Bunkers article 8. Rights to compensation are extinguished unless an action is brought within three years from the date the damage occurred. In no case may an action be brought more than six years from the date of the incident which caused the damage. Where the incident consists of a series of occurrences, the six-year period runs from the date of the first occurrence.

Against CLC 92: substantially identical - CLC article VIII sets the same three-year and six-year periods on the same terms. This is one of the places where the drafters deliberately mirrored CLC. Note the wording: the right is extinguished, not merely barred, so it cannot be revived.

6. (d) Exclusions

Three different things are commonly called exclusions and the answer should separate them:

  1. Excluded damage - the CLC carve-out. Article 4.1: the Convention does not apply to pollution damage as defined in the CLC, whether or not compensation is payable under it. This is the hinge that stops the two regimes overlapping. A laden tanker's bunker spill is a CLC matter, not a Bunkers matter.
  2. Excluded ships. Article 4.2: it does not apply to warships, naval auxiliaries or other State-owned or operated ships used on government non-commercial service, unless a State Party decides otherwise.
  3. The shipowner's defences - strictly not exclusions at all but a different route to no liability. Under article 3.3 the shipowner is not liable if the damage resulted from an act of war, hostilities, civil war, insurrection or a natural phenomenon of an exceptional, inevitable and irresistible character; was wholly caused by a third party's act or omission done with intent to cause damage; or was wholly caused by the negligence or wrongful act of a Government or authority responsible for maintaining lights or other navigational aids. Contributory fault of the claimant may also reduce or exonerate.

Against CLC 92: the defences are the same three, in the same terms. The excluded-ship provision is likewise mirrored. The CLC carve-out itself has no CLC equivalent, because CLC is the senior regime.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The printed question gives you your own headings - (a), (b), (c), (d) - so the structure is free. The mark is lost in a different way: by defining the four terms and never doing the comparison the stem asks for. Every one of steps 3 to 6 therefore ends with an explicit 'Against CLC 92' sentence. That single habit converts a half answer into a full one.

Step 7 exists because the most important difference does not live under any of the four definitions. It is structural - who is liable, what limit applies, and what stands behind it. Putting it in a table at the end banks a large number of comparison marks quickly.

The idea underneath the question

CLC is a cargo regime. Bunkers is a fuel regime. Everything else follows. CLC could afford exclusive channelling to the registered owner and a dedicated fund because the industry paying for the fund - oil receivers - is identifiable and finite. There is no equivalent constituency to levy for bunkers, so the Bunkers Convention got neither a fund nor its own limit, and had to widen the definition of 'shipowner' instead so that a claimant has more than one solvent defendant.

Common mistakes

  • Omitting lubricating oil from the definition of bunker oil. It is expressly in the text.
  • Saying bunker oil must be persistent. It need not be - that is a CLC requirement, not a Bunkers one.
  • Writing that liability is channelled to the registered owner. That is CLC. Under Bunkers it reaches the bareboat charterer, manager and operator as well.
  • Saying the Bunkers Convention has its own limits of liability. It does not - it borrows the LLMC.
  • Confusing the six-year long stop with the three-year period, or giving only one of the two.
  • Treating the shipowner's defences as if they were the article 4 exclusions. They are separate provisions doing separate work.
  • Forgetting the CLC carve-out - it is the single most important exclusion and the one that makes the system coherent.

Examiner traps

  • 'Whether or not compensation is payable under CLC.' Those words in article 4.1 matter. Even where the CLC claim fails, the Bunkers Convention does not step in. The claimant does not get a second bite.
  • The insurance thresholds are different units. Bunkers is 1,000 GT, a tonnage of the ship. CLC is 2,000 tonnes of oil carried, a quantity of cargo. Candidates frequently swap them.
  • 'Extinguished', not 'barred'. The right ceases to exist.
  • Being asked what happens after the LLMC limit is exhausted on a bunker spill. The honest answer is that the convention regime provides nothing further.
  • Being asked whether the Bunkers Convention applies on the high seas. It applies to pollution damage in the territory, territorial sea and EEZ - but to preventive measures wherever taken.

Likely oral follow-up

  • A laden tanker spills bunkers. Which convention? CLC - and Bunkers is excluded by article 4.1.
  • A container ship spills lube oil. Which convention? Bunkers - lubricating oil is expressly within article 1.5.
  • Who can the claimant sue? Any of the registered owner, bareboat charterer, manager or operator, jointly and severally; and the insurer directly.
  • Why is there no bunker fund? There is no identifiable cargo-receiving industry to levy, as there is for oil under the Fund Convention.
  • Is India a party? Yes, but write the answer on the Convention - the question asks about the Convention.

Model written answer

1. Why the hull form governs the power the ship needs

Total resistance divides into frictional resistance, which depends on wetted surface area and hull roughness; wave-making resistance, the energy left behind in the wave system; and viscous pressure or form resistance, arising from the way the flow separates and thickens around the hull. For a full-form, relatively slow merchant ship friction dominates, but wave-making and form resistance are the components the designer can actually change by reshaping the hull.

Hull form also sets the second term in the propulsion equation. Delivered power is resistance times speed divided by the propulsive coefficient, and the hull efficiency element of that coefficient - the relationship between wake fraction and thrust deduction - is a direct product of the aftbody shape. The hull therefore appears on both sides of the problem, which is why it is the largest single design lever available.

2. How hull form optimisation is done, and what changed

The modern method is parametric hull modelling coupled to computational fluid dynamics. The hull is described by a set of driving parameters, thousands of variants are generated and evaluated by CFD - potential flow for wave making, viscous RANS solvers for the wake field - and the promising forms are verified by model tank testing. CFD proposes; the tank still confirms.

The decisive change is what the hull is optimised for. Historically a hull was optimised at one design speed and one design draught. Since slow steaming became normal, ships spend their lives away from that point. Optimisation is now carried out over a matrix of speeds and draughts weighted by the ship's real operating profile, which is also why retrofit optimisation of existing ships is worthwhile - many were shaped for a speed they will never see again.

3. (i) Forebody optimisation

Scope. The bulbous bow - its volume, length, shape and vertical position; the waterline entrance angle; the fairing of the forward shoulder; and the stem profile. On existing ships this is the classic bow retrofit: the bulb is cut off and replaced, or removed altogether in favour of a straight stem.

Impact. The forebody governs the wave-making component. A bulb works by generating a wave system in antiphase with the bow wave so that the two partially cancel - but that cancellation is tuned to a particular speed and draught, and off that point the bulb can add resistance rather than remove it. A bulb designed for a laden design speed may be actively harmful in ballast or at slow steaming speed. Reshaping also improves added resistance in waves, which matters because the ship meets real seas rather than calm water, and improves ballast-condition performance where the old bulb sat near the surface.

4. (ii) Aftbody optimisation

Scope. The run of the buttock lines, the choice between U-form and V-form sections, the stern bulb, the shaft bossing and skeg arrangement, and the propeller aperture and tip clearances.

Impact. The aftbody's real job is not resistance but the quality of the water delivered to the propeller. A full stern produces a thick, distorted boundary layer, so each blade passes through a strongly varying inflow once per revolution. That non-uniform wake causes cavitation, pressure pulses, hull vibration and erosion, and lowers propulsive efficiency. Optimisation aims at a more uniform, better organised wake field, which allows a higher blade loading without cavitation and improves the hull efficiency term. There is a genuine trade-off: a wake that is too uniform loses some of the wake gain that makes a single-screw ship efficient in the first place.

Keep the distinction clear. Pre-swirl ducts, stators, fins and boss cap fins are energy saving devices bolted to the hull. They exploit a poor wake; they are not aftbody form optimisation, and an answer that treats them as the same thing has missed what the question asked.

5. (iii) Twin skeg design

Scope. Two separate skegs, each carrying its own shaft line and propeller, in place of a single centreline screw. It suits ships with high block coefficient, large beam and restricted draught, and high installed power - large LNG carriers, ro-ro and car carriers, and bulkers and tankers on draught-limited trades.

Impact - what it buys. On a shallow-draught ship, propeller diameter is the binding constraint. Two smaller propellers permit a larger total disc area and lower revolutions than one propeller squeezed under a flat stern, and open-water efficiency improves accordingly. Each propeller sits in cleaner, more uniform inflow outboard of the centreline, so cavitation and vibration fall. The hull can carry a fuller midbody without the usual penalty, protecting cargo capacity. Redundancy is a real operational benefit.

Impact - what it costs. Two skegs add wetted surface area, and therefore frictional resistance, which on a friction-dominated hull is a significant debit. Two shafts, sterntubes, bearings and sets of machinery raise capital cost and maintenance. Twin skeg is therefore a net gain only where the propulsive efficiency benefit outweighs the added friction - which is precisely the high-block, restricted-draught, high-power case.

6. What limits the gains, and where they are measured

The gains are design-specific and do not simply add: improving the wake field alters the flow the bulb sees, so the hull must be optimised as a whole. Because hull form is a design property it is one of the few genuine levers on attained EEDI for a newbuilding and on attained EEXI for an existing ship, both under MARPOL Annex VI. In service the benefit appears as reduced fuel per unit of transport work and therefore in the operational carbon intensity indicator, in force since 1 January 2023 - which is the commercial reason the subject has become urgent again.

Why this structure scores

The question names three items and asks for scope and impact of each. The highest-scoring habit on this question is trivially simple: write the words Scope and Impact as sub-labels under each of steps 3, 4 and 5. It guarantees that both halves of the question are answered for all three items, and it makes the examiner's marking obvious.

Steps 1 and 2 are not padding. Without the resistance breakdown you cannot explain why the forebody and aftbody are optimised for different objectives, which is the single insight that separates a good answer from a list.

The idea underneath the question

Forward and aft are solving different problems. The forebody fights resistance. The aftbody feeds the propeller. Almost every mark on this question can be reasoned out from that one sentence: it explains why bulb design is speed- and draught-sensitive, why aftbody work is discussed in terms of wake and cavitation rather than drag, and why twin skeg - which is an aftbody decision - is justified by propeller diameter and inflow rather than by reduced resistance.

Common mistakes

  • Treating a bulbous bow as always beneficial. It is tuned to a design point and can add resistance off it.
  • Discussing ducts, stators and fins as 'aftbody optimisation'. They are appendages.
  • Claiming twin skeg reduces resistance. It increases wetted surface and frictional resistance; the gain is in propulsive efficiency.
  • Giving a single percentage saving for each measure as though it were a constant. It is design-specific.
  • Forgetting that hull form optimisation is now done for an operating profile, which is the whole reason retrofits are viable.
  • Answering only 'scope' and never 'impact', or the reverse.

Examiner traps

  • Why remove a bulbous bow? Because the ship no longer runs at the speed and draught the bulb was tuned for. This is the classic follow-up and catches candidates who learned that bulbs are simply good.
  • Is a perfectly uniform wake ideal? No. Some wake non-uniformity is what gives a single-screw ship its wake gain; the aim is a well-organised wake, not a flat one.
  • Twin skeg on a fine-form fast ship? Poor choice - friction dominates the debit and there is no draught constraint to justify it.
  • Being asked which of EEDI, EEXI and CII hull form affects. All three, but EEDI and EEXI are design/technical indices while CII is operational.
  • Being asked to quantify. Decline to invent a number and explain what it depends on - ship type, operating profile, and how far the existing form is from its optimum.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Name the components of ship resistance and say which dominates on a loaded bulk carrier. Frictional.
  • What is thrust deduction, and why does the aftbody affect it? The propeller lowers pressure ahead of itself and increases hull resistance; the stern shape governs how strongly.
  • How is a candidate hull verified before build? CFD screening, then model tank test.
  • Why do twin skeg ships tend to be LNG carriers and car carriers? Large beam, restricted draught, high power, and cargo capacity that must not be sacrificed.

Model written answer

1. What the cylinder oil must do, and why it is once-through

In a two-stroke crosshead engine the cylinder oil is separate from the system oil and performs five jobs: it maintains an oil film between piston rings and liner; it neutralises the acid formed from the fuel's sulphur; it keeps the ring pack and piston crown clean; it helps seal the rings against the liner; and it carries wear debris and combustion residue down to the scavenge space. It is a total-loss or once-through system - every drop is burnt or scraped down and none returns. That single fact is why the quantity injected is simultaneously a running cost and the principal wear variable, and it is why the question asks about optimising it.

2. The system layout - what the sketch must show

A labelled line diagram should run in this order:

  1. Cylinder oil storage tank, with the grades carried - modern ships commonly hold more than one base number grade since the 2020 sulphur limit.
  2. Cylinder oil service (daily) tank, gravity-fed and heated, sited above the engine, with level alarm and a low-level cut-out.
  3. Heater and filter in the supply line, to bring the oil to the correct viscosity and protect the pump elements.
  4. Lubricator pump unit - one per cylinder on a modern engine, containing individual plunger elements, driven electrically or by servo oil pressure and controlled by a solenoid valve.
  5. Accumulator holding the delivery pressure so that a complete metered slug can be injected quickly.
  6. High-pressure delivery pipes to a set of injection quills spaced around the liner circumference at the level of the ring pack near the top of the stroke, each with its own non-return valve.
  7. Control unit taking crank-angle position, engine load and the fuel sulphur setting, with feedback of actual delivery.
  8. Scavenge space and drain, from which scrape-down samples are drawn.

Naming a make, the MAN B&W Alpha Lubricator arrangement is the standard example: a common-rail servo-oil-driven lubricator with one unit per cylinder, several quills per liner, and electronic timing referenced to the crankshaft.

3. How the dose is timed and controlled

The essential difference from the old mechanical lubricator is that the modern unit is electronically timed and independently metered. The control system knows crank angle continuously, and injects a discrete measured slug at the moment the piston ring pack is passing the quills, so the oil is laid down onto the rings themselves rather than sprayed onto bare liner and wasted. Delivery is not a continuous drip proportional to shaft revolutions; it is a pulse, and on some engines it is delivered every few revolutions at low load rather than every revolution, which is exactly what makes low-load running possible without over-lubricating. Because timing and quantity are decoupled from engine speed, the rate can be commanded from load and fuel sulphur rather than being fixed by a mechanical drive.

4. Why sulphur content drives both the base number and the quantity

Sulphur in the fuel burns to sulphur dioxide and sulphur trioxide. Where the liner wall runs below the acid dew point, water vapour in the combustion gas condenses and the oxides dissolve in it to form sulphuric acid on the liner surface. Left unneutralised this produces cold corrosion - corrosive wear of liner and rings, and in bad cases very rapid loss of liner material.

The cylinder oil carries an alkaline reserve - overbased detergent additive - measured as its Base Number. The quantity of acid produced is proportional to the mass of sulphur burnt, which is fuel consumption times sulphur percentage. Therefore the alkali delivered to the liner must track the sulphur burnt, and the engineer has two independent knobs to deliver it: the base number of the oil selected, and the feed rate. Alkali delivered is essentially base number multiplied by quantity - so a high-BN oil at a low rate and a lower-BN oil at a higher rate can deliver the same neutralising capacity, which is why the choice of grade must be made before the rate is set.

5. Setting the optimum feed rate in service

The procedure is: choose the grade, then set the quantity, then prove it.

  • Select the base number grade appropriate to the fuel actually in use, following the engine maker's and oil supplier's matrix. On the 0.50% global limit and the 0.10% ECA limit this normally means carrying and switching between grades.
  • Set the feed proportional to engine load and to fuel sulphur. The control system does this automatically on a modern engine: a base rate scaled by load, multiplied by a factor proportional to the sulphur percentage entered by the engineer. The sulphur figure must be entered from the bunker delivery note - the system cannot measure it.
  • Respect the maker's minimum feed rate. Below it the film fails regardless of what the acid calculation says, because the oil is also there to lubricate and to clean.
  • Optimise downward in small steps. Reduce by a modest increment, then hold and monitor for a defined running period before the next reduction. Never make a large single reduction, and never optimise during a period of unstable load or immediately after an overhaul.

6. How the engineer verifies the feed rate is right

7. The traps - changeover, over-lubrication and the minimum

  • Changing fuel without changing the setting. Entering an ECA and burning 0.10% sulphur fuel while the system is still set for the previous grade over-delivers alkali; the reverse under-delivers and invites cold corrosion. The sulphur entry and the grade selection are part of the changeover procedure.
  • Over-lubrication is not a safe error. Excess alkaline oil leaves hard calcium deposits on the piston top land and in the ring grooves, causes bore polishing which destroys the liner's oil-retaining honing pattern, sticks rings, and raises consumption cost. Candidates routinely assume more oil is always safer; it is not.
  • Never go below the maker's minimum, whatever a calculation suggests.
  • Watch the low-sulphur trap. On very low sulphur fuel there is little acid, but the oil is still needed for film strength and detergency, so the answer is a suitable lower-BN grade at an adequate rate, not simply less of a high-BN oil.

Model written answer

1. The 2023 IMO GHG Strategy - the frame everything else serves

The governing instrument is the 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships, resolution MEPC.377(80), adopted on 7 July 2023, which revoked the 2018 Initial Strategy from that date. Its vision is to phase out GHG emissions from international shipping as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition.

Levels of ambition: carbon intensity of new ships to decline through strengthened energy efficiency design requirements; carbon intensity of international shipping to decline, reducing CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 against 2008; uptake of zero or near-zero GHG technologies, fuels and energy sources to reach at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030; and GHG emissions to reach net zero by or around, i.e. close to, 2050.

Indicative checkpoints on the way to net zero: reduce total annual GHG emissions by at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, and by at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040, both against 2008.

2. The technical measures in force - EEDI and EEXI

The Energy Efficiency Design Index under MARPOL Annex VI chapter 4 requires a new ship's attained EEDI to be no greater than the required EEDI for its type and size, the required value tightening in successive phases. It is a design measure: it forces efficiency into the hull, propeller and machinery at the drawing board.

The Energy Efficiency Existing Ship Index, regulations 23 and 25, in force 1 January 2023, extends the same design-type calculation to existing ships, which was the gap in the original scheme. Compliance is assessed once, at the first annual, intermediate or renewal survey on or after 1 January 2023, and is commonly achieved by engine or shaft power limitation or by energy saving devices. Both indices are one-off design compliance; neither limits how the ship is subsequently operated.

3. The operational measures in force - enhanced SEEMP and the CII

The Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan, regulation 26, was enhanced to three Parts. Part I is the ship-specific energy efficiency plan; Part II is the fuel oil consumption data collection plan; and Part III, required on board on or before 1 January 2023, is the plan describing how the ship will achieve its required CII, including implementation, monitoring and self-evaluation. The operative guidance at this sitting is the 2022 SEEMP Guidelines, resolution MEPC.346(78).

The operational carbon intensity indicator, regulation 28, applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above and is the genuinely new operational measure. The ship calculates its attained annual operational CII, compares it with the required CII - the reference line reduced by an annual reduction factor - and receives a rating from A to E. A ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E for a single year, must develop a plan of corrective actions in SEEMP Part III. 2023 was the first full data year, so at this sitting the first ratings had not yet been issued.

4. The data foundation - the fuel oil consumption data collection system

Regulation 27 has required ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above to collect and report fuel oil consumption data since calendar year 2019. The Administration verifies the data, issues a Statement of Compliance and transmits the data to the IMO Ship Fuel Oil Consumption Database. This is not a reduction measure in itself, but every carbon-intensity calculation and every future policy decision depends on it, and the Strategy expressly relies on it for annual emission estimates.

5. What is under development - the basket of mid-term measures

This is the heart of what was ongoing at this sitting. MEPC.377(80) requires a basket of candidate mid-term measures comprising both:

  • a technical element - a goal-based marine fuel standard regulating the phased reduction of the GHG intensity of marine fuel; and
  • an economic element - a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism.

The Strategy sets the timeline: the basket should be finalised and agreed by the Committee by 2025, with dates of entry into force to be defined thereafter, and it must be developed on a well-to-wake basis and accompanied by a comprehensive assessment of impacts on States, with particular attention to least developed countries and small island developing States. None of it had been adopted at this sitting.

6. The mandated review, and the work running alongside

  • A review of the short-term measures - the technical and operational measures now in force - shall be completed by 1 January 2026, under regulations 25.3 and 28.11 and the Strategy at paragraph 4.3.
  • Life cycle assessment. The Guidelines on life cycle GHG intensity of marine fuels, resolution MEPC.376(80), were adopted alongside the Strategy on 7 July 2023 and underpin the well-to-wake basis of the mid-term work.
  • Safety. MSC has initiated development of a safety regulatory framework to support GHG reduction using new technologies and alternative fuels - the safety counterpart to the environmental measures.
  • Other candidate mid-term work named in the Strategy: measures addressing methane and nitrous oxide, incentives for first movers, onshore power supply and port-side developments, and further development of the LCA guidelines.

7. What has not yet happened at this sitting

Stating the boundary is part of a correct answer to a question about ongoing work. As at February 2024: no mid-term measure had been adopted; there was no IMO carbon price or fuel GHG-intensity standard in force; the first CII ratings had not yet been issued; and the next session of MEPC was still five weeks away, so the work described at step 5 stood exactly where the 2023 Strategy left it. The only carbon price then reaching ships was regional, not IMO - the EU Emissions Trading System, extended to maritime transport from 1 January 2024 - and it should be identified as such and not confused with an IMO measure.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question says technical and operational, which is IMO's own name for the two families of short-term measures. Giving them separate steps - 2 and 3 - guarantees both are covered and makes the classification visible to the examiner. The commonest way to lose marks here is to write a general essay on decarbonisation that never distinguishes a design index from an operating indicator.

Step 7 is unusual and is worth writing. A question about ongoing developments is a question about a timeline, and a candidate who states plainly what has not yet been adopted demonstrates command of that timeline. It also protects against the answer's single biggest risk - describing future work as though it were law.

The distinction that carries the question

Technical measures act on the ship as built. Operational measures act on the ship as run. EEDI and EEXI are calculated from design parameters and are satisfied once; a ship can pass EEXI and still burn a great deal of fuel by trading badly. The CII closes exactly that gap by measuring what the ship actually did over a calendar year. Say that sentence in the answer - it is the conceptual mark.

Common mistakes

  • Presenting the mid-term measures - the fuel standard and the carbon price - as adopted or in force. At this sitting they were candidate measures under development.
  • Attributing the SEEMP requirement to regulation 22. That is the pre-2021 numbering, superseded when MEPC.328(76) took effect; the SEEMP is regulation 26.
  • Confusing the DCS (regulation 27) with the CII (regulation 28). One collects data; the other rates the ship on it.
  • Quoting the 2018 Initial Strategy's targets. It was revoked on 7 July 2023.
  • Saying net-zero 'by 2050'. The adopted wording is 'by or around, i.e. close to, 2050', and the qualification was hard-fought.
  • Treating the EU ETS as an IMO measure. It is regional.
  • Omitting the checkpoints, or confusing the 40% carbon intensity ambition with the 20%/70% total emissions checkpoints. They measure different things.

Examiner traps

  • The two 2030 numbers. 40% is CO2 per transport work (carbon intensity). 20% striving for 30% is total annual GHG. Both are against 2008 and they are not the same thing.
  • 'By or around, i.e. close to, 2050' - quote it as adopted.
  • Which regulation? EEXI 25, SEEMP 26, DCS 27, CII 28. In numerical order, which is the easiest way to hold them.
  • Being asked what happens to a ship rated E. A plan of corrective actions in SEEMP Part III - and note that a single E triggers it, whereas D takes three consecutive years.
  • Being asked whether CII is a legal limit. It is not a hard limit; it is a rating with a corrective-action consequence, which is one of the criticisms made of it.
  • Being asked about the well-to-wake basis. It comes from the LCA guidelines, MEPC.376(80).

Likely oral follow-up

  • Difference between EEDI and EEXI? EEDI applies to new ships in phases; EEXI applies the same style of index once to existing ships.
  • How is EEXI usually achieved on an existing ship? Engine or shaft power limitation, or energy saving devices.
  • What are the three parts of the SEEMP? Energy efficiency plan; data collection plan; CII plan.
  • What is the technical element of the mid-term basket? A goal-based marine fuel standard regulating phased reduction of fuel GHG intensity.
  • When must the short-term measures be reviewed by? 1 January 2026.

Model written answer

1. The three cost families, and what distinguishes them

The three families are separated by what makes the money go out, not by how large they are. That test is what allows the Chief Engineer to see where he has leverage:

FamilyTriggered byBehaves like
CapitalOwning the ship at allFixed, committed before the ship traded
OperatingKeeping the ship manned, maintained and fit to tradeLargely fixed per day, incurred whether or not she is employed
VoyagePerforming a particular voyageVariable - stops when the voyage stops

2. What falls into each family

  • Capital costs - the newbuilding or purchase price, debt service and interest, depreciation, and the return required on the owner's equity. Major capital improvements belong here rather than in maintenance.
  • Operating costs - crew wages, victualling and travel; stores and consumables including lubricating oil and chemicals; spares; repairs and maintenance; drydocking and survey costs; insurance, both hull and machinery and protection and indemnity; registration and flag fees; and shore management and administration.
  • Voyage costs - bunker fuel, which normally dominates; port dues and charges; canal transit dues; and pilotage and towage. Cargo-handling costs are treated by some texts as a fourth family; here they are taken as voyage-related, and the convention is stated rather than assumed.

Bunkers are a voyage cost, not an operating cost. They are triggered by performing the voyage and stop when she stops. Misclassifying them is the commonest error on this question.

3. Where the Chief Engineer has leverage, and where he has none

Being honest about this is what separates a management answer from a list.

  • Capital cost - effectively no short-run influence. The price was paid and the loan signed before he joined. His influence is indirect and long-term: through the condition in which the ship is maintained, her residual value, and the technical advice he gives on major upgrades.
  • Voyage cost - his largest lever, because fuel is the dominant voyage cost and fuel consumption is a direct function of machinery and hull condition and of how the plant is operated.
  • Operating cost - shared with the office. Crew cost, insurance and flag fees are set ashore. Maintenance, spares, stores, lubricating oil and the drydock specification are substantially his.

4. Optimising voyage cost

  • Hull and propeller condition. Fouling raises the power required for a given speed and is often the largest single recoverable loss on a ship. Systematic performance monitoring, timely propeller polishing and hull cleaning, and a coating specification matched to the trade.
  • Machinery condition and performance. Indicator and pressure-rise data, turbocharger condition, air and gas differential pressures, cooler and economiser fouling - each degradation shows as fuel before it shows as a defect.
  • Correct operation for the loading in use. Slow steaming saves fuel but pushes the engine into a region it was not optimised for; running it there without the appropriate measures costs more in wear than it saves in fuel.
  • Fuel management. Bunker quantity survey and sampling at delivery, correct treatment and settling, avoiding commingling of incompatible fuels, and minimising unpumpable residues.
  • Auxiliary and hotel load. Generator loading, boiler and steam management, shaft generator use where fitted, and load reduction in port.
  • The regulatory and fiscal overlay. Under MARPOL Annex VI regulations 23, 25 and 28, EEXI and the operational CII - both in force since 1 January 2023 - make efficiency a compliance outcome and a data-integrity obligation, not merely a cost. At this date the only carbon price reaching the voyage account is regional: the EU Emissions Trading System extended to maritime transport from 1 January 2024. There is no IMO carbon price.

5. Optimising operating cost

  • Maintenance strategy. Breakdown maintenance is the most expensive way to run machinery; time-based maintenance overhauls items that were still serviceable; condition-based maintenance spends the money when the evidence says it is needed. The Chief Engineer's real contribution is choosing the right strategy for each item rather than applying one to all.
  • Off-hire avoidance. A day off-hire usually costs more than the repair that caused it. Ranking maintenance by the consequence of failure rather than by the cost of the part is the discipline that follows.
  • Lubricating oil management. Feed-rate optimisation, the correct base number for the fuel in use, and onboard and laboratory analysis to catch wear early.
  • Drydock specification. The docking is the single largest operating-cost event the Chief Engineer materially controls. A specification written from real condition data, rather than repeated from last time, is where the money is saved or lost.
  • Spares and stores control - which is the third part of the question.

6. Inventory control - classifying the stock

Modern inventory control begins by recognising that not all items deserve the same control. The techniques are ways of deciding which item gets which treatment:

TechniqueClassifies byUsed for
ABC analysisAnnual consumption valueConcentrating control effort on the few items carrying most of the value
VED analysisCriticality - Vital, Essential, DesirableDeciding what must never be out of stock regardless of price
FSN analysisMovement - Fast, Slow, Non-movingFinding dead stock and obsolescence
SDE analysisProcurement difficulty - Scarce, Difficult, EasySetting safety stock by how hard the item is to obtain

The important move is to combine them. ABC alone will under-protect a cheap but vital item; VED alone will over-protect an expensive but non-critical one. The practical shipboard method is the ABC-VED matrix, and the criticality axis is anchored in ISM Code element 10.3, which requires the Company to identify equipment whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations. That identification is the ship's own definition of 'Vital'.

7. Inventory control - ordering, and the limits of just-in-time at sea

  • Economic order quantity balances the cost of ordering against the cost of holding, giving the order size at which their sum is least. A useful idea, but it assumes steady demand and a stable lead time.
  • Reorder level and minimum-maximum. Reorder level is average consumption over the lead time plus a safety stock. On a ship the lead time is the hard variable, because it depends on where the ship will be, not on how quickly the supplier can act.
  • Safety stock buffers variability in both demand and lead time, and should be set by criticality rather than uniformly.
  • Lead-time and voyage-plan integration. Ordering is planned against the trading pattern so that deliveries fall where the ship will actually be, and against the maintenance plan so parts arrive before the overhaul.
  • Standardisation and fleet pooling. Common makers and models across a fleet cut the number of distinct items held and allow stock to be shared between ships.
  • Vendor-managed inventory and framework agreements with makers and service providers, which move some holding cost and obsolescence risk onto the supplier.

Just-in-time deserves a qualification rather than a recommendation. Minimising holding is sound for consumables and for non-critical items that are easily obtained worldwide. It is not appropriate for critical spares: a ship is not a factory beside a motorway, the lead time is measured in ports rather than hours, and the consequence of a stock-out is an unseaworthy ship rather than an idle production line. The correct answer is selective - just-in-time where the item is easy and the failure tolerable, and protected stock where element 10.3 says the failure is not.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three tasks, roughly a third of the marks each. The failure mode is predictable: candidates write at length on cost families, moderately on the Chief Engineer's role, and then produce two lines on inventory control because time has gone. Steps 6 and 7 are a full third of the question and the route is deliberately built so they are reached with time in hand.

Step 3 - being candid about where the Chief Engineer has no influence - is what makes this read as a management answer rather than a recitation. Saying plainly that capital cost is beyond his short-run reach earns more credit than inventing influence he does not have.

The idea underneath the question

Classify by trigger, not by size. Capital is triggered by owning, operating by keeping her fit to trade, voyage by performing the voyage. Once you classify by trigger you can immediately see which costs stop when the ship stops - and therefore which ones the person running the machinery can actually move.

Common mistakes

  • Classing bunkers as an operating cost. They are a voyage cost.
  • Listing the three families and never answering the second and third tasks.
  • Claiming the Chief Engineer optimises capital cost. He does not, in any short-run sense.
  • Naming ABC and VED without saying what each classifies by or how they are used together.
  • Recommending just-in-time for critical spares without qualification.
  • Treating drydock cost as unavoidable. The specification is where it is decided.
  • Forgetting that cargo-handling costs are treated differently by different texts - say which convention you use.

Examiner traps

  • 'Is fuel a voyage or operating cost?' Voyage. The commonest single trap.
  • 'Would you apply just-in-time on board?' Selectively - and never to items identified under ISM element 10.3.
  • 'ABC says this item is class C. Do you let it run out?' Not if VED says it is Vital. That is why the two are combined.
  • 'What is the largest recoverable fuel loss on a ship?' Usually hull and propeller fouling.
  • Being asked what happens when a ship goes off-hire. The daily operating cost continues while the revenue stops - which is why off-hire avoidance dominates the arithmetic.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which costs continue when the ship is laid up? Capital and most operating costs; voyage costs cease.
  • How would you decide the safety stock for a turbocharger spare? By criticality under VED and by procurement difficulty under SDE, not by its price.
  • What does ISM element 10.3 require? Identification of equipment whose sudden operational failure may result in hazardous situations, with specific measures to promote reliability.
  • Name three maintenance strategies and say when each is right. Breakdown, time-based, condition-based - chosen per item by consequence of failure.
  • How do you demonstrate a fuel saving is real? Systematic performance monitoring normalised for draught, weather and speed.

Model written answer

(a) The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification

1. What the HSSC is, and the problem it solved

Before harmonization each convention imposed its own surveys, its own certificate validity and its own expiry date. A single ship therefore held certificates falling due at different times, and had to be surveyed on several separate occasions by several separate attendances - with the ship out of service each time. The Harmonized System of Survey and Certification aligns the survey requirements and certificate validity periods of the principal statutory instruments into one common cycle with one common anniversary date, so that the ship can be surveyed for all of them at essentially the same time.

2. The instruments that carry it, and the edition in force

The HSSC was introduced by the 1988 Protocol to SOLAS 1974 and the 1988 Protocol to the Load Lines Convention 1966, by resolution MEPC.39(29) for MARPOL and MEPC.132(53) for MARPOL Annex VI, and by corresponding amendments to the IBC, IGC and BCH Codes; the Ballast Water Management Convention included it from the outset. The Guidelines now extend across SOLAS 74/88, the Load Lines Convention 66/88, MARPOL, the BWM Convention, the IBC, IGC and BCH Codes and the Polar Code.

The operative edition at this sitting is resolution A.1186(33), the Survey Guidelines under the HSSC, 2023, adopted 6 December 2023, which revoked A.1156(32) and states that it takes into account amendments entering into force before or on 31 December 2023.

3. The key objectives behind its establishment

  1. One survey occasion instead of many - align the surveys required by different instruments so they can be carried out together.
  2. A common certificate cycle and anniversary date, so all statutory certificates run and expire together.
  3. Minimise the time the ship is out of service and the commercial disruption that repeated attendances cause.
  4. Reduce the administrative burden on Administrations, recognized organizations and shipowners alike.
  5. Provide flexibility - a window within which a survey may be done - without lowering the standard being surveyed to.
  6. Remove conflict and duplication between the survey requirements of different instruments, so that a single coherent regime applies.

4. How the system works - survey types, cycle and time window

The Guidelines describe seven types of survey: initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal, inspection of the outside of the ship's bottom, and additional. The harmonized system provides for a one-year standard interval between surveys based on those types as appropriate - with the exception of MARPOL Annex IV, which is based on initial and renewal surveys only.

Certificates run for a maximum of five years on a common anniversary date. Flexibility is built in: the renewal survey may be completed within three months before the expiry date with no loss of the certificate's period of validity, and annual and intermediate surveys have a six-month time window - from three months before to three months after the anniversary date. The Guidelines also set out the conditions for extending a certificate, for revalidation, for surveys after transfer of flag, and - in this edition - for the application of remote surveys, which were brought in following the experience of the pandemic and advances in technology.

(b) The HSSC and port State control

5. (b) How the HSSC helps port State control

Efficiency - the officer meets one coherent certificate set on one anniversary date, in harmonised forms with a standard record of surveys and endorsements. The initial document check that begins every inspection is therefore faster and can be done consistently by officers in different regions. Because the endorsements are standard, an officer does not need to know each convention's separate survey vocabulary to read them.

Effectiveness - alignment makes a gap visible. If one certificate is unendorsed or a survey is overdue against its window, that stands out immediately against the others, and gives the officer clear grounds to move from a document check to a more detailed inspection under SOLAS regulation I/19 and the port State control procedures, which at this sitting are those of resolution A.1185(33). It also removes the situation in which a ship was technically in order on one certificate and lapsed on another, which previously allowed genuine deficiencies to hide in the mismatch. Fewer, better-documented surveys mean the officer can target effort rather than duplicate what the flag has already verified.

(c) Implementation

6. (c) The challenges and complexities of implementation

  • Getting an existing ship onto a single anniversary date in the first place, which requires certificates to be shortened or extended and is administratively awkward.
  • The window may not suit the ship. A trading pattern or a drydock booking may not place the ship where a surveyor can attend within the three-month-either-side window.
  • Surveyor availability - the world fleet is global and recognized organizations are not equally present in every port.
  • Multiple recognized organizations. Where a flag delegates different certificates to different societies, the coordination the HSSC was meant to simplify returns in another form.
  • Keeping the Guidelines current. Eight parent instruments are amended on their own timetables, so the Guidelines themselves must be reissued repeatedly - this edition revoked its predecessor after only two years.
  • Consistency of interpretation between Administrations and between recognized organizations, which determines whether the same standard is actually applied.
  • A survey is a snapshot. Alignment does not stop condition deteriorating between attendances.

The idea underneath the question

The HSSC harmonises the CALENDAR, not the standard. It does not change what a ship must comply with; it changes when and how often she is looked at, and makes all the looking happen together. Every objective, every port State control benefit and every implementation difficulty falls out of that one sentence - including the honest limitation that aligning the calendar does nothing about what happens between the dates.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing intermediate and periodical surveys. They are separate types; periodical attaches to the Cargo Ship Safety Equipment and Cargo Ship Safety Radio Certificates.
  • Saying the HSSC 'raised safety standards'. It harmonised the survey calendar; the standards live in the conventions.
  • Giving the window as three months rather than a six-month window, three months either side of the anniversary.
  • Forgetting that the renewal survey may be completed three months before expiry with no loss of validity - a favourite oral question.
  • Naming only SOLAS and MARPOL. The Guidelines cover eight instruments including the Polar Code.
  • Answering limb (c) only on challenges and never on how they are addressed.
  • Quoting the wrong edition of the Guidelines. See the trap below.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Name the seven survey types. Initial, annual, intermediate, periodical, renewal, inspection of the outside of the ship's bottom, additional.
  • What is the anniversary date? The day and month corresponding to the expiry date of the certificate, against which the annual and intermediate windows are set.

What abandonment changes - and what it does not. When the owners abandon, the practical initiative passes to the State, and the grounded vessel and her cargo fall to be dealt with as wreck in the hands of the receiver. But abandonment does not extinguish the owner's liability: the Act's pollution provisions allow the authorities to act and then recover the cost from the owner, and the compulsory insurance and direct-action machinery of the liability conventions means the claim in practice lies against the insurer.

2. Immediate action - life first, then report

  • Safety of life comes before ship, cargo or environment. While anyone remains on board, the master's duty is to them, and the Indian Coast Guard coordinates search and rescue.
  • The casualty is reported to the Administration through the Directorate General of Shipping and to the nearest port and local authorities, under the Act's casualty-reporting provisions.
  • The master, while he remains in command, takes measures to prevent escalation - securing the ship, sounding tanks, assessing hull damage and pollution risk, and preventing further movement of the vessel on the ground.

3. The pollution powers - Part XA

This is the machinery that answers the question directly where the owner has walked away. Part XA, Prevention and Containment of Pollution of the Sea by Oil, provides:

  1. Section 356J - power to give notice to the owner, etc., of a polluting ship. The Central Government may serve notice on the owner, master or agent requiring specified measures to prevent, reduce or contain pollution.
  2. Section 356K - powers to take measures for preventing or containing oil pollution. Where the notice is not complied with, or where urgency requires it, the Central Government may itself take such measures as it thinks fit - and the expenses are recoverable from the owner. On these facts, with the owners having abandoned, section 356K is the operative provision.
  3. Section 356L - power to give directions to certain ships to render services, which allows other vessels to be directed to assist in the response.

Operationally the response is conducted under the National Oil Spill Disaster Contingency Plan, for which the Indian Coast Guard is the central coordinating authority.

4. The wreck regime - the Receiver of Wreck under Part XIII

  • Section 391 - the Central Government appoints Receivers of Wreck for specified districts of the coast. Section 390 defines what 'coasts' means for this Part.
  • Section 392 - duty of the receiver where a vessel is in distress. On notice that a vessel is stranded or in distress on the coast, the receiver proceeds to the place, takes command, and directs the measures to be taken for the preservation of the vessel, of the lives of those on board and of the cargo. This is the single most directly responsive provision in the Act to the facts of this question.
  • Sections 393 and 394 - the receiver may pass over adjoining lands to reach the vessel, and may suppress plunder and disorder by force, which is a real risk on a stranded and abandoned ship.
  • Sections 395 and 397 - the procedure to be observed by persons finding wreck, and the notice the receiver must give.
  • Section 398 - immediate sale of wreck by the receiver in certain cases, for example where it is perishable or of little value.
  • Section 399 - the owner may claim the wreck or its proceeds by establishing his claim to the receiver's satisfaction within one year, on paying salvage and other charges.
  • Sections 400 and 401 - prohibition of certain acts in respect of wreck, and search warrants where wreck is concealed.

5. Salvage, and the place-of-refuge decision

Section 402 provides for salvage payable for saving life, cargo or wreck, with section 403 saving other rights and section 404 the rule-making power under which the 1974 Rules were made. In the ordinary case the owner engages salvors commercially; where the owner has abandoned, the State may have to procure salvage itself and look to the owner and the wreck proceeds for the cost.

If the casualty leads to a request to move the vessel to sheltered water, that request is assessed under the Guidelines on places of refuge for ships in need of assistance, resolution A.1184(33), adopted 6 December 2023, which revoked A.949(23). The Guidelines recognise that a coastal State is under no obligation in international law to grant a place of refuge, and provide a framework for balancing the ship's need for assistance against the State's interest in protecting its coastline.

7. Investigation, and recovery of the cost

  • Section 396 provides for investigation of certain matters in respect of vessels wrecked, stranded or in distress, which the receiver conducts.
  • Separately, a marine safety investigation falls to be conducted under the Casualty Investigation Code, resolution MSC.255(84), made mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/6. A grounding involving total loss or severe damage to the environment is a very serious marine casualty. That investigation is conducted to prevent future casualties and is separate and distinct from, and must not seek to apportion, blame or liability.
  • Cost recovery runs on two tracks: the expenses of measures taken under section 356K are recoverable from the owner, and the proceeds of the wreck in the receiver's hands stand charged with salvage and other charges under Part XIII before any balance reaches the owner.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question asks two things explicitly - what steps and who should initiate them - and most candidates answer only the first. Step 6 is a dedicated 'who does what' table for exactly that reason, and it is the fastest marks in the answer.

The three scenario facts each do work: Indian flag means the Merchant Shipping Act applies to her as an Indian ship; off the coast of India brings in the Part XIII wreck machinery and the coastal State's pollution powers; and abandonment is what makes the State's powers, rather than the owner's contracts, the operative mechanism. Say each of those in one line - they are the analysis marks.

The idea underneath the question

Abandonment does not create a legal vacuum. It shifts who acts, not whether anyone can. The Merchant Shipping Act anticipates precisely this: Part XA lets the Government act when the owner will not and bill him afterwards, and Part XIII puts a public officer - the Receiver of Wreck - in command at the scene. Once that is understood, the answer builds itself.

Common mistakes

  • Answering on general salvage law, Lloyd's Open Form and SCOPIC. The question says 'under the provisions of the Merchant Shipping Act' - the marks are in the Act.
  • Treating abandonment as ending the owner's liability. It does not.
  • Naming no authority at all, when the question asks who.
  • Forgetting the Receiver of Wreck entirely - he is the officer the Act actually puts in charge at the scene.
  • Putting property before life in the sequence.
  • Answering only on pollution, or only on wreck. The question says safety of the ships AND the marine environment.
  • Citing the wrong Act. See the trap below - this is the single largest risk on this question.

Examiner traps

  • Which Act is in force? At this sitting the governing statute is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958. Writing an answer on a later consolidating statute would be an anachronism, and it is the easiest way to fail this question.
  • 'The owner abandoned - so who pays?' The owner still does. Section 356K expenses are recoverable from him, and the insurer is reachable by direct action under the compulsory-insurance conventions.
  • 'Must India provide a place of refuge?' No. A coastal State is under no obligation in international law to grant one; A.1184(33) provides the assessment framework, not an obligation.
  • 'Who takes command at the scene?' Under section 392 the Receiver of Wreck, on a vessel stranded or in distress on the coast.
  • 'How long has the owner to claim the wreck?' One year from the time it came into the receiver's possession, under section 399, on paying salvage and charges.
  • Being asked whether the casualty investigation apportions blame. It does not - the Casualty Investigation Code requires it to be separate and distinct from any investigation of blame or liability.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is a 'wreck' for the purposes of the Act? The Act's wreck provisions cover vessels stranded, derelict and in distress on the coasts, and the cargo and articles belonging to them.
  • Who appoints Receivers of Wreck? The Central Government, under section 391.
  • What can the receiver do about looting? Section 394 gives him power to suppress plunder and disorder by force.
  • When may the receiver sell wreck immediately? Under section 398, in defined cases such as where it is perishable or of little value.
  • Which agency coordinates the oil spill response? The Indian Coast Guard, as central coordinating authority under the national contingency plan.

Model written answer

(a) The IMO and ILO human element regimes

1. (a) The human element, and how IMO and ILO divide the work

IMO's own term is the human element, and resolution A.947(23) treats it as a complex multi-dimensional issue affecting maritime safety, security and marine environmental protection, involving the entire spectrum of human activity - ships' crews, shore-based management, regulatory bodies, recognized organizations, shipyards and legislators. It is not simply 'crew error'.

The two organisations divide the work along a clean line: IMO regulates the seafarer as an operator of the ship - competence, safe operation, fitness for duty. ILO regulates the seafarer as a worker - employment, living and working conditions, health and welfare. Together they cover the same person from two directions.

2. The IMO regime - competence, safe operation and fitness for duty

  • STCW 1978, as amended by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force 1 January 2012 - the standards of training, certification and watchkeeping. Regulation I/6 on training and assessment, I/14 on company responsibilities, and chapter VIII with section A-VIII/1 on fitness for duty and minimum hours of rest.
  • The ISM Code, mandatory through SOLAS chapter IX - safety management, dealt with in limb (b).
  • Resolution A.1047(27), Principles of Minimum Safe Manning - the framework by which an Administration determines the manning a ship must carry.
  • MSC.1/Circ.1598, Guidelines on fatigue, of 24 January 2019, which address fatigue across the whole system rather than only on board.
  • Resolution A.947(23), the human element vision, principles and goals, which is the policy statement underlying all of the above.
  • The III Code and the mandatory Member State Audit Scheme, which audit whether a flag State actually gives effect to these obligations.

3. The ILO regime - MLC 2006, the fourth pillar

The Maritime Labour Convention, 2006 consolidated a large body of earlier ILO maritime instruments and is called the fourth pillar of international maritime regulation, alongside SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW. Its structure is distinctive: Articles and Regulations setting out the obligations, and a Code in two parts - Part A, mandatory Standards, and Part B, Guidelines to which due consideration must be given.

It is arranged in five Titles: 1 minimum requirements for seafarers to work on a ship; 2 conditions of employment; 3 accommodation, recreational facilities, food and catering; 4 health protection, medical care, welfare and social security protection; and 5 compliance and enforcement.

Title 5 is what makes it real. A ship carries a Maritime Labour Certificate and a Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance - Part I drawn up by the competent authority, Part II by the shipowner and certified by the authority or an authorised recognized organization - and is subject to flag State inspection, port State control, and on-board and onshore complaint procedures.

The position at this sitting. The amendments in force are those of 2014 (in force 18 January 2017, adding financial security for repatriation and for shipowners' liability), 2016 (in force 8 January 2019) and 2018 (in force 26 December 2020). The 2022 amendments were adopted but had NOT yet entered into force at this examination.

4. Where the two organisations act together

  • Medical fitness - joint IMO/ILO guidelines on medical examinations of seafarers, where the STCW medical certificate and the MLC health requirements meet.
  • Fair treatment of seafarers in the event of a maritime accident - joint guidelines addressing detention and criminalisation.
  • Abandonment of seafarers, addressed jointly and reflected in the MLC financial security requirements.
  • Hours of rest is the clearest overlap: STCW section A-VIII/1 sets rest hours for watchkeeping safety, and MLC Title 2 sets hours of work and rest as a labour standard. The two must be read together, and where they differ the more demanding applies.

(b) The human factor and the ISM Code

5. (b) The ISM Code - what it is actually trying to do

The ISM Code exists because a series of casualties were traced not to defective equipment but to defective management - unclear responsibility, commercial pressure on the master, and shore organisations that did not know what was happening on their own ships. The Code therefore regulates a system, not a component. Its objectives are to ensure safety at sea, prevention of human injury or loss of life, and avoidance of damage to the environment; and to that end it requires safeguards against all identified risks and continuous improvement of the safety management skills of personnel ashore and aboard.

That is why the human factor is not one topic within the Code - it is what the Code is made of. A safety management system is a set of human commitments written down.

7. Why implementation fails, and what makes it succeed

Where it fails:

  • Paper compliance. Checklists completed in advance, drills recorded but not run - the documentation is in order and the practice is not.
  • A system the crew do not own. An SMS written by an outside consultant, in a register the users do not read comfortably, describing a ship's operation that its own crew would not recognise.
  • Documentation overload. Excessive paperwork consumes the very hours the rest-hour rules protect, and fatigue then degrades the operation the system was meant to make safe.
  • Blame culture. If a report produces punishment, reporting stops - and element 9, the Code's learning mechanism, dies quietly while the certificate remains valid.
  • A DPA without independence, or a master whose overriding authority is understood by everyone to be career-limiting to use.
  • Familiarisation skipped on short crew turnarounds, and language barriers on multinational crews.

What makes it succeed:

  • A just culture that separates honest error from wilful violation, so that near-miss reporting is safe and therefore happens.
  • A proportionate system, written for the ship and in the users' working language, that the crew recognise as their own.
  • Visible leadership - the master and Chief Engineer using the system themselves, and the Company demonstrably accepting a safety decision that costs money.
  • A closed feedback loop, so crews see their reports produce change. Nothing sustains reporting like evidence that reporting works.
  • Management review that acts on trends rather than closing findings individually - which is where the latent organisational failures are caught, as distinct from the active failures at the sharp end.

In short: the ISM Code can require a system, but only people can make it a real one. That is how the human factor plays its role in implementation.

Common mistakes

  • Attributing the MLC to IMO. It is an ILO Convention.
  • Describing MLC Part B as mandatory. Part A carries the mandatory Standards; Part B is Guidelines to which due consideration must be given.
  • Answering limb (b) by listing the twelve ISM elements. The question asks how the human factor plays a role in implementing the Code.
  • Omitting the designated person ashore and the master's overriding authority - the two most explicitly human safeguards in the Code.
