Understand this first

Limiting liability is not the same as insuring against it, and the two are constantly confused. LLMC does not pay anybody; it caps what a claimant can recover from the shipowner or salvor, whatever the true size of the loss, and it does so by reference to the ship's tonnage rather than to who was at fault. Read the convention family as a division of labour: CLC and the other pollution conventions carry their own limits and compulsory insurance, so LLMC stands out of their way; conventions such as Bunkers and Nairobi create liability but no limits of their own and simply borrow LLMC's.

Model written answer

1. (a) The three principles the LLMC regime is built on

The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 was adopted on 19 November 1976 and entered into force on 1 December 1986. It rests on three organising ideas.

  1. A right to limit that is, in practice, unbreakable. Article 1 gives it to shipowners, charterers, managers and operators, to salvors, to those for whose acts they answer, and to their insurers. Article 4 removes it only for that person's personal act or omission, committed with intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result. Because the fault must be personal, negligent navigation by the master does not break it.
  2. The limit is fixed by tonnage, not by fault or by the size of the loss. Article 6 computes a global amount from gross tonnage, with a higher tranche for loss of life and personal injury than for property; Article 7 deals separately with passenger claims.
  3. One fund per occurrence, distributed rateably. Articles 11 to 13 allow a single limitation fund; claimants are confined to it and rank rateably, and other assets are protected from arrest for those claims.

2. How LLMC links to the other liability conventions

LLMC is the general, residual regime, and the specialised conventions relate to it in two opposite ways.

  • Conventions with their own limits stand outside it. The CLC 1992 creates strict, channelled liability on the registered owner for tanker oil pollution, with its own limits, compulsory insurance and direct action. LLMC therefore expressly excepts CLC oil pollution claims at Article 3.
  • Conventions with no limits of their own borrow LLMC's. The Bunkers Convention 2001 imposes strict liability but sets no limits; Article 6 preserves the right to limit under the applicable regime, in practice LLMC. The Nairobi Wreck Removal Convention 2007 does the same at Article 10(2).
  • So a bunker spill and a wreck removal limit in the LLMC fund; a tanker cargo spill under CLC. States may, however, reserve against LLMC applying to wreck removal.

3. (b) The liabilities that are subject to limitation

Article 2 lists the limitable claims, whatever the basis of liability may be:

  • loss of life or personal injury and loss of or damage to property occurring on board or in direct connexion with the operation of the ship or with salvage operations, including consequential loss;
  • loss from delay in the carriage of cargo, passengers or luggage;
  • loss from infringement of rights other than contractual rights in direct connexion with those operations;
  • raising, removal, destruction or rendering harmless of a wrecked or sunken ship and its cargo, subject to reservation;
  • claims by a person other than the person liable for measures taken to avert or minimise loss.

4. The claims left outside, and the conduct that breaks the limit

  • Article 3 excepts salvage and contribution in general average - a salvor's earned award cannot itself be cut down by the regime;
  • CLC oil pollution damage and nuclear damage, each having its own regime;
  • claims by servants of the shipowner or salvor, where the law governing the contract of service does not permit limitation;
  • and the limit falls away under Article 4 on proof of personal intentional or reckless conduct - a burden on the claimant, very rarely discharged.

5. (c) The principles of the Salvage Convention 1989

The International Convention on Salvage, 1989 was adopted on 28 April 1989 and entered into force on 14 July 1996, replacing the Brussels Convention of 1910:

  • salvage is a voluntary service to property in danger, outside any pre-existing duty, and no cure, no pay is the base rule - a reward is earned only by a useful result;
  • the reward is fixed on the Article 13 criteria - salved value, skill and efforts in preventing environmental damage, success, danger, time and expenses, risk of liability, promptness, availability of craft - and may not exceed the salved value;
  • Article 14 special compensation corrects the 1910 blind spot: where the vessel threatened environmental damage and the salvor earned no reward or a lesser one, the owner pays his expenses plus up to 30% where such damage was minimised or prevented, raisable to 100% of expenses if fair and just;
  • Article 8 imposes duties both ways - due care and best endeavours to prevent environmental damage on the salvor, full co-operation on owner and master - and commercially it is worked through Lloyd's Open Form, with SCOPIC as a tariff-rate alternative to assessing Article 14.

6. Why salvors may limit, and the limit that applies

The rationale is a specific failure of the previous regime. In The Tojo Maru a salvage company's diver, working in the water and not on board the salvage tug, negligently caused an explosion in the casualty. Under the 1957 Convention a salvor could limit only when operating from a vessel and where the act was done on board or in her navigation or management; the diver's act was neither, so the salvor faced unlimited liability.

LLMC 1976 answered it directly. Article 1 makes salvors persons entitled to limit, and Article 1(3) defines a salvor broadly as any person rendering services in direct connexion with salvage operations - covering the diver in the water. Because such a salvor may have no ship, Article 6(4) supplies a notional figure: limits calculated according to a tonnage of 1,500 tons.

That deemed tonnage falls in the lowest band, not exceeding 2,000 gross tonnage. In a State party to the 1996 Protocol as amended by the 2012 amendments, in force 8 June 2015, the limits are 3.02 million SDR for loss of life or personal injury and 1.51 million SDR for property claims. A State bound only by the unamended 1976 Convention applies lower figures, so the instrument must be identified before quoting any figure. India is a party through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act 1958, the 1996 Protocol and its 2012 amendments being given effect by rules made in 2015 and 2017.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The paper prints 5 + 5 + 6 and the route puts two headings under each limb, so an examiner marking limb by limb can see all three answered without hunting. The commonest way to lose marks here is to write everything you know about LLMC and never reach limb (c), which carries the most marks and the only question that needs a number.

Limitation is not insurance, and not a compensation fund

The convention family divides into liability instruments, which say who pays and on what basis; compensation instruments, which provide a fund that actually pays claimants, such as the IOPC Funds; and limitation instruments, which cap exposure. LLMC is purely the third. It creates no cause of action, provides no money and protects no claimant - it protects the defendant. The limitation fund is money the shipowner puts up out of his own pocket or his P&I cover, not a pooled international fund. Candidates who describe LLMC as "the fund that compensates victims" have confused it with the IOPC Fund Convention.

Why the limit is almost never broken

Article 4 looks like a normal fault test but is not. Three things narrow it almost to vanishing: the act must be the liable person's own, so vicarious fault does not count; the state of mind must be intent or recklessness with knowledge that such loss would probably result, which is far above gross negligence; and the burden lies on the claimant. In a corporate owner the search is for the directing mind, which is why the limit survives most casualties. This is deliberate policy: predictable exposure is what makes marine liability insurable at all, and an insurable industry is what allows claimants to be paid.

Common mistakes

  • Treating LLMC as a compensation convention, or confusing the limitation fund with the IOPC Funds.
  • Saying the limit is broken by "negligence" or by "the master's recklessness" - Article 4 requires the liable person's personal conduct.
  • Trying to limit a salvage award or a general average contribution. Article 3 excepts both.
  • Quoting a salvor's limit as a fixed sum without explaining the deemed 1,500 tons, or without saying which instrument the State has accepted.
  • Answering limb (c) on salvage only and never reaching the limitation half, which is where the marks concentrate.
  • Citing the Salvage Convention as though it displaced Lloyd's Open Form. It underlies LOF; it does not replace contractual salvage.

Examiner traps

  • "Its linkage with two other IMO Liability and compensation Conventions" - naming two conventions scores little. The mark is in the relationship: one excludes LLMC, the other borrows it.
  • "The current liability limit applicable to them" - the word current is doing work. It asks for the post-2012 figures, not the 1976 originals.
  • The three principles are not a printed list anywhere. Any defensible three anchored to articles will score; three unsupported slogans will not.

The limit figures are those of the 1996 Protocol as amended in 2012 and apply only in States bound by it; a State party only to the unamended 1976 Convention applies lower figures. Understand this first

CII does not measure how much a ship emits. It measures how much she emits per unit of transport work, and for most ship types the work is taken as deadweight capacity multiplied by distance sailed - not cargo actually carried. Almost everything the industry complains about follows from that one design choice, because a ship can improve her rating by sailing further with less on board while her absolute emissions rise. Hold that idea and both limbs of this question answer themselves: limb (a) is the machinery of an efficiency ratio, and limb (b) is what happens when an efficiency ratio is used as though it were an emissions cap.

Model written answer

1. a) What CII is, and where it sits in MARPOL

The Carbon Intensity Indicator is the operational half of the IMO's short-term GHG measure, imposed by MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28, 'Operational carbon intensity'. It applies to ships of 5,000 gross tonnage and above - the threshold of the IMO Data Collection System under regulation 27, on whose fuel data it runs. It must be distinguished from EEXI, a one-off design index applying from 400 gt: EEXI asks what the ship is capable of, CII what she actually did last year.

2. How attained and required CII are set, and how a ship is rated

  • The ship calculates an attained annual operational CII - CO2 per unit of transport work over the calendar year. For most cargo ship types this is the AER, CO2 divided by deadweight capacity multiplied by distance sailed; cruise and ro-pax types use a different metric.
  • The required annual operational CII is the 2019 reference line for that ship type and size reduced by a factor Z: required CII = reference value x (1 - Z/100). Z is 5% for 2023, 7% for 2024, 9% for 2025 and 11% for 2026, extended by resolution MEPC.400(83) to 13.625%, 16.250%, 18.875% and 21.500% for 2027 to 2030.
  • The attained value is verified annually and the ship rated A to E, C being the required standard, the rating recorded with the Statement of Compliance issued under the DCS.
  • The factors were set so that, with the rest of Annex VI, shipping reduces CO2 per transport work by at least 40% by 2030 compared with 2008.

3. What follows from a poor rating

There is no fine and no detention. The consequence is administrative: a ship rated D for three consecutive years, or E for one year, must develop a corrective action plan showing how the required index of C or above will be achieved. It is incorporated in SEEMP Part III and agreed before the Statement of Compliance is issued, so a ship that ignores it cannot keep her documentation in order. Incentives to A- and B-rated ships are encouraged.

4. The commercial impact on the shipping business

The commercial effect far exceeds the regulatory sanction, because the rating is a public, comparable letter grade on an asset:

  • Chartering. Charterers screen tonnage on rating and some will not fix D or E ships. Because a time charterer controls speed and routing - the very things that set the rating - CII clauses are now standard, notably the BIMCO CII Operations Clause 2022. Who bears a bad rating is a negotiated term.
  • Operational control shifts. Owners reserve rights over speed, hull and propeller cleaning and voyage optimisation, so slow steaming becomes contractual - with the machinery consequences of prolonged low-load running that follow.
  • Finance. Lenders under the Poseidon Principles report portfolio climate alignment, so rating feeds into the cost and availability of debt.
  • Asset value. A persistent D or E shortens commercial life, depresses second-hand values and brings forward retrofit or scrapping decisions.
  • Cargo owners. Shippers reporting their own scope 3 emissions demand rating data, so pressure comes from the cargo side too.

5. b) The shortcomings the industry has identified

  • It rewards capacity, not cargo. The AER denominator is deadweight capacity times distance, not cargo carried, so a ship can improve her rating by sailing further with less on board while absolute emissions rise - a genuine perverse incentive.
  • Waiting is punished, sailing is not. Idling at anchor adds fuel but no distance. Port congestion and 'sail fast then wait' lie outside the ship's control, and the incentive to keep moving can increase emissions.
  • Trade patterns are penalised structurally. Short-sea, multi-parcel chemical and product, offshore and ice-class vessels and ships with long port stays are disadvantaged by a metric built around long laden passages, however efficiently run.
  • Responsibility and control are split. The rating attaches to the ship and the owner's documentation, but a time charterer determines speed, route, waiting and cargo intake.
  • It is an efficiency ratio used as a climate instrument. Being indexed rather than absolute, improving average CII does not itself guarantee a fall in total emissions.

6. The remedial measures actually adopted

The regulation anticipated this. Regulation 28.11 required a review of the CII requirements to be completed by 1 January 2026, covering effectiveness, reinforced corrective action, enforcement, the data collection system and revision of the Z factor and reference values.

  • Phase 1 was completed at MEPC 83 in April 2025: implementation experience was gathered and analysed and a list of challenges and gaps identified.
  • Resolution MEPC.400(83), adopted 11 April 2025, amended the G3 Guidelines to set the 2027 to 2030 reduction factors, aligned with the 2023 GHG Strategy's carbon-intensity ambition.
  • The SEEMP Guidelines were also amended to allow other CII metrics to be developed at future sessions - the door through which a cargo-based metric would have to come.
  • Phase 2 opened on 1 January 2026 through intersessional and correspondence groups, expected to conclude around spring 2027, addressing the SEEMP framework, further CII metrics and synergies with the IMO Net-Zero Framework.
  • The industry's own remedies have been contractual - the BIMCO clause - and operational, through just-in-time and virtual arrival schemes aimed at waiting time.

As at this sitting the honest position is that the shortcomings were formally acknowledged and the review was under way but not complete: the factors had been extended to 2030, but the metric itself - the source of most criticism - remained under examination in Phase 2.

Why an indexed measure behaves this way

Any ratio can be improved by growing the denominator instead of shrinking the numerator. CII's denominator is transport work, and transport work was defined using deadweight capacity because capacity is a known, verifiable number that appears on every certificate, whereas cargo actually carried is commercially sensitive, unevenly recorded and open to manipulation. The choice was made for auditability, and the perverse incentives are the price of that choice. This is worth understanding rather than memorising, because it explains why the fix is hard: a cargo-based metric would need a trustworthy, global, verifiable source of cargo data that does not currently exist.

CII, EEXI, EEDI and the Net-Zero Framework - keeping them apart

  • EEDI - design index, new ships, regulation 22. What the ship was built to do.
  • EEXI - design index applied retrospectively to existing ships from 400 gt, regulation 23. A one-off calculation, often satisfied by engine or shaft power limitation.
  • CII - operational, annual, ships of 5,000 GT and above, regulation 28. What she actually did.
  • The Net-Zero Framework - the proposed new Chapter 5, a fuel-intensity standard with a pricing mechanism, which as at this sitting was approved but not adopted. See Q8 of this paper. CII is in force and enforceable; the Net-Zero Framework was not.

Model written answer

1. a) What FSA is, and who it is for

Formal Safety Assessment is set out in MSC-MEPC.2/Circ.12/Rev.2, Revised Guidelines for Formal Safety Assessment (FSA) for use in the IMO rule-making process, of 9 April 2018. The title states its purpose: a structured, auditable methodology by which the Organization decides whether, and how, to regulate a hazard, balancing safety benefit against cost. It is not a shipboard risk assessment or a permit-to-work system. The question is therefore how a proportionate rule for this cargo was, and is being, arrived at.

2. Step 1 - identifying the hazards of this cargo

  • Thermal runaway - an exothermic internal reaction that becomes self-sustaining and generates its own oxidant, so it cannot be smothered and continues without external air;
  • propagation cell to cell and module to module, and thence vehicle to vehicle in a close stow;
  • initiators - internal cell defects, mechanical damage in handling or lashing, over-charge, high state of charge on shipment, charging on board;
  • vent gas - flammable and toxic, able to accumulate in an enclosed vehicle space and produce a gas explosion rather than a simple fire;
  • re-ignition hours or days after apparent extinction, defeating a normal fire-out judgement;
  • mis-declaration and concealment - cells as cargo may be undeclared or wrongly classified, and a battery electric vehicle is not a labelled package at all, so the energy is invisible to the stowage plan.

3. Step 2 - assessing the risk on a ro-ro deck

Risk is frequency times consequence, estimated from casualty returns, incident and near-miss data, cell testing and fire modelling - with the Rev.2 requirement that the reliability, uncertainty and validity of that data be assessed and reported rather than assumed.

  • Frequency is low per vehicle but rising with fleet electrification, and exposure is concentrated - one deck may hold thousands of units;
  • consequence escalates because a ro-ro space is a large undivided volume, vehicles stowed centimetres apart, with no cargo-hold fire divisions and ramps and ventilation trunks carrying heat and smoke between decks;
  • on a ro-pax the dominant risk is to life; on a pure car and truck carrier it is total loss of the ship, and a series of well-known casualties supplied the evidence;
  • manual firefighting on a loaded deck is near impossible, so the model must assume fixed systems and boundaries are what will actually be available.

4. Steps 3 and 4 - risk control options and whether they pay

Risk control options are generated and grouped, then tested by cost-benefit assessment using indices such as the cost of averting a fatality, so a measure is recommended only where the safety gain justifies the cost across the world fleet:

  • detection - earlier, better-located, individually identifiable so the seat of fire is known, with combined smoke and heat sensing;
  • suppression - drencher and water-based systems of adequate coverage and capacity, aligned with detection sections so the right section operates;
  • containment - insulated boundaries, ventilation shut-down and control of openings;
  • operational and design - state-of-charge limits on shipment, segregation and dedicated stowage, restrictions on charging at sea, video monitoring, crew procedures;
  • options are ranked, and those effective but disproportionately costly are rejected transparently - which is why the outcome is a specific set of measures rather than everything technically possible.

5. Step 5 - what the recommendations actually became

The risk-based work on ro-ro fire safety - notably the EMSA FIRESAFE I and FIRESAFE II studies of fire dynamics, system performance and operational factors - entered the regulatory system in two stages:

  • MSC.1/Circ.1615, Interim Guidelines for Minimizing the Incidence and Consequences of Fires in Ro-Ro Spaces and Special Category Spaces, carried key outcomes into guidance while mandatory work continued;
  • resolution MSC.550(108) then amended SOLAS chapter II-2 regulations 7 and 20 and MSC.555(108) amended FSS Code chapters 7 and 9, both adopted at MSC 108 in May 2024 and in force on 1 January 2026 - five weeks before this examination;
  • requirements include individually identifiable fixed detection with combined smoke and heat detectors, detection aligned with deluge sections, detection on weather decks used for vehicles, an effective video monitoring system with at least seven days of replay at a continuously manned control station, and A-60 boundaries on passenger ships carrying more than 36 passengers;
  • they apply to ships constructed on or after 1 January 2026, existing ships in scope complying not later than the first survey on or after 1 January 2028;
  • further amendments aimed at new energy vehicles were still in development, so the regulatory answer is partly delivered and continuing.

6. b) Measures by stakeholder

  • IMO - complete the new-energy-vehicle amendments, keep IMDG provisions current, maintain interim guidance meanwhile;
  • flag State and class - approve and survey the amended detection, suppression and boundary arrangements, and issue guidance ahead of the compliance dates;
  • shipper, manufacturer and packer - correct IMDG classification, packing and marking, state-of-charge control, testing and quality control, and no offering of damaged, defective or recalled batteries;
  • port and terminal - screen and refuse visibly damaged vehicles, check declared state of charge, separate electric vehicles in marshalling areas, inform the ship promptly;
  • ship operator - a written stowage and segregation policy, limits on charging on board, training in lithium fire behaviour, vehicle-deck drills, thermal imaging, and a plan that assumes re-ignition;
  • ship's staff - verify cargo against the declaration, maintain detection and drencher readiness, patrol during and after loading, and fight fire by early activation of fixed systems and boundary cooling rather than entering a loaded deck;
  • insurers and industry bodies - loss-prevention guidance, incident and near-miss data sharing, and premium signals rewarding compliant stowage - the same data that feeds the next FSA.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The 10-mark limb is a method question, and method questions are marked on whether the method is visible. Naming the five steps and then walking the cargo through them is what separates a top answer from a general essay about battery fires. Step 5 is where most candidates stop short: an FSA that produces no recommendation has not been applied, so ending with the actual resolutions is what closes the loop.

Why FSA exists at all

Before FSA, IMO regulation was largely reactive - a casualty happened, and a rule followed. FSA was introduced after the Herald of Free Enterprise and Piper Alpha era to allow the Organization to act proactively and proportionately, on evidence rather than on the emotional weight of the most recent disaster, and to be able to say publicly why a proposed rule was or was not worth its cost. That is why the Guidelines insist on auditability, traceability and the explicit reporting of uncertainty: an FSA is as much an accountability document as a technical one.

The lithium hazard in engineering terms

Thermal runaway begins when a cell's internal temperature passes the point at which exothermic decomposition of the electrolyte and cathode outpaces heat loss. The decomposition liberates oxygen from the cathode material, which is why the reaction sustains itself without air and why smothering agents - CO2, foam blanket, an inert atmosphere - do not stop it. Cooling is the only lever the ship holds, and it works by removing heat from cells that have not yet reached onset temperature, so its real objective is to stop propagation to neighbouring vehicles rather than to extinguish the cell already in runaway. That is also why re-ignition is normal rather than surprising: the cells that were merely heated may still cross the threshold hours later.

Vehicles, containers and packages are three different regulatory problems

  • Cells and batteries as cargo - dangerous goods under the IMDG Code, class 9, with UN numbers such as 3480 and 3481 for lithium-ion and 3090 and 3091 for lithium-metal, subject to packing, marking, documentation and segregation.
  • Batteries in or with equipment - the same UN family but different provisions, and often carried under exceptions.
  • A battery electric vehicle - carried as a vehicle, not as a declared dangerous goods package. This is the regulatory gap: the ship may know exactly how many tonnes of declared lithium cells she has in containers, and not know how much energy is sitting on the vehicle deck.

Common mistakes

  • Describing FSA as a shipboard risk assessment. Read the circular's title.
  • Listing the five steps and then never using them - a hazard essay wearing an FSA hat.
  • Saying water must never be used on a lithium fire. Cooling and boundary protection with copious water is the accepted tactic.
  • Saying the fire can be smothered with CO2. The reaction supplies its own oxidant.
  • Declaring the fire out when flames stop. Re-ignition must be assumed and monitored for.
  • Treating a battery electric vehicle as though it were declared dangerous goods, or vice versa.

Model written answer

1. (A) What the human element means in STCW

STCW refuses to treat competence as something time served automatically confers. It replaces experience with demonstrated competence, then addresses the conditions - fitness, rest, teamwork and company responsibility - under which competence can actually be exercised. The instrument is the STCW Convention and Code, 1978, as amended, most significantly by the Manila amendments of 2010, in force from 1 January 2012.

2. Competence-based standards

  • The Part A tables of competence specify, for every function and level, the competence required, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency behind it, the methods for demonstrating it and the criteria for evaluating it - so assessment is against a published standard, not an examiner's impression.
  • Part A is mandatory; Part B is guidance - a distinction worth stating.
  • The Manila amendments added mandatory training in leadership and teamworking at operational level and leadership and managerial skill at management level - where engine-room and bridge resource management, situational awareness and workload management enter the Convention. It is the explicit recognition that technically competent individuals still fail as a team.
  • Regulation I/6 governs training and assessment, and regulation I/8 requires a quality standards system, so the training system itself is audited rather than assumed.
  • Regulation I/11 requires periodic revalidation, and chapter VI mandatory safety familiarisation and basic training before a seafarer is assigned duties.

3. Fitness for duty - the physical human being

  • Regulation VIII/1 requires each Administration, for the purpose of preventing fatigue, to establish and enforce rest periods for watchkeeping personnel and for those with designated safety, security and pollution-prevention duties in accordance with section A-VIII/1, and to require that watch systems are arranged so that efficiency is not impaired by fatigue.
  • The Manila-amended A-VIII/1 minimum rest is 10 hours in any 24-hour period and 77 hours in any 7-day period. The daily rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive periods of rest shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Authorised exceptions are tightly conditioned: weekly rest may not fall below 70 hours in any 7-day period, exceptions from the 77-hour norm may not run for more than two consecutive weeks, and daily rest may then be divided into no more than three periods.
  • The Manila amendments also introduced alcohol limits for masters, officers and other seafarers with designated safety, security and environmental duties.
  • Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards, and regulation VIII/2 with section A-VIII/2 governs watchkeeping arrangements and principles.

4. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

Regulation I/14 makes the company responsible for ensuring seafarers hold valid certificates, are familiarised with their duties and the ship's arrangements, and that documentation is maintained - so the human element is not left as an individual's problem. Administrations operate quality standards and are subject to independent evaluation and reporting to IMO.

5. (B) The IMO guidance on fatigue

The current guidance is MSC.1/Circ.1598, "Guidelines on fatigue", of 24 January 2019, approved at the 100th session of the Maritime Safety Committee and finalized by the HTW Sub-Committee. It supersedes MSC/Circ.1014 of 2001. It is recommendatory, not mandatory - the binding rest-hour requirements remain in STCW chapter VIII and in MLC 2006. The Guidelines are arranged in six modules, each addressed to a different party:

  • Module 1 - Fatigue: what it is, and its causes, grouped as sleep and rest, the biological clock and circadian rhythm, health and stress, environmental factors such as noise, vibration and motion, sleep disorders and operational factors. It defines sleep debt as insufficient accumulated sleep over multiple consecutive 24-hour periods.
  • Module 2 - Fatigue and the Company: assessing, mitigating and managing fatigue risk in the safety management system, in manning decisions and in scheduling.
  • Module 3 - Fatigue and the Seafarer: practical guidance for the master, officers and ratings on sleep quality, diet, and the limits of countermeasures - notably that caffeine is not a substitute for sleep.
  • Module 4 - Fatigue Awareness and Training.
  • Module 5 - Fatigue and Ship Design: mitigation designed in - accommodation location, noise, vibration, lighting and habitability.
  • Module 6 - Fatigue, the Administration and Port State Authorities: how regulators and inspectors should approach it.

6. How the guidance and the rules fit together

The Guidelines are explicit that hours of rest are set by a prescriptive formula in STCW chapter VIII, and that complying with the formula is not the same as being rested: managers must allow for circadian rhythm and sleep debt, and - in the Guidelines' words - rest means rest, not substituting a different form of work. The same theme runs through MLC 2006 Regulations 2.3, 2.4 and 2.7. A management-level engineer should therefore treat recorded compliance as the floor, and a rest-hour record that is always exactly compliant as a warning rather than a reassurance.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two printed limbs of 8 marks each, so split the answer evenly and head each limb clearly. Limb A rewards structure - competence tables, fitness for duty, company responsibility - and limb B rewards naming the actual circular. A candidate who writes generally about tiredness for the second limb without producing MSC.1/Circ.1598 and its module structure will lose most of eight marks, because "the IMO guidance" is a specific document.

The organising idea for limb A

STCW attacks the human element on three fronts, and saying so gives the limb a spine: can they do it (competence standards, assessed against published criteria), are they fit to do it now (fitness for duty - rest, medical, alcohol), and who is responsible for making that possible (regulation I/14, the company). The Manila amendments strengthened all three, and added the fourth dimension the older Convention lacked - can they work as a team, through mandatory leadership and teamworking training.

The numbers you must get right

10 hours in any 24, and 77 hours in any 7 days. Divided into no more than two periods, one of at least 6 hours, with no more than 14 hours between consecutive rest periods. Exceptions may not go below 70 hours in any 7 days and may not run more than two consecutive weeks. Learn 10, 77, 2, 6, 14, 70 - and be careful, because 70 and 77 are both real figures doing different jobs: 77 is the norm, 70 is the floor under an authorised exception. Quoting 70 as the normal requirement is the classic error, and it is exactly the trap that a pre-2010 copy of the Code will lead you into.

Currency - what applies and what does not

The operative regime is STCW 1978 as amended, with the Manila amendments in force from 1 January 2012. A comprehensive review of the STCW Convention and Code has been under way through the HTW Sub-Committee with completion targeted around 2027 - one of the acknowledged gaps being the treatment of hours of rest. Nothing had been adopted at the date of this paper. So write the Manila regime as current, and mention the review only as work in progress. Do not present any reviewed text as though it were in force.

Model written answer

1. What the Act means by an unseaworthy ship

The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 does not define unseaworthiness in a definitions section. The description sits inside section 334, which provides that a ship is unseaworthy when the following are not such as to render her in every respect fit for the proposed voyage or service:

  • the materials of which she is made and her construction;
  • the qualifications of the master;
  • the number, description and qualifications of the crew, including officers;
  • the weight, description and stowage of the cargo and ballast;
  • the condition of her hull and equipment, boilers and machinery.

Two features deserve emphasis. The test is relative - fitness for the proposed voyage or service - so a ship adequate for a sheltered coastal run may be unseaworthy for an ocean passage. And it is far wider than material condition: an undermanned ship, one whose officers hold the wrong certificates, or a well-maintained ship with badly stowed cargo is unseaworthy under the Act though nothing is mechanically defective.

2. The offence, and the defences the Act allows

  • s.334(1) - every person who sends or attempts to send an Indian ship to sea from any port in India in such an unseaworthy state that the life of any person is likely to be thereby endangered is guilty of an offence, unless he proves he used all reasonable means to insure her being sent to sea in a seaworthy state, or that going to sea in that state was reasonable and justifiable.
  • s.334(2) - a master who knowingly takes such a ship to sea commits the offence, with only the second defence.
  • s.334(3) permits a person charged to give evidence; s.334(4) provides that no prosecution shall be instituted except by, or with the consent of, the Central Government.

Liability is therefore not automatic: the threshold is danger to life, the defence burden lies on the accused, and the standard is reasonable means, not a guarantee.

3. What an unsafe ship is, and the power to detain her

Section 336 addresses a different problem - stopping the ship rather than punishing anyone. An unsafe ship is one which, by reason of the defective condition of her hull, equipment or machinery, or by reason of overloading or improper loading, is unfit to proceed to sea without serious danger to human life, having regard to the nature of the service for which she is intended. The procedure is staged:

  1. the Central Government, having reason to believe on complaint or otherwise that she is unsafe, may order her provisionally detained for survey;
  2. a written statement of the grounds must be served forthwith on the master;
  3. the matter is referred to the court of survey for the port, or a competent person is appointed to survey and report;
  4. on the report she is either released or finally detained.

4. The real difference between the two

  • Unseaworthy is a state; unsafe is a trigger for a power. Section 334 describes a condition and attaches criminal liability to sending her to sea in it; section 336 describes a condition entitling the Government to stop her.
  • One looks backwards, the other forwards. The offence punishes a decision taken; detention prevents the voyage happening at all.
  • Unseaworthiness is the wider concept - it expressly takes in crew numbers and qualifications and the stowage of cargo and ballast. The unsafe-ship definition lists a narrower set of causes.
  • The thresholds differ - life likely to be endangered under s.334; unfitness to proceed without serious danger to human life under s.336.
  • Practically, a ship unsafe within s.336 will invariably be unseaworthy within s.334, but a ship may be unseaworthy - one deck officer short - without being unsafe.

5. The owner's obligation to the crew

Section 335 converts seaworthiness from a matter between owner and cargo into a duty owed to the people on board. In every contract of service, express or implied, between the owner of an Indian ship and the master or any seaman, and in every contract of apprenticeship, there is implied - notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary - an obligation that the owner, the master and every agent charged with loading, preparing her for sea or sending her to sea shall use all reasonable means to ensure the seaworthiness of the ship for the voyage at the time when the voyage commences, and shall keep her in a seaworthy state during the voyage.

Three points follow. It is non-excludable - a crew-agreement term waiving it is void. It is a due diligence standard, not a guarantee of result. And it is continuing through the voyage, unlike the doctrine of stages. It also binds persons other than the owner - the loading agent is named.

6. How the obligation is policed, and what changed afterwards

Section 335(2) allows the Central Government to arrange a survey of the hull, equipment or machinery. In practice the duty is policed through the wider system - class and flag State statutory survey and certification, ISM auditing and port State control under SOLAS I/19 - with s.336 detention as the last resort.

One point of currency must be stated rather than assumed. The Merchant Shipping Act 1958 was the operative law at the date of this examination. It was repealed by section 324(1) of the Merchant Shipping Act 2025, in force 15 March 2026 - some five weeks after this paper was sat. The answer is therefore correctly given on the 1958 Act.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem asks three questions and prints no limbs, so the examiner has no printed structure to mark against and will mark against the three questions asked. Answering them in the order asked, with the middle one made explicitly comparative, is what makes the script easy to mark. The comparison in step 4 is the highest-value paragraph in the answer and the one most often missing: candidates define both terms and then never say how they differ, which is literally what was asked.

Why the Act splits the idea in two

A criminal offence needs fault, a trial and proof beyond reasonable doubt, and it operates after the event - which is no use at all to the crew of a ship that is about to sail. An administrative detention power needs only a reasonable belief and can act in hours. The 1958 Act therefore does both: section 334 attaches consequences to a decision, and section 336 provides the fast preventive lever, with a survey and a right to be told the grounds as the safeguards against its misuse. Recognising that the two sections exist to solve different problems is the whole of the distinction.

Seaworthiness has three different legal lives - keep them apart

  • Statutory and criminal - MSA 1958 s.334, this question. A state of the ship, punishable, with a due-diligence defence.
  • Contractual, owed to the crew - MSA 1958 s.335. Implied, non-excludable, continuing through the voyage.
  • Insurance and carriage - a warranty or a duty of due diligence, depending on whether the policy is a voyage or time policy and on the contract of carriage. Under Indian law that is the Marine Insurance Act 1963, not the English Act of 1906, and a breach affects recovery rather than attracting a penalty.

The examiner asks about the first two. Answering about the third is the commonest way to lose this question.

Common mistakes

  • Describing seaworthiness in general engineering terms without quoting the section 334 formula.
  • Leaving crew numbers, certification and stowage out of the definition - they are expressly inside it.
  • Treating unseaworthy and unsafe as interchangeable, or asserting the difference is one of degree only.
  • Saying the owner guarantees seaworthiness. The duty is to use all reasonable means.
  • Forgetting that the section 335 duty continues during the voyage.
  • Answering under the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 or the doctrine of stages.
  • Answering under the Merchant Shipping Act 2025, which was not in force on the examination date.

Examiner traps

  • 'Within the meaning of the Merchant Shipping Act (MSA), 1958, as amended' - the examiner is asking for the statutory meaning, and a general definition cannot score the top band.
  • 'What according to you is the difference' invites an opinion, but the marks are for the legal difference, not a preference.
  • 'Obligations of owners to crew' - plural owners, and section 335 also binds the master and the loading agent, so an answer confined to the registered owner is incomplete.

Understand this first

General average is the one place in marine practice where deliberately damaging your own ship can be recovered, and accidental damage cannot. That inversion is the whole subject. If a bearing fails on passage through overload, it is the owner's loss alone; if the same bearing is burnt because the master knowingly drove the engine hard to save the ship and cargo from a common danger, every interest that was saved contributes to it. So when a scenario asks whether damage is general average, do not ask how bad it was - ask whether somebody chose it, on purpose, for everybody's benefit.

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

General average is the principle that where one interest is deliberately sacrificed to save the whole, the loss is shared rateably by all the interests saved. It is given effect not by statute but by the York-Antwerp Rules, incorporated by contract; the current edition is the 2016 Rules. In India the statutory definition appears in section 66 of the Marine Insurance Act 1963.

Rule A states the test: there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure. The words when, and only when matter - the conditions are cumulative.

2. The essential features, all of which must be present

  1. Extraordinary - outside the ordinary incidents of the voyage. Fuel burnt on a normal passage, or wear and tear, is not; nor is anything the owner was already bound to do.
  2. Intentional - a deliberate act. This is the heart of the doctrine: accidental loss, however catastrophic, is never general average. Cargo washed overboard is particular average; cargo jettisoned is general average.
  3. Reasonable - judged on the circumstances as they appeared at the time, reinforced by the Rule Paramount: in no case shall there be any allowance for sacrifice or expenditure unless reasonably made or incurred.
  4. For the common safety - for the whole adventure. Action to save only the ship, or only the cargo, is not general average.
  5. A real peril - real and substantial, though it need not be immediate or inevitable.
  6. A common maritime adventure, and a useful result - more than one interest at risk, or there is nobody to contribute, and something must be saved, since contribution is assessed on the values preserved.

3. What is excluded, and who must prove it

  • Rule C allows only the direct consequence of the general average act, and excludes demurrage, loss of market, any loss or expense by reason of delay and any indirect loss whatsoever, and environmental damage or loss consequent on the escape of pollutants.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected though the event was due to the fault of a party, without prejudice to remedies or defences for that fault. Contribution and liability are separate questions.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming; failing particulars within twelve months the adjuster may estimate.

4. b) Testing the casualty against the rules

Taking the Rule A elements against the facts - aground, the main engine worked ahead and astern to shake her loose, bearings burnt through excessive load:

  • extraordinary - burnt bearings from deliberate overload are outside the ordinary incidents of the voyage, and are not wear and tear;
  • intentional - working the engine hard astern to refloat is a deliberate act of the master;
  • for the common safety - a grounded vessel endangers ship, cargo and freight alike;
  • direct consequence - the damage arose immediately from that act, satisfying Rule C;
  • peril - the one element the question does not settle, and it must be examined rather than assumed.

5. Rule VII, which decides the point

The Rules deal with this precise situation expressly. Rule VII, Damage to Machinery and Boilers: Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage; but where a ship is afloat no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be allowed as general average.

Three conditions and one absolute bar. The conditions: ashore; in a position of peril; machinery worked with an actual intention to float her for the common safety, at the risk of such damage. The bar: if she is afloat, machinery damage from working the engines is never allowed.

6. Conclusion, and what must be done to sustain it

Yes - the bearing damage can be included in general average, provided the Rule VII conditions are established. The first is satisfied: she was aground. The other two are questions of evidence:

  • Position of peril. Hard aground on a falling tide, on a lee shore or on rock, taking water, or with weather deteriorating - peril is readily shown. Merely touching soft ground in sheltered water, certain to float on the next tide, is not; the act was one of convenience and the claim fails.
  • Actual intention, accepting the risk. The master must have worked the engine knowing it might be damaged and accepted that to free the ship. Engines worked to test the ground, or in routine hope of coming off, do not meet the rule.
  • Evidence. Timed deck and engine log entries, the master's recorded assessment of the danger, engine parameters and alarms, soundings, weather, owner or salvor advice. The onus is on the claimant under Rule E.

Two points complete the answer. The damage must be reasonable in extent under the Rule Paramount. And by Rule D, negligent navigation causing the grounding does not lose the right to contribution, though cargo may resist under the contract of carriage. In practice the owner declares general average, appoints an average adjuster and releases cargo against a general average bond and an average guarantee.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (b) is worth 8 marks and asks for justification, which means the marks are in the reasoning chain, not the verdict. An answer that says 'yes, it is general average because the engine was used to save the ship' has given the conclusion and none of the justification. Running the Rule A elements first and then reaching Rule VII lets the candidate show the general test and the specific rule, and - crucially - identify the two facts the question deliberately leaves open.

The question is testing whether you know Rule VII exists

This scenario is not a general knowledge problem; it is drafted straight onto a named rule. Every element in the stem maps to a phrase in Rule VII: ran aground to 'ashore', main engine used ahead and astern to shake the vessel loose to 'in endeavouring to refloat', bearings got burnt to 'damage caused to any machinery'. A candidate who cites Rule A alone can reach the right answer by general reasoning and will score respectably; one who cites Rule VII and quotes the afloat proviso is demonstrably answering the question that was set.

Why the law inverts ordinary intuition

The rule that deliberate damage is recoverable and accidental damage is not looks perverse until you see what it is for. General average is a cost-allocation mechanism, not a compensation scheme. Its purpose is to remove the master's conflict of interest at the moment of crisis: if the shipowner alone bore the cost of sacrificing the ship's machinery, or of jettisoning cargo, the person best placed to save the adventure would have a financial reason to hesitate. By promising that a reasonable, deliberate sacrifice will be shared by everyone who benefits, the doctrine makes the right decision the cheap decision. Accidental loss needs no such encouragement, which is why it stays where it falls.

The afloat proviso, and why it is absolute

Rule VII's second half is unusually emphatic - in any circumstances. The reason is evidential rather than moral. When a ship is afloat, working the propelling machinery is exactly what she is supposed to do, so there is no way to distinguish an extraordinary sacrifice from ordinary use, and a claim would be unanswerable and unverifiable. When she is ashore, the act is visibly abnormal and the intention can be tested. The Rules solve the evidential problem by drawing a bright line.

Common mistakes

  • Concluding 'no' because the damage was caused by overload. Overload was the mechanism; the question is whether it was accepted deliberately, in peril, to refloat.
  • Concluding 'yes' unconditionally, without identifying peril and intention as things to be proved.
  • Citing only Rule A when Rule VII is directly on point.
  • Forgetting the afloat proviso, which is half of Rule VII and the source of the best follow-up question.
  • Saying negligence defeats general average. Rule D says it does not - it may found a separate claim.
  • Confusing general average with salvage or with particular average.
  • Calling the York-Antwerp Rules a convention or statute. They are contractual terms.

Examiner traps

  • The stem says a large vessel ran aground and stops. It does not say she was in peril, and that omission is deliberate - it is the fact the answer must treat as conditional.
  • 'Give proper justification' is a direction about form. A bare verdict cannot score, however correct.
  • Limb (a) is a quotation - 'General average is based on certain essential features' - and quotation stems reward candidates who enumerate the features explicitly rather than writing an essay around them.

Understand this first

The single idea behind this question is that agreeing the words and being bound by them are two separate events, often years apart. A conference settles the text - that is adoption, and at that moment the convention binds nobody at all. Each State then decides separately whether to take it on, and the treaty itself specifies how many must do so, and how much of the world's shipping they must represent, before it becomes law for anyone. That second condition is why a technical convention carries a tonnage threshold: a safety rule in force only among States that own no ships would achieve nothing. The two terms named in the question are simply two routes to the same destination - one where a State signs first and confirms later, once its parliament has passed the legislation it needs, and one where it never signed at all and joins afterwards in a single step. And because the gap between adoption and force can run to a decade, conventions carry a faster machinery for amending themselves.

Model written answer

1. Adoption is not entry into force

A convention is adopted either by the competent IMO organ - Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text and does no more: it binds no State, and an adopted convention may never enter into force at all. The text is then opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General of IMO as depositary.

2. How a State expresses consent to be bound

Between adoption and entry into force sits the step that matters: each State must express its consent to be bound, by one of three routes -

  • signature alone, where the treaty so provides or the negotiating States so agreed;
  • signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, the normal course for multilateral conventions;
  • accession, where the State did not sign within the signature period.

In the second and third cases the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument with the depositary, who records it, counts it towards the entry into force condition and notifies all other States.

3. The two terms the question names

(ii) Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval. The State signs, but signature does not bind it; it indicates an intention to proceed. The State becomes a party only on later depositing an instrument of ratification. Its modern purpose is to give the State time to enact domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements before committing internationally. Acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names, used where a constitution does not demand the fuller procedure: VCLT Article 14(2) provides that consent is expressed by acceptance or approval under conditions similar to those which apply to ratification. In legal effect the three are identical.

(i) Accession. The means by which a State becomes party to a treaty it did not sign - because the signature period closed, or it was not a negotiating State. It is a single step: no prior signature, simply deposit of an instrument of accession, permitted by VCLT Article 15 where the treaty so provides. Once deposited it has exactly the same legal effect as ratification. The difference is the route, not the destination.

4. The entry into force provision, and why it matters

Every convention contains a final clause fixing entry into force, normally a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. Its importance is fourfold:

  • it prevents an instrument binding a handful of States while the rest operate to another standard, which would distort competition;
  • it fixes a certain date from which obligations, surveys and certificates run, so Administrations, ROs, owners and yards work to one deadline;
  • it provides the interval for flag States to legislate, class to develop rules and industry to build or retrofit;
  • it gives legitimacy - a convention in force across most world tonnage is enforceable through port State control against the remainder, which is what makes no more favourable treatment work.

5. Conventions of an important technical nature

For a convention of important technical nature - one dictating construction, equipment or operation - States observe a stiffer double condition: a number of States and a percentage of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping. A count of States alone could be met by many small flags while the major fleets stayed outside, leaving the convention in force on paper and irrelevant at sea.

  • SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, requiring not less than 25 States whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; in force 25 May 1980.
  • The 1978 MARPOL Protocol - in force twelve months after not less than fifteen States constituting not less than fifty per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage had become Parties.

Older conventions typically required two-thirds of contracting States, which occasionally meant more acceptances were needed to bring an instrument into force than to amend it.

6. Keeping the convention current once in force

A technical convention is worthless if it cannot keep pace, and explicit acceptance failed on that point: amendments to the 1960 SOLAS Convention adopted between 1966 and 1973 never received enough acceptances and none entered into force.

IMO's answer was the tacit acceptance procedure, used in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL and the collision regulations. It reverses the default: an amendment to the technical annexes is deemed accepted on a specified date unless objections are received beforehand from an agreed number of Parties. For SOLAS 1974 an amendment is deemed accepted at the end of two years from communication to Contracting Governments, unless objected to by more than one-third of Contracting Governments, or by Governments whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross merchant tonnage. Amendments now enter into force typically within 18 to 24 months. Once in force internationally each flag State must still give the instrument effect domestically - in India through the Merchant Shipping Act and rules made under it - and it is that domestic instrument which is enforced against the ship.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no limbs but makes four separate demands, and the commonest failure is to answer the first and drift. Following one instrument through its life from adoption to amendment answers all four in a single narrative, and putting the two named terms side by side in step 3 lets the contrast be drawn explicitly - which is what an explain the terms instruction is asking for.

Why the machinery looks so cumbersome

A treaty binds sovereign States, and no State can be bound without its own consent - that single constitutional fact generates the whole apparatus. Adoption cannot bind, because the conference does not speak for any government. Signature cannot ordinarily bind, because most constitutions require a legislature to act before the executive can commit. Hence the two-step route, the deposited instrument, the depositary who counts, and the threshold that has to be reached before anyone is obliged to do anything. Tacit acceptance is the one place where the system bends: consent is still required, but silence is treated as consent, and that inversion is what made a technical convention maintainable.

Why this matters to a Chief Engineer

It explains a situation seen constantly in practice: an amendment has been adopted, the industry press is full of it, class has issued guidance - and it does not yet apply to the ship. It also explains why a ship may be subject to a requirement her flag State has not ratified, through port State control and the no more favourable treatment principle, and why the certificate on the bulkhead is issued under national law rather than under the convention directly. The Net-Zero Framework in Q8 of this paper is a live illustration of an instrument stalled between approval and adoption.

Four words that are not synonyms

  • Adoption - the text is settled. Nobody is bound.
  • Signature - intent indicated. Ordinarily still nobody is bound.
  • Ratification / acceptance / approval - a signatory State becomes bound on deposit. The three differ in domestic formality only.
  • Accession - a non-signatory State becomes bound on deposit, in one step.

And separately: entry into force of the convention is not entry into force for a particular State, which usually follows some months after that State deposits its own instrument.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a convention is in force once adopted, or once signed.
  • Using ratification and accession as synonyms, or inventing a difference between ratification, acceptance and approval.
  • Giving only a number of States for a technical convention and omitting the tonnage percentage - the double condition is the point of that part of the question.
  • Describing tacit acceptance as the way a convention enters into force. It is the way an amendment does.
  • Forgetting that the flag State must still legislate domestically.
  • Confusing a reservation with a declaration, or asserting that reservations are freely available - many technical conventions restrict them.

  • The two terms are printed as (i) and (ii) with no marks attached, which tempts candidates to treat them as an afterthought. They are a named, explicit demand and should be answered properly.

Model written answer

1. What the revised strategy is, and what it is not

The 2023 IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships was adopted by resolution MEPC.377(80) on 7 July 2023 at MEPC 80, replacing the Initial Strategy of 2018. Its vision commits the Organization to reducing GHG emissions from international shipping and, as a matter of urgency, phasing them out as soon as possible, while promoting a just and equitable transition.

It is essential to be clear what the Strategy is: an adopted policy resolution stating intent, ambitions and candidate measures. It is not mandatory law and binds no ship. Obligations arise only when measures are written into MARPOL Annex VI.

2. The four levels of ambition

  1. Carbon intensity of the ship to decline - review with the aim of strengthening the energy efficiency design requirements for new ships;
  2. carbon intensity of international shipping to decline - reduce CO2 per transport work, as an average across international shipping, by at least 40% by 2030, compared to 2008;
  3. uptake of zero or near-zero GHG technologies, fuels and/or energy sources to increase - to at least 5%, striving for 10%, of the energy used by international shipping by 2030;
  4. GHG emissions to reach net zero - peak as soon as possible and reach net-zero by or around, i.e. close to, 2050, taking account of different national circumstances, consistent with the Paris Agreement temperature goal.

The ambitions are assessed well-to-wake under the LCA guidelines (resolution MEPC.376(80)), so emissions cannot simply be shifted from the funnel to the fuel production chain.

3. The indicative checkpoints and the guiding principles

Two indicative checkpoints are added, expressed - unlike the ambitions - in total annual GHG emissions: reduce them by at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, and by at least 70%, striving for 80%, by 2040, both compared to 2008.

The guiding principles include non-discrimination and no more favourable treatment as enshrined in MARPOL; common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in the light of different national circumstances; effect to be given by all ships regardless of flag; and consideration of impacts on States, particularly LDCs and SIDS. The tension between the second and third is precisely what later stalled adoption.

4. The basket of measures - technical and economic

Short-term measures are already in force under Annex VI - EEXI, the CII under regulation 28, and the SEEMP. For the mid-term the Strategy identifies a basket of two complementary elements: a technical element, a goal-based marine fuel standard phasing down the GHG intensity of marine fuel; and an economic element, a maritime GHG emissions pricing mechanism. The two were to be delivered as a single instrument, the standard setting the trajectory and the price making non-compliance dearer than compliance.

5. The proposed new Chapter 5 - the Net-Zero Framework

That instrument was finalised as the IMO Net-Zero Framework and approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 as amendments introducing a new Chapter 5, Regulations on the IMO Net-Zero Framework. As approved in draft it would:

  • apply to ships over 5,000 gross tonnage, about 85% of international shipping's CO2;
  • require an annual GHG Fuel Intensity (GFI) in gCO2eq/MJ, well-to-wake, against a 2008 reference of 93.3, with zero or near-zero fuels at not more than 19.0;
  • set two tiers - a Base Target and a stricter Direct Compliance Target - at 4% and 17% for 2028, 8% and 21% for 2030 and 30% and 43% for 2035;
  • require a ship falling short to buy remedial units at USD 100 per tonne CO2eq (Tier 1) and USD 380 (Tier 2), the higher tier penalising the most carbon-intensive fuels;
  • allow ships beating the Direct Compliance Target to earn surplus units, transferable, bankable for up to two years, or cancellable;
  • direct contributions to an IMO Net-Zero Fund rewarding low-emission ships and supporting innovation, training and technology transfer in developing countries - the mechanism through which the just-transition principle would operate.

The first reporting period was to begin on 1 January 2028.

6. Where the amendments actually stood at this sitting

Here an answer must be accurate rather than optimistic. A second extraordinary session of the MEPC met from 14 to 17 October 2025 specifically to adopt the draft amendments. Consensus could not be reached. The principal points of contention were the acceptance procedure - tacit versus explicit, a proposed clause recognising the Framework as the sole global mid-term measure, and concerns over overlap with regional regimes, compliance cost and administrative burden on developing States.

On 17 October 2025 the Committee agreed to adjourn the session for one year, Member States continuing to work towards consensus meanwhile. At the date of this examination the amendments were therefore approved but neither adopted nor in force, and no entry-into-force date existed. If subsequently adopted under tacit acceptance, entry into force would follow about 16 months later.

The position is best summarised thus: the Strategy is adopted policy; the short-term measures are in force; the mid-term measures remain draft law awaiting agreement. The design is settled; adoption is a political question, not a technical one.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem contains two demands and no limbs, so the risk is an undifferentiated essay. Steps 1 to 3 discharge the Strategy, step 4 is the hinge that explains why Chapter 5 exists at all, and steps 5 to 6 discharge the amendments. Step 6 is what makes the answer worth full marks in February 2026: an otherwise excellent answer that assumes the framework is law has failed on the one fact the examiner's own wording - amendments being considered - shows he knew.

The examiner's wording is a dated fingerprint

Read the stem again: amendments being considered under MARPOL Annex VI, including the inclusion of new Chapter-V. That is not loose drafting. It is an accurate description of the position in February 2026 and it tells you the paper was set with the October 2025 outcome in view. When an examiner's own words describe an instrument as under consideration, an answer that describes it as in force contradicts the question.

Strategy, approval, adoption, entry into force

This question is the live illustration of the machinery examined in Q7 of this same paper, and the two should be revised together. The sequence is: a strategy states intent; a committee approves a draft text; a session adopts it, which is what makes it an amendment to the convention; and it then enters into force, ordinarily by tacit acceptance after a stated period. The Net-Zero Framework reached stage two and stalled between stages two and three. That is why the dispute at ES.2 over tacit versus explicit acceptance was not a procedural quibble: tacit acceptance would bind objecting States unless enough of them formally objected, and explicit acceptance would not, so the choice determines whether a State can stay outside.

Why consensus failed, in substance

  • The principles collide. No more favourable treatment and all ships regardless of flag demand a uniform global measure. Common but differentiated responsibilities demands that developing States not bear equal burden. A single carbon price applied to every ship is hard to reconcile with both.
  • Regional overlap. The EU had already brought shipping into its Emissions Trading System and adopted FuelEU Maritime. A clause declaring the IMO framework the sole global mid-term measure was an attempt to force regional schemes to give way, and was resisted.
  • Cost and burden. Remedial unit prices of USD 100 and USD 380 per tonne CO2eq are a material operating cost, and several delegations doubted the administrative machinery could be built by 2028.

Common mistakes

  • Stating that the Net-Zero Framework was adopted in October 2025, or that it enters into force in 2027. Neither is true; both appear widely in material written before October 2025.
  • Treating the 2023 Strategy as mandatory. It is a policy resolution.
  • Mixing the levels of ambition with the indicative checkpoints - intensity and fuel uptake against total annual emissions.
  • Dropping the striving for figures, which are part of the adopted wording.
  • Omitting the 2008 baseline, without which every percentage is meaningless.
  • Forgetting the well-to-wake basis, which is what stops emissions being exported upstream.
  • Confusing GFI with CII. GFI is the carbon intensity of the fuel; CII is the operational carbon intensity of the ship.

  • 'Amendments being considered' - the status is part of the question, not background.
  • 'To achieve the decarbonisation goal and transition to a Net Zero future' invites a rhetorical answer. The marks are in resolution numbers, percentages, baselines and status.

The phrase in the Exclusive Economic Zone of India tempts everyone into the same error. The EEZ is not Indian territory. Model written answer

1. a) The zones, and what the coastal State holds in each

UNCLOS measures zones from the baselines, and the coastal State's authority thins progressively seaward - from full sovereignty to defined functional rights and finally to none.

  • Internal waters - landward of the baselines. Full sovereignty, with no right of innocent passage.
  • Territorial sea, up to 12 n.m. - sovereignty, extending to airspace, seabed and subsoil, subject to innocent passage.
  • Contiguous zone, to 24 n.m. - not sovereignty but control, to prevent and punish infringement of customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws.
  • Exclusive Economic Zone, to 200 n.m. - sovereign rights, not sovereignty.
  • Continental shelf - sovereign rights over seabed and subsoil, existing independently of occupation or proclamation.
  • High seas - no State may validly claim sovereignty; the high seas freedoms apply.

2. What the coastal State may actually do

  • Territorial sea - full prescriptive and enforcement jurisdiction; its criminal and civil law applies. It may legislate on navigation, cables, fishing, pollution and customs, but must not hamper innocent passage, which ceases to be innocent on acts prejudicial to peace, good order or security.
  • Contiguous zone - powers confined to those four subjects, preventive and punitive only.
  • EEZ - Article 56 gives sovereign rights for exploring, exploiting, conserving and managing the natural resources, plus jurisdiction over artificial islands and installations, marine scientific research and protection of the marine environment. Article 73 permits boarding, inspection and arrest for fisheries offences.
  • The limit is the point: outside those purposes the coastal State has no general jurisdiction in the EEZ. It is not territory.

3. The flag State - nationality, freedoms and exclusive jurisdiction

  • Article 91 - each State fixes the conditions for granting nationality and registration; a genuine link must exist.
  • Article 92 - a ship sails under one flag only and is subject to the flag State's exclusive jurisdiction on the high seas.
  • Article 94 - the flag State must effectively exercise jurisdiction and control in administrative, technical and social matters - construction, equipment, seaworthiness, manning, labour conditions, training - and hold an inquiry into serious casualties.
  • Article 58 - in the EEZ all States retain the freedoms of navigation and overflight, and Articles 88 to 115, including Article 92, apply so far as not incompatible. The flag State's authority therefore travels with the ship into the EEZ.

4. How the two are reconciled - due regard

UNCLOS resolves the overlap by a reciprocal standard, not a hierarchy: the coastal State exercises its EEZ rights with due regard to other States' rights, and other States exercise their freedoms with due regard to the coastal State's, complying with its laws made in conformity with the Convention. Residual rights are resolved on equity and all relevant circumstances. Across every zone, Article 98 obliges every master to render assistance to any person in danger of being lost at sea.

5. b) The missing cadet - which law applies

The tempting answer is that Indian law applies because the ship was in India's EEZ. That is wrong. The EEZ is not Indian territory; India's authority there is functional - resources, installations, research, the marine environment - and a person missing from a ship engages none of it. Because Article 58 carries Article 92 into the EEZ, shipboard matters remain under the exclusive jurisdiction of the flag State. The determining question is whose flag the vessel flies.

  • Indian-flagged - Indian law applies in full through the flag. Under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958: s.358, loss of life by reason of a casualty on board is a shipping casualty, the master giving immediate notice to the officer appointed by the Central Government; s.359, that officer reports in writing and may hold a preliminary inquiry; s.452, the proper officer inquires into the cause of death, endorses the official log book as to whether the stated cause is true, and if death appears caused by violence or other improper means reports to the Director-General. Indian criminal law reaches the ship through s.1(5) of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 - any offence on a ship registered in India wherever it may be.
  • Foreign-flagged - the flag State's law governs the investigation and any prosecution, and its Administration conducts the casualty investigation. India acts as coastal State and, decisively, as the State responsible for search and rescue in its search and rescue region - a duty owed regardless of flag under Article 98 and the SAR Convention. India may also be a substantially interested State if the cadet was an Indian national.

6. The actions to be taken

  1. Immediate. Raise the man overboard alarm and mark the position; muster and account for every person on board to establish who is missing and when last seen, since the search datum must be reconstructed. Commence a search, return along the track, post lookouts, and broadcast to the MRCC and ships in the vicinity.
  2. Preserve. Secure the cabin and last known location; preserve CCTV, gangway and access records, rest-hour records and personal effects; take statements early; make full official log book entries.
  3. Notify. Owner and manager, the DPA, the flag State, the coordinating coastal State authority, the P&I club, the manning agent, and - through the company - the next of kin.
  4. Report and investigate. Loss of life makes this a very serious marine casualty, so a safety investigation follows under the Casualty Investigation Code alongside the flag State's inquiry; for an Indian ship the s.358 notice and s.359 report apply.
  5. Company review and welfare. Treat it under the ISM Code as an accident and non-conformity - safety of access, work overside, lifebuoy provision, fatigue, and bullying, harassment and isolation examined honestly - and discharge MLC 2006 obligations on repatriation of remains, compensation for death and support to dependants, while supporting the remaining crew.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb (a) asks about both States and most answers describe only the coastal one. Splitting the limb into coastal State, flag State and the due-regard reconciliation guarantees that the flag half is present, and it sets up limb (b), which cannot be answered without it. Limb (b) is only 6 marks but it contains the single highest-value insight in the question, so it should be led with the jurisdictional point and not with a list of actions.

Why the EEZ is the way it is

The EEZ was the great compromise of UNCLOS III. Coastal States - especially newly independent ones - wanted the fish and the oil off their coasts; maritime States wanted their ships and aircraft to keep moving. The zone gives each side what it actually needed: economic control to the coastal State and navigational freedom to everyone else. Understanding that bargain tells you immediately what the coastal State can and cannot do, without memorising a list: if the issue is a resource, an installation, research or pollution, it is the coastal State's; if it is about the ship as a ship - her navigation, her crew, her internal affairs - it remains the flag State's.

Sovereignty, sovereign rights, jurisdiction - three different words

  • Sovereignty - the full authority of a State over its territory. Internal waters and territorial sea.
  • Sovereign rights - exclusive rights for a defined purpose. The EEZ and continental shelf. Exclusive, but purposive.
  • Jurisdiction - the power to prescribe and enforce law over specified subject matter, such as artificial islands or marine scientific research in the EEZ.

The question uses the phrase sovereign rights and jurisdictions deliberately, and an answer that treats the three as interchangeable has missed what is being tested.

Missing is not the same as dead

The scenario says the cadet goes missing, not that he was seen to fall or that a body was recovered, and that distinction has practical and legal consequences. Practically, the search datum must be reconstructed from the last confirmed sighting rather than from a position, which is why the muster is the critical first step and why gangway records and CCTV matter so much. Legally, a presumption of death normally requires a formal process and takes time, which affects wages, the seafarer employment agreement, insurance, and the family's ability to deal with the estate. A candidate who writes as though a death certificate follows automatically has misunderstood the situation the crew is actually in.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming Indian law applies because the incident was in India's EEZ. It is not territory.
  • Forgetting to ask what flag the ship flies - the question does not say, and that omission is the test.
  • Describing only the coastal State's rights in limb (a) and ignoring the flag State.
  • Saying the coastal State has sovereignty in the EEZ, or that it has no rights at all there. Neither is right.
  • Citing the Indian Penal Code, replaced by the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 on 1 July 2024.
  • Treating search and rescue as depending on jurisdiction. It does not - Article 98 and the SAR Convention apply regardless of flag.
  • Jumping straight to actions in limb (b) without answering the question asked, which is which laws will apply.

Examiner traps

  • 'Coastal state and Flag state' - both are named in the stem and both must be answered.
  • 'In the Exclusive Economic zone of India' - written to invite the wrong answer.
  • 'Which laws will apply' comes before 'what actions will be taken'. Answer them in that order or the analysis reads as an afterthought.
