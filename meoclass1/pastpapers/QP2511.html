Understand this first

These ships were not lost because they were badly loaded or badly handled. They were lost because a structural member nobody was required to look at closely failed, and because the ship had no defence once it had. That is the whole question, and it explains the shape of the answer: a chain of events, and a chapter of regulations written to cut the chain in as many places as possible. Hold the chain - frame, shell, hold, bulkhead, bow - and you can derive most of chapter XII from first principles even if the regulation numbers escape you. It also explains why the response came in two parts that this same examination paper asks about separately: strengthen and equip the ship, which is chapter XII, and make somebody look at it, which is the enhanced survey regime.

Model written answer

1. What was happening

The early 1990s saw a spate of bulk carrier losses, and their character is what made them so serious. These were not slow foundering casualties. Ships were being lost suddenly, often without a distress message and frequently with the whole crew, in weather that similar ships had survived for years. A ship that sinks in minutes gives nobody time to launch a boat, which is why the response had to be about preventing the flooding rather than improving the escape.

No figure is given here for ships or lives lost. IMO's own material describes the losses without quantifying them, published figures differ substantially, and an unverifiable number is better omitted than guessed.

2. The primary mechanism - side shell to flooded hold

The dominant failure chain was understood to run like this, and it explains the suddenness:

  • The ships were mostly single-side-skin designs, in which the side shell frames and their end brackets are directly exposed in the cargo hold.
  • Those frames corrode and fatigue faster than anything else on the ship. They alternate between wet and dry service, they are abraded by grabs and bulldozers during discharge, and coating breaks down first at the bracket toes where the stress concentration also is.
  • The foremost hold is the worst place for this. It takes the heaviest slamming and the greatest wave impact, and it is the least accessible for inspection.
  • A frame failure lets the side shell go, and No.1 hold floods.
  • The flooded hold now loads the transverse watertight bulkhead between holds 1 and 2 with a head of water it was never designed to hold. If that bulkhead fails, two holds flood.
  • With two forward holds full of water and dense cargo, the ship loses bow buoyancy and longitudinal strength together and goes down by the head, very quickly.

3. The secondary mechanism - hatch covers and progressive flooding

  • Water ingress through hatch covers and other openings is the other entry route IMO identifies - covers of inadequate strength, worn securing arrangements, or damaged coamings, in a seaway that puts green water on the foredeck.
  • Alternate-hold loading made it worse. Loading heavy cargo in alternate holds concentrates weight and raises local stresses, and once a hold is flooded the sloshing mixture of cargo and water attacks the boundaries and drives progressive flooding into the next space.

4. Why it was happening then

  • An ageing fleet. Many of the ships were built in the boom of the late 1960s and 1970s and were reaching the age at which accumulated wastage matters.
  • High-tensile steel. HTS gives the same strength in a thinner plate - which means the same percentage of corrosion removes a larger proportion of the remaining margin, and fatigue performance is not improved in proportion to yield strength.
  • Dense cargoes. Iron ore and similar cargoes fill only part of the hold volume but load the structure heavily, and they are discharged by mechanical means that damage the very frames that matter.
  • Maintenance and inspection. Coatings in cargo holds were often not maintained, and nothing obliged anyone to look closely at side shell frames within arm's reach on a routine basis.
  • Commercial pressure. Fast turnarounds, aggressive discharge and thin margins on old tonnage all worked against condition.

5. IMO's response - what chapter XII is

SOLAS chapter XII - Additional safety measures for bulk carriers - was adopted in November 1997 and entered into force on 1 July 1999, with a further significant package of amendments in 2004. It was one of the first and most consequential products of the Formal Safety Assessment methodology: IMO ran FSA studies on bulk carrier safety following the re-opened investigation into the loss of the Derbyshire, and MSC 76, in December 2002, approved the resulting package including chapter XII and regulation XII/12.

The chapter attacks the failure chain in section 2 at every link, which is why it works and is the structure this answer follows.

6. The structural and stability provisions

  • Survive the flooded hold. New bulk carriers of 150 m and above carrying solid bulk cargoes of density 1,000 kg/m3 and above must be able to withstand flooding of any one cargo hold, taking into account dynamic effects. This is the single most important provision: it breaks the chain at the point where one flooded hold became two.
  • Strengthen what failed. Requirements for the structural strength of bulk carriers - in particular the transverse watertight bulkhead and the double bottom in the flooded condition, and the side structure itself - gave statutory force to the classification-society work of the same period on frame scantlings and corrosion margins.
  • Protect the steel. Regulation XII/6 is the bulk carrier limb of the mandatory Performance Standard for Protective Coatings (resolution MSC.215(82)). Coating breakdown is the leading indicator of exactly the wastage that caused these losses.
  • Offer a better hull form. The 2004 amendments introduced double-side-skin construction as an alternative - which removes the exposed side shell frame from the cargo hold altogether.
  • Restrict the dangerous loading. The same package added restrictions on sailing with any hold empty for the ships and conditions most at risk.

7. Detection, operational and survey provisions

  • Know that the hold is flooding. Regulation XII/12, effective 1 July 2004, requires water level detectors with alarms in cargo holds, ballast tanks and dry spaces - at 0.5 m above the inner bottom, and a second at not less than 15 per cent of hold depth but not more than 2.0 m. Crews had been losing ships without knowing a hold was full. This is the cheapest provision in the chapter and arguably the one that saves most lives.
  • Be able to do something about it. Regulation XII/13 requires that the means for draining and pumping dry space bilges and ballast tanks be operable from a readily accessible enclosed space - because the alarm is useless if reaching the valves means going forward on a flooded deck.
  • Load it correctly. Regulation XII/11 requires an approved loading instrument on bulk carriers of 150 m in length and upwards, so shear force and bending moment are monitored at every pour and every de-ballasting step. A cargo density declaration is required so that the ship is loaded on the right assumptions.
  • Look at it properly. Chapter XII carries survey and maintenance requirements and requires information on compliance to be carried aboard; these run together with SOLAS XI-1/2 and the 2011 ESP Code, under which close-up survey of the side shell frames within arm's reach and thickness measurement by an approved firm became a planned, mandatory programme rather than a matter of judgement.

8. The contribution argued, and its limits

The contribution is best stated as defence in depth against one specific chain of events, and that is what makes chapter XII a good regulatory answer rather than merely a strict one:

  • Prevent the frame failure - coatings, scantlings, double-side skin.
  • Contain the flooding if it happens - bulkhead and double bottom strength in the flooded condition, and survival with any one hold flooded.
  • Detect it early - water level alarms.
  • Respond to it - remotely operable pumping and draining.
  • Verify continuously - loading instrument, cargo density declaration, enhanced survey.
  • Any single measure could be defeated; the point is that the chain now has to break in several places at once.

Its limits should be stated too. Chapter XII is largely prospective - the strongest structural and stability requirements attach to new ships and to defined size and density thresholds, so an older ship below the threshold is reached mainly by the detection, survey and operational provisions rather than by the design ones. It cannot compel good maintenance, only inspect for its absence. And it does not touch the commercial pressure - fast discharge with grabs and bulldozers, short port stays, thin margins on old tonnage - that produced the condition in the first place. What it did do was remove the possibility of a ship being lost because nobody was required to look, and that is the honest measure of its contribution.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Two tasks, and the examiner has weighted them for you: briefly discuss the reasons, then explain how chapter XII contributes. So keep the history tight - roughly a third - and spend the rest on the chapter. The best answers do one thing above all: they make the two halves match. Set out the failure chain first, then show chapter XII attacking it link by link. An answer that lists causes and then separately lists regulations has written two answers; an answer that says this provision exists because of that failure has written one.

The failure chain in one sentence

Learn it as a chain and the whole question unlocks: corroded and fatigued side shell frame -> side shell opens -> No.1 hold floods -> the transverse bulkhead sees a head of water it was never designed for -> two holds flood -> the bow loses buoyancy and the ship goes down by the head in minutes. Every element of chapter XII is a cut across one of those arrows, and you can derive the chapter from the chain if you forget the regulation numbers.

Why the FOREMOST hold, and why HIGH-TENSILE STEEL

Two details separate a competent answer from a good one. The foremost hold is where the ship takes the heaviest slamming and wave impact, where the structure is most highly stressed in a seaway, and where inspection access is worst - so the damage is greatest exactly where it is least likely to be seen. High-tensile steel is the subtler point and examiners like it: HTS delivers the same strength in a thinner plate, so the same rate of corrosion consumes a much larger proportion of the margin, and fatigue strength does not rise in proportion to yield strength. A design that is perfectly sound as built has less tolerance for the wastage that then occurs. This is also why the surveyor must know from the scantling drawings which plates are HTS - the same measured thickness can be acceptable in one plate and a rejection in another.

This paper asks the same question twice

Read this with Q5 of this same paper, on the Enhanced Survey Programme. The two are the same casualty record answered from opposite ends. Chapter XII is the construction and operational answer - survive the flooded hold, protect the coatings, detect the water, restrict the loading. The ESP is the inspection answer, which is exactly why Annex A of the ESP Code is the bulk carrier annex and why the regime concentrates on close-up survey of side shell frames and end brackets within arm's reach. In one sentence: the ships were lost through corrosion and fatigue of side structure that nobody was required to look at closely, and IMO responded by both strengthening the ship and mandating that somebody look. If you can write that sentence you can answer either question.

The regulatory point worth making

Chapter XII is one of the earliest and most consequential products of Formal Safety Assessment. IMO did not respond to these losses by tightening a rule at random; it ran structured FSA studies on bulk carrier safety after the re-opened investigation into the loss of the Derbyshire, and MSC 76 in December 2002 approved the resulting package. If a question on FSA ever asks you for a real example of the method producing regulation, this is the strongest one available - and it works the other way round too, because saying so here shows the examiner you know how the rule came to exist and not merely that it does.

Common mistakes

  • Quoting a number of ships or lives lost. The published figures differ substantially and IMO's own material does not quantify them - characterise the record instead.
  • Saying chapter XII made double-side-skin construction mandatory. It was introduced as an alternative.
  • Treating the losses as a stability problem. They were a structural problem whose consequence was a stability and buoyancy failure.
  • Listing regulations without connecting each to the failure it prevents. The stem says contribute towards the safety - that is a causal claim and must be argued.
  • Forgetting the detection provisions. The water level alarms are the cheapest measure in the chapter and probably the highest-value one.
  • Claiming chapter XII fixed everything. Its strongest structural provisions are largely prospective and threshold-limited, and it cannot compel good maintenance.

Examiner traps

"Why did the ships sink so fast?" Because the failure was structural and progressive: a flooded forward hold overloads the bulkhead behind it, and when that goes, two holds full of water and dense cargo take the bow down in minutes. That is also why there was often no distress message.

"What must a bulk carrier survive under chapter XII?" New ships of 150 m and above carrying cargoes of density 1,000 kg/m3 and above must withstand flooding of any one cargo hold, taking into account dynamic effects.

"Where are the water level alarms set?" 0.5 m above the inner bottom, and a second at not less than 15 per cent of hold depth but not more than 2.0 m - regulation XII/12.

"Which ships need a loading instrument?" Bulk carriers of 150 m in length and upwards, regulation XII/11.

"Why does the pumping requirement specify where you operate it from?" Because regulation XII/13 is useless otherwise - the alarm has just told you a forward hold is flooding, and that is precisely when nobody should be going forward on deck.

Understand this first

The engine and the propeller are matched to each other on one diagram, and everything in this question is a movement on that diagram. The propeller absorbs power as roughly the cube of its speed; the engine builder draws that curve alongside the engine's own limits and then deliberately lays the engine out a few per cent to the right of it, because he knows the hull will foul and the weather will be bad. That deliberate gap is the light running margin. Torque rich simply means the ship has used up that margin and gone past it - the propeller now demands more torque than the engine can give at that speed, so the operating point drifts toward the torque limit, which exists because of thermal load. Hold that picture and the rest is derivable: the causes are anything that adds resistance, the indications are departures from the reference condition rather than absolute readings, and the cure is to stop pushing, find what added the resistance, and take it away.

Model written answer

1. The propeller curve and the load diagram

A fixed pitch propeller absorbs power according to the propeller law: for a given hull condition, power varies roughly as the cube of the revolutions and torque roughly as the square. Plotted as power against rpm, that gives the propeller curve. The engine builder plots the engine's own limits on the same axes as the engine layout and load diagram, and the ship is designed so that the propeller curve sits comfortably inside them.

In MAN Energy Solutions' presentation of the load diagram, line 6 is the light propeller curve - for a clean hull and calm weather. That is the reference condition, and it is deliberately not where the engine is laid out.

2. What torque rich means

Torque rich - the term the examiner uses - is what the engine builder calls heavy running. The propeller is absorbing more torque at a given speed of rotation than the light propeller curve predicts. Equivalently, and this is the more useful way to say it:

  • for the same power, the engine runs at lower rpm than it should;
  • for the same rpm, the engine must develop more torque, which means more fuel per cycle and higher mean effective pressure;
  • the operating point moves up and to the left on the load diagram, away from the light propeller curve and towards the torque limit.

That last movement is the whole problem, because of what the limit is. Line 4 of the load diagram is the torque/speed limit of the engine, and it is limited mainly by the THERMAL LOAD on the engine. Note also that line 5, the maximum mean effective pressure for continuous operation, is a limit only at high loads and speeds - at lower speeds line 4 is the stricter limit, which is precisely the region a heavy-running ship is driven into. A torque rich engine is therefore not merely inefficient; it is being pushed toward a limit that exists to protect it from thermal overload.

3. Why the light running margin exists

Because heavy running is expected, the engine is deliberately laid out to the right of the light propeller curve. The gap is the light running margin:

LRM = (nL - nH) / nH x 100 per cent, where nL is the light propeller curve speed and nH the heavier engine layout curve speed.

MAN Energy Solutions recommends a light running margin of 4 to 7 per cent, depending on the specific ship and operational profile, and up to 10 per cent in special cases. It sits alongside the sea margin - typically 10 to 30 per cent, historically 15 per cent - and the engine margin, historically 10 per cent and typically 10 to 30 per cent. So a new ship on trials is light running by design. Torque rich means the ship has consumed that designed-in margin and gone past it.

A single figure gives the sensitivity, and it is worth remembering: a 20 per cent increase in required power from added resistance corresponds to about 1 per cent heavy running - one per cent lower rpm for the same engine power. The rpm shift is small and the power penalty behind it is very large, which is exactly why the condition is missed.

4. The reasons

Anything that increases the resistance of the ship, or the load on the propeller, at a given speed of rotation.

  • Hull fouling. The first and commonest cause. Frictional resistance increases with fouling of the hull by the growth of algae, sea grass and barnacles, and frictional resistance is the dominant component for most merchant hull forms at service speed. Long idle periods in warm water are the classic history.
  • Propeller fouling and roughness. A fouled, pitted or damaged blade surface degrades efficiency directly, and the propeller is a far smaller area than the hull - so a given amount of roughness there matters disproportionately.
  • Propeller damage. A bent, eroded or broken blade tip changes the absorbed torque and adds vibration.
  • Weather and sea state. Wind, waves and swell, particularly on the bow. This is the condition the sea margin exists for; heavy weather simply uses it up.
  • Shallow water. In general there is no influence when the water depth is more than ten times the ship's draught. Below that the resistance can rise significantly - the pressure field around the hull is intensified and the wave system enlarged, water flows back around the restricted section, and the propeller draws water past the hull faster than in deep water.
  • Adverse current, and restricted or confined water. Canals and narrow channels combine shallow-water and bank effects.
  • Displacement and trim. A deeper draught than the design condition, or a trim away from the optimum, increases resistance.
  • Towing, pushing, or an ice-bound passage, and in the limit the bollard pull condition, which is heavy running taken to its extreme: a heavy running factor of 15 to 20 per cent relative to the light propeller curve has been experienced there - equivalent to roughly a 60 to 100 per cent increase in power for the same rpm, which is why full rpm simply cannot be reached at bollard pull.
  • An over-pitched or wrongly matched propeller, or a propeller repaired to the wrong pitch. Here the ship is heavy running from new or from the repair, not through fouling.
  • On a controllable pitch propeller, excessive pitch demand for the rpm - a mis-set or drifting combinator curve, or a fault in the pitch control or feedback.
  • Hull deformation or appendage damage, and a damaged or permanently offset rudder.

5. Indications from the engine

Separate what the engine tells you from what the ship tells you, because the diagnosis needs both.

  • The defining indication: rpm and fuel index disagree with the reference condition. At a given rpm the fuel pump index is higher than the shop and sea trial data, or the sea trial curve, would give - the engine is working harder for the revolutions it is making.
  • Rated rpm cannot be reached at full load. The engine comes up against its limit before it reaches its speed.
  • Higher exhaust gas temperatures and rising thermal load, since more fuel is being burnt per cycle at a lower air throughput.
  • Scavenge air pressure is low for the fuel being burnt. Turbocharger speed follows exhaust energy and engine rpm, so at reduced rpm the air supply lags the fuel demand - the air-fuel ratio falls, combustion deteriorates, and black smoke appears.
  • Higher mean effective pressure and altered indicator diagram - compression and maximum pressures shifting relative to the reference set.
  • Turbocharger surging in a severe case, and higher exhaust deposit and fouling rates over time.
  • The engine's own protection acting - the governor at the index limit, or the scavenge air pressure or torque limiter cutting back the fuel, is the control system telling you the operating point has reached line 4.

6. Indications from the ship

  • Speed loss. Lower speed over the ground and through the water for the same power, once current and weather are allowed for.
  • Fuel consumption per mile rises for the same passage and condition.
  • Performance monitoring trends. Plotted against the reference curve, the ship's operating points drift steadily to the left over weeks and months - which is what distinguishes fouling from a single weather event.
  • On the load diagram itself, the plotted operating point sits above the light propeller curve and approaches line 4.

7. Confirm and exclude, before acting

The instruction to the second engineer begins here, and it matters, because acting on a faulty sensor wastes a hull cleaning.

  • Take a full set of engine performance readings - indicator or pressure-rise diagrams, fuel index, rpm, scavenge air pressure and temperature, exhaust temperatures, turbocharger speed - and compare them against the shop and sea trial reference data for the same condition.
  • Verify the instruments before believing them. Tachometer, shaft power meter or torsionmeter, thermocouples, pressure gauges and the fuel index scale. A drifting torsionmeter has sent ships to dry dock unnecessarily.
  • Correct for the obvious. Draught, trim and displacement, weather, current and water depth. If the ship is in ten metres of water at deep draught she is heavy running for a reason that will disappear at the next sea buoy.
  • Exclude engine-side causes that mimic heavy running: fouled turbocharger or air cooler, high air cooler differential pressure, blocked scavenge ports or exhaust gas boiler, incorrect injection timing or VIT setting, leaking exhaust valves, and poor fuel quality, viscosity or calorific value. These reduce available power at rpm and look similar on the instruments, but the cure is entirely different.

8. Protect the engine now

  • Bring the operating point back inside the diagram - reduce the fuel index and accept the lower rpm. Do not chase the revolutions with more fuel; that is what drives the engine into the torque limit.
  • Do not run in the overload area other than as the maker permits - overload running is allowed only for limited periods, one hour per twelve hours, or when required in an emergency, because the thermal load is high.
  • Confirm the limiters are working - scavenge air pressure limiter, torque limiter, load programme - rather than defeating them.
  • Increase surveillance while the condition persists: exhaust temperature deviation between units, liner wall temperatures, scavenge space inspection for signs of poor combustion, and turbocharger condition.

9. Remedy, report, and where authority stops

  • Propeller polishing first. It is the cheapest and quickest intervention, it can be done by divers in many ports, and the gain per square metre cleaned is the highest on the ship.
  • Underwater hull cleaning, subject to the port's rules on in-water cleaning and biofouling discharge, and hull cleaning and recoating at the next dry docking.
  • Underwater inspection of the propeller for damage, erosion and blade deformation, and of the rudder and appendages.
  • On a CPP, check and calibrate the pitch setting, feedback and combinator curve, and confirm that the demanded pitch at each rpm is what the maker intended.
  • Optimise trim and ballast for the loaded condition where the ship's trim guidance allows it.
  • Log and trend everything, so the case rests on data rather than impression, and feed it into the ship's performance monitoring.
  • Report to the Chief Engineer, who informs the master and the Company. Speed and consumption are charter-party matters and the shore organisation must know.
  • Escalate the diagnosis, do not decide it. If the ship remains heavy running with a clean hull and a sound propeller, the fault is a matching problem, and the remedy - re-pitching, de-pitching or replacing the propeller, or a re-rating - is an owner's decision taken with the propeller designer, the engine builder and class. The second engineer establishes and preserves the evidence; he does not prescribe the cure.
  • Consult the ship's own documentation throughout. The light running margin actually built into your vessel is in her project papers and her engine load diagram - not in any general figure, including the ones quoted above.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four printed limbs and the marks are evenly spread, so answer all four visibly and head each one. The examiner has told you where to start: "Referring to propeller Curve". That is not scene-setting, it is the instruction - the definition in limb (a) is only precise relative to the propeller curve, and an answer that says 'the engine is overloaded' without drawing or describing the curve has not defined anything. Sketch the load diagram if you can: light propeller curve, engine layout curve, the torque limit, and the operating point sitting between them. Everything in limbs (b), (c) and (d) can then be pointed at on the diagram, which is far faster to write than to describe in prose.

Torque rich IS heavy running

The Indian examination uses torque rich; the engine builders' documentation says heavy running. They are the same condition, and knowing both names is worth a line in the answer, because every maker's manual, load diagram and service letter you will ever consult uses the second one. The complementary term is light running - which is not a fault but the designed condition: a new ship on trials with a clean hull runs light on purpose, and the deliberate gap is the light running margin.

The one number that explains why it is missed

MAN's rule of thumb: a 20 per cent increase in required power from added resistance corresponds to about 1 per cent heavy running - one per cent lower rpm for the same power. Read that twice. The rpm change is tiny and the power penalty behind it is enormous. A watchkeeper glancing at a tachometer will never see it; only comparison with the reference condition will. That single relationship is the argument for systematic performance monitoring, and quoting it in limb (c) shows you understand why the indications have to be compared rather than merely read.

Why the torque limit is where it is

Line 4 of the load diagram is the torque/speed limit, and it is limited mainly by the THERMAL load on the engine. That is the sentence to remember, because it explains why torque rich is dangerous rather than merely uneconomic. At reduced rpm the turbocharger turns more slowly and delivers less air, while the fuel demanded per cycle has gone up - so the air-fuel ratio falls exactly when more heat is being released. Higher exhaust temperatures, black smoke, fouling and thermal stress all follow from that one imbalance. Note too that line 5, the mep limit, bites only at high loads and speeds; at lower speeds line 4 is the stricter limit, which is precisely the corner a heavy-running ship is pushed into.

The trap in limb (d): confirm before you clean

The instinctive answer to limb (d) is 'clean the hull and polish the propeller'. That is the right ending and the wrong beginning, and examiners notice. Several engine-side faults imitate heavy running on the instruments - a fouled turbocharger or air cooler, incorrect injection timing or VIT, leaking exhaust valves, poor fuel quality - and so does a drifting torsionmeter or tachometer. All of them reduce the power available at a given rpm and all of them look similar. The disciplined sequence is confirm, exclude, protect, then remedy: verify against reference data, check the instruments, correct for draught, weather and water depth, exclude the engine side, reduce load to get off the torque limit, and only then arrange the diver. A candidate who sends the ship for an underwater clean because a thermocouple has drifted has answered the question badly even though the words look right.

Common mistakes

  • Defining torque rich without reference to the propeller curve, when the stem expressly says to refer to it.
  • Saying the engine is 'overloaded'. It is mismatched - operating off its design curve toward the torque limit. Overload is a different and defined region of the diagram.
  • Treating light running as a fault. It is the design intent, and the light running margin exists precisely so the ship can go heavy in service and still make her power.
  • Answering limb (d) with 'clean the hull' and nothing else - no confirmation, no instrument check, no engine-side exclusion, no immediate protection.
  • Chasing the rpm with more fuel. That is the single most damaging response, because it drives the engine straight into the thermal limit the diagram exists to keep it away from.
  • Quoting a fuel penalty percentage for fouling. Published values vary enormously with coating, trade, water temperature and lay-up; explain the mechanism instead.
  • Forgetting the CPP case, where a mis-set combinator curve produces the identical symptoms with a perfectly clean hull.

Examiner traps

"Your ship is 1 per cent down on rpm at the same power. Is that significant?" Yes - roughly a 20 per cent increase in required power. The smallness of the rpm figure is the trap.

"Why not just increase the fuel to get the revolutions back?" Because you would be driving the engine into line 4, the torque/speed limit, which is set by thermal load. You would be trading a fuel penalty for liner, piston and exhaust valve damage.

"How much overload running is permitted?" Only for limited periods - one hour per twelve hours, or when required in an emergency, because the thermal load is high.

"When does shallow water matter?" In general there is no influence when the depth is more than ten times the draught. Below that it can be significant.

"The hull was cleaned last month and she is still heavy. What now?" Suspect the propeller - damage, roughness, or wrong pitch after a repair - or, on a CPP, the pitch control. If the hull and propeller are both sound, it is a matching problem and it goes to the owner, the propeller designer, the engine builder and class. It is not a decision for the engine room.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the propeller law? Power varies roughly as the cube of rpm and torque roughly as the square, for a given hull condition.
  • What is the light running margin, and what does the maker recommend? The rpm margin between the light propeller curve and the engine layout curve; MAN Energy Solutions recommends 4 to 7 per cent, and up to 10 per cent in special cases.
  • Which line on the load diagram is the torque limit, and what sets it? The torque/speed limit line, set mainly by the thermal load on the engine.
  • Name three causes of heavy running that have nothing to do with fouling. Heavy weather; shallow water; an over-pitched or damaged propeller. Add restricted water, deep draught, towing, and CPP pitch control faults.
  • What is the first indication you would look for? Fuel index higher than the reference value at the same rpm.

The propeller is absorbing more torque at a given speed of rotation than the light propeller curve predicts - the maker's term is heavy running. Equivalently: for the same power the engine runs at lower rpm; for the same rpm it must develop more torque, so more fuel per cycle and higher mean effective pressure; and the operating point moves up and to the left on the load diagram, away from the light propeller curve and toward the torque/speed limit.

Defining it as generic 'overload' loses the marks. It is a mismatch against a specific reference curve.

The rpm margin between the light propeller curve and the engine layout curve: LRM = (nL - nH) / nH x 100 per cent. MAN Energy Solutions recommends 4 to 7 per cent depending on the specific ship and operational profile, and up to 10 per cent in special cases. It sits alongside the sea margin - typically 10 to 30 per cent, historically 15 - and the engine margin, historically 10 per cent.

It shows that light running is the design intent, not a fault, and that torque rich means the designed margin has been consumed.

As a rule of thumb, a 20 per cent increase in required power from added resistance corresponds to about 1 per cent heavy running - that is, 1 per cent lower rpm for the same engine power.

The single most useful number in the topic: the rpm change is tiny and the power penalty behind it is enormous, which is exactly why the condition goes unnoticed without performance monitoring.

The torque/speed limit line of the engine load diagram - line 4 in MAN's numbering - which is limited mainly by the THERMAL load on the engine. The mean effective pressure limit is a constraint only at high loads and speeds; at lower speeds the torque/speed limit is the stricter one, which is precisely the region a heavy-running ship is driven into.

It explains why torque rich is a damage risk and not merely a fuel-economy problem.

In general shallow water has no influence when the water depth is more than ten times the ship's draught; below that it can increase resistance significantly. Because the engine is already being driven toward its torque/speed limit, which is set mainly by thermal load. Model written answer

1. The governing instrument

Ship health and vector control are governed by the International Health Regulations (2005), a binding instrument of the World Health Organization, as amended in 2014, 2022 and 2024. The 2024 amendments entered into force on 19 September 2025. The IHR work through designated points of entry, competent authorities at those points, and a small set of ship documents.

2. The role of WHO

  • It makes the rules. WHO adopts the IHR and prescribes the model certificates in Annex 3 and the Ship Declaration of Health in Annex 8, so the documents are the same worldwide.
  • It identifies where vector control is needed. Under Annex 5, WHO publishes on a regular basis a list of areas where disinsection or other vector control measures are recommended for conveyances arriving from them, through temporary or standing recommendations.
  • It advises methods and materials. Where WHO has advised methods for disinsection, deratting, disinfection or decontamination, those should be used unless the competent authority is satisfied that others are as safe and reliable (Article 27), and States should accept measures applied elsewhere by WHO-advised methods.
  • It certifies and publishes. On a State Party's request WHO may, after appropriate investigation, certify that a port meets the IHR requirements (Article 20(4)), publishes certification guidelines and the list of certified ports (20(5)), and publishes the lists of authorized ports notified by States Parties (20(3)).
  • It provides technical guidance - notably the WHO Handbook for inspection of ships and issuance of ship sanitation certificates - and declares Public Health Emergencies of International Concern with temporary recommendations.

3. National agencies in India

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare - the health authority for IHR purposes, under which the National IHR Focal Point sits.
  • Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), and under it the Port Health Organisation, headed by the Port Health Officer - the competent authority at the point of entry. It inspects ships, baggage, cargo and containers, supervises control measures and issues the ship sanitation certificates, working under the Indian Port Health Rules, 1955 with the IHR 2005.
  • Directorate General of Shipping, through its Maritime Health Branch, on the seafarer side - approving medical examiners and governing seafarers' medical fitness examination and certification.
  • Port authorities and the Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways support these functions at the ports themselves.

4. The ship's certificates

  • Ship Sanitation Control Exemption Certificate (SSCEC) - issued where the competent authority is satisfied that the ship is free of infection and contamination, including vectors and reservoirs, so that no control measures are required (Article 39(6)).
  • Ship Sanitation Control Certificate (SSCC) - issued where control measures were required and have been satisfactorily completed, and it records the evidence found and the measures taken (Article 39(5)). Under Annex 5 the presence of vectors on board and the measures used to eradicate them are recorded on this certificate. If a satisfactory result could not be obtained, the authority notes that on the certificate (Article 39(7)).

Both follow the model in Annex 3, and they replaced the former Deratting and Deratting Exemption Certificates. Alongside them the master submits a Ship Declaration of Health under Article 37, to the model in Annex 8 - a declaration, not a certificate. Separately, and concerning the crew rather than the ship, seafarers hold medical fitness certificates under MLC 2006 and STCW.

5. Issue in India, validity and extension - the ship

  • In India they are issued by the Port Health Officer at a port authorized for the purpose and notified to WHO. Under Article 20(3) a State Party notifies which ports may issue the SSCC, which may issue the SSCEC only, and which may grant the one-month extension.
  • Validity: a maximum of six months, for both the SSCEC and the SSCC (Article 39(1)).
  • Extension: by one month, where the inspection or the required control measures cannot be accomplished at that port - the extension carries the ship to a port where the certificate can be obtained.
  • Where no valid certificate is produced, or evidence of a public health risk is found on board, the State Party may proceed under Article 27(1) - treating the ship as affected and requiring disinfection, decontamination, disinsection or deratting.
  • Control measures are to be carried out whenever possible when the ship and holds are empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading (Article 39(4)).
  • For Indian coastal trade calling where no Port Health Officer is available, DG Shipping has provided for a Statement of Compliance issued by an authorized medical examiner, to be endorsed at the earliest available opportunity.

6. The crew's medical certificate, validity and extension

The stem's first sentence names the ship and the crew, and asks for the certificates "in India" without confining them to the ship. The seafarer's own certificate therefore belongs in the answer, and it has its own validity and its own extension - which is what makes it worth the marks.

  • The certificate. A medical fitness certificate under MLC 2006 Regulation 1.2 and Standard A1.2. Standard A1.2(3) provides that a certificate issued under STCW is accepted for these purposes, so a single examination satisfies both instruments.
  • Who issues it. A duly qualified medical practitioner, or, for a certificate concerning eyesight alone, a person recognized by the competent authority as qualified to issue it (A1.2(4)). In India that means a medical examiner approved by the Directorate General of Shipping, which acts under the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 and the rules made under it.
  • Validity. A maximum of two years; one year where the seafarer is under 18; and a certification of colour vision, a maximum of six years (A1.2(7)). These are maxima - a shorter period may be required by the specific duties or under STCW, and a State may set shorter periods.
  • Extension. Where a certificate expires in the course of a voyage it continues in force until the next port of call at which the seafarer can obtain a certificate from a qualified medical practitioner, provided the extension does not exceed three months (A1.2(9)). Separately, in urgent cases, the competent authority may permit a seafarer whose certificate has recently expired to work for a limited period (A1.2(8)).

So the answer to "validity and extension options" is two pairs of numbers, not one. The ship: six months, extendable by one month, by the Port Health Officer under the IHR. The crew: two years, extendable by up to three months to the next port, under MLC Standard A1.2. Different instrument, different authority, same shape of rule - and both extensions exist for the same practical reason, that a ship at sea cannot always reach the person who issues the document.

In the original 2005 text it was the "Maritime Declaration of Health", and that older name is what almost every textbook, coaching note and older syllabus still uses. Using the current name, and being able to say why it changed, is a cheap and genuine mark.

Ship health and crew health are two different systems

The question asks about "the ship and the crew", and the two run on separate tracks with separate authorities. Ship health is WHO and the IHR, administered in India by the Port Health Organisation under DGHS and the Ministry of Health, and evidenced by the SSCC or SSCEC. Crew health is MLC 2006 and STCW, administered in India by DG Shipping through approved medical examiners, and evidenced by seafarers' medical fitness certificates. Candidates routinely put DG Shipping in charge of ship sanitation certificates. It is not - the Port Health Officer issues those.

Common mistakes

  • Calling the SSCC/SSCEC a Deratting Certificate. Those were the predecessors under the earlier Regulations and were replaced under the IHR 2005 - the scope widened from rodents to all infection and contamination including vectors and reservoirs.
  • Saying the certificates are valid for six months extendable by six months. The extension is one month, and only where the inspection or control measures cannot be accomplished at that port.
  • Confusing the SSCC with the SSCEC. Exemption means no measures were needed; Control means measures were required and were completed.
  • Treating the Ship Declaration of Health as a certificate. It is a declaration by the master, to the Annex 8 model, and a State may dispense with it entirely under Article 37(4).
  • Naming WHO as an enforcement body. WHO recommends, certifies ports on request and publishes; the competent authority at the point of entry enforces.

Examiner traps

"Your certificate expired yesterday and the port cannot inspect - what happens?" The one-month extension in Article 39(1) exists precisely for this, and under Article 20(3)(c) States notify which of their ports may grant it, so that the ship can reach a port where the certificate is obtainable.

"When should the inspection be done?" Whenever possible with the ship and holds empty, and for a ship in ballast before loading - Article 39(4). Answering "any time" misses a printed requirement.

"What does WHO actually do about mosquitoes?" It publishes the list of areas where vector control is recommended for conveyances arriving from them, and advises the methods. Model written answer

1. The framework these processes sit inside

A ship using LNG as fuel is designed and operated under SOLAS chapter II-1 part G and the IGF Code; the bunkering operation itself is specified by ISO 20519 - equipment, procedures, competence and documentation. Safety rests on a two-stage emergency shutdown system, with the delivery facility and the receiving ship joined by an ESD link so both act together. Before transfer, a safety zone is set, compatibility and mooring agreed, and the checklist and bunkering plan completed.

2. Gassing up

Carried out when a tank is being brought back into service after refit, repair or inerting - before any liquid is introduced. The tank is full of inert gas or nitrogen, and that atmosphere must be replaced with methane vapour.

  • Why. Any carbon dioxide in inert gas would freeze at LNG temperature and block valves, filters and lines. Nitrogen does not freeze but is non-condensable: it accumulates in the vapour space, raises tank pressure, degrades the fuel's calorific value and upsets reliquefaction and gas supply.
  • How. LNG is taken from the supply through a vaporiser to produce warm methane vapour, which is admitted at the top of the tank. Being lighter than nitrogen, it forms a layer that pushes the heavier inert gas down and out through the bottom liquid line.
  • Where it goes. The displaced gas is led to the vent mast with attention to dispersion, or returned to the supply.
  • Completion. Sampling at the outlet continues until the hydrocarbon content reaches the specified value and carbon dioxide falls below its limit. The figures are set by the ship's manual and the containment system, not by a universal rule.

3. Cooling down

Once the tank holds methane vapour it is still warm. Cooling down brings the tank, pipework and manifold gradually towards LNG temperature, about minus 162 degrees Celsius, before bulk filling begins.

  • Why. To avoid thermal shock and excessive thermal stress in the tank structure and insulation; and to avoid the violent flash-off that occurs when LNG meets warm surfaces, which would over-pressurise the tank and swamp the vapour handling capacity.
  • How. LNG is admitted in small controlled quantities through spray nozzles at the top of the tank, evaporating as it falls and cooling the structure evenly.
  • Rate control. The permitted rate of temperature fall is limited by the tank designer and class, and temperatures are watched at fixed sensors through the tank to keep the structure cooling uniformly rather than locally.
  • Completion. When the sensors reach the target and are stable, bulk transfer starts at a low initial rate and is ramped up only once pressures and temperatures are seen to be steady.

4. Vapour management

LNG boils continuously from heat ingress, and filling a tank displaces the vapour already in it. Vapour management is the control of tank pressure within the design limit throughout, and it is the process that actually governs how fast bunkering can proceed.

  • Vapour return line - the primary method. A connection back to the bunker source returns the displaced vapour to its tanks, balancing pressure on both sides and removing the need to vent to atmosphere.
  • Consumption - burning the gas in the ship's engines, boilers or generators where the plant is running.
  • Reliquefaction where fitted, returning boil-off to the tank as liquid.
  • Gas combustion unit - burning off surplus gas safely where it cannot be used.
  • Controlled venting to the vent mast - a last resort only, because released methane is a potent greenhouse gas and venting is operationally and environmentally constrained.
  • Supporting controls. Spray filling to condense vapour; monitoring against the relief valve setting; level alarms and ESD trips; and attention to stratification and rollover where LNG of a different density is loaded onto an existing heel.

5. The emergency release coupling procedure

The emergency release system exists so that the transfer connection can be parted quickly and without spilling if the two vessels move beyond safe limits, if moorings fail, or in fire. Its heart is the emergency release coupling - a dry-break coupling with interlocked isolating valves which close before the halves separate, so the liquid held in the line is contained and the release of LNG and gas is minimised.

  • ESD-1 - stop the pumps and close the manifold valves on both sides. Transfer ceases. Triggered manually from either end or automatically by tank level, pressure, gas detection or fire.
  • Drain and reduce inventory in the transfer line so far as the time available allows.
  • ESD-2 - the release stage. The coupling's isolating valves close first, then the coupling parts at its designed point. Initiation is manual, or automatic from mooring load or vessel position monitoring exceeding its limits.
  • After release - both sides are purged with nitrogen, the parted hose end is made safe, and personnel stay clear of the release zone while residual liquid boils off.
  • Before transfer the ESD link must be connected and function-tested, the coupling confirmed armed and certified, communications and agreed emergency signals established, and the release covered in the bunkering plan and drilled.

The emergency release is the fourth because it accepts that despite all of that, the connection may still have to be broken - and even then the design goal is the same, to part the line without releasing what is inside it. The order is also the order of the operation: you cannot cool a tank you have not gassed up, and you cannot fill one you have not cooled.

Why gassing up comes before cooling down

Because the freezing problem has to be solved while everything is still warm. Inert gas from a generator carries carbon dioxide, which becomes a solid well above LNG temperature. Introduce liquid into a tank that still holds carbon dioxide and you produce dry ice inside valves, strainers and spray lines - exactly where you least want a blockage. Gassing up removes it first. Nitrogen is a gentler problem but a persistent one: it will not freeze, but because it does not condense it stays in the vapour space, builds pressure, and thins the gas going to the engines. So gassing up is not a formality before the real work; it is what makes the cooling stage survivable.

Common mistakes

  • Reversing the order - cooling down before gassing up. Liquid must not enter a tank still holding inert gas.
  • Saying gassing up removes air. Air was removed earlier by inerting; gassing up removes the inert gas or nitrogen and replaces it with methane vapour.
  • Describing cooling down as "filling slowly". It is spray cooling through nozzles at a controlled rate of temperature fall, not a slow bulk fill.
  • Treating venting as normal vapour management. It is a last resort; the vapour return line is the primary route and methane venting carries a real environmental penalty.
  • Describing the emergency release coupling as a hose that simply breaks. Its defining feature is the interlocked isolating valves that close before separation.
  • Conflating ESD-1 and ESD-2. Stopping the transfer and parting the connection are two different stages, and ESD-2 follows ESD-1.

Examiner traps

"Why not use inert gas from the generator and go straight to loading?" Carbon dioxide in the inert gas freezes at LNG temperature and blocks the system. That single sentence is what the gassing-up limb is really testing.

"What limits how fast you can bunker?" Not the pump - the vapour handling capacity and the tank pressure limit. Bunkering is paced by where the gas can go.

"The bunker vessel starts to drift. What happens first?" ESD-1 stops the transfer and closes the manifold valves; only then does ESD-2 operate the release coupling. Going straight to "the hose disconnects" misses the sequence.

"You loaded a different LNG on top of your heel. What is the risk?" Stratification and rollover - a sudden mixing of layers of different density releasing a large volume of vapour and spiking tank pressure.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the purpose of gassing up? To displace inert gas or nitrogen with methane vapour before liquid is admitted, chiefly to remove carbon dioxide that would freeze.
  • Why is warm vapour admitted at the top of the tank? It is lighter than nitrogen, so it layers above and pushes the heavier gas out through the bottom line.
  • What is the approximate temperature of LNG? About minus 162 degrees Celsius at atmospheric pressure.
  • What is the primary route for displaced vapour during bunkering? The vapour return line back to the bunkering facility.
  • What does an emergency release coupling do that an ordinary quick-release does not? Its interlocked isolating valves close before the coupling parts, containing the line inventory.
  • What links the two ESD systems? A ship-to-ship or ship-to-shore ESD link, so that shutdown on either side acts on both.

Model written answer

1. What the Enhanced Survey Programme is

The ESP is a mandatory regime of intensified hull inspection for the two ship types whose structural failures have historically been catastrophic. It sits in the 2011 ESP Code, adopted as resolution A.1049(27) on 30 November 2011 and made compulsory by SOLAS regulation XI-1/2. Annex A covers bulk carriers, Annex B oil tankers, each split between single- and double-skin or double- and non-double-hull construction, and the Code is closely aligned with IACS Unified Requirement Z10.

It works by requiring, in addition to the ordinary periodical survey, a planned programme of close-up survey of critical structure within arm's reach, thickness measurement by an approved firm, and tank testing - all governed by a Survey Programme agreed with the Administration or its recognized organization before the renewal survey begins.

2. What a Condition Assessment Programme survey is

CAP is a voluntary, additional classification-society service, carried out at the owner's request. Nothing in SOLAS or MARPOL requires it. Its purpose is commercial: it produces a numerical rating of the ship's actual condition, judged not against the minimum acceptable standard but against a new-build standard, and its scope is selectable - hull, machinery, cargo systems, or a combination. Owners buy it because charterers, oil-major vetting inspectors, buyers and financiers want an independent grading of an older ship, and it involves far more extensive gauging and testing than an ESP survey does.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three printed limbs, and the marks are unevenly available. Limb (a) is where the real discrimination happens, because the difference is conceptual, not a matter of listing two definitions. Limbs (b) and (c) are pure recall and reward organised lists - so give them structure rather than a stream of items. Splitting (b) into documents needed to plan and records of the ship's history and condition makes it far easier to write completely under pressure, and it is how the survey programme itself is organised.

The idea behind limb (a)

ESP asks a pass/fail question; CAP asks a scoring question. The ESP exists to establish that the structure is still at or above the minimum standard the ship must meet to hold its certificates - a floor. CAP exists to say how far above that floor the ship actually sits, measured against a new-build standard - a grade. That single distinction generates every other difference: because ESP answers a legal question it is mandatory, flag-driven and hull-focused, and failure stops the ship trading. Because CAP answers a commercial question it is voluntary, owner-requested, scope-selectable, and failure costs charters rather than certificates.

The abbreviation trap that decides this question

CAP is not CAS. The Condition Assessment Programme is the voluntary classification-society grading described above. The Condition Assessment Scheme is a mandatory regime under MARPOL Annex I applying to single-hull oil tankers, adopted by IMO resolution. A candidate who answers limb (a) by describing CAS has answered the wrong question and has, worse, said that the thing is mandatory when the question is precisely about a voluntary scheme. If you are asked to distinguish ESP from CAP and you find yourself writing "MARPOL", stop - you have taken the wrong turning.

Why the paperwork in limb (b) exists

None of it is bureaucracy for its own sake, and saying why earns more than the list alone. A thickness reading is meaningless without the scantling drawing that says what the plate started at and whether it is high-tensile steel, because HTS carries a different diminution allowance. The cargo and ballast history tells the surveyor where to look, since a tank that has carried corrosive cargo or has been used for ballast corrodes differently. The coating condition and the Coating Technical File predict where wastage will appear next. And the Survey Report File makes the ship's structural condition a trend rather than a snapshot - which is the whole point of an enhanced programme.

Common mistakes

  • Describing CAP as a statutory requirement, or confusing it with the Condition Assessment Scheme under MARPOL Annex I.
  • Saying CAP replaces or extends class or statutory surveys. It does neither, and a good rating does not maintain class.
  • Saying the ESP applies to all ships. It applies to bulk carriers and oil tankers.
  • Omitting that the Survey Programme must be agreed before the survey starts, and that a planning meeting is held.
  • Treating the safe access plan as a generic risk assessment. It is specific - which means of access, for which space, reaching which structure.
  • Forgetting that thickness measurement must be carried out by a firm approved by the society, not by the crew.

Examiner traps

"The owner has a CAP 1 rating. Can the special survey be relaxed?" No. CAP has no statutory effect whatever; the ESP survey stands in full.

"Which is more demanding?" In gauging volume and testing, CAP usually is - but it is the voluntary one. Demanding and mandatory are different axes, and that is the point of the comparison.

"When may a tank be rafted for close-up survey?" Only under conditions - clearance beneath the deckhead, controlled water level, adequate lighting and attendance, and the coating in good condition with no structural failure. "Whenever the tank is flooded" is wrong.

"Why does the surveyor want to know about high-tensile steel?" Because the permissible diminution differs, so the same measured thickness can be acceptable in one plate and a rejection in another.

Model written answer

1. Secure the evidence and establish the facts

  • Do not dress, grind or blend the crack. Material removed before assessment destroys the evidence class, the underwriter and the root-cause investigation all need.
  • Record it. Photographs and video with a scale in frame, and a dimensioned sketch.
  • Locate it precisely - blade number from the stamping on the boss, pressure or suction face, radial position as a fraction of the blade radius, distance from the leading or trailing edge and from tip and root, and the crack's length and orientation.
  • Examine the whole propeller, not just the one blade - the remaining blades, the leading and trailing edges, the tips, the boss, the keyway and the fairing cone. A crack is rarely isolated.
  • Look for related damage - rudder, stern frame, rope guard, stern tube seal, hull marks - which would point to contact or grounding.
  • Assemble the service history - vibration or unusual noise, shaft power and revolution anomalies, any grounding, ice contact or rope entanglement, stern tube oil analysis and the planned maintenance record.

2. Establish the nature and extent

  • Clean the area only as far as the surveyor permits, then carry out liquid dye penetrant testing. Propellers are cast in non-ferromagnetic alloys - nickel-aluminium bronze or manganese bronze - so magnetic particle inspection cannot be used. Where depth must be established, eddy current or ultrasonic techniques are applied.
  • Have the testing done by a qualified operator from an approved firm and obtain a written report.
  • Establish what the defect actually is: a genuine fatigue crack, a casting defect, or cavitation erosion pitting that resembles cracking. The three have different consequences and different causes.

3. Report to the Company

  • Inform the Master first, then report to the Company - the technical superintendent and, under the ISM Code, the Designated Person Ashore - and raise it in the safety management system as a hazardous occurrence or non-conformity so that it enters the analysis and corrective-action process.
  • The report must be factual and complete: description and precise location, dimensions, NDT method and result, photographs and sketch, propeller particulars (maker, material, drawing and serial number, diameter and pitch), relevant service history, and the effect on the dry-dock programme and undocking date.
  • State the options and a recommendation, with the implications of each for cost and time - but recognise the division of responsibility: the Chief Engineer establishes the facts and recommends; the Company decides and instructs.
  • Request instructions, and confirm who will liaise with class, the repairer and the underwriter.

4. Involve classification and settle the repair

  • Notify the classification society and request the attending surveyor to examine. The owner is under a duty to report any damage or defect which affects or may affect classification, and concealing it puts class - and with it the statutory certificates and the insurance - at risk.
  • The surveyor assesses the crack against the society's rules. Acceptance depends heavily on where on the blade it lies: the highly stressed region is the most restricted and repairs there are limited or prohibited, while damage near the tip is treated far more permissively. ISO 484 governs the tolerances against which repaired geometry is judged.
  • The possible outcomes are: accept as found; grind out and blend to a smooth radius within permitted limits; weld repair by an approved procedure; crop and re-form; or replace the blade or the propeller.
  • Where a repair is done it must be by a class-approved repairer to an approved welding procedure with the pre-heat and post-weld heat treatment the alloy requires, followed by re-testing, a check of pitch and geometry, and static balancing.
  • Obtain the surveyor's endorsement. If the repair is deferred, expect a condition of class with a time limit rather than a clean survey.
  • Consider separately whether the occurrence is reportable to the flag Administration. That turns on its cause and on whether seaworthiness was affected - damage arising from a grounding or contact is reportable; wastage found at a routine docking generally is not.

5. Insurance and preservation of the claim

  • Through the Company, notify the hull and machinery underwriters, and the P&I club if any third-party element arises, and arrange attendance of the underwriter's surveyor before repairs begin.
  • Whether a claim lies depends on the cause. Damage from an insured peril such as contact or grounding is recoverable; ordinary wear and tear and cavitation erosion are not, and a latent defect is recoverable only so far as the policy provides for it.
  • Do not commit to a repair method or cost without the underwriter's agreement where a claim is contemplated, and retain removed material and samples for metallurgical examination.

6. Root cause, prevention and close-out

  • Establish the cause: cavitation erosion; fatigue initiating at cavitation pitting or a casting defect acting as a stress raiser; impact with a submerged object or ice; dezincification of manganese bronze; galvanic attack where anodes or the impressed current system are defective; or sustained overload from heavy running or excessive astern working.
  • Act on it - review hull and propeller roughness, fouling and polishing intervals, confirm cathodic protection is functioning, monitor shaft vibration, and review astern working and heavy running.
  • Close the loop: file the NDT and survey reports, repair procedure, welder qualifications and balancing record; update the machinery history, planned maintenance system and underwater inspection schedule; feed the lessons through the safety management system; and hand over fully.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The command is state the steps, so the answer must be a sequence, not an essay about propellers. Six headed steps in a defensible order will outscore a longer piece of prose every time. Notice what the question actually asks: it is a management question with a technical trigger. The examiner wants to see that a Chief Engineer knows who must be told, in what order, and what must not be touched in the meantime. Metallurgy earns marks only where it changes a decision.

The instinct you have to suppress

The engineer's reflex on finding a surface crack in a dock, with the undocking date approaching, is to dress it out and carry on. That reflex is the most expensive mistake available here. Grinding the crack away before anyone has seen it destroys three things at once: the class surveyor's ability to assess what was actually there, the underwriter's ability to accept a claim, and the root-cause investigation that would stop it happening on the sister ship. The propeller can be repaired later; none of that evidence can be recovered. So the first step is not a repair step at all - it is to stop, record, and report.

Why location on the blade decides almost everything

A propeller blade is not uniformly stressed. Bending stress from thrust and torque is greatest towards the root, and the blade is thinnest towards the tip and the edges, so a crack of the same length means very different things depending on where it is. Classification rules divide the blade into zones accordingly: in the highly stressed region, welding is restricted or not permitted at all and the realistic outcomes are limited grinding or blade replacement; near the tip the same defect may be blended out without difficulty. This is why the answer must record blade number, face, radius and distance from the edge before anything else - those four facts, not the crack's length, are what determine the outcome.

Common mistakes

  • Proposing magnetic particle inspection. Propellers are nickel-aluminium or manganese bronze and are not ferromagnetic, so MPI simply does not work. Use dye penetrant.
  • Grinding or welding before class attends and before the extent is established.
  • Reporting to the Company but not to class, or assuming the superintendent will do it.
  • Treating every crack as a claim. Cavitation erosion and ordinary wear are not insured perils.
  • Deciding the repair yourself. The Chief Engineer recommends; the Company instructs and class approves.
  • Omitting balancing and geometry check after repair - a repaired blade that is out of pitch or out of balance introduces vibration and a new fatigue problem.
  • Stopping at the repair and never establishing the cause, which guarantees a repeat.

Examiner traps

"Why not MPI?" Because the material is not ferromagnetic. If you cannot answer this you have shown you have never actually stood at a propeller in a dock.

"The dock is due to flood tomorrow. What do you do?" Report and get class to attend. Commercial pressure does not change the sequence, and undocking with an unreported defect affecting class is a far worse outcome than a delay.

"Is this a general average or a hull claim?" Neither is automatic - it depends entirely on cause. Establish that first.

"You blended out the crack and it looked fine. Any problem?" Yes - the blade geometry and pitch may now be outside tolerance, the blade is out of balance with the others, and you have removed the evidence. Blending is permitted only within limits and with class agreement.

"Must you report it to the flag State?" It depends on cause and on whether seaworthiness was affected - not automatically.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Which NDT method for a bronze propeller, and why? Dye penetrant - the alloy is not ferromagnetic, so magnetic particle inspection is unusable.
  • What four facts locate the defect? Blade number, pressure or suction face, radial position, and distance from the leading or trailing edge.
  • Who approves a propeller weld repair? The classification society, and it must be carried out by an approved repairer to an approved procedure.
  • What is done after a blade repair? Re-testing, a pitch and geometry check against tolerance, and static balancing.
  • What happens if the repair is deferred? A condition of class with a time limit may be imposed.
  • Name three causes of blade cracking. Cavitation erosion leading to fatigue, impact with a submerged object, and a casting defect acting as a stress raiser.
  • Who do you tell first on board? The Master, then the Company and the Designated Person Ashore.

Model written answer

1. What the Convention is, and what it exists to do

The Convention on Facilitation of International Maritime Traffic, 1965 - the FAL Convention - was adopted on 9 April 1965 and entered into force on 5 March 1967. It is IMO's trade facilitation instrument, and it is the odd one out among the conventions a Chief Engineer meets: SOLAS, MARPOL and STCW tell you what the ship, its equipment and its people must be. FAL tells public authorities what they may demand of a ship when she arrives, stays and departs.

Its objectives, in the Organization's own words, are to prevent unnecessary delays in maritime traffic, to aid co-operation between Governments, and to secure the highest practicable degree of uniformity in formalities and other procedures. The problem it was written to solve was simple and expensive: every port demanded different documents, in a different format, in a different number of copies, and a ship could lose more time to paperwork than to cargo.

2. How it works

  • The obligations sit in an annex, expressed as Standards - measures necessary and practicable in order to facilitate international maritime traffic - and Recommended Practices, measures the application of which is desirable.
  • A Contracting Government that finds it impracticable to comply with a Standard, or that considers it necessary to adopt differing requirements, must notify the Secretary-General of the difference (article VIII). The obligation is therefore not absolute, but a departure must be declared rather than simply operated.
  • The annex prescribes a maximum, not a minimum: under Standard 2.1bis public authorities shall not require more information than is provided for in the standardised declarations.
  • Those declarations are the FAL Forms 1 to 7 - the General Declaration, Cargo Declaration, Ship's Stores Declaration, Crew's Effects Declaration, Crew List, Passenger List and Dangerous Goods manifest - together with the Declaration of Health and documents required under the Universal Postal Convention.
  • The annex is amended by tacit acceptance: adopted under article VII(2)(a) and deemed accepted under article VII(2)(b) unless a stated proportion of Contracting Governments object by a stated date.

3. Why it matters

  • Time is the cargo owner's money and the ship's cost. Port time is the least productive time a ship has; every hour saved at the interface is earned twice, once in charter and once in fuel.
  • Uniformity is what makes a ship tradeable worldwide. A master and crew cannot learn a different clearance regime for every port, and the FAL Forms mean they do not have to.
  • A ceiling on demands protects the ship from the port. Standard 2.1bis is a genuine protection: it limits what an authority may lawfully require.
  • It is the foundation the digital layer was built on. Nothing else could have been standardised electronically if the content had not first been standardised on paper.

4. The recent amendments - what is actually in force

Two annex amendments matter at this point, and both are already in force.

  • Resolution FAL.14(46), adopted 13 May 2022, in force 1 January 2024. This is the important one. It made the single window mandatory: under Standard 1.3quin as amended, public authorities shall introduce arrangements enabling all information required under Standard 2.1 to be provided to a single window by electronic means, and shall consider the single window as the channel for communicating their decisions. The annex defines a single window as an environment that allows for the submission or provision of standardized and harmonized information and declarations to a single entry point, typically by electronic means. It also carries Standards 2.1bis and 2.1.2 on the information ceiling and on pre-arrival and pre-departure information.
  • Resolution FAL.15(47), adopted 17 March 2023, in force 1 January 2025 - ten months before this examination. It amends Recommended Practice 7.11 on national facilitation programmes, requiring them to be established and maintained taking into account the need to combat illicit activities. It was adopted under article VII(2)(a) and its operative paragraph 2 determines, under article VII(2)(b), that it enters into force on 1 January 2025 unless one third of Contracting Governments objected before 1 October 2024. No such objection was made.

The single window was not a standing start. Electronic exchange of information had already become mandatory from 9 April 2019, with a minimum twelve-month transition; the 2022 amendments took the further step of requiring a single entry point rather than merely an electronic one.

5. What is NOT yet an annex requirement

This distinction is worth marks and is easy to get wrong. The FAL 49 session (10 to 14 March 2025) approved a number of facilitation items - a Recommended Practice 6.24 on vaccinations and medical care, revised Maritime Single Window Guidelines, and a new edition of the IMO Compendium. Approved is not adopted, and adopted is not in force. Those items were not annex requirements at this point.

Nor are all recent FAL resolutions annex amendments at all. FAL.18(49) on mooring personnel and FAL.17(48) on wildlife smuggling are guidelines resolutions - adopted, but they do not alter a Standard or a Recommended Practice. So the accurate statement of the position is: the amendments to answer on are FAL.14(46) and FAL.15(47), and there is no annex amendment adopted and awaiting entry into force.

6. Impact - digitalization

  • One submission instead of many. Where a single window is properly implemented the ship's agent submits a harmonised data set once, and customs, immigration, port health, port authority and security each draw from it. The master's staff stop retyping the same crew list into five formats.
  • Standardised data, not merely electronic data. The IMO Compendium on Facilitation and Electronic Business, developed with the World Customs Organization, UN/CEFACT and ISO, provides the harmonised reference data model - so that 'port of call' means the same field to every system. Without it, a single window would simply be a shared inbox.
  • Clearance moves off the critical path. Pre-arrival submission under Standard 2.1.2 means formalities can be completed before the ship is alongside, so berth time is spent working cargo.
  • Data protection becomes an operational concern. Concentrating crew, cargo and voyage data at one entry point raises the value of that point to an attacker, which is where facilitation meets cyber risk management.
  • The obligation falls on the AUTHORITY, not the ship. The Convention requires public authorities to establish and maintain the single window; the ship's benefit is derivative, and where a State has not implemented one the ship gains nothing - which is exactly the unevenness the Convention exists to reduce.

7. Impact - sustainability

  • The environmental gain runs through TIME. Reliable, standardised pre-arrival information is what makes just-in-time arrival possible: if a ship knows when the berth will actually be free, she can reduce speed on passage instead of steaming hard to anchor and wait. Fuel burn falls with speed sharply, so waiting at sea at reduced power is materially cheaper in fuel and emissions than arriving early and idling.
  • It supports the emissions regime rather than duplicating it. The Convention sets no emissions standard and claims none; it removes an administrative obstacle to operating efficiently, which is a different and more modest kind of contribution.
  • Paper itself. Eliminating multi-copy paper declarations at every port is a real if small resource saving, and removes courier and physical-attendance movements at the ship-shore interface.
  • Better port planning reduces congestion, and congestion is idling main and auxiliary machinery at anchor - emissions produced for no transport work at all.

State the limit honestly, because the question says 'analyze'. The benefits above are conditional on implementation. A single window that is a web form wrapping the same seven paper documents delivers the compliance and none of the benefit; just-in-time arrival requires the charterparty to permit it, since a laytime regime that starts the clock on arrival rewards the ship for steaming hard and waiting. The Convention removes the administrative obstacle. The commercial obstacle is untouched by it.

8. The limits - and what the Convention does not do

  • It does not regulate the ship - no construction, equipment, manning or emission requirement.
  • It does not override customs, immigration or quarantine law; it standardises the interface with them.
  • It does not make a Recommended Practice binding. Only Standards carry the article VIII difference obligation.
  • It does not, by itself, deliver a working single window in any particular State.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three tasks are set in one running stem and the third is the one that separates answers. Explain the objectives and importance; discuss the recent amendments; analyze their impact, particularly in relation to digitalization and sustainability. The examiner has named the two lenses, so give each its own heading - an answer that discusses 'modernisation' generally has not done what was asked. Budget roughly a third to the Convention itself and two thirds to amendments and impact, and make sure the word analyze is earned by saying where the benefit does not arrive.

The distinction that carries the amendment limb

Approved is not adopted, and adopted is not in force - and on the FAL Convention there is a fourth trap on top of those three: not every FAL resolution is an annex amendment. A resolution adopting guidelines changes no Standard and no Recommended Practice. So when a question asks for 'the recent amendments', the disciplined answer names the resolutions that actually amended the annex and says what each did - here FAL.14(46) and FAL.15(47) - rather than listing everything the Facilitation Committee has been busy with. Being able to say 'and there is no annex amendment currently adopted and awaiting entry into force' is worth more than an extra paragraph, because it shows you know the difference between a committee's work programme and the law.

The single window is an obligation on the STATE

This is the point most often inverted. Standard 1.3quin requires public authorities to introduce the arrangements and to treat the single window as the channel for communicating decisions. Nothing in it obliges the ship to do anything new; the ship's benefit is entirely derivative of the State having done its part. That is why the practical experience of the single window is so uneven port to port, and why an honest analysis has to say so. If you write that 'the FAL Convention requires ships to submit electronically to a single window', you have got the duty-holder wrong.

Where the sustainability marks actually are

Candidates reach for 'less paper' and stop. Less paper is real but trivial. The environmental argument runs through time and speed. Fuel consumption rises steeply with speed, so the difference between steaming hard to an anchorage and then waiting, and slowing down on passage to arrive when the berth is free, is a large fuel and emissions difference for the same voyage and the same cargo. Reliable standardised pre-arrival data is what makes that choice possible - which is how a trade facilitation convention ends up on a decarbonisation answer. Then add the limit: it only works if the charterparty lets it, because under a conventional laytime regime the ship is rewarded for arriving and tendering notice, not for arriving late and efficiently.

Common mistakes

  • Treating FAL as a safety or pollution convention. It regulates formalities, not the ship.
  • Saying the single window obliges the ship. It obliges public authorities.
  • Listing FAL Committee outputs as 'amendments' when they are guidelines resolutions or merely approved items.
  • Giving the adoption year as the operative date. Adopted 1965, in force 5 March 1967; the single window amendments in force 1 January 2024.
  • Claiming a percentage fuel saving for just-in-time arrival. The figures vary enormously with trade and port; explain the mechanism instead.
  • Answering 'impact' with advantages only. The command verb is analyze, and an analysis states the conditions under which the benefit fails to appear.

Model written answer

1. The Convention and when it bit

The Hong Kong International Convention for the Safe and Environmentally Sound Recycling of Ships, 2009 was adopted at a diplomatic conference in Hong Kong, China in May 2009 and entered into force on 26 June 2025 - more than fifteen years later. The delay is explained by Article 17, which imposes a triple condition: twenty-four months after not less than 15 States have consented to be bound, whose combined fleets are not less than 40 per cent of world gross tonnage, and whose combined maximum annual ship recycling volume over the preceding ten years is not less than 3 per cent of their combined tonnage - the third condition making the Convention impossible to bring into force without the major recycling States. At entry into force there were 24 Parties representing about 57 per cent of world tonnage, including Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Turkiye.

2. What it requires of ships

  • An Inventory of Hazardous Materials in three parts - materials in the structure and equipment, operationally generated wastes, and stores - with Parts II and III completed before recycling.
  • Survey and certification: an International Certificate on Inventory of Hazardous Materials, and, after a final survey, an International Ready for Recycling Certificate.
  • Application to ships of 500 gross tonnage and above on international voyages; existing ships must comply by 26 June 2030 or before going for recycling, whichever is earlier.
  • Control or prohibition of listed hazardous materials - asbestos, PCBs, ozone-depleting substances, and anti-fouling systems containing organotin compounds or cybutryne.
  • Recycling only at a facility authorised under the Convention in a Party State, working to a ship-specific Ship Recycling Plan, with notification of intent to recycle and a statement of completion.

3. What it requires of recycling facilities

An IMO instrument normally regulates ships; this one reaches ashore and regulates the yard.

  • Authorisation by the competent authority of the State, evidenced by a Document of Authorisation to conduct Ship Recycling.
  • A Ship Recycling Facility Plan covering worker safety and training, emergency preparedness and response, and the management of hazardous materials.
  • Safe-for-entry and safe-for-hot-work conditions to be established and maintained.
  • Controlled downstream management of the wastes removed - which is where the real burden falls.

4. India's position and domestic implementation

  • India is one of the world's principal ship-recycling States, centred on the Alang-Sosiya yards in Gujarat, and it acceded on 28 November 2019, the Director General of Shipping depositing the instrument at the thirty-first IMO Assembly. That accession was decisive: it took the count to the required fifteen States and contributed heavily to the recycling-volume condition.
  • Domestically the Convention is given effect by the Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 and the Recycling of Ships Rules, 2019, which anticipated entry into force by six years and apply Convention standards to ships recycled in India.
  • The maritime administration is the competent authority, authorising yards and approving ship recycling plans.
  • Indian ship recycling is governed by its own statute, not by the general merchant shipping legislation. The Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 is separate legislation with its own machinery, and the general statute standing alongside it at this examination is the Merchant Shipping Act 1958. The Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 had received the assent of the President on 18 August 2025, three months before this paper, but assent is not commencement and it was not yet in force; it does not govern anything at this sitting. Either way the 2019 Act stands on its own feet.

5. How India stands to benefit

  • Legitimacy and access to better tonnage. Owners who avoided South Asian yards on reputational grounds can now send ships to an authorised Indian facility with a defensible answer, which should raise both volume and quality of tonnage.
  • A level playing field. Competing recycling States must now meet the same standard, so the money India's yards spent upgrading ahead of entry into force is rewarded rather than punished on price.
  • Worker safety and environmental gain. Enforceable safe-for-hot-work and safe-for-entry requirements, training, protective equipment and controlled waste handling address the fatality and contamination record that has defined the industry's reputation.
  • Downstream industry and investment. Formalisation supports the steel re-rolling and second-hand equipment trades, and certification makes yards financeable and contractable on longer terms.

6. How it stands to be impacted, and the balance

  • Cost of compliance. Impermeable floors, containment, lifting equipment, and hazardous waste storage and treatment are capital-intensive. Small operators may not afford them, so the industry consolidates and some yards close.
  • Downstream waste capacity is the binding constraint. The Convention's obligations do not end at the yard gate, and treatment and disposal capacity for asbestos and other hazardous waste must exist for compliance to be real rather than documentary.
  • Price and leakage. Higher costs mean a lower price per light displacement tonne, creating an incentive to recycle elsewhere - including through a change of flag before the last voyage to a State outside the Convention.
  • Convention compliance is not European acceptance. The Convention does not expressly prohibit beaching, whereas the EU Ship Recycling Regulation maintains its own list of approved facilities on stricter terms. An Indian yard may be fully Convention-compliant and still not be EU-listed.
  • Enforcement is the credibility test. Authorisation has to be inspected and enforced; the industry remains under close external scrutiny, and a documentary-only regime would forfeit the reputational gain that is the main prize.
  • On balance, entry into force is net positive for India: it converts an early and expensive investment into a competitive advantage and legitimises the industry. The gain is conditional on downstream waste infrastructure and real enforcement keeping pace with certification - without them, the cost is incurred and the reputational benefit is not.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

Three tasks are set inside one running stem, and candidates routinely answer only the first. Budget the paper deliberately: roughly a third on the Convention, and two thirds on India, split between benefit and impact. The word "or" in "benefit or be impacted" is not offering you a choice - it is asking for both sides and a judgement. An answer that only lists advantages has not done what was asked. Finish with an explicit balance; the archetype here is evaluation, and evaluation questions are marked on whether a reasoned conclusion actually appears.

The feature that makes this Convention different

Almost every IMO instrument regulates the ship - its construction, equipment, certificates and crew. The Hong Kong Convention is one of the very few that reaches ashore and regulates a facility on land, requiring the yard itself to be authorised, planned and inspected. That is why it took so long to bring into force: it needed the consent of States whose domestic industry, not merely whose fleet, would be regulated. And it is why Article 17's third condition exists - a recycling-volume threshold that made it impossible to bring the Convention into force over the objections of the recycling States. Read that way, India's accession in 2019 is not a footnote; it is one of the two or three events that made the Convention real.

The distinction that separates a good answer from a very good one

The Convention does not ban beaching. It requires safe and environmentally sound recycling, authorisation, planning and control - but it does not prescribe the method by which a ship is brought ashore. The EU Ship Recycling Regulation takes a stricter line and maintains its own list of approved facilities, which is why the acceptability of the Alang yards to European owners has been contested for years and is not settled by entry into force. So the accurate proposition is: Convention compliance is necessary for legitimacy but is not sufficient for European acceptance. A candidate who writes that the Convention has ended beaching, or that compliance automatically opens the EU market, has overstated it in exactly the way the examiner is watching for.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Convention bans beaching, or that it makes Indian yards automatically acceptable to EU owners.
  • Giving the adoption year as the operative date. Adopted 2009, in force 26 June 2025 - the gap is the interesting part.
  • Reciting only the ship-side obligations and omitting the facility obligations, which are half the Convention.
  • Confusing the ICIHM (held through service life) with the Ready for Recycling Certificate (issued after the final survey, immediately before recycling).
  • Answering only the benefits when the question says benefit or be impacted, and giving no concluding judgement.
  • Attributing India's recycling law to the Merchant Shipping Act of any year. It is the separate Recycling of Ships Act, 2019 - and at this sitting the general Act is the 1958 one, the 2025 Act having been assented but not commenced.
  • Quoting a percentage share of world recycling. The figures vary widely between sources; name Alang-Sosiya instead.

Understand this first

The point of this question is that a convention passes through several distinct stages, and at every stage it is not yet law for your ship. Adoption fixes the words. Signature usually promises nothing. Ratification or accession binds the State, and only when the instrument is physically deposited. Entry into force starts the obligations running. And even then, what is actually enforced against the ship in an Indian port is the domestic legislation giving effect to the convention. A Chief Engineer who can place an instrument on that ladder can answer 'does this apply to me?' - which is what the question is really about. This paper is unusually generous to anyone who has understood it, because it carries three live cases of the ladder at three different rungs: a condition satisfied, an amendment tacitly accepted, and an adoption convened and not taken.

Model written answer

1. Adoption is not entry into force

A convention is adopted either by the competent IMO organ - the Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text and does no more: it binds no State, and an adopted convention may never enter into force at all. The text is then opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General of IMO as depositary.

2. How a State expresses consent to be bound

Between adoption and entry into force sits the step that actually matters: each State must express its consent to be bound, by one of three routes -

  • signature alone, where the treaty so provides or the negotiating States so agreed;
  • signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, which is the normal course for multilateral conventions;
  • accession, where the State did not sign within the signature period.

In the second and third cases the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument with the depositary, who records it, counts it towards the entry into force condition, and notifies all other States.

3. The two terms the question names

(ii) Signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval. The State signs, but the signature does not bind it; it indicates an intention to proceed. The State becomes a party only on later depositing an instrument of ratification. Its modern purpose is to give the State time to enact the necessary domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements before committing internationally. Acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names, used where a constitution does not demand the fuller procedure: VCLT Article 14(2) provides that consent is expressed by acceptance or approval under conditions similar to those which apply to ratification. In legal effect the three are identical.

(i) Accession. The means by which a State becomes party to a treaty it did not sign - because the signature period has closed, or because it was not a negotiating State. It is a single step: no prior signature, simply deposit of an instrument of accession, permitted by VCLT Article 15 where the treaty so provides. Once deposited it has exactly the same legal effect as ratification. The difference is the route, not the destination.

4. The entry into force provision, and why it matters

Every convention contains a final clause fixing entry into force, normally a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. Its importance is fourfold:

  • it prevents an instrument binding a handful of States while the rest operate to another standard, which would distort competition;
  • it fixes a certain date from which obligations, surveys and certificates run, so Administrations, recognised organisations, owners and yards all work to one deadline;
  • it provides the interval in which flag States legislate, class develops rules, and industry builds or retrofits;
  • it gives legitimacy - a convention in force across most world tonnage can be enforced through port State control against ships of non-parties, which is what makes no more favourable treatment work.

5. Conventions of an important technical nature

For a convention of important technical nature - one dictating construction, equipment or operation - States observe a stiffer double condition: a number of States and a percentage of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping. A count of States alone could be satisfied by many small registries while the major fleets stayed outside, leaving a convention in force on paper and irrelevant at sea.

  • SOLAS 1974 - adopted 1 November 1974, requiring not less than 25 States whose combined merchant fleets constitute not less than 50 per cent of the gross tonnage of the world's merchant shipping; in force 25 May 1980.
  • The 1978 MARPOL Protocol - in force twelve months after not less than fifteen States constituting not less than fifty per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage had become Parties.

Older conventions typically required two-thirds of contracting States, which occasionally meant more acceptances were needed to bring an instrument into force than to amend it.

This paper supplies its own worked example, and it is a good one because the condition was actually satisfied only months before this examination. The Hong Kong Convention uses a triple condition under Article 17 - 24 months after ratification by 15 States, representing 40 per cent of world merchant shipping gross tonnage, whose combined maximum annual ship recycling volume over the preceding ten years is not less than 3 per cent of their combined tonnage. The third limb is what made it so slow: it required the major recycling States to join, not merely the major fleet States. It entered into force on 26 June 2025.

6. Keeping the convention current once in force

A technical convention is worthless if it cannot keep pace, and explicit acceptance failed on exactly that point: amendments to the 1960 SOLAS Convention adopted between 1966 and 1973 never received enough acceptances and none entered into force.

IMO's answer was the tacit acceptance procedure, used in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL, the FAL Convention and the collision regulations. It reverses the default: an amendment to the technical annexes is deemed accepted on a specified date unless objections are received beforehand from an agreed number of Parties. For SOLAS 1974 an amendment is deemed accepted at the end of two years from communication to Contracting Governments, unless objected to by more than one-third of Contracting Governments, or by Governments whose combined fleets are not less than 50 per cent of world gross merchant tonnage. Amendments now enter into force typically within 18 to 24 months.

The procedure can be quoted exactly, from an instrument that took effect earlier in the year of this examination. Resolution FAL.15(47), adopted 17 March 2023 under FAL Convention article VII(2)(a), determines under article VII(2)(b) that the amendments shall enter into force on 1 January 2025 unless, prior to 1 October 2024, at least one third of Contracting Governments have notified the Secretary-General in writing that they do not accept them. No such objection was made and the amendments took effect on 1 January 2025. That is tacit acceptance in operative words, with both of its dates.

7. Adoption is not automatic either

It is worth ending on the point the question begins with, because a case running at the date of this examination proves it. Under MARPOL article 16(2)(a) the Secretary-General circulated the draft revised Annex VI - the IMO Net-Zero Framework - on 11 April 2025, expressly for consideration with a view to adoption at an extraordinary session under article 16(2)(b), (c) and (d). That session, MEPC/ES.2, met from 14 to 17 October 2025 - one month before this paper - and adjourned for twelve months without adopting the Framework. Circulation had occurred; the adoption step had been convened and not taken. A candidate who understands this question can say precisely why that instrument bound nobody at the date of the examination.

Finally, once a convention is in force internationally each flag State must still give it domestic effect. In India at this examination that is the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 and the rules made under it, and it is that domestic instrument which is enforced against the ship.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no limbs but contains four separate demands and then names two terms. The route answers them in the order asked and gives the two named terms a heading of their own, because the examiner has singled them out and an answer that defines them in passing inside a general narrative will not be credited for them. Steps 6 and 7 are the pay-off: a candidate who can name tacit acceptance, quote a real determination with its dates, and then point to an adoption that did not happen has shown he understands the machinery rather than memorised it.

Four words that are not synonyms

Adoption settles the text. Signature may or may not bind, depending on what the treaty says. Ratification, acceptance, approval or accession is the act that binds the State, and it binds on deposit, not on decision at home. Entry into force is when the instrument starts to operate, for those States and thereafter for later parties. Candidates use all four as though they meant the same thing, and the whole question is built on their not meaning the same thing.

This paper answers its own question three times over

Revise this question together with two others on the same paper, because between them they supply every stage of the ladder as a real case. The Hong Kong Convention shows an entry into force condition satisfied - a triple condition, met after sixteen years, in force from 26 June 2025. The FAL amendments show tacit acceptance completed - determined in 2023, unopposed, effective 1 January 2025. And the Net-Zero Framework shows an adoption step convened and not taken, one month before this examination. Abstract machinery in the morning; three live cases on the same question paper.

The same problem exists in domestic law

An Indian Act is passed, then receives assent, then commences on a date appointed by notification - and it is not law until it commences. That is structurally the same three-stage problem as adoption, ratification and entry into force. The example current at this examination is exact: the Merchant Shipping Act, 2025 had received the assent of the President on 18 August 2025, three months before this paper, and had not commenced. It was brought into force only on 15 March 2026. At this sitting the operative statute was still the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 - and a candidate who wrote that the 2025 Act governed would have been wrong for exactly the reason this question is about.

Why tonnage and not just a headcount

The double condition looks like bureaucratic detail and is in fact the most commercially significant sentence in the answer. A safety convention that binds fifty small registries and none of the major fleets creates a competitive distortion rather than a safety improvement: compliant ships carry the cost and non-compliant ships take the trade. Requiring a percentage of world gross tonnage ensures the instrument binds the fleet that actually carries the cargo, which is what then makes port State control and no more favourable treatment effective against the remainder. The Hong Kong Convention goes one better and requires a percentage of world recycling capacity too, because a ship recycling convention that the recycling States had not joined would have regulated nothing.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a convention applies once IMO adopts it. Adoption binds nobody.
  • Treating accession as a lesser status. Once deposited it has identical effect to ratification.
  • Saying ratification is deposit with IMO of a signed convention. It is deposit of a separate formal instrument.
  • Giving the entry into force condition as a number of States only, and omitting the tonnage percentage, on a question that expressly asks about conventions of important technical nature.
  • Describing tacit acceptance as automatic amendment. It is deemed acceptance in the absence of objection by a specified proportion.
  • Stopping at entry into force and never mentioning that the flag State must still give domestic effect - which is what actually binds the ship.

Examiner traps

  • 'State the provision and its importance' - two demands. Describing the provision without saying why it matters loses half of them.
  • 'For a convention of important technical nature' - this signals the double condition of States plus tonnage. It is the single most specific thing the question asks for.
  • 'Explain the terms (i) Accession (ii) Signature subject to Ratification, acceptance or approval' - two terms, expressly numbered. Answer them as such, and contrast them: one is a single step, the other is two.
  • A currency trap in reverse: the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 is easy to reach for as the domestic instrument, and at this examination it had received assent but had not commenced. The answer is the 1958 Act.

15-second recall
Adoption is not entry into force. A convention is adopted by an IMO organ - Assembly, MSC, MEPC or Legal Committee - or by a diplomatic conference. Adoption settles the text only and binds nobody; the text is opened for signature and deposited with the Secretary-General as depositary. Consent to be bound comes by one of three routes: signature alone where the treaty so provides; signature subject to ratification, acceptance or approval, the normal course, where signature does not bind and the State is bound only on depositing a formal instrument; or accession, where the State did not sign in time or was not a negotiating State. (ii) Signature subject to ratification is a two-step route whose purpose is to give the State time to enact domestic legislation and satisfy constitutional requirements; acceptance and approval are the same act under less formal names (VCLT Art 14(2)). (i) Accession is a one-step route - no prior signature, deposit alone (VCLT Art 15) - with identical legal effect. The entry into force provision is a final clause setting a threshold of acceptances plus a waiting period. For conventions of important technical nature a double condition is used - a number of States and a percentage of world merchant gross tonnage - because a headcount alone could be met by small registries while the major fleets stayed out. Q9 Entry into Force of an IMO Convention
16 marks		 Adoption is not entry into force → How a State expresses consent to be bound → The two terms the question names → The entry into force provision, and why it matters → Conventions of an important technical nature → Keeping the convention current once in force → Adoption is not automatic either The entry into force final clause of the convention concerned, read with the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties Articles 14(2) and 15; and for amendment, the tacit acceptance procedure in SOLAS 1974, MARPOL article 16 and FAL Convention article VII(2)(b).
SOLAS 1974: adopted 1 November 1974, in force 25 May 1980; condition 25 States and 50% of world merchant gross tonnage		 Treating adoption, signature, ratification and entry into force as one event. Adoption settles the text and binds nobody; signature ordinarily does not bind either; the State is bound only when it deposits a formal instrument; and the convention operates only when its own entry into force condition is met. The second trap is answering the technical-convention limb with a number of States and omitting the percentage of world merchant gross tonnage, which is the specific thing that limb asks for. The third is a currency trap in reverse: naming the Merchant Shipping Act 2025 as the domestic instrument. It had received assent on 18 August 2025 but did not commence until 15 March 2026, so at this sitting the operative statute was the Merchant Shipping Act 1958 - which is the very distinction this question is about.
