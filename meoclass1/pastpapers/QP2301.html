Understand this first

A propeller does not push water straight backwards. It leaves the water behind it spinning, and that spin is wasted energy - it does nothing to drive the ship, and on a typical single-screw ship it is worth several per cent of the power delivered to the shaft. The rudder is the one large structure that sits directly in that swirling, accelerated stream, so it is the obvious place to try to get some of the waste back. Every device in this question is a different way of doing that. Two of them work on the swirl itself, turning rotation into forward push; one works on the hole in the middle of the stream behind the propeller boss, where a vortex forms and drags; and one puts a second, free-spinning wheel in the flow so that the inner part is driven round like a turbine while the outer part, out in undisturbed water, acts as a propeller. The important habit of mind is scepticism about the numbers: a gain measured in a model basin at one draught and one speed is not a gain on a ship that spends its life part-loaded and slow-steaming.

Model written answer

1. Where the recoverable energy is

A propeller converts shaft power into an accelerated race of water, but it does so imperfectly. Part of the delivered power is left in the race as rotation - the water leaves the disc spinning in the direction of propeller rotation - and on a single-screw ship this rotational loss is commonly of the order of several per cent of delivered power. A second, separate loss forms behind the propeller boss, where the flow closing in behind the hub rolls up into a strong hub vortex that both dissipates energy and drags.

The rudder is the only large appendage standing directly in that accelerated, swirling stream. Every device below is therefore a means of recovering energy already paid for, not of creating thrust from nothing - which is why the honest claims are single-digit percentages and why they cannot simply be added together.

2. (a) Rudder thrust fins

Horizontal fins are fitted to each side of the rudder, within the slipstream. Because the incoming flow is rotating, it meets the fin at an angle of attack, and the fin develops lift in the same way as any hydrofoil. As the fin is horizontal, that lift resolves into a component acting forward along the ship's axis - recovered thrust - together with a small drag penalty. The swirl is in opposite senses above and below the shaft line, so the fins are handed, set at opposite angles port and starboard and above and below. Gains of about 2 to 5 per cent are claimed at the design condition.

3. (b) Asymmetric rudders

For the same reason, a conventional symmetrical rudder is working at an incident angle over most of its span even with the helm amidships: above the shaft the flow arrives from one side, below it from the other. An asymmetric or twisted rudder divides the blade at the shaft line and gives each half its own section, offset or twisted to align with the local flow direction. The result is less flow separation and less drag, materially less rudder cavitation and erosion on the leading edge and in way of the horn gap, and better course-keeping so the ship carries less average helm. Claims run to about 2 to 6 per cent, and on a highly loaded propeller the cavitation and erosion benefit is often worth more than the fuel figure.

4. (c) Rudder bulbs

A rudder bulb is a streamlined body fitted to the rudder leading edge, sized and positioned to line up with the propeller boss so that it effectively continues the hub aft. It fills the low-pressure region behind the boss and so suppresses the hub vortex, removing its drag and allowing the flow to close smoothly. It also increases the effective rudder section near mid-span. The bulb is commonly combined with thrust fins, the bulb carrying the fin roots, and is claimed at about 2 to 5 per cent, more where the propeller is heavily loaded and the hub vortex correspondingly strong.

5. (d) Grim vane wheels

The Grim vane wheel is a freely rotating wheel of larger diameter than the propeller, mounted on its own bearing abaft the propeller and absorbing no shaft power. Its inner span lies within the propeller race and is driven round by it, acting as a turbine; its outer span extends beyond the race into slower water and, being driven round by the inner span, acts as a propeller and produces additional thrust. It is the most complete of these ideas because it attacks both the rotational loss and the axial loss. Claimed gains approached 10 per cent, but service experience was poor: the long, thin blades suffered fatigue cracking and blade loss, with bearing and vibration problems, and very few installations remain.

6. Judging whether a claimed gain is real

Quoted percentages are almost always derived from model tests or CFD at the design draught, trim and speed. Scale effects on the wake field and on boundary-layer thickness tend to flatter the model, and a ship that trades part-laden or slow-steaming is not at the design point. Critically, the gains do not add: a bulb, a set of fins and a twisted section are all competing to recover the same rotational and hub energy. A retrofit case should therefore be built on the ship's actual operating profile and verified afterwards by hull and propeller performance monitoring to ISO 19030, with proper correction for draught, weather and fouling - not accepted from the brochure figure.

Common mistakes

  • Describing an asymmetric rudder as a steering improvement only. Its efficiency benefit is drag and cavitation, and that is what the question wants.
  • Confusing a rudder bulb with a propeller boss cap fin. The bulb is on the rudder; the boss cap fin is on the propeller hub. Both fight the hub vortex, only one is asked for.
  • Saying the vane wheel is driven by the engine. It is free-running and takes no shaft power - that is the entire point of it.
  • Adding the percentages up. Five devices at 4 per cent each is not a 20 per cent saving.
  • Quoting a gain as achieved. They are claims, at design condition.

Examiner traps

  • Grim vane wheels are the trap. The honest answer says the concept is sound and the service record poor. A candidate who presents it as current best practice shows he has read a textbook and not a drydock report.
  • The question says propulsion effectiveness, not fuel saving. Course-keeping and reduced cavitation erosion are legitimate parts of the answer.
  • Being drawn into naming proprietary products. Describe the mechanism; trade names earn nothing.

Likely oral follow-up

  • How would you verify a claimed saving after a retrofit? - ISO 19030, corrected for draught, trim, weather and hull condition, over a long enough baseline.
  • Why are the fins handed? - the swirl reverses either side of the shaft line.
  • What would you inspect on a rudder bulb at drydock? - the weld at the bulb-to-rudder joint, erosion on the leading edge, and clearance to the boss.
  • Why did vane wheels fail? - blade fatigue on a long, unsupported, free-running blade.

Understand this first

Anything that lives in the sea will grow on a ship's hull, and a fouled hull costs a great deal of fuel. The old cure was to put poison in the paint - a compound that leached out steadily and killed whatever tried to settle. It worked extremely well, and that was the problem: the most effective of those poisons did not stop at the hull. It accumulated in harbour sediment and deformed shellfish at concentrations far below anything anyone had thought to measure. So the international response was not to ban fouling control but to ban particular poisons, one at a time, as the evidence against each matured - which is why the regime is a list that grows rather than a principle. That shape explains everything else. Because the ban is on named substances, compliance is proved by paperwork about what is on the hull, not by inspecting the hull. And because the industry still has to stop fouling, the alternatives divide into two families: those that still use a biocide but release it in a cleverer, more controlled way, and those that give up on poison altogether and make the surface too slippery to hold on to.

Model written answer

1. a) The Convention and the harm it addresses

The International Convention on the Control of Harmful Anti-fouling Systems on Ships, 2001 - the AFS Convention - was adopted by IMO in October 2001 and entered into force on 17 September 2008. It exists because the anti-fouling systems that worked best were also environmentally the worst. Organotin compounds, and in particular tributyltin (TBT), gave outstanding fouling control, but they proved persistent and bioaccumulative, concentrating in harbour and estuarine sediment and causing documented harm to non-target organisms - notably shell deformation in oysters and imposex in marine gastropods - at concentrations far below those previously regarded as significant.

The Convention's architecture follows from that. It does not regulate fouling control in general and it sets no performance standard. It prohibits named substances listed in Annex 1, and it proves compliance through the survey and certification regime in Annex 4. Because the control is on substances rather than on outcomes, the list is designed to grow as the evidence against a further substance matures - and that is exactly what has just happened.

2. a) The controls in force at this sitting

Organotins. Anti-fouling systems containing organotin compounds acting as biocides could not be applied to any ship from 1 January 2003. From 1 January 2008 ships either had to have such systems removed, or had to carry a sealer or barrier coat forming a barrier preventing the underlying organotin from leaching out.

Cybutryne - and this is the live point at this examination. Resolution MEPC.331(76), adopted on 17 June 2021, amended Annex 1 to add cybutryne, and it entered into force on 1 January 2023. This paper is sat in that same month, so the controls are days old. From that date no anti-fouling system containing cybutryne may be applied or re-applied. Ships already carrying one must have it removed, or over-coated with a barrier coat, by the next scheduled renewal of the anti-fouling system and in any event within 60 months of the last application - a period that has only just begun to run. There is a corresponding transitional arrangement for the certificate: the International Anti-fouling System Certificate was amended in form, and a ship may still legitimately be holding the earlier form at this date.

3. a) Documents and records demonstrating compliance

Compliance under this Convention is demonstrated on paper, because what is controlled is what was applied, and that cannot be read off a hull afloat. The following are carried:

  • International Anti-fouling System Certificate (IAFS Certificate) - required for ships of 400 GT and above engaged in international voyages, issued after survey. It has no expiry date in the ordinary sense; it is reissued whenever the system is changed.
  • Record of Anti-fouling Systems - attached to and forming part of the certificate. It records the system applied, the date of application, the name of the applying company and facility, and the name of the manufacturer.
  • Declaration on Anti-fouling System - for ships of 24 m or more in length but under 400 GT on international voyages. Signed by the owner or authorised agent and accompanied by supporting documentation such as a paint receipt or contractor invoice.
  • Manufacturer's technical data sheets and product declarations for the coating actually applied, identifying the biocide content.
  • Paint receipts, invoices and the applicator's or shipyard's records, together with the drydock specification and report - the evidential trail behind the declaration.
  • Where a previous organotin or cybutryne system has been sealed rather than removed, the record of the barrier coat applied.

Survey is carried out on initial application of the system and again on any change or replacement. The relevant IMO guidance current at this sitting is resolution MEPC.358(78), the 2022 Guidelines for survey and certification of anti-fouling systems on ships.

4. c) Controlled depletion polymer (CDP)

CDP coatings are the direct descendant of traditional rosin-based soluble matrix paints. The binder is a rosin system that is slightly soluble in seawater; as it dissolves it exposes and releases the biocide dispersed within it. The weakness is that dissolution is not linear: as the surface depletes, a leached layer of spent binder builds up, through which both seawater and biocide must diffuse, so the release rate falls away with time and the coating becomes progressively rougher. CDP is cheap, tolerant and simple to apply, and it suits shorter intervals of the order of 36 months and lower-activity or slower ships, but its protection is weakest at the end of the docking interval, which is when it is most needed.

5. c) Self-polishing copolymer (SPC)

In an SPC the biocide is chemically bonded into the polymer backbone rather than merely dispersed in it. Seawater hydrolyses the outermost molecular layer at a controlled rate, releasing the biocide and simultaneously making that layer soluble so it is washed away by the passing flow. Two consequences follow, and they are the reason SPC dominates deep-sea trading. First, release is near-linear and predictable over the whole life, because a fresh surface is continuously exposed and no thick leached layer forms. Second, the coating polishes smoother in service, so hull roughness improves rather than degrades. Tin-free chemistries in use are copper acrylate, silyl acrylate and zinc acrylate. SPC supports intervals up to about 60 months and high-activity ships, at a higher price and with a greater sensitivity to correct surface preparation.

6. c) Hybrid TBT-free paints

Hybrids blend rosin and copolymer chemistry in one film, and sit deliberately between the two. They polish more predictably than a pure CDP and cost less than a full SPC, giving useful performance over an intermediate interval, broadly 36 to 60 months. They are the commercial compromise chosen where the trading pattern does not justify an SPC but a straight CDP would not last the docking cycle.

7. c) Fouling release coatings

Fouling release coatings abandon biocide altogether and work by physics rather than chemistry. A silicone or fluoropolymer elastomer gives a surface of very low surface energy and very low micro-roughness, to which fouling organisms cannot form a strong bond; what does settle is sheared off by the water flow once the ship is at speed. The advantages are real: no biocide is released at all, and the surface is exceptionally smooth, so the fuel benefit can exceed that of a biocidal system. The limitations are equally real. Performance depends on sustained speed and high activity, so a ship that lies idle or trades slowly will foul; the film is soft and easily damaged by ice, tugs and mechanical cleaning; it is expensive; and it demands a tie-coat and careful surface preparation, with repair at a later drydocking being difficult.

8. Choosing between them

The choice is a commercial and operational judgement, not a technical preference. The determining factors are the trading pattern and service speed, the proportion of time spent idle or at anchor, the intended drydock interval, whether the work is a newbuilding or a retrofit, and the cost of removing the existing system. A high-speed liner in continuous service is the classic case for fouling release; a deep-sea bulk carrier on a five-year cycle is the classic case for SPC; a coastal or lower-activity ship on a shorter cycle may be properly served by CDP or a hybrid. The pressure on that decision has increased sharply, because hull smoothness now bears directly on the efficiency requirements that took effect at the start of this month, and a coating decision has become a compliance decision as well as a fuel one.

The idea that makes the documents list memorable

The Convention bans named substances, not fouling. The two-family map for limb c)

Four names, two families. CDP, SPC and hybrid all still use a biocide and differ only in how cleverly they control its release - dissolving matrix, controlled hydrolysis, or a blend. Fouling release uses no biocide at all and works by being too slippery to grip. Say which family each belongs to and the comparison at the end becomes obvious.

Common mistakes

  • Writing that the Convention 'bans TBT' and stopping. It prohibits organotins acting as biocides, and it now prohibits cybutryne too - and the second one is the topical part.
  • Forgetting that a prohibited system may be sealed under a barrier coat rather than removed. That option is on the face of the Convention.
  • Giving only the certificate. The Record of Anti-fouling Systems and, for smaller ships, the Declaration, are separate documents and separate marks.
  • Describing SPC as 'dissolving'. It hydrolyses - the biocide is chemically bound into the backbone, and that is precisely what makes release linear.
  • Presenting fouling release as simply better. It fails on a ship that sits idle.

Understand this first

A ship at sea puts three quite different things at risk at the same moment, and no single policy sensibly covers all three. There is the ship herself, which the owner has paid for and can lose. There is what she is carrying, which usually belongs to somebody else entirely. And there is the harm she can do - to another ship, to a berth, to the sea, to the people aboard - which has no upper limit fixed by the value of anything. Marine insurance is organised around that split, and once you see it the list of policy types stops being a list to memorise. The first two are property risks: something you own has a value, and you insure that value, so the cover is capped by it. The third is a liability risk, where the loss is whatever a court or a convention says it is, and that is why it is handled quite differently - not by a commercial underwriter selling a policy for a premium, but by shipowners pooling their exposures in a mutual club. The exclusions follow the same logic: an insurer will carry the ordinary hazards of the sea, but not the owner's own neglect, not the certainties of trade like wear and tear, and not the catastrophic political risks that no ordinary premium could fund.

Model written answer

1. How marine insurance divides

A marine adventure puts four distinct things at risk, and the market is organised around them: the ship, the cargo, the freight or earnings, and the shipowner's liability to third parties. The first three are property risks - something identifiable has a value and the cover is capped by it. The fourth is a liability risk, where the size of the loss is fixed by law rather than by the value of any asset, and that difference is why liability is not sold as an ordinary policy at all but pooled mutually.

In India the contract is governed by the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, which follows the English Marine Insurance Act 1906 closely. Two of its rules underlie everything below: the assured must have an insurable interest in the subject matter, failing which the contract is a wager and void; and the contract is one of the utmost good faith, so that material non-disclosure entitles the insurer to avoid it.

2. Hull and Machinery

Who takes it. The shipowner, or a demise charterer who has the ship in his possession. What it is. Cover on the vessel, her machinery and equipment, usually as a time policy on the Institute Time Clauses (Hulls), and almost always a valued policy.

Salient liabilities of the insurer. Total loss, actual or constructive; partial loss and particular average damage; the ship's contribution in general average and salvage; sue and labour expenses incurred to avert a loss; and three-fourths of the collision liability to the other ship under the running-down clause. The perils insured include perils of the sea, fire, explosion, violent theft, jettison, piracy, and - under the Inchmaree clause - latent defect in hull or machinery and the negligence of master, officers, crew or repairers, provided the loss is not caused by want of due diligence by the assured.

Salient exclusions. Ordinary wear and tear and gradual deterioration; inherent vice; the cost of correcting the defective part itself, as opposed to the damage it causes; want of due diligence by the assured, owners or managers; wilful misconduct; delay; and war, strikes, terrorism and nuclear perils, which are excluded by the standard clauses and bought back separately. The remaining one-fourth of collision liability is not covered here and falls to the P&I club.

3. Cargo insurance

Who takes it. The shipper, consignee or buyer - whoever carries the risk under the sale contract, which the Incoterm decides. What it is. Normally a valued policy written at CIF value plus a percentage for expected profit, on the Institute Cargo Clauses in one of three grades: Clauses A, an all-risks cover; Clauses B; and Clauses C, each a narrower list of named perils.

Salient liabilities of the insurer. Physical loss of or damage to the goods from the covered perils during the transit described, together with general average and salvage contributions and the costs of forwarding after an interruption. Cover attaches on a warehouse-to-warehouse basis. On payment the insurer takes subrogated rights and pursues the carrier.

Salient exclusions. Insufficient or unsuitable packing; inherent vice or the ordinary nature of the goods; ordinary leakage, loss in weight and wear and tear; wilful misconduct of the assured; delay, even where caused by an insured peril; insolvency of the carrier; and unseaworthiness where the assured is privy to it. War and strikes are again excluded and separately written back.

4. Freight, and loss of hire

Freight is insurable where it is at risk - that is, payable only on delivery, so that the carrier loses it if the cargo is lost. Loss of hire, taken by the owner, indemnifies the earnings lost while the ship is off-hire under repair following a casualty that is itself recoverable under the hull policy. Both are covers on consequential earnings rather than on property, both are subject to a deductible expressed in days and a maximum indemnity period, and both fail if the underlying casualty is not itself an insured peril. Neither responds to commercial loss of employment or to a fall in the freight market.

5. Protection and Indemnity

Who takes it. The shipowner, and also the demise or time charterer through charterers' cover. What it is. Not a commercial policy but a mutual: the members of a P&I club are simultaneously the assureds and the insurers, contributing by advance and supplementary calls rather than paying a fixed premium, and cover is defined by the club rules rather than by a policy schedule.

Salient liabilities of the club. The third-party exposures the hull policy will not touch: crew injury, illness, death and repatriation; passenger claims; cargo claims brought against the ship; pollution; wreck removal; damage to fixed and floating objects such as berths and buoys; fines; stowaways; and the one-fourth collision liability left by the hull policy. Large claims are shared through the International Group pooling agreement and its market reinsurance.

Salient exclusions. The pay-to-be-paid rule, requiring the member to have discharged the liability before he can recover; liabilities arising from the member's wilful misconduct or from operating outside the club rules or the terms of entry; claims properly falling under the hull policy; war risks beyond the club's written-back limits; and liabilities the member has voluntarily assumed under contract without the club's agreement.

6. The exclusions that run across every policy

Certain exclusions recur in every class above, and it is worth stating them as a group because they express a single principle - an insurer carries fortuity, not certainty and not the assured's own fault:

  • Wilful misconduct of the assured - the insurer is never liable for it.
  • Ordinary wear and tear, ordinary leakage and breakage, and inherent vice - these are certainties of trade, not accidents.
  • Delay, even when proximately caused by an insured peril.
  • Unseaworthiness and want of due diligence where the assured is privy to it.
  • War, strikes, riots, terrorism and nuclear risks - excluded as standard, then bought back by separate clauses at separate premium.
  • Breach of warranty, and avoidance for material non-disclosure under the duty of utmost good faith.

7. The agencies in India which deal with marine insurance

The Indian market has a statutory regulator, a body of insurers, a national reinsurer and a professional layer, and it is best given in that order:

  • Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) - the statutory regulator, constituted under the IRDA Act, 1999. It licenses insurers, brokers, surveyors and loss assessors, and regulates products, solvency and market conduct.
  • The public sector general insurers - The New India Assurance Company, United India Insurance, National Insurance and The Oriental Insurance Company - which write the bulk of Indian hull and cargo business.
  • General Insurance Corporation of India (GIC Re) - the national reinsurer, through which a share of Indian marine business is reinsured.
  • Private general insurers licensed by IRDAI, which since the opening of the sector compete for the same hull and cargo business.
  • General Insurance Council - the representative body of the general insurance industry.
  • P&I cover is not written domestically as a mutual: Indian owners enter their ships in the International Group clubs, with local correspondents appointed to handle claims in Indian ports.
  • The professional layer - average adjusters, who state the general average adjustment, and IRDAI-licensed surveyors and loss assessors, together with salvage and cargo surveyors, who establish cause and quantum.

The idea that generates the whole taxonomy

Three things are at risk and they are different in kind: the ship you own, the cargo somebody else owns, and the harm you can do. The first two are property, so the cover is capped by a value. The third has no cap fixed by any asset, which is exactly why it is handled by a mutual club rather than sold for a premium. Freight is the fourth, and it is simply the earnings that ride on the other three.

Getting the collision split right

The single most quoted fact in this area, and the one most often garbled: the hull policy carries three-fourths of the collision liability to the other vessel, and the remaining one-fourth falls on the P&I club. It is a historical division designed to leave the owner with an interest in defending the claim.

Common mistakes

  • Listing the types and then giving one generic paragraph of exclusions. The question says related with each case.
  • Treating P&I as a policy. It is a mutual: calls, not premium; rules, not schedule; members, not policyholders.
  • Forgetting freight altogether. It is a distinct insurable interest and it is easy marks.
  • Saying war risks are simply 'not covered'. They are excluded and then written back under separate clauses at separate premium.
  • For the Indian limb, naming only one company. The question says agencies, plural, and the regulator and the reinsurer are the parts that show real knowledge.

Examiner traps

  • The Indian limb is the discriminator. Most candidates write a good answer on cover and then produce nothing for the third sentence. Understand this first

Both halves of this question are about who bears a burden nobody wants. In the first half, a salvor is asked to go and help a casualty that is threatening to pollute. Under the old rule he was paid only if he saved property, so if the ship sank anyway he got nothing - which is precisely the case where society most wants him to try. A special payment was invented to cure that, but it was written so vaguely that salvors spent years in arbitration arguing about what it was worth, and a salvor who cannot predict his money and cannot get security will not sail. SCOPIC is the industry's answer: a pre-agreed price list the salvor can switch on himself, backed by security from the owner's liability insurer. In the second half, two ships collide far from anyone's coast, and the question is which country's officials may act. The instinct is to say the country of the other ship, or of the nearest coast, and that instinct is wrong. The law deliberately confines the power to punish to the ship's own flag State or the person's own country, and confines the power to take away a certificate to the State that issued it - because a mariner should not be exposed to the courts of every State whose vessel he might meet. Compensation is different in kind: nobody is being punished, so those protections simply do not apply.

Model written answer

1. a) The problem SCOPIC was written to cure

Salvage at common law and under Article 13 of the International Convention on Salvage, 1989 is 'no cure, no pay': the reward is fixed by reference to the salved fund and cannot exceed it, so a salvor who saves nothing earns nothing. That rule breaks down exactly where society most needs a salvor - a laden tanker on a lee shore, where the property may well be lost but the pollution averted is enormous.

Article 14 was written to bridge the gap. Where the vessel or her cargo threatened damage to the environment and the salvor earned no, or an insufficient, Article 13 reward, he could recover special compensation equal to his expenses, increased by up to 30 per cent - exceptionally 100 per cent - where he prevented or minimised environmental damage. In practice it failed the salvor on four counts. He had to prove the environmental threat and that his services met the test; the meaning of the 'fair rate' for equipment and personnel was construed as expenses without any profit element, so the tariff rewarded him at cost; the assessment came long afterwards, in arbitration, which was slow and expensive; and security was slow to arrive, leaving him funding a costly operation from his own resources. A salvor who cannot predict his income and cannot fund his fleet does not sail.

2. a) What SCOPIC is

The Special Compensation P&I Clause (SCOPIC) is a supplementary clause incorporated into Lloyd's Open Form, agreed between the International Salvage Union, the International Group of P&I Clubs and property underwriters. It was first issued in 1999 and has been revised periodically; SCOPIC 2020, effective 1 January 2020, is the current version at this examination. It does not amend the Convention - it is a contractual substitute for Article 14 between the parties who sign it.

Its mechanism is as follows. The salvor may invoke SCOPIC by written notice at any time of his choosing, and - critically - irrespective of whether there is any threat to the environment and irrespective of where the casualty lies. From the moment of invocation, Article 14 is replaced and remuneration is assessed on the SCOPIC tariff: published day rates for personnel, tugs, craft and equipment, plus a bonus of 25 per cent on that tariff. The shipowner must provide initial security in a standard sum within two working days, and if he fails to do so the salvor may withdraw from SCOPIC. The owners of the vessel may then, at their sole option, appoint a Special Casualty Representative (SCR) to attend the salvage operation, monitor it and report; and the hull and machinery underwriter and one cargo owner or underwriter may each appoint a Special Representative, at their own expense, to observe and report. Both parties have defined termination rights on notice.

The salvor pays for the option. If the Article 13 award ultimately exceeds the SCOPIC remuneration, his Article 13 award is discounted by 25 per cent of the difference. SCOPIC is therefore a safety net that costs something to hold, which is what stops it being invoked reflexively on every service.

3. a) How it answers each of the salvor's difficulties

  • Uncertainty of amount - replaced by a published tariff agreed in advance, rather than an arbitrator's later assessment of a 'fair rate'.
  • Proving entitlement - removed entirely. No environmental threat need be shown, and the geographical restriction of Article 14 is irrelevant.
  • No profit - cured by the 25 per cent bonus on tariff rates, which the Article 14 'fair rate' was held not to include.
  • Slow or absent security - cured by security within two working days, from the P&I club: a solvent, identified and readily traceable payer.
  • Cash flow - interim payments against the tariff let the salvor fund an operation while it is still running.
  • Delay and litigation - reduced by an agreed rate, an on-scene SCR whose reports become the evidential record, and clear termination rights.

4. b) The governing principle - Article 97 of UNCLOS

A collision on the high seas occurs outside the territorial jurisdiction of any State, so the question is not which coast is nearest but which State the law permits to act. Article 94 of UNCLOS places the general duty of jurisdiction and control in administrative, technical and social matters on the flag State. Article 97 then deals specifically with penal jurisdiction in matters of collision, and it deliberately narrows the field. It reverses the position reached in the Lotus case, where a State whose vessel was struck was held entitled to prosecute the other ship's officer, and it follows the Brussels Convention of 1952 on penal jurisdiction in matters of collision. Note at the outset that Article 97 is about punishment: it says nothing about who pays.

5. b) i) Disciplinary action against the master or crew

Article 97(1) provides that where a collision or other incident of navigation on the high seas engages the penal or disciplinary responsibility of the master or of any other person in the service of the ship, proceedings may be instituted only before the judicial or administrative authorities of either:

  • the flag State of the ship concerned; or
  • the State of which the person is a national.

No other State has the right, and the responsibility for taking such action accordingly rests with those two. The justification is practical and protective: a seafarer navigating international waters would otherwise be exposed to the criminal process of every State whose vessel he might encounter, which would make service at sea intolerable and would let a State with an interest in the outcome sit in judgment on the other ship's officers. Article 97(3) reinforces it from the other side: no arrest or detention of the ship, even as a measure of investigation, may be ordered by any authorities other than those of the flag State.

6. b) ii) Cancellation of a certificate of competency

This is narrower still, and it is the discriminating part of the question. Under Article 97(2), in disciplinary matters the State which issued the certificate of competency or licence is alone competent, after due legal process, to pronounce its withdrawal - and it may do so even if the holder is not one of its nationals.

So the two heads do not run together. A State of nationality may institute disciplinary proceedings under 97(1), but it cannot cancel a certificate issued by another State. The reason is simply that the certificate is the issuing Administration's own instrument: it attests to that Administration's satisfaction as to competence, and only the body that gave it can take it away. The scheme is consistent with STCW, under which certificates are issued and endorsed by, and remain under the control of, the issuing Administration.

7. b) iii) Payment of compensation

Compensation is a civil matter, and Article 97 does not apply to it at all - nobody is being punished, so the protections above are simply not engaged. The applicable convention is the Collision Convention 1910 (the Brussels Convention for the Unification of Certain Rules of Law with respect to Collisions between Vessels), whose salient points are:

  • Where the collision is caused by the fault of one vessel, that vessel bears the loss.
  • Where both are at fault, liability is apportioned in proportion to the degree of fault of each.
  • Where the degrees of fault cannot be established, or appear equal, liability is apportioned equally.
  • There is no legal presumption of fault merely from a breach of the collision regulations - fault must be proved.
  • Liability for loss of life and personal injury is joint and several as against third parties, with a right of recourse between the ships.
  • Where the collision is accidental, or caused by force majeure, or its cause is left in doubt, the damages are borne by those who suffered them.

Which forum hears the civil claim is governed by the Brussels Convention of 1952 on civil jurisdiction in matters of collision, which gives the claimant a choice: the court where the defendant resides or has a place of business, the court where the ship has been arrested or security given, or the court of the place of collision where that occurred in ports or inland waters. The shipowner may then limit his liability under the LLMC, given effect in India through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, and the claim is answered in practice by his P&I club, with three-fourths of the collision liability to the other ship falling on the hull underwriter.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The examiner has printed three numbered heads in limb b), so three of the marks are pre-allocated and the order is fixed. The mistake is answering all three with one sentence about the flag State. They have three different answers - two States, then one State, then a different regime altogether - and the marks are in seeing that. Adding a short principle step before them costs four lines and makes all three explicable.

The thread that ties the two limbs together

Both halves are about allocating an unwanted burden. SCOPIC allocates the cost of responding to a casualty nobody wants to attend, by guaranteeing the salvor a price. Article 97 allocates the power to punish after a casualty nobody wants to answer for, by confining it to the States with a legitimate connection. Saying so in one sentence makes the answer read as one question rather than two.

The three heads, in one line each

  • i) Punish - flag State or State of nationality. Two options.
  • ii) Cancel the COC - the issuing State alone. One option, and not necessarily either of the first two.
  • iii) Compensation - Article 97 is irrelevant; it is civil, under the Collision Convention 1910, with forum under Brussels 1952 and a cap under the LLMC.

Common mistakes

  • Answering all three heads with 'the flag State'. Only head i) has the flag State as one of two answers; head ii) is the issuing State; head iii) is not an Article 97 question at all.
  • Saying the coastal State of the nearest coast has jurisdiction. On the high seas it has none.
  • Describing SCOPIC as an amendment to the Salvage Convention. It is a contractual clause supplementing LOF; the Convention is untouched.
  • Forgetting the 25 per cent discount on the Article 13 award. It is what makes SCOPIC a considered option rather than a free one.
  • Saying SCOPIC requires an environmental threat. Removing that requirement is one of its principal purposes.

Examiner traps

  • Head ii) is the trap. The issuing State is 'alone competent', and may act even against a non-national. Candidates who have learned '97 means flag State' lose this mark every time.
  • No presumption of fault from breach of the collision regulations under the 1910 Convention. Many candidates assert the opposite.
  • The 1910 Convention apportions in proportion to fault, and only where that cannot be established does it divide equally. The old 'equal division' rule is the thing it replaced.
  • Article 97(3) - no arrest even for investigation other than by the flag State. Easy extra mark on head i).

Likely oral follow-up

  • Who actually pays SCOPIC? - the shipowner's P&I club; it is a liability, not a property, exposure, which is why property underwriters were content with the arrangement.
  • What does the SCR do? - the Special Casualty Representative, appointed at the owner's option, attends on scene, monitors and reports; his reports become the agreed record of what was done.
  • Why does the salvor accept a 25 per cent discount? - it is the price of holding an option he can exercise at any moment without proving anything.
  • Which case narrowed the Article 14 'fair rate'? - the House of Lords decision on the Nagasaki Spirit, holding it covered expenses without a profit element.
  • Can the coastal State intervene at all? - not under Article 97, but the Intervention Convention 1969 allows measures on the high seas to prevent pollution threatening its coast.

Understand this first

Limitation of liability is a bargain, not a defence. The shipowner is not saying he did nothing wrong; he accepts that he is liable and says that the total he must pay for one occurrence is capped by a figure worked out from the size of his ship, however large the actual loss turns out to be. Why should the law allow that? Because a ship can cause harm out of all proportion to what she is worth - one collision can destroy a berth, a cargo, a coastline and many lives - and if that exposure were open-ended nobody could insure it at a price anyone could pay, so ships would not trade. The cap buys insurability. What the claimant gets in return is that the cap is almost automatic: the owner does not have to prove he was careful to rely on it, and it applies whatever the legal basis of the claim. So the whole Convention is a trade - near-certainty for the owner, in exchange for a fund the claimant can reach without a fight. The four terms in the second limb are best learned as the vocabulary of that trade: who is attacking the cap, who is relying on it, the old and easier way of breaking it, and what happens when it breaks.

Model written answer

1. a) What the Convention is, and the bargain it strikes

The Convention on Limitation of Liability for Maritime Claims, 1976 - the LLMC - was adopted on 19 November 1976 and entered into force on 1 December 1986. Its 1996 Protocol, in force 13 May 2004, substantially raised the limits and introduced a tacit acceptance procedure for raising them again; amendments adopted under that procedure in 2012 took effect on 8 June 2015. India gives effect to the regime through Part XA of the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958, which is the statute in force at this examination.

Its purpose is to allow a shipowner to limit his total liability arising out of a single occurrence to a fund calculated by reference to the tonnage of the ship, irrespective of the actual size of the loss. The policy is commercial rather than moral. A ship can cause harm out of all proportion to her own value, and if that exposure were open-ended it would be uninsurable at any acceptable premium, so the trade would not be carried on. The Convention therefore buys certainty and insurability for the owner; and in exchange it gives the claimant a fund that is almost unbreakable but almost automatic, because by Article 2 the listed claims are subject to limitation 'whatever the basis of liability may be'. The owner need not prove absence of fault in order to rely on it.

2. a) The two heads of claim under Article 6

Article 6 sets the general limits, and it does so under two separate heads, each with its own fund calculated on tonnage:

  • Claims for loss of life or personal injury - the larger of the two funds, reflecting the priority the Convention gives to human loss.
  • Any other claims - in practice damage to property, wreck and cargo claims, and consequential loss.

The two funds are distinct and are not interchangeable, but they are linked in one direction: where the life and personal injury fund is insufficient to meet those claims in full, the unsatisfied balance ranks rateably with the property claims against the other fund. Note that passenger claims are not one of the two heads; they have their own separate limit under Article 7, calculated by reference to the number of passengers the ship is certified to carry. Stating that distinction is what separates a precise answer from an approximate one.

3. a) Who may limit, and what cannot be limited at all

Article 1 defines the persons entitled to limit, and it is considerably wider than 'the owner':

  • Shipowners - and 'shipowner' is defined to mean the owner, charterer, manager and operator of a seagoing ship.
  • Salvors - any person rendering services in direct connexion with salvage operations, including wreck removal and pollution response.
  • Any person for whose act, neglect or default the shipowner or salvor is responsible - so master and crew are covered.
  • The insurer of liability for claims subject to limitation, entitled to the benefits of the Convention to the same extent as the assured.

Against that, Article 3 lists claims excepted from limitation altogether: claims for salvage and for contribution in general average; oil pollution claims falling within the CLC; nuclear damage claims; and claims by servants of the shipowner or salvor whose duties are connected with the ship, where the governing law does not allow limitation. These are outside the fund entirely, and they are a favourite examination point because they are counter-intuitive - a salvor may limit his own liability, but a claim against an owner for salvage cannot be limited.

4. b) Plaintiff and Defendant

The plaintiff is the party who brings the action - the claimant who has suffered the loss and is seeking to recover it, and therefore the party attacking the limit. The defendant is the party against whom the action is brought - here the shipowner or other person entitled to limit, who invokes the limitation and constitutes the fund. In a limitation action the procedural roles may appear inverted, because the shipowner often moves first to have the limit declared; but the substantive position is unchanged, and the burden of breaking the limit rests on the claimant.

5. b) Privity - and why it is the old test

Privity means knowledge and concurrence: an owner is privy to a fault where he knew of it and assented to it, expressly or by turning a blind eye. Its importance is historical, and saying so is the mark of a good answer. Under the regime that preceded the LLMC, an owner lost his right to limit if the loss occurred with his 'actual fault or privity', and - critically - the burden lay on the owner to prove that it did not. That made limitation genuinely contestable.

Article 4 of the LLMC replaced that test. A person is not entitled to limit only if the loss resulted from his personal act or omission, committed either with intent to cause such loss, or recklessly and with knowledge that such loss would probably result. Two things changed at once: the burden moved to the claimant, and the standard rose from negligence-with-knowledge to something close to intention. That is why the modern limit is described as almost unbreakable, and why 'privity' is now the vocabulary of the old law rather than the test to apply.

6. b) Unlimited liability

Unlimited liability means that the defendant's liability is not capped by any fund: he must meet the claims in full, however large. It arises by three routes:

  • The Article 4 conduct is proved - the personal act or omission with intent, or recklessly and with knowledge that the loss would probably result. Rare, and deliberately so.
  • The claim is one of the Article 3 excepted claims - salvage, general average contribution, CLC oil pollution, nuclear damage - which fall outside the Convention altogether, so there is no fund to limit to.
  • The forum State is not party to the Convention, or the person entitled fails to constitute the fund or is otherwise unable to invoke the right.

The practical consequence is severe and worth stating: once liability is unlimited the exposure is effectively uninsurable, the P&I club's cover may be prejudiced where the conduct was the member's own, and the loss falls on the owner personally. That is precisely the outcome the bargain in step 1 exists to avoid.

Saying that in one sentence converts a glossary into an argument.

Getting the two heads right

The commonest error is to give the two heads as property and passengers. They are loss of life or personal injury and any other claims. Passengers are a third, separate limit under Article 7, computed on the certified passenger number. Add the linkage - an unsatisfied life claim ranks rateably against the property fund - and the step is complete.

Common mistakes

  • Describing limitation as a defence to liability. It is a cap on quantum; liability is admitted or established first.
  • Limiting 'shipowner' to the registered owner. Article 1 includes the charterer, manager and operator, and separately the salvor and the liability insurer.
  • Giving privity as the current test. It is the pre-1976 test; Article 4 governs now.
  • Forgetting that salvage and general average contribution cannot be limited. It is counter-intuitive and it is examined.

Understand this first

General average rests on an old and rather fair idea: when a ship is in danger, everything aboard is in danger together, and if something has to be deliberately given up to save the whole, the loss should not fall by accident on whoever happened to own it. If the master throws one merchant's cargo overboard to lighten a grounded ship, that merchant has been singled out by chance in an emergency that threatened everybody - so everyone whose property was saved pays a share in proportion to what they saved. The word to hold on to is deliberate. Damage the sea does to you is your own misfortune, and your own insurer's problem. Damage you choose to accept, knowingly, to save the whole adventure is shared. That single distinction decides the second half of this question. Working an engine hard enough to burn its bearings is normally the last thing anyone would choose - but if the ship is aground and in danger, and the master runs her ahead and astern knowing he may wreck the machinery and doing it anyway to get her off, then the damage is not an accident. It is a sacrifice, and it is shared.

Model written answer

1. a) What a general average act is

The classical definition is in Rule A of the York-Antwerp Rules: there is a general average act when, and only when, any extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure is intentionally and reasonably made or incurred for the common safety, for the purpose of preserving from peril the property involved in a common maritime adventure.

Two preliminary points frame the whole answer. First, the York-Antwerp Rules are not legislation: they are a codification maintained by the Comité Maritime International and they bind because they are incorporated by contract into the bill of lading or charterparty. The 2016 Rules are the current CMI text at this examination, though a contract may incorporate an earlier set. Second, the underlying principle is also recognised in Indian law through the Marine Insurance Act, 1963, and the shipping statute in force is the Merchant Shipping Act, 1958.

2. a) The essential features

The statement in the question is correct, and the features are these - each of them necessary, so that the failure of any one defeats the claim:

  • A common danger. The peril must threaten the whole adventure - ship, cargo and freight together - and it must be real and substantial, though it need not be immediate. A danger apprehended only in the mind of the master is not enough.
  • An extraordinary sacrifice or expenditure. It must be outside the ordinary performance of the contract of carriage. Fuel burned on the ordinary voyage is not general average; fuel burned in a deliberate refloating attempt may be.
  • Intentional and voluntary. This is the heart of it. The act must be a deliberate choice by the master or those in charge. Damage that simply happens to the ship is not a sacrifice.
  • Reasonable. Both the decision to act and the manner of acting must be reasonable in the circumstances as they appeared at the time - reinforced by the Rule Paramount.
  • For the common safety. The purpose must be to save the whole adventure. An act done to save only the ship, or only one parcel of cargo, is not general average.
  • Success, and a saved fund. There must be something preserved to contribute. If all is lost there is no fund and no contribution, however meritorious the attempt.

3. a) The lettered rules that qualify the definition

Rule A does not stand alone, and the qualifying rules are where marks are won:

  • Rule of Interpretation - the Rules are applied to the exclusion of any inconsistent law or practice, and the numbered Rules prevail over the lettered Rules where they conflict. This matters directly in limb b).
  • Rule Paramount - no sacrifice or expenditure is allowed unless reasonably made or incurred. It overrides everything, including a numbered rule.
  • Rule C - only loss, damage or expense that is a direct consequence of the general average act is allowed. Loss from delay, whether demurrage or loss of market, and any indirect loss, is excluded.
  • Rule D - rights to contribution are not affected even though the event giving rise to the sacrifice may have been due to the fault of one of the parties; but this shall not prejudice any remedies or defences which may be open against or to that party in respect of such fault. So the adjustment proceeds and the fault is litigated separately - and note the rule cuts both ways: it preserves the faulty party's defences as well as the others' remedies, which is the residue the New Jason Clause was written to close.
  • Rule E - the onus of proof is on the party claiming, who must also give notice to the average adjuster within the stated period.

4. a) How the loss is shared, and what general average is not

An average adjuster is appointed, usually by the shipowner, and states the adjustment. Contribution is rateable on the saved values of ship, cargo and freight at the time and place where the adventure ends, so each interest pays in proportion to what it saved. Before cargo is released the shipowner takes security: an average bond from the receiver and an average guarantee from the cargo underwriter, or a cash deposit.

It is worth saying what general average is not. Particular average is accidental partial loss - it is not deliberate, it is not shared, and it lies where it falls, on the owner of the property damaged and his own insurer. Salvage is a reward to a third party for services rendered, though salvage paid for the common benefit is itself brought into the general average adjustment. Note also that a claim for contribution in general average cannot be limited under the LLMC - it is an excepted claim.

5. b) The rule that governs the refloating scenario

The scenario is governed by Rule VII - Damage to Machinery and Boilers, and because it is a numbered rule it prevails over the lettered rules where they differ. Rule VII draws a sharp line:

  • Damage caused to any machinery and boilers of a ship which is ashore and in a position of peril, in endeavouring to refloat, shall be allowed in general average - but only when shown to have arisen from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety at the risk of such damage.
  • But where a ship is afloat, no loss or damage caused by working the propelling machinery and boilers shall in any circumstances be made good as general average.

So there are three conditions on the allowable side - the ship must be ashore, she must be in a position of peril, and there must be an actual intention to refloat for the common safety, accepting the risk of that very damage - and one absolute bar: if she is afloat, the answer is no, whatever the circumstances.

6. b) Applying it - when the damage is allowed, and when it is not

On the facts given, the damage can be included in general average. The vessel ran aground, so she was ashore; a large vessel hard aground is self-evidently in a position of peril, exposed to bottom damage, flooding, pounding and change of weather; working the main engine ahead and astern to shake her loose is precisely an endeavour to refloat; and the burnt bearings are damage to machinery arising from that endeavour. It satisfies Rule A as well - it is extraordinary, intentional, reasonable if the alternative was to remain aground, and directed to the common safety of ship and cargo together.

The justification turns on one word: deliberate. Overloading main engine bearings is not an ordinary incident of the voyage. If the master and chief engineer worked the engine hard knowing that they might burn the bearings and accepting that risk in order to get her off, the damage is not an accident but a sacrifice - and a sacrifice made for everybody is shared by everybody.

What must be proved, and by whom. Under Rule E the onus is on the party claiming. The evidence is the deck and engine room log books, the engine movement and bridge movement books, the master's and chief engineer's statements, the note of protest, and any salvage or refloating plan recording that the attempt was made in the knowledge of the risk. The point to record contemporaneously is the decision and the risk accepted, not merely the damage that followed.

When it would not be allowed. If the vessel were afloat - merely touching, or already refloated - the second limb of Rule VII bars the claim in any circumstances. If there were no real peril, for instance a soft grounding on a rising tide with the ship in no danger, the common-danger requirement fails. If the engine were worked for some other purpose, such as ordinary manoeuvring or swinging on the anchor, there is no intention to refloat. And if the damage arose simply from pre-existing defect or wear rather than from the refloating effort, it is particular average and lies where it falls. Finally, Rule D means that even if the grounding was caused by negligent navigation or unseaworthiness, the contribution is still payable - but the cargo interests keep their remedies against the shipowner, and may recover the contribution back through that route.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb a) invites a definition and limb b) is a decided problem, so the temptation is to write a long essay on general average and two lines of 'yes, it is allowed'. That inverts the marks. Limb b) is where the examiner can see whether you can apply a rule, so it needs a rule identified by name, its conditions listed, the facts mapped onto them, the evidence named, and the negative cases stated. Four steps for limb a) and two substantial ones for limb b) is the balance.

The one word that decides the whole question

Deliberate. Damage the sea does to you is particular average and lies where it falls. Damage you choose to accept, knowing the risk, to save the whole adventure is general average and is shared. Every essential feature in limb a) is an elaboration of that, and limb b) is that distinction applied to a set of bearings.

Why Rule VII draws the line at 'afloat'

The bar on a ship that is afloat exists to stop an owner recasting ordinary engine wear as a sacrifice. A ship afloat can always claim she was working her engine hard for the common good; a ship ashore and in peril is in a situation nobody chose, where the deliberate acceptance of machinery damage is genuinely extraordinary. Knowing the reason for the line makes the rule impossible to forget.

Common mistakes

  • Answering limb b) 'yes' without naming Rule VII or its conditions. The rule is the answer.
  • Missing that the ship must be ashore AND in peril. Both are required, and the afloat bar is absolute.
  • Forgetting the evidential requirement under Rule E. The claim fails without proof of the intention and the accepted risk.
  • Saying fault defeats the claim. Rule D says the opposite - contribution is payable, remedies are preserved.
  • Treating the York-Antwerp Rules as law. They apply by contractual incorporation.
  • Confusing general average with particular average or with salvage.

Examiner traps

  • The afloat/ashore distinction is the trap. Change the facts to a ship that has just refloated and is working her engine to hold position, and the answer reverses completely.
  • Numbered rules prevail over lettered rules. If Rule VII allows it, you do not then argue it out again under Rule A - though the Rule Paramount still requires reasonableness.
  • Rule C - the burnt bearings are a direct consequence and allowable; the hire or freight lost while they are repaired is loss by delay and is not.
  • A claim for general average contribution cannot be limited under the LLMC. A neat link to the limitation question on this same paper.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What documents would you produce to support the claim? - engine room and deck logs, engine and bridge movement books, master's and chief engineer's statements, note of protest, and any refloating or salvage plan.
  • Who appoints the average adjuster? - usually the shipowner, but he acts impartially for all interests.
  • What security is taken before cargo is released? - average bond from the receiver and average guarantee from the cargo underwriter, or a cash deposit.
  • If the grounding was caused by the owner's unseaworthiness, must cargo still contribute? - yes under Rule D, but cargo keeps its remedies and may recover the contribution back.
  • Is the fuel burned in the refloating attempt allowable? - yes, as extraordinary expenditure, provided the same conditions are met.

Understand this first

When a ship is lost, two quite different inquiries want to happen at once, and they pull in opposite directions. One asks who is to blame - it is looking for a person to punish or a party to make pay, and everyone it questions therefore has a powerful reason to say as little as possible. The other asks why it happened, so that it does not happen to the next ship - and that inquiry only works if the people who were there tell the whole truth, including the parts that make them look bad. You cannot run both from the same room. So the international system deliberately separates them: it creates a safety investigation whose sole purpose is prevention, which is expressly forbidden to apportion blame or determine liability, and which is kept independent of any criminal or civil process. That one decision explains everything else in this question - why the obligation to investigate is written into the safety and pollution conventions rather than into a liability convention, why the Code protects the people it interviews, and why the findings are published for everybody to learn from rather than handed to a prosecutor. The definitions at the end simply mark out how bad a casualty has to be before that machinery must be switched on.

Model written answer

1. A. The mandatory basis in SOLAS and MARPOL

SOLAS. Two regulations matter, and it is worth giving both. Regulation I/21 (Casualties) provides that each Administration undertakes to conduct an investigation of any casualty occurring to any of its ships subject to the Convention when it judges that such an investigation may assist in determining what changes in the present Regulations might be desirable, and to supply the Organization with the pertinent findings. The same regulation adds a protective rule that shows the whole philosophy: no report or recommendation of the Organization based upon that information shall disclose the identity or nationality of the ships concerned, or in any manner fix or imply responsibility upon any ship or person.

Regulation XI-1/6 is the operative mandatory hook and is the one the question is really after. It requires that each Administration shall conduct investigations of marine casualties and marine incidents in accordance with the Casualty Investigation Code. It is regulation XI-1/6 that converts the Code from guidance into binding obligation.

MARPOL. Article 12 (Casualties to ships) provides that each Administration undertakes to conduct an investigation of any casualty occurring to any of its ships subject to the Convention if such casualty has produced a major deleterious effect upon the marine environment, and to supply the Organization with information concerning the findings. The trigger is therefore environmental, where the SOLAS trigger is regulatory improvement.

Standing behind both is UNCLOS Article 94(7), which obliges the flag State to cause an inquiry to be held into every marine casualty or incident of navigation on the high seas involving a ship flying its flag which has caused loss of life or serious injury to nationals of another State, or serious damage to ships, installations or the marine environment.

2. B. What the Code is, and how it is structured

The Code of the International Standards and Recommended Practices for a Safety Investigation into a Marine Casualty or Marine Incident - the Casualty Investigation Code - was adopted by resolution MSC.255(84) on 16 May 2008 and became mandatory through SOLAS regulation XI-1/6 with effect from 1 January 2010. It is in three parts:

  • Part I - General provisions, including the definitions on which the whole obligation turns.
  • Part II - Mandatory standards, which States shall apply.
  • Part III - Recommended practices, which States should apply.

Its sole objective is stated at the outset and governs everything: a marine safety investigation is conducted to prevent future casualties, by establishing the circumstances and the causes. It is not conducted to establish liability or apportion blame.

3. B. The salient mandatory standards - Part II

  • A marine safety investigation shall be conducted into every VERY SERIOUS MARINE CASUALTY. This is the core obligation and the reason limb C matters.
  • For other marine casualties and marine incidents, the flag State shall consider whether an investigation would yield safety lessons, and record its decision.
  • The safety investigation shall be separate from, and independent of, any other form of investigation - criminal, civil or administrative.
  • It shall not seek to apportion blame or determine liability.
  • The flag State is responsible for conducting it, or may agree with another substantially interested State which shall be the lead investigating State.
  • States shall co-operate, giving access to the ship, to witnesses, to the wreck and to evidence such as VDR data, and shall not unreasonably delay the investigation.
  • The investigation shall be unbiased, and the investigator given functional independence and the necessary powers.
  • Certain records are protected - witness statements, communications between persons involved, medical and private information - from disclosure for purposes other than the safety investigation, unless a court determines the public interest outweighs it.
  • A final report shall be produced and submitted to IMO, and made publicly available; where the report affects another State, a draft is circulated for comment.

4. B. The salient recommended practices - Part III

  • Investigate other marine casualties and marine incidents where safety lessons may be learned, not merely the mandatory category.
  • Notify substantially interested States promptly and invite them to participate.
  • Secure and take evidence early - VDR and voyage data, log books, charts, machinery records, alarm printouts - because it degrades or is overwritten quickly.
  • Interview seafarers with proper regard to their rights, informing them of the purpose of the interview and respecting the privilege against self-incrimination; interviews should be conducted so that the seafarer is not intimidated.
  • Give any person whose conduct may be criticised a fair opportunity to see and comment on the draft report.
  • Consider the human element systematically, using a structured method rather than stopping at the last operator error.
  • Publish the report so that the industry can learn, and re-open an investigation where significant new evidence emerges.
  • Provide trained investigators and adequate resources, and report safety lessons to IMO for wider dissemination.

5. C. Very serious marine casualty

A very serious marine casualty is defined in the Code as a marine casualty involving the total loss of the ship, or a death, or severe damage to the environment.

Three points make the answer precise. First, the three limbs are alternatives, not cumulative - any one is sufficient, so a single fatality aboard an otherwise undamaged ship makes the casualty very serious. Second, this is the only category for which a safety investigation is mandatory; everything else is a matter for the flag State's judgement. Third, it sits at the top of a family of terms which should be distinguished - and one of them is not in the Code, which is itself a scoring point:

  • Marine casualty - an event, or sequence of events, resulting in death or serious injury, loss of a person overboard, loss or presumed loss of a ship, material damage, stranding, disabling, collision, damage to marine infrastructure, or severe damage to the environment; but excluding a deliberate act intended to harm the safety of a ship or persons.
  • Serious marine casualty - a casualty not qualifying as very serious, but involving matters such as fire, explosion, grounding, contact, heavy weather or ice damage, hull cracking or defect, resulting in immobilisation, structural damage rendering the ship unfit, or pollution. This term is not defined in the Code. The Code's chapter 2 defines twenty-two terms and this is not among them; the definition comes from the IMO harmonized casualty reporting procedures, and it matters because it belongs to the reporting stream rather than to the obligation to investigate.
  • Marine incident - an event or sequence of events, other than a marine casualty, which has occurred directly in connection with the operations of a ship and which endangered or, if not corrected, would endanger the safety of the ship, its occupants or the environment. In effect, the near miss.

The practical consequence for a Chief Engineer is direct: the classification decides the obligation. Once an event meets any limb of the very serious definition, a safety investigation must follow, evidence must be preserved from that moment, and the report will in due course be public.

Understand this first

Most things that go wrong at sea go wrong because of a person, and for a long time the regulatory answer to that was simply to require more training. The deeper insight behind the modern rules is that a competent, well-trained officer will still make serious mistakes if he is exhausted, ill, working in a team that will not speak up to him, or operating equipment designed without much thought for the person using it. So the international response works on two fronts at once. The first is to stop treating training as time served and start treating it as demonstrated ability - a seafarer must show he can actually do the thing, assessed against a written standard, and that includes the social skills of leading a team and using all the resources on a bridge or in an engine room. The second is to accept that the human being has physical limits that no certificate can override: he must be medically fit, he must be rested, and there must be enough people aboard to allow that. Fatigue is where the second front is at its weakest, because it cannot be inspected the way a certificate can - a tired officer looks exactly like a rested one - which is why the rules on it are backed by guidance aimed at everybody who can influence it, from the designer of the ship down to the individual keeping the watch.

Model written answer

1. A. What the human element means in STCW

The human element is the complex of human factors bearing on the safe and efficient operation of a ship - competence, health and medical fitness, alertness and fatigue, communication, teamwork and leadership, safety culture, and the fit between the person and the task he is given. It is addressed by the International Convention on Standards of Training, Certification and Watchkeeping for Seafarers, 1978, as amended, whose Manila amendments entered into force on 1 January 2012 with full implementation required by 1 January 2017.

STCW does not address the human element by exhortation. It does so through mandatory standards in Part A of the STCW Code, working on two fronts: making certification depend on demonstrated ability rather than time served, and recognising that the seafarer is a physical human being with limits that no certificate overrides.

2. A. Competence-based standards

The central mechanism is the competence table. For each function and level, Part A of the Code specifies the competence, the knowledge, understanding and proficiency underpinning it, the methods for demonstrating competence, and the criteria for evaluating competence. A candidate is certificated because he has been assessed against a written standard, not because he has served a period.

  • Regulation I/6 - training and assessment: those training and assessing must themselves be qualified for the task.
  • Regulation I/8 - quality standards systems: training, assessment and certification are continuously monitored, and Administrations are subject to independent evaluation.
  • Regulation I/11 - revalidation of certificates at intervals not exceeding five years, with refresher and updating training, so competence does not silently decay.
  • Chapter VI - safety familiarisation, basic training and instruction for all seafarers.

The Manila amendments made the most explicitly human-element additions by bringing the non-technical competences into the mandatory tables: leadership and teamworking skills, managerial skills at management level, and bridge and engine-room resource management - the recognition that a technically competent officer in a team that will not challenge him is still a hazard. Marine environment awareness and security-related training were added in the same revision.

3. A. Fitness for duty

The second front recognises the body. Regulation I/9 sets medical fitness standards and requires a valid medical certificate, with standards for eyesight and hearing and a bar on conditions likely to be aggravated by service at sea.

Regulation VIII/1 and Code section A-VIII/1 govern fitness for duty and are the provisions the question really points at:

  • A minimum of 10 hours of rest in any 24-hour period, and 77 hours in any 7-day period.
  • Rest may be divided into no more than two periods, one of which shall be at least 6 hours, and the interval between consecutive rest periods shall not exceed 14 hours.
  • Musters, drills and inspections shall be conducted so as to minimise disturbance of rest periods and not induce fatigue.
  • Records of daily hours of rest shall be maintained and made available for inspection.
  • Exceptions may be permitted by the Administration within stated bounds, and must be recorded; they are not a general dispensation.
  • Prevention of drug and alcohol abuse, including a blood alcohol limit for seafarers performing designated safety, security and environmental duties.

Regulation VIII/2 and section A-VIII/2 then govern watchkeeping arrangements and principles, including the composition of the watch and, for the engine room, the conditions under which a periodically unattended machinery space may be operated.

4. A. Responsibility placed on companies and Administrations

STCW deliberately places duties on the organisation, not only on the individual. Regulation I/14 requires the company to ensure that seafarers assigned to its ships hold appropriate certificates, that the ship is manned in compliance with the safe manning requirements of the Administration, that documentation is kept, and that seafarers receive ship-specific familiarisation and can communicate effectively and co-ordinate in an emergency.

The ISM Code reinforces the same ground from the safety-management side, requiring the company to ensure the ship is appropriately manned with qualified and medically fit seafarers, and to establish procedures for familiarisation and for identifying training needs. Administrations, for their part, approve training, issue and endorse certificates, and are themselves subject to independent evaluation - so the standard is audited rather than assumed.

5. B. The IMO guidance on fatigue

The current IMO guidance at this examination is MSC.1/Circ.1598, Guidelines on fatigue, dated 24 January 2019. It replaced the earlier fatigue guidance and is a substantial rewrite rather than a reissue, reflecting the intervening research on sleep, circadian rhythm and performance.

Its structure is its most useful feature: it is organised into modules addressed to the different parties who can actually influence fatigue, rather than as a single text aimed at the seafarer alone. The modules cover, in substance:

  • Fatigue itself - its nature, its causes, how it degrades attention, judgement, reaction time and decision-making, and why sleep loss accumulates as a debt that cannot be repaid by a single long sleep.
  • The seafarer - practical measures: sleep hygiene, use of off-watch time, the effect of caffeine, alcohol and diet, recognising fatigue in oneself and in others.
  • The master and the shipboard management team - designing watch schedules, planning port calls and maintenance so that work is not concentrated, and managing workload.
  • The company and the ship operator - manning levels, voyage and port scheduling, realistic administrative burden, and a culture in which reporting fatigue is safe.
  • The Administration - how to take fatigue into account in safe manning determinations, in surveys and in casualty investigation.
  • Training providers and those who design and build ships - accommodation, noise, vibration, lighting, motion and workspace layout all affect the quality of rest actually obtained.

It is important to classify it correctly: the Guidelines are recommendatory. They do not create obligations. They explain how to manage a risk that the mandatory instruments address only at the level of a minimum.

6. B. How the guidance and the mandatory rules fit together

The mandatory rules set an enforceable floor - the rest hours in A-VIII/1, running in parallel with the hours of work and rest required by the Maritime Labour Convention, 2006, as amended, and with the manning and resource requirements of the ISM Code. The guidance addresses the risk that remains above that floor.

That distinction is the point of the whole limb, and it is worth stating explicitly: compliance with the minimum rest hours does not mean the crew is not fatigued. Rest hours can be met by a schedule that is nevertheless badly fragmented, timed against the body clock, or taken in accommodation too noisy for real sleep. Fatigue is also invisible to inspection in a way that a certificate or a rest-hour record is not - a tired officer looks like a rested one, and the record may show compliance that the watch schedule did not deliver. That is precisely why IMO addressed the guidance to every actor who can influence the outcome, from the naval architect to the individual on watch, and why in practice fatigue must be managed through the ship's safety management system - realistic watch schedules, port workload planning, honest rest records, and a reporting culture in which an officer can say he is too tired to take the watch.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Limb A is enormous - the human element in STCW could fill the paper - and limb B is narrow and specific. The failure mode is to write everything known about STCW and then two lines on fatigue. Four route steps for A and two for B keeps the balance, and the final step, which explains how guidance and mandatory rules relate, is the management-level judgement that separates a Class I answer from a Class II one.

The two-front idea that organises limb A

STCW attacks the human element on two fronts: demonstrated competence (what the person can do, assessed against a written standard) and physical limits (medical fitness, rest, manning). If you present limb A that way, every regulation you cite has an obvious home, and you will not produce an undifferentiated list of regulation numbers.

The sentence that earns limb B

Complying with the minimum rest hours does not mean the crew is not fatigued. Rest can be fragmented, badly timed against the body clock, or taken in accommodation too noisy to sleep in. That single sentence shows you understand why guidance exists alongside a mandatory minimum, and it is the natural bridge into the practical measures.

Common mistakes

  • Reciting regulation numbers with no organising idea. Group them: competence, fitness, duties.
  • Getting the rest hours wrong. It is 10 in 24 and 77 in 7 days, in no more than two periods, one of at least six hours, with intervals not over 14 hours.
  • Omitting the Manila additions - leadership, teamwork, managerial skills, resource management. They are the most explicitly human-element part of the Convention.
  • Treating the fatigue Guidelines as mandatory. They are recommendatory.
  • Confusing the STCW rest hours with the MLC hours of work and rest. They run in parallel and the MLC offers an alternative formulation based on maximum hours of work.

Examiner traps

  • The Guidelines are guidance. Saying so, and then explaining why guidance is the right tool for a risk that cannot be inspected, is the mark of a good answer.
  • Exceptions to rest hours exist but are bounded and must be recorded. Candidates either forget them or treat them as a general dispensation.
  • The fatigue guidance is addressed to designers and Administrations as well as seafarers - a point almost nobody makes, and an easy distinguishing mark.
  • Do not date the STCW comprehensive review. At this sitting no revision had been adopted; asserting a session number or a target adoption year is a claim about a later period.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What are the minimum rest hours? - 10 in any 24 and 77 in any 7 days, in no more than two periods, one at least 6 hours, intervals not exceeding 14 hours.
  • How would you manage fatigue during a heavy port call? - plan the workload in advance, stagger the watch schedule, use shore assistance where available, defer non-critical maintenance, and record rest honestly rather than to the form.
  • Who is responsible for safe manning? - the Administration determines it and issues the safe manning document; the company must man the ship in compliance with it.
  • What is engine-room resource management? - the effective use of all available resources, human and technical, including communication, assertiveness, workload allocation and situational awareness; brought into the mandatory tables by the Manila amendments.
  • How does the ISM Code bear on this? - it requires appropriately manned ships with qualified, medically fit seafarers and procedures for familiarisation and training needs.

Understand this first

A convention only works if the States that signed it can see what is actually happening. IMO has no inspectors and no ships of its own; almost everything it knows comes from what governments choose to send it. For decades that meant paper circulars - a State would report a casualty or a problem, the Secretariat would summarise it, and by the time anyone could use the information it was old and scattered. GISIS is the answer to that: one place where the information States are required or invited to report is collected, stored and made searchable, so that the same fact can serve a policy debate, a port State inspector and a shipowner planning a voyage. The fuel oil part of the question is a good illustration of why it matters. The rules cap how much sulphur may be burned, but they also recognise that a ship cannot burn fuel that is not for sale - so if compliant fuel genuinely cannot be obtained, the ship reports that fact rather than being punished for it. Those individual reports are not very interesting one at a time. Collected in one system they become something quite different: evidence about which ports actually have compliant fuel and which suppliers keep failing, which is exactly what a regulator, an inspector and a chartering department each need.

Model written answer

1. What GISIS is, and the problem it solves

The Global Integrated Shipping Information System (GISIS) is a web-based information platform developed and maintained by the IMO Secretariat. Its existence answers a structural feature of the Organization: IMO has no inspectorate and no ships. Almost everything it knows about how its instruments are working in practice comes from what Member States report to it. Before GISIS that reporting was done by paper circular - slow to compile, scattered across documents, and very difficult to search or compare. GISIS consolidates into one system the information that States are required or invited to report under the various instruments.

It should be classified correctly at the outset: GISIS is an administrative platform, not an instrument. It creates no obligation of its own; the obligations live in the conventions, and GISIS is the channel through which they are discharged and the information made useful.

2. The aim of the system

  • To provide a single point of collection and dissemination for information related to IMO instruments.
  • To support Member States in implementing and enforcing those instruments, by giving Administrations and port State control access to information held by others.
  • To give the Organization the evidence base on which instruments are reviewed, amended and developed - so that policy rests on reported experience rather than assertion.
  • To promote transparency and information sharing between Administrations, and with industry where the data is public.
  • To reduce duplicated reporting, each item being collected once and made available to all who need it.

3. The functions - how it works and what it holds

GISIS is organised into modules, each corresponding to a reporting obligation or a defined information set. Those in use include - and this list is not exhaustive - marine casualties and incidents; piracy and armed robbery against ships; reception facilities, including reports of their inadequacy; contact points for Administrations and national authorities; recognized organizations authorised by flag States; ship and company particulars; port State control information; and modules covering pollution prevention equipment, survey and certification, maritime security and facilitation.

The operating model is straightforward. Data is entered by designated national focal points in each Member State, and the Secretariat administers the system rather than generating the content. Access is tiered: some modules are open to the public, others are restricted to Member States or to registered users, according to the sensitivity of the data. Records are searchable and exportable, and that is the feature which converts a pile of individual reports into usable evidence. In doing so the system facilitates data sharing among Member States, letting one Administration see what another has reported without bilateral correspondence.

4. The fuel oil module and MARPOL Annex VI

MARPOL Annex VI regulation 14 caps the sulphur content of fuel oil used on board - 0.50 % m/m globally since 1 January 2020, and 0.10 % m/m within emission control areas. But the Annex also recognises a practical reality: a ship cannot burn fuel that is not on sale. Regulation 18, on fuel oil availability and quality, therefore requires Parties to take reasonable steps to promote the availability of compliant fuel oil, and provides that where a ship cannot obtain compliant fuel oil despite best efforts, it reports that fact to its Administration and to the competent authority of the port of destination - the mechanism commonly known as the fuel oil non-availability report. Regulation 18 also provides for the ship to notify its Administration and the port State where a supplier has failed to meet the requirements, for example on the bunker delivery note or the representative sample.

The GISIS module collects these reports centrally. It should be distinguished from the separate Annex VI reporting stream under regulation 27, the collection and reporting of ship fuel oil consumption data, which is a different obligation with a different purpose.

5. Usefulness for reporting

  • It gives a single standard channel and format, replacing ad hoc correspondence with several authorities in several forms.
  • The ship's own report becomes a preserved record, which is precisely what supports its position if the fuel used is later questioned at a port State inspection.
  • The report identifies the port and the supplier concerned, so it is actionable rather than merely noted.
  • Reports of genuine non-availability are kept distinct from reports of supply that failed to meet requirements - two different problems needing two different responses.
  • The Administration and the port State receive the same record, which removes an obvious source of dispute about what was reported and when.

6. Usefulness for analysing

The analytical value is much greater than the reporting value, because aggregation changes what the data means. A single non-availability report tells you about one ship on one day. Several hundred, searchable by port and by date, tell you something quite different:

  • Where compliant fuel is genuinely unavailable - a map of the real supply position, as opposed to the assumed one.
  • Which ports recur, and which suppliers repeatedly fail on quality or documentation.
  • Risk-based targeting for port State control: inspectors can concentrate on ships that have bunkered where problems cluster, rather than inspecting at random.
  • Evidence for IMO in reviewing how regulation 18 is working and whether availability is adequate - the review of fuel availability is itself an obligation that needs data.
  • Separating genuine non-availability from evasion. This is the point that matters most to an honest operator: if a ship claims non-availability at a port where everyone else bunkered compliant fuel that week, the claim stands out - and conversely, a genuine claim is corroborated by the pattern.
  • Commercial use - bunker planning, supplier selection and voyage planning, where the data is publicly accessible.

7. The limits of the data

A management-level answer must state the limitations, because they govern how much weight the data will bear:

  • It is a reporting system. Its completeness depends entirely on States and ships actually reporting, and under-reporting is likely - a ship that quietly bunkers non-compliant fuel does not file a report.
  • Therefore the absence of reports from a port is not evidence that compliant fuel was available there. It may equally mean nobody reported.
  • Module membership and structure are administrative: modules are added, merged and reorganised without any instrument being amended, so any list of modules dates quickly, and the list above is given as non-exhaustive for that reason.
  • A GISIS record is evidence that a report was made, not a finding of fact. It does not determine whether the ship's efforts to obtain compliant fuel were genuinely reasonable - that remains for the Administration and the port State.
  • It supplements and does not replace the ship's own evidence: the bunker delivery note and its retained representative sample, the oil record book, and the correspondence showing the attempts made to obtain compliant fuel.
Study guide

Why this structure scores

The stem prints no lettered limbs but sets two tasks, and the second one names its own two halves: reporting and analysing. Splitting those into separate sections is the single easiest structural decision on this question, because most candidates blur them into one paragraph about 'usefulness' and only ever answer the reporting half. Closing on the limits of the data is what makes it a management-level answer rather than a description of a website.

The idea that makes GISIS explicable

IMO has no inspectors. It knows only what States tell it. GISIS exists so that what they tell it arrives in one place, in one format, and can be searched. Every aim and every function follows from that one fact, and so does every limitation - a system built entirely on voluntary reporting can never be complete.

Why aggregation is the real answer to 'usefulness'

One non-availability report is an anecdote. Several hundred, searchable by port and date, are evidence - about where compliant fuel actually is, which suppliers keep failing, and which non-availability claims look genuine against the pattern. If you make that point explicitly you have answered 'analysing' properly; if you do not, you have written the reporting half twice.

Common mistakes

  • Describing GISIS as if it imposed obligations. It is a platform; the obligations are in MARPOL Annex VI.
  • Answering 'usefulness' only in terms of reporting, and never addressing analysis.
  • Presenting a module list as complete and current. It is administrative and changes without any amendment - say so.
  • Confusing the regulation 18 availability and quality stream with the regulation 27 fuel oil consumption data reporting stream.
  • Omitting the limitations. At management level, knowing what data cannot prove is part of the answer.
