1
What is IoT (Internet of Things)? Where would you use it in the engine room?
MACHINERYAUTOMATION

15-Second Answer

IoT is a network of physical devices — sensors, actuators, controllers — fitted with connectivity so they collect and exchange operational data automatically, without human data entry. In the engine room it appears as smart sensors feeding condition-based/predictive maintenance, remote maker diagnostics, performance monitoring and automated reporting. Regulatory anchor: IMO cyber-risk management — MSC.428(98) requires cyber risk in the SMS, and IACS UR E26/E27 make cyber resilience mandatory for new ships contracted from 1 July 2024.

60-Second Answer

The Internet of Things is the architecture of connected physical devices: field sensors and actuators with embedded electronics and network connectivity, streaming data to shipboard servers and, via satellite, to shore analytics platforms. In the engine room it is used for: continuous condition monitoring (bearing vibration, TC speed, exhaust temperature spread, oil-mist and lube-oil condition sensors) feeding condition-based and predictive maintenance; remote maker diagnostics (engine builder connecting to the ME control system to analyse alarms and tune performance); vessel performance/fuel monitoring supporting SEEMP and CII management; and automated data logging replacing manual noon-report entries. Because these devices connect operational technology (OT) to shore networks, they carry cyber risk: IMO Resolution MSC.428(98) requires cyber risk management within the ISM SMS (verified since the first annual DOC audit after 1 January 2021), guided by MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.2, and IACS UR E26 (ships) / E27 (systems) impose cyber-resilience requirements on newbuildings contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2024.

Governing instrument

IMO Res. MSC.428(98) — cyber risk management within the SMS (first DOC verification after 1 Jan 2021) — with the MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.2 cyber risk guidelines; classification society digital/smart-ship notations govern shipboard IoT fits.

Definition

IoT = physical assets + embedded sensors/electronics + connectivity + data analytics. The value chain is: sense → transmit → analyse → predict → act. Onboard, the "things" are machinery-mounted smart sensors; the analytics increasingly sit ashore in the operator's or maker's fleet-performance centre.

Engine-room applications

  • Condition-based maintenance (CBM): vibration sensors on purifier/pump/motor bearings, TC vibration and speed, main-bearing temperature — replacing fixed calendar overhauls with data-triggered intervention.
  • Predictive analytics: shore platforms trending exhaust-temperature deviation, scavenge conditions, cylinder-lubrication feed, detecting degradation before an alarm limit is reached.
  • Remote diagnostics: engine maker's service centre connecting to the electronic ME control system to interrogate faults and advise parameter changes.
  • Performance & emissions: automated fuel flow, shaft power and speed data supporting SEEMP Part III, CII monitoring and DCS/MRV reporting with fewer manual-entry errors.
  • Inventory & logistics: connected spares/consumables tracking integrated with the PMS.

The cyber-risk flip side

  • MSC.428(98): cyber risks must be addressed in the SMS under ISM Code objectives (verified at DOC audit).
  • MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.2: IMO guidelines on maritime cyber risk management — identify, protect, detect, respond, recover.
  • IACS UR E26/E27: cyber-resilient design of the ship and of onboard systems — network segregation of OT from IT, secure remote access — mandatory for ships contracted on/after 1 July 2024.

Trap point

Do not present IoT as only a convenience feature — the examiner is testing whether you connect it to cyber risk management in the SMS. Any answer that names engine-room uses but omits MSC.428(98)/UR E26-E27 is incomplete at management level.

CE Oral Tip (Simon): Give the one-line definition, then go straight to two concrete engine-room examples you can defend — TC vibration monitoring feeding CBM, and remote maker diagnostics on the electronic main engine — and close with the cyber hook: "and because these connect OT to shore, cyber risk sits in our SMS under MSC.428(98)." The common weak answer is a consumer-tech description ("smart devices talking to each other") with no shipboard system and no regulatory anchor.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE owns the boundary between OT and IT onboard: approving remote-access sessions for maker diagnostics, ensuring the PMS reflects CBM data honestly, keeping USB/network hygiene in the ECR, and verifying that sensor-driven maintenance deferrals are documented decisions, not silent drift from the class-approved PMS intervals.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Can you extend an overhaul interval because condition-monitoring data looks good?" A: Only within a class-approved CBM/PMS scheme — the machinery survey arrangement (CMS/PMS notation) defines what condition data can justify, and any extension outside it needs class agreement. Q: "What's the biggest risk of remote maker access?" A: An unsecured remote channel into the OT network — access must be controlled, logged, time-bounded and covered by the SMS cyber procedures.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Consumer-level definition with no engine-room system named; no mention of cyber risk/MSC.428(98); confusing condition monitoring (data collection) with a class-approved CBM survey scheme (an approved maintenance regime); not knowing UR E26/E27 exist for newbuildings.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

MSC.428(98) — cyber in SMS, first annual DOC verification after 1 Jan 2021; UR E26/E27 — ships contracted on/after 1 Jul 2024; MSC-FAL.1/Circ.3/Rev.2 — the five NIST-style functions.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

The 2017 NotPetya attack on Maersk's shore IT systems (≈US$300 million impact) is the industry's landmark demonstration that connected maritime operations carry systemic cyber exposure — the driver behind MSC.428(98) and the IACS cyber URs.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: Define IoT → engine-room applications → CBM vs planned maintenance → who approves interval changes → cyber risk and how the SMS covers it.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

On a Maersk container vessel: electronic main engine with maker remote-diagnostic capability, online TC and bearing condition monitoring, automated performance data flowing to the fleet performance centre for CII/SEEMP tracking, and SMS cyber procedures governing remote-access sessions and removable media in the ECR.

2
High voltage switchboard safety — explain the full safety framework, beyond just vacuum breaker earthing.
MACHINERYHV

15-Second Answer

Marine High Voltage is any system above 1,000 V AC — typically 3.3, 6.6 or 11 kV onboard. HV switchboard safety is a management system, not a single device: designated Authorised/Senior Authorised Persons, a formal Permit-to-Work with sanction-to-test, the isolation–lock–prove-dead–earth-down sequence, approved live-line testers with proving units, arc-flash PPE, insulated matting, and STCW-mandated HV competence — all sitting inside the SMS.

60-Second Answer

HV safety rests on three pillars. (1) People: only trained, formally appointed personnel — an Authorised Person (AP) works under a Senior Authorised Person (SAP), and HV operational competence is a mandated STCW management-level competence (Table A-III/2, "manage operation of electrical and electronic control equipment", introduced by the 2010 Manila amendments; A-III/6 for ETOs). (2) Procedure: a documented HV safety management system in the SMS — Permit-to-Work, switching programme approved by the SAP/CE, sanction-to-test certificate for testing on isolated apparatus, and key-safe control of isolation locks. (3) Practice at the board: isolate and lock off; prove the tester on a proving unit; test dead at the point of work; apply circuit earths (integral earthing switch or portable earths) to discharge stored charge and guard against re-energisation/induced voltage; screen off adjacent live sections; use insulated matting and arc-rated PPE; observe safe approach distances; and before re-energising, run insulation-resistance checks and confirm space heaters have kept the board dry. Design-side protections — vacuum/SF6 breakers, interlocked withdrawable trucks, earthing switches interlocked against live closing — come from class rules and IEC 60092-503 (AC systems above 1 kV up to 15 kV).

Governing instrument

IEC 60092-503 (a.c. high-voltage installations 1–36 kV in ships), SOLAS Ch. II-1 Part D electrical requirements, STCW Tables A-III/1 and A-III/2 HV competences, and the company's HV safety rules and Permit-to-Work system under the ISM Code.

Why HV is different from LV

  • Stored charge: HV cables and capacitive apparatus retain lethal charge after isolation — hence mandatory earthing-down before touching.
  • Arc flash/blast: fault energy at 6.6 kV can be fatal without contact — hence arc-rated PPE, remote switching where possible, and closed-door racking of breaker trucks.
  • Induced voltage: parallel HV runs can induce voltage on an isolated conductor — earths remain applied for the duration of work.

The safe-isolation sequence (the answer the examiner wants in order)

  1. Plan: switching programme written and approved; risk assessment; PTW raised.
  2. Isolate at all points of supply (including transformers' both sides); rack out breaker truck; lock off; keys to the key safe; caution notices posted.
  3. Prove tester → test dead → re-prove tester on a proving unit, using an approved HV voltage indicator.
  4. Earth down — close the integral earthing switch or apply portable earths at the point of work.
  5. Screen & demarcate adjacent live equipment; insulated matting in place.
  6. Issue PTW / sanction-to-test; work proceeds under AP supervision.
  7. Restore: work complete → PTW cancelled → earths removed and counted → IR test → locks removed under SAP control → re-energise per switching programme.

Trap point

"Prove dead" is a three-step act: prove the tester, test the conductor, prove the tester again — a dead tester reading on a live board has killed people. And earths stay on for the whole job, not just the initial discharge.

CE Oral Tip (Nair): Nair asked this "broadly" because most candidates only recite breaker earthing. Structure the answer as People → Permit system → Point-of-work sequence, and say the isolation litany in order: isolate – lock – prove tester – test dead – re-prove – earth down – screen – permit. The two facts he listens for: HV = above 1 kV (marine 3.3/6.6/11 kV), and HV competence is an STCW A-III/2 requirement, not just company policy. The common failure is jumping straight to "close the earthing switch" with no permit framework around it.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

On an HV ship the CE typically holds the SAP role: approving switching programmes, controlling the key safe, authorising sanction-to-test, and auditing that the AP register, tester calibration/proving unit, portable earths and arc-flash PPE are all in date — these are DOC/SMC audit items.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Why earth down if you've already tested dead?" A: Stored capacitive charge and induced voltage from adjacent live circuits can re-appear on an isolated conductor; earths also protect against inadvertent re-energisation. Q: "Can an LV-experienced electrician work on the 6.6 kV board?" A: No — only formally trained and appointed HV Authorised Persons, under a permit issued by the SAP. Q: "Vacuum breaker is racked out — is the busbar safe?" A: No — the busbar side remains live; safety comes from the full isolation of the section plus earthing, screening and permits.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Defining HV wrongly (e.g. "above 440 V"); giving only the earthing switch; omitting prove–test–re-prove; forgetting sanction-to-test as distinct from PTW; no STCW/SOLAS anchor; forgetting IR test and earth-count before re-energising.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

HV threshold > 1,000 V AC; marine HV levels 3.3 / 6.6 / 11 kV; IEC 60092-503 scope >1 kV to 15 kV; STCW Table A-III/2; SOLAS II-1/45.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

MAIB and flag-state investigations into shipboard HV fatalities repeatedly find the same chain — isolation assumed rather than proved, or earths omitted — which is why the prove–test–re-prove and earth-down steps are written as absolute, not discretionary, in HV safety rules.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair: What is HV / threshold → why is HV treated differently → the safe-isolation sequence in order → PTW vs sanction-to-test → who is authorised (SAP/AP, STCW basis) → design interlocks on the board.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Large Maersk container vessels with 6.6 kV systems (shaft generator/reefer capacity/thruster supplies) run a formal HV SMS module: SAP/AP appointments in writing, switching programmes filed with the PTW, key safe in the ECR, proving unit and calibrated HV indicators in the HV locker, and annual HV refresher verification during internal audits.
