Fixed CO₂ fire system — what is the relief valve setting, and how is the line pressure tested?
FIRECO2

15-Second Answer

The manifold relief valve protects any section of pipework that can be isolated between valves against trapped-liquid or leaking-cylinder pressure — its exact setting is a design value on the class-approved system drawings, not a universal number. Line testing per MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1: every 2 years (cargo-ship survey cycle) blow-through of discharge piping with dry air/nitrogen to prove nozzles clear, and at the 10-year inspection the discharge pipelines are pressure-tested to the maximum working pressure of the respective sections.

60-Second Answer

The FSS Code Chapter 5 requires that where any section of CO₂ distribution piping can be isolated (e.g. between the cylinder manifold and a closed distribution valve), means are provided to relieve over-pressure — a relief valve or safety disc venting to open deck. This protects against pressure build-up from a leaking cylinder valve into a blocked section, or thermal expansion of trapped liquid CO₂. The setting is system-specific: it comes from the maker's class-approved drawings, set above normal cylinder pressure (~52 bar at 15°C, and higher in hot climates) but below the design pressure of the manifold section — so in the oral, name the governing document rather than inventing a universal figure. Line testing: under MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1, at every intermediate/periodical/renewal survey the discharge piping and nozzles are proven clear by isolating the piping and blowing dry air or nitrogen from test cylinders through each section; hoses are replaced at maker intervals not exceeding 10 years; and at the 10-yearly inspection the discharge pipelines are pressure-tested to the maximum working pressure of the section (flag/class practice, e.g. IRS fixed-fire-fighting annex). Control valves get an internal examination at least every 5 years.

Governing instrument

SOLAS II-2/10 with FSS Code Ch. 5 (fixed gas fire-extinguishing systems); maintenance and testing per MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1; the relief setting itself is a class-approved design value on the ship's system drawings.

Why the Relief Arrangement Exists

  • Trapped liquid: liquid CO₂ isolated in a pipe section expands with temperature — hydraulic lock can burst pipework without a relief path.
  • Leaking cylinder valve: a passing pilot/cylinder valve slowly pressurises a closed manifold; the relief valve vents this safely to open deck instead of into the CO₂ room.
  • Outlet routing: the relief discharge is led to open deck, clear of accommodation and ventilation intakes — the same principle as the cylinder safety-disc vent line.

Honest Boundary — Ship-Specific Value

Relief settings and section design pressures vary by maker and system (e.g. two-valve pilot systems vs. direct release). The pass answer names the source: "set per the class-approved CO₂ system drawings and maker's manual for my ship" — then gives the physics of why it exists.

Trap point

There is no universal CO₂ relief setting — it is a class-approved drawing value; and line tightness is proven with air/N₂ test methods, never by releasing CO₂ charge.

Give the physics (trapped liquid CO₂, leaking cylinder valve), the venting-to-open-deck principle, then the testing regime with its three numbers: blow-through at each survey, hoses ≤10 yr, pipelines pressure-tested at 10 yr. Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE owns the CO₂ system PMS lines: weekly visual of the room, quarterly level checks (ultrasonic liquid-level gauging in situ), survey-linked blow-throughs with class attendance, hose renewal records and the hydrostatic-test date register per cylinder. Any blow-through is done under a rigorous isolation/PTW regime — accidental release incidents almost always trace to maintenance done with the system not positively secured.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Why not test the line with CO₂ itself?" A: Cost, asphyxiation risk and residue — dry air/nitrogen proves the pipes clear safely with the system isolated. Q: "Where does the relief valve discharge?" A: To open deck, clear of openings — never into the protected space or CO₂ room. Q: "What pressure is in the cylinders?" A: Saturation pressure of liquid CO₂ — ~52 bar at 15°C, rising steeply with temperature; not a "charged" pressure like nitrogen.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Quoting an invented universal relief setting; confusing the manifold relief valve with the cylinder safety (bursting) disc; not knowing the blow-through medium is dry air/N₂; missing the ≤10-yr hose rule and 5-yr control-valve internal exam from Circ.1318/Rev.1.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

CO₂ quantity: 35% of machinery-space gross volume (30% excluding casing); 85% in 2 min for machinery spaces; cylinder pressure ~52 bar @ 15°C; hoses ≤10 yr; control valves internal exam ≤5 yr; pipeline pressure test at 10 yr.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

UK MCA Safety Bulletin 12 (accidental CO₂ releases on two UK merchant vessels) — maintenance without positive isolation caused releases into manned spaces, driving the tightened Circ.1318/Rev.1 regime and in-situ level-gauging practice.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Senthil: System quantity/discharge requirements → release procedure and interlocks → relief valve purpose/setting source → testing regime and intervals → what if a cylinder leaks in the room (bridging to Q2).

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: high-pressure cylinder bank protecting engine room, cargo holds (if fitted) and purifier room; two-valve release cabinet with time delay and alarms; PMS lines mirror Circ.1318/Rev.1 intervals; approved drawings in the CO₂ room and CE's office define the relief settings for each isolatable section.

Fire in the CO₂ room — how do you fight it, and where is the relief valve outlet?
FIRECO2

15-Second Answer

Fight it from outside: raise the alarm, shut ventilation, and apply boundary cooling with water — do not enter, because heated cylinders will self-vent through their safety (bursting) discs, whose relief line discharges to open deck, and a heat-weakened cylinder can fail violently. The CO₂ room is deliberately located with access from open deck and separated from protected spaces (SOLAS II-2/10.4, FSS Code Ch.5) precisely so it can be fought externally.

60-Second Answer

A fire inside the CO₂ room is one of the few scenarios where the extinguishing installation is itself the casualty. Actions: (1) alarm, muster, inform bridge/master; (2) stop ventilation to the room and close its openings; (3) boundary cooling of the room's bulkheads and, if safely accessible, water spray onto the cylinder bank through the door/vent openings from a protected position — the aim is to keep cylinder metal temperature down; (4) do not commit a BA team inside while cylinders are heating: each cylinder carries a safety disc that ruptures on over-pressure, and the collective relief piping discharges to open deck clear of accommodation, muster stations and ventilation intakes — so expect and account for a massive CO₂ cloud at that outlet, and cordon it; (5) after the fire, treat the system as unavailable — inform class/flag, quantify remaining cylinders (level gauging), and apply interim fire-safety measures for the now-unprotected spaces. Design context: SOLAS II-2/10.4.3 and FSS Code Ch.5 require the storage room to be used for no other purpose, sited with access preferably from open deck, ventilated, and independent of the protected spaces — the layout assumes external firefighting.

Governing instrument

FSS Code Ch. 5 and SOLAS II-2 (CO₂ room arrangement and ventilation); cylinder safety-disc relief lines routed per the approved system drawings; response actions per the ship's fire training manual (SOLAS II-2/15).

Why You Fight It From Outside

  • Cylinder over-pressure: CO₂ pressure rises steeply with temperature (above the critical point 31.1°C the full cylinder behaves near-hydraulically) — discs rupture, and a defective disc path can mean cylinder failure.
  • Asphyxiation: venting cylinders can flood the room and adjacent areas with CO₂ — an entering team faces an oxygen-deficient, zero-visibility space.
  • Missile hazard: a heat-failed high-pressure cylinder or sheared valve is a projectile.

Relief Valve / Safety Disc Outlet

Each cylinder's safety disc and the manifold relief valve vent into a common relief line led to open deck, terminating clear of openings, intakes and normally-manned areas — know the physical location of this outlet on your own ship and include it in the incident cordon.

Trap point

Entering the CO₂ room to fight the fire is the fail — heated cylinders self-vent through their safety discs (relief piped to open deck); the answer is outside boundary cooling with ventilation shut.

The failing answer is "release the CO₂" (into its own room — meaningless) or committing a BA team onto a heating cylinder bank.
Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The CE's contingency: know the relief-outlet location, keep the room's boundary-cooling access points identified in the fire plan, ensure the CO₂ room appears in drill rotation, and have the "fixed system unavailable" procedure ready — extra patrols, hot-work prohibition, port-authority notification if alongside.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Can you release the system to fight this fire?" A: No — the fire is in the storage room itself; the system protects other spaces. Q: "Cylinders are venting — is the danger over?" A: No — the disc is doing its job, but the escaping CO₂ is an asphyxiant and remaining heated cylinders are still a failure risk; maintain cordon and cooling. Q: "Ship's fixed system is now empty — can you sail?" A: Only with flag/class agreement — the system is a statutory requirement (Safety Equipment Certificate); expect conditions/dispensation with interim measures.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Proposing to enter with BA immediately; not knowing where the relief line discharges; forgetting the room's ventilation must be stopped; no post-fire statutory follow-up (class, certificate impact, interim measures).

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

CO₂ critical temperature 31.1°C; cylinder pressure ~52 bar @ 15°C rising steeply when heated; room requirements: dedicated use, open-deck access preferred, independent ventilation — SOLAS II-2/10.4.3.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Multiple flag-state accidental-release investigations (e.g. UK MCA Safety Bulletin 12) show CO₂ escaping into occupied spaces kills without fire — the same asphyxiation mechanics that make entering a venting CO₂ room during a fire indefensible.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: CO₂ room on upper deck level with direct external access; relief line outlet on the open deck above, marked on the fire plan; boundary-cooling positions from the adjacent deck; contingency procedure in the SMS emergency manual covers loss of fixed fire-extinguishing capability.

Bursting disc — how does it burst?
FIREMACHINERY

15-Second Answer

A bursting (rupture) disc is a thin, precisely manufactured metal membrane — often pre-scored or domed — clamped across the pressure path. It bursts by mechanical failure of the membrane itself: when the pressure differential across it exceeds its rated burst pressure at the operating temperature, the material's stress passes its ultimate strength and the disc tears open along the score lines (or reverses and shears, in reverse-acting types), giving instant, full-bore, non-reclosing relief.

60-Second Answer

Unlike a spring-loaded relief valve, a bursting disc is a one-shot, sacrificial device. The disc is a calibrated diaphragm (nickel, stainless steel, Inconel or graphite) whose thickness, dome shape and scoring are manufactured so that it fails at a certified burst pressure ±tolerance at a stated temperature. Forward-acting (tension) discs: pressure loads the concave side; membrane stress rises with pressure until the material yields and tears — score lines control the opening into petals that fold back cleanly without fragmenting. Reverse-acting (buckling) discs: pressure loads the convex side; at the set pressure the dome snaps through (buckles) and is cut open by a knife ring or its own score pattern — these give tighter tolerance and better fatigue life under pulsating pressure. Because burst pressure is temperature-dependent (material strength falls when hot), the disc is rated for its service temperature — critical on a CO₂ cylinder, where the disc's real job is over-temperature protection: heating the cylinder raises the CO₂ pressure until the disc bursts and vents the charge through the relief line to open deck, instead of the cylinder failing. Once burst, the disc must be replaced — it cannot reseat.

Governing instrument

FSS Code Ch. 5 cylinder overpressure protection; disc characteristics per the manufacturer's rating to pressure-equipment standards (ISO 4126-2 family); installed values per the class-approved drawings.

Where You Meet Them Onboard

  • CO₂ cylinder safety caps — over-temperature/over-pressure protection, venting to the common relief line.
  • Main engine crankcase explosion relief valves — note: these are spring-loaded self-closing valves per IACS UR M9/SOLAS, not bursting discs — a classic confusion.
  • Starting-air line bursting caps — relief of a starting-air-line explosion on some designs.
  • Boiler/economiser and thermal-oil systems, refrigeration plants — where instant full-bore relief or isolation of a relief valve from corrosive media is needed (disc fitted below a relief valve).

Key Characteristics vs Relief Valve

  • Non-reclosing — total loss of contents; system down until disc renewed.
  • Instant full-bore opening — better for explosion/rapid-transient relief.
  • No moving parts, perfect tightness until burst — no simmer, no leakage.
  • Rated at temperature — the marked burst pressure applies at the stamped temperature; hotter means lower actual burst pressure for metal discs.

Trap point

A bursting disc is single-use and non-reclosing — confusing it with a reseating relief valve (or assuming it can be 'set' like one) is the standard trap.

Distinguish forward-acting from reverse-acting if pushed. Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Discs are PMS items with certificates: verify the stamped burst pressure/temperature against the approved drawing when renewing, never refit a burst or dented disc, and after any disc operation investigate the cause (why did pressure/temperature rise?) before restoring the system.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Can a bursting disc reseat after operating?" A: No — non-reclosing; replace it. Q: "Why fit a disc under a relief valve?" A: To isolate the valve from corrosive/fouling media and guarantee zero leakage in normal service; the valve then controls the relief after the disc opens. Q: "What happens to a metal disc's burst pressure when hot?" A: It falls — material strength drops with temperature; discs are rated at a stated temperature.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Describing only "it bursts at high pressure" with no material-failure mechanism; confusing discs with spring-loaded relief valves or crankcase relief doors; ignoring temperature dependence; not knowing discs fragment-free by design (scored petals).

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

ISO 4126-2; CO₂ cylinder pressure ~52 bar @ 15°C, rising steeply above critical 31.1°C — the temperature-pressure ramp that operates the cylinder disc; disc burst tolerance typically stated as ±% on the certificate — quote the certificate, not a universal number.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Starting-air line explosions (historic casualties from leaking air-start valves igniting oil films) are the design driver for bursting caps/relief devices on air-start manifolds — instant full-bore relief where a spring valve is too slow.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Senthil: How does it burst → forward vs reverse acting → disc vs relief valve → where used onboard → what do you check when renewing → crankcase relief (the contrast trap).

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container vessel: CO₂ cylinder safety caps (disc type per maker), discs in the refrigeration plant relief arrangement, bursting caps on ME starting-air branch pipes per the engine maker's design — each with certificates filed against the PMS renewal job.

CO₂ bottle pressure testing — what is the regime?
FIRESURVEYS

15-Second Answer

Per MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1 (25 May 2021): high-pressure CO₂ cylinders are periodically tested at intervals not exceeding 10 years — at the 10-year inspection at least 10% get internal inspection and hydrostatic test (one failure → 50%; further failures → 100%) — and before the 20-year anniversary, and every 10 years thereafter, ALL cylinders are hydrostatically tested. Test standard: ISO 18119:2018; test pressure per the cylinder's stamped marking.

60-Second Answer

The governing document is MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1, which revised the 2009 guidelines specifically to clarify the hydrostatic-testing regime and align it with MSC.1/Circ.1432. The regime: (1) every survey (intermediate/periodical/renewal for cargo ships; biennially for passenger ships) — visual inspection of all cylinders for damage/corrosion/loose mounting, check of hydrostatic-test dates, level verification of contents; (2) 10-year anniversary — internal inspection + hydrostatic test of at least 10% of the cylinders, with the sampling escalation 10% → 50% → 100% on failures, and different cylinders selected at the next cycle; (3) 20-year anniversary and every 10 years after — 100% of cylinders hydro-tested; (4) flexible discharge hoses renewed at maker intervals, not exceeding 10 years. The test itself follows ISO 18119:2018 (periodic inspection and testing of seamless steel cylinders) at the test pressure stamped on the cylinder neck — a cylinder-marking value, not a single universal figure. When cylinders are landed for test, the remaining quantity must still satisfy FSS Code Ch.5 §2.2.1 (SOLAS II-2/14.2 allows the arrangement to be agreed) — so testing is staged, or replacement cylinders arranged. Contents are checked without discharge, typically by ultrasonic liquid-level gauging; a cylinder more than 10% short of charge is refilled or replaced.

Governing instrument

MSC.1/Circ.1318/Rev.1 (25 May 2021) — maintenance and inspection of fixed CO₂ systems: the 10-year hydrostatic regime with 10% → 50% → 100% escalation — as implemented by flag and class instructions.

The Regime at a Glance

  • Every survey: visual + dates + contents check (all cylinders).
  • 10 yr: ≥10% internal + hydro (escalate 50% → 100% on failures; rotate the sample).
  • Before 20 yr, then every 10 yr: 100% hydro.
  • Hoses: ≤10 yr renewal. Control valves: internal exam ≤5 yr.
  • Standard: ISO 18119:2018; flag interpretations vary slightly (e.g. Indian flag requires overdue ships to test at next scheduled dry-docking) — check your flag's circular.

Trap point

Mixing the hydrostatic regime with routine content checks — Circ.1318/Rev.1's 10-year/10%→50%→100% testing is distinct from periodic level/weight verification (refill at 10% loss).

Checking the contents — weighing versus level gauging

The Code's named method for CO₂ is weighing. FSS Code Ch.5, 2.1.1.3 requires means for the crew to safely check the quantity of medium without moving the containers completely from their fixing position, and for carbon dioxide systems it specifies hanging bars for a weighing device above each bottle row, or other means. Surface indicators are the option the Code offers for other media — not for CO₂.

  • By weight: every cylinder is stamped with its tare (empty) weight. Suspend it from the hanging bar, weigh it, subtract the tare, and the difference is the CO₂ actually on board. More than 10% short of the stamped charge — refill or replace.
  • By ultrasonic level: a probe traversed up the cylinder wall finds the liquid/vapour interface. Faster, needs no lifting gear, no disconnection, and can be done in situ with the bank in service.
  • Why the examiner draws the contrast: weighing measures the mass the Code makes you prove; ultrasonic gauging infers it from a level, so it depends on charge density and reads only the liquid column. Both are acceptable under “or other means”; the practical regime is ultrasonic routinely, with weighing as the reference check.

Stored pressure, and the alarm arrangement

  • High-pressure cylinders hold liquid CO₂ at its own saturated vapour pressure, so stored pressure is set by temperature rather than by a setting: about 5.1 × 106 Pa (51 bar) at 15°C and about 5.8 × 106 Pa (58 bar) at 20°C. Above the critical temperature of 31°C (7.38 × 106 Pa) there is no liquid phase at all — which is why the cylinder room is ventilated and kept cool, and why the test pressure remains a cylinder-marking value rather than a universal figure.
  • Low-pressure (refrigerated bulk) units are the arrangement the Code puts a number on. FSS Code Ch.5, 2.2.4.2 requires the rated liquid CO₂ to be stored under a working pressure in the range 1.8 to 2.2 N/mm² — that is 1.8 × 106 to 2.2 × 106 Pa — with the normal liquid charge limited to not more than 95% of the vessel's volumetric capacity.

Alarms. Under FSS Code Ch.5, 2.2.4.3 a low-pressure unit must carry a pressure gauge, a high-pressure alarm set no higher than the relief valve setting, a low-pressure alarm set not less than 1.8 N/mm², a liquid CO₂ level indicator and two safety valves. Ch.5, 2.2.4.11 then requires audible and visual alarms at a central control station when the vessel pressure reaches either the low or the high value, when any one refrigerating unit fails to operate, or when the lowest permissible liquid level is reached. A pressure falling towards the low alarm with the refrigeration plant healthy is the leak signature — that alarm is what surfaces it.

What actions do you take if the emergency fire pump is not working during a Class survey?
FIRESURVEYSISM

15-Second Answer

Be transparent with the surveyor — never conceal it. Troubleshoot immediately (prime/suction loss, starting system, discharge valve line-up are the usual suspects) and rectify if possible during the survey. If it cannot be rectified, the surveyor will record it — typically a condition of class / statutory deficiency with a time limit — and I raise an SMS non-conformity, apply risk-assessed interim fire-safety measures, inform the company and flag, and arrange repair with re-survey. The pump is a statutory item: SOLAS II-2/10.2 and FSS Code Ch.12 (≥40% of total required fire-pump capacity, minimum 25 m³/h, fully independent of the engine room).

60-Second Answer

Immediate technical actions: attempt a systematic restart in front of the surveyor — check the prime (the classic failure: loss of suction/priming on a high-mounted pump; verify the priming unit, foot valve/suction line air-tightness), starting arrangement (battery/hydraulic/compressed-air starter of the independent diesel drive, or emergency generator supply if motor-driven), fuel (dedicated tank level), sea suction valve open, and discharge line-up to the main correct. Many "failed" demonstrations are line-up or priming errors and are recoverable within the survey. If genuinely defective: tell the surveyor plainly and show your troubleshooting — credibility with class matters. The surveyor's options: allow rectification during the survey; or impose a condition/recommendation of class and statutory deficiency with a completion window, potentially with operational restrictions, since the emergency fire pump is required by the Safety Equipment Certificate. Management actions (what the examiner is really testing): raise an SMS non-conformity with root-cause analysis; risk assessment and interim measures — continuous availability of main/other fire pumps, restricted hot work, additional fire patrols, port authority notification if alongside; inform the company (DPA) and, through class, the flag; order spares/shore assistance; close out with repair, test in the surveyor's presence, and deletion of the condition. Requirements to quote: FSS Code Ch.12 — capacity not less than 40% of the total fire-pump capacity required by SOLAS II-2/10.2 and in any case not less than 25 m³/h, two jets of water, self-priming or with an approved priming arrangement, situated with its sea suction, power source and fuel outside the space containing the main fire pumps.

Governing instrument

SOLAS II-2/10.2 (fire pumps including the emergency fire pump) and FSS Code Ch. 12; the statutory/class survey regime under SOLAS Ch. I, with deficiency handling through the society's Condition-of-Class machinery.

Likely Failure Causes — the Diagnostic Sequence

  1. Priming/suction: lost prime, leaking suction glands/joints, choked strainer or foot valve — commonest cause on high-located pumps.
  2. Starting: flat battery / low hydraulic accumulator or air pressure on the independent prime mover; emergency switchboard supply faults on motor-driven units.
  3. Driver: diesel fuel (dedicated tank), cooling, governor; motor overload trips.
  4. Hydraulic path: discharge/isolating valve line-up, relief bypassing, worn wear rings giving pressure but no flow.

Trap point

Concealing the defect from the surveyor is the fail — declare it, troubleshoot transparently, and accept the Condition-of-Class route if not immediately rectifiable; the ship does not automatically lose class.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Pre-survey, the CE runs the emergency fire pump on load (two jets, pressure at the most remote hydrant) — not just a start check. The recurring institutional failure is testing it unloaded monthly and discovering at survey it cannot hold prime under real suction lift.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Surveyor is not aboard yet — do you still report it?" A: Yes — concealment discovered later is far worse for ship and CE; SMS requires the non-conformity regardless. Q: "Can the ship sail with the pump defective?" A: Only within whatever condition/dispensation class/flag impose, with documented interim measures — not on the CE's own authority. Q: "Why must its services be outside the engine room?" A: It exists for the scenario where the main space (and its pumps/power) is lost to fire — sea suction, prime mover, fuel and location must all be independent.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Jumping to "inform class" without any troubleshooting sequence; not knowing the 40%/25 m³/h figures; treating it purely as a technical fault with no SMS non-conformity or interim measures; suggesting concealment or "test it later".

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Engine-room fire casualties repeatedly show total loss of main fire pumps with the space — the emergency pump's independence requirements exist because of exactly those fires; PSC detention statistics consistently rank inoperative emergency fire pumps among the most common detainable deficiencies.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Maersk container vessel: emergency fire pump in the bow thruster/forward compartment with its own sea chest and emergency switchboard supply; weekly start and monthly on-load test to the foredeck hydrants recorded in the PMS; priming unit condition is a standing internal-audit check item.

Polar Code — what is the latest amendment, and what is its CE relevance?
SOLASPOLAR

15-Second Answer

The latest amendment is Resolution MSC.538(107) (adopted 8 June 2023, in force 1 January 2026): it inserts new chapters 9-1 (safety of navigation) and 11-1 (voyage planning) into Part I-A, extending mandatory navigation and voyage-planning requirements to non-SOLAS ships — fishing vessels ≥24 m, pleasure yachts ≥300 GT, and cargo ships 300–<500 GT — together with the parallel SOLAS Chapter XIV amendments (MSC.532(107)). Existing such ships have until 1 January 2027 to comply.

60-Second Answer

The Polar Code (in force 1 January 2017 via SOLAS Ch. XIV and MARPOL amendments) sets safety (Part I) and pollution-prevention (Part II) requirements for ships in Arctic/Antarctic waters. The latest amendment package, MSC.538(107) with the companion SOLAS XIV amendment MSC.532(107), entered into force 1 January 2026. Its substance: new chapters 9-1 and 11-1 in Part I-A applying safety-of-navigation and voyage-planning measures to previously-exempt non-SOLAS categories, plus tightened navigation-equipment provisions — e.g. ice-strengthened ships built on/after 1 Jan 2026 need two independent echo-sounders (or one with two independent transducers); ships ≥500 GT need two independent non-magnetic heading devices on main and emergency power; ships going above 80° latitude need a GNSS-independent position input; and Category A/B ships built on/after 1 Jan 2026 need enclosed bridge wings. CE relevance: although navigation-focused, the machinery consequences are the CE's: emergency-power reliability feeding the duplicated navigation equipment, low-temperature operability of machinery and fuel/hydraulic/cooling systems per the ship's Polar Ship Certificate and Polar Water Operational Manual (PWOM) — the PWOM's machinery chapters (heating, sea-water suction ice protection, materials at Polar Service Temperature) are CE-owned documents at survey.

Governing instrument

Polar Code, mandatory via SOLAS Ch. XIV; latest amendment Res. MSC.538(107) (adopted 8 Jun 2023, in force 1 Jan 2026) inserting chapters 9-1 and 11-1 into Part I-A.

Amendment Chain — Say It in Order

  • MSC.385(94)/MEPC.264(68): Polar Code adopted; in force 1 Jan 2017.
  • MSC.538(107) + MSC.532(107) (SOLAS XIV): in force 1 Jan 2026 — non-SOLAS extension (Ch. 9-1/11-1) + navigation equipment upgrades; grace to 1 Jan 2027 for existing ships in the new categories.
  • Also 1 Jan 2026 (context): the same amendment wave brought SOLAS II-1/3-13 lifting appliances, FSS MSC.555(108), LSA MSC.535/554 — useful to show currency if the examiner broadens the question.

Trap point

Quoting the base Polar Code without the amendment is the stale answer — MSC.538(107) (chapters 9-1 and 11-1) applies from 1 Jan 2026, including to some non-SOLAS craft.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

For a polar-trading ship the CE demonstrates at survey: machinery and EER equipment functional at the Polar Service Temperature, sea-inlet/cooling ice protection, fuel-oil heating and viscosity management in extreme cold, hydraulic oils rated for PST, and the emergency source of power supporting the (now expanded) navigation fit — all documented in the PWOM against the Polar Ship Certificate.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Does MSC.538(107) apply to your container ship?" A: The new Ch. 9-1/11-1 target the additional non-SOLAS categories — a SOLAS cargo ship ≥500 GT was already fully covered by the Code; the 2026 navigation-equipment provisions bite by construction date and operation area. Q: "What are the Polar ship categories?" A: A (at least medium first-year ice), B (at least thin first-year ice), C (open water/lesser ice). Q: "Is the Polar Code only about safety?" A: No — Part II amends MARPOL: effectively zero discharge of oil/NLS in polar waters, stricter garbage/sewage rules.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Answering with the 2017 entry-into-force as "the latest"; not knowing the resolution number or the 2027 grace date; unable to name one concrete new requirement (echo-sounders, non-magnetic heading, >80° GNSS-independent fix, enclosed bridge wings); no machinery/PWOM angle when asked for CE relevance.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

MSC.538(107) — EIF 1 Jan 2026, grace 1 Jan 2027; new scope: fishing ≥24 m, yachts ≥300 GT, cargo 300–<500 GT; >80° latitude GNSS-independent position; Code baseline EIF 1 Jan 2017; categories A/B/C.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

Explorer (2007) — the cruise ship sunk by ice in the Antarctic; the loss demonstrated that conventional SOLAS ships were not fit for polar hazards and directly accelerated the mandatory Polar Code's development.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Nair/Simon: Latest Polar amendment → what changed and for whom → categories A/B/C → PWOM contents → machinery at PST → MARPOL polar discharge rules → STCW polar training (Reg. V/4).

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

Maersk container tonnage does not routinely trade polar waters, so no Polar Ship Certificate is held — the CE relevance is exam-level currency plus the transferable engineering: cold-weather precautions (sea-chest steam clearing, fuel heating, hydraulic oil selection) applied on winter North-Pacific/North-Atlantic routings.

What is the IGC Code?
SOLASGAS CARRIER

15-Second Answer

The International Code for the Construction and Equipment of Ships Carrying Liquefied Gases in Bulk — mandatory under SOLAS Chapter VII for gas carriers built on/after 1 July 1986. It sets design/construction/equipment standards (survival capability, cargo containment tank types, materials, vent systems, fire protection) for carrying liquefied gases such as LNG and LPG, evidenced by an International Certificate of Fitness. The current comprehensive edition is the MSC.370(93) rewrite (in force 1 July 2016), amended since — most recently MSC.523(106) (high-manganese austenitic steel, in force 1 Jan 2026) and MSC.566(109) (in force 1 Jul 2026).

60-Second Answer

The IGC Code is the mandatory international standard for gas carriers, made compulsory by SOLAS VII/11–13. Its goal: minimise risk to ship, crew and environment from cargoes that are flammable, toxic, asphyxiant, reactive or cryogenic. Structure highlights: Ch.2 survival capability and cargo-tank location (damage stability by ship type 1G/2G/2PG/3G — the more hazardous the cargo, the more severe the assumed damage and the further inboard the tanks); Ch.4 cargo containment — independent tanks Type A (prismatic, full secondary barrier), Type B (e.g. Moss sphere, partial secondary barrier justified by crack-propagation analysis), Type C (pressure vessels, no secondary barrier), plus membrane tanks (full secondary barrier); Ch.5–6 process pressure vessels, piping and cryogenic materials; Ch.8 vent/relief systems; Ch.11 fire protection (water spray, dry chemical powder); Ch.13–17 instrumentation, gas detection, personnel protection; Ch.16 use of cargo as fuel; Ch.19 the product table. Certification: International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk. Amendment chain after the 2016 rewrite (MSC.370(93)): MSC.411(97) in force 1 Jul 2018, MSC.441/447(99) in force 1 Jan 2020 (Certificate of Fitness forms), MSC.523(106) in force 1 Jan 2026 (Table 6.3 — high-manganese austenitic steel for cryogenic service, mirrored in the IGF Code by MSC.524(106)), and MSC.566(109) in force 1 Jul 2026. Ships built before 1986 follow the non-mandatory GC/eGC Codes.

Governing instrument

IGC Code, mandatory under SOLAS Ch. VII Part C for gas carriers built on/after 1 Jul 1986 — current text Res. MSC.370(93) with the amendment chain to MSC.566(109); gas as fuel on other ships is the IGF Code (SOLAS II-1 Part G).

Do Not Confuse

  • IGC Code — ships carrying liquefied gas as cargo (gas carriers).
  • IGF Code — ships using gas/low-flashpoint fuels (e.g. LNG-fuelled container ships) — SOLAS II-1 Part G.
  • IP Code — Industrial Personnel (SOLAS Ch. XV, MSC.527(106)) — an entirely different code despite the similar acronym.

Trap point

IGC governs carriage of liquefied gas cargo; gas as fuel on other ship types is the IGF Code — applying IGC to a gas-fuelled container ship is the trap.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

Even on a container ship the IGC/IGF boundary is live for a Maersk engineer: dual-fuel newbuildings run under the IGF Code, whose cryogenic-material and gas-safety philosophy is lifted from the IGC Code — bunker tank types (usually Type C), gas detection zoning, ESD philosophy and vent mast arrangements all trace to IGC concepts.

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

Q: "Why does a Type B tank need only a partial secondary barrier?" A: Its design is validated by refined stress/fatigue and crack-propagation analysis ("leak-before-failure") so a small drip tray suffices; Type A relies on conventional analysis, hence a full barrier. Q: "Which certificate does an IGC ship carry?" A: The International Certificate of Fitness for the Carriage of Liquefied Gases in Bulk. Q: "Ship built 1984 — which code?" A: The GC Code (non-mandatory, recommendatory) — IGC applies from 1 Jul 1986 builds.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Confusing IGC with IGF (carrier vs fuel-user); no tank-type knowledge; claiming membrane tanks are Type B; not knowing the CoF; presenting the 1983/1986 text as current instead of the MSC.370(93) 2016 rewrite; missing that cargo-as-fuel is IGC Ch.16 on a gas carrier but IGF on any other ship.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Mandatory from 1 Jul 1986 builds; rewrite MSC.370(93) in force 1 Jul 2016; amendments in force 1 Jul 2018 / 1 Jan 2020 / 1 Jan 2026 (MSC.523(106)) / 1 Jul 2026 (MSC.566(109)); ship types 1G/2G/2PG/3G; tank types A/B/C + membrane.

Deep-Dive: Casualty Link

The gas-carrier safety record is the code's own evidence: no major loss-of-containment casualty on an IGC-class LNG carrier in decades of trading — the leak-before-failure and secondary-barrier philosophy is the cited reason, which is exactly the engineering argument an examiner enjoys hearing.

Maersk's methanol dual-fuel newbuildings sit outside both codes' gas chapters (methanol is under the IGF Code's alternative-fuel provisions via the interim guidelines MSC.1/Circ.1621), but the fleet's LNG-era studies and the IGC-derived safety philosophy — zoning, ESD, vent masts — inform the company's low-flashpoint fuel procedures the CE administers.

Free-fall lifeboat — what certificate does it carry, what are the height, seating and launching requirements, and what liferafts must be provided as well?
SOLASLSA

15-Second Answer

A free-fall lifeboat is launched over the stern down a ramp under gravity, so it clears the ship without falls. Its certificate of approval states the free-fall certification height — the greatest height for which the boat is approved — together with the required launching ramp length and the ramp angle for that height. The installed height must never exceed it.

60-Second Answer

Under the LSA Code section 4.7 a free-fall lifeboat must meet everything in section 4.6 and more besides. It must make positive headway immediately on water entry and must not touch the ship when launched against a trim of up to 10° and a list of up to 20° either way, in four separate loading conditions. Structurally it must survive a launch from 1.3 times the certification height fully loaded, and it must protect occupants from harmful acceleration at the certification height. The release system has two independent activations, operable only from inside, and a factor of safety of 6. On a cargo ship it replaces boats on each side, but liferafts are still required on each side, those on at least one side served by a launching appliance.

1. The certificate — what is actually on it

This is the half of the question candidates rarely prepare. Under LSA Code 4.7.7, in addition to the general lifeboat approval particulars, the certificate of approval for a free-fall lifeboat must state three things:

  • the free-fall certification height;
  • the required launching ramp length; and
  • the launching ramp angle for that certification height.

The general particulars alongside them include the number of the certificate of approval, the hull material, the total mass fully equipped and fully manned, the measured towing force, and a statement of which LSA section the boat is approved against.

2. Height — get the definition exactly right

Free-fall certification height (LSA Code 1.1.4) is the greatest launching height for which the lifeboat is to be approved, measured from the still water surface to the lowest point on the lifeboat when the lifeboat is in the launch configuration. It is a property of the boat's approval, not of the ship. The installation is then constrained so that the actual launching height does not exceed the height the boat is certified for — which is why the ramp length and angle appear on the same certificate.

A currentness point worth knowing: the Code once carried a separate defined term, required free-fall height, measured with the ship in its lightest seagoing condition, and a paragraph 4.7.3.3 saying that height shall never exceed the certification height. Both were deleted by resolution MSC.218(82), in force 1 July 2008. Section 4.7.3 today has only .1 and .2. Quoting the deleted paragraph, or the lightest-seagoing-condition wording as if it defined certification height, is a dated answer.

3. Seating and occupant protection

  • Seat width at least 430 mm.
  • Free clearance in front of the backrest at least 635 mm.
  • Backrest extending at least 1,000 mm above the seatpan.
  • Carrying capacity is the number of persons who can be seated without interfering with propulsion or the operation of any equipment.

The seat geometry is not comfort — it is the restraint geometry that keeps the spine aligned with the acceleration vector during entry. LSA Code 4.7.5 requires the boat to protect occupants from harmful acceleration when launched from the certification height in calm water under unfavourable conditions of trim up to 10° and list up to 20°, in the same four loading conditions, which is why occupants must be properly secured before release.

4. Launching performance and the tanker case

Under 4.7.3.1 the boat must make positive headway immediately after water entry and must not contact the ship, launched from the certification height against trim up to 10° and list up to 20° either way, when fully equipped and loaded with: its full complement; occupants placed to put the centre of gravity most forward; occupants placed to put it most aft; and its operating crew only.

4.7.3.2 adds a case examiners like: for oil tankers, chemical tankers and gas carriers whose final angle of heel calculated under MARPOL exceeds 20°, the lifeboat must be capable of free-fall launch at that final angle of heel and on the final waterline.

5. Release system

  • Two independent activation systems, operable only from inside the boat, marked in a contrasting colour.
  • Capable of releasing under any load from no-load up to at least 200% of the normal fully equipped, fully manned load.
  • Protected against accidental or premature use.
  • Designed so the release system can be tested without launching the boat.
  • Designed with a factor of safety of 6 on the ultimate strength of the materials.

6. Liferafts — the part of the question that is not about the boat

Choosing a free-fall boat does not reduce the raft requirement. Under SOLAS III/31 a cargo ship over 85 m may carry free-fall lifeboat(s) launched over the stern instead of lifeboats on each side, but it must still carry one or more liferafts on each side, with the liferafts on at least one side served by a launching appliance. A rescue boat is required as well. The examiner's point is that the free-fall arrangement is an alternative to the boats, not to the survival-craft philosophy of redundancy on both sides.

7. Drills

SOLAS III/19.3.3.4 sets the pattern. At least once every three months during an abandon-ship drill the crew board, secure themselves in their seats and run the launch procedure up to but not including actual release; the boat is then either free-fall launched with only the operating crew aboard, or lowered by the secondary means of launching without the operating crew and manoeuvred in the water. At intervals of not more than six months the boat must either be launched by free fall with the operating crew only, or a simulated launching carried out in accordance with the Organization's guidelines.

Deep-Dive: Numbers to Memorise

Deep-Dive: Trap Questions

"Is the certification height measured in the lightest seagoing condition?" — No. That wording belonged to required free-fall height, a defined term deleted by MSC.218(82) along with paragraph 4.7.3.3. Certification height is measured to the lowest point of the boat in the launch configuration and is a property of the boat's approval. "You have a free-fall boat, so you need no liferafts?" — Wrong; rafts are required on each side, one side with a launching appliance. "Can you test the release without launching?" — Yes, and the Code requires the system to be designed for exactly that.

Deep-Dive: Common CE Failures

Quoting a single fixed free-fall height as though it were a rule — there is no universal figure; the height is whatever the boat is certified for and it is on the certificate. Describing the release as a hook system: a free-fall boat has no falls to release on load, and its release gear is a different animal from the on-load hooks that section 4.4.7.6 governs for davit-launched boats. Forgetting the secondary means of launching, which is what makes the three-monthly drill possible without dropping the boat.

Deep-Dive: Examiner Chain

Simon: What certificate does the free-fall boat carry → what is stated on it → define certification height → how is the ramp angle related → what trim and list must it launch at → what if it is a tanker heeled beyond 20° → how strong must the hull be → how does the release work and can you test it → what liferafts do you still need → how often do you drill it → what is the secondary means of launching for.

Deep-Dive: CE Relevance

The Chief Engineer owns the hardware that makes the drill lawful: the hydraulic or mechanical release gear, the secondary launching winch and its brake, the ramp rollers and their greasing, and the boat's engine starting arrangement after months of stowage at a steep ramp angle. The three-monthly drill is the point where those items are proven, and the six-monthly obligation is the one that forces a decision between a real free-fall launch and a simulated launching — a decision taken on the condition of the release gear and the recovery arrangements, which is an engineering judgement before it is a deck one.

Deep-Dive: On My Vessel

On a stern-mounted free-fall installation the certificate is kept with the Record of Equipment, and the certification height, ramp length and ramp angle are the three figures cross-checked against the as-fitted arrangement at every safety equipment survey. Between drills the release gear is exercised in the test position — the Code's "test without launching" provision — and the ramp is inspected for corrosion at the boat's resting points, which is where wastage concentrates.
