Understand this first

A large two-stroke engine and its turbocharger are matched to each other at one region of load, usually somewhere around three-quarters power. Slow steaming does not simply make the engine do less of the same thing - it moves the engine to a place the matching was never designed for. Too little air for the fuel, and too little heat to keep the liner above the temperature at which acid condenses on it. Almost every drawback in this question is one of those two facts working its way outward.

Model written answer

1. Why speed reduction is attractive, and what "low load" costs

Propulsion power varies roughly with the cube of ship speed, so a modest speed reduction gives a large fuel and CO2 saving at no capital cost - which is why MARPOL Annex VI regulation 25 (EEXI) and regulation 28 (CII), in force since 1 January 2023, are often met by slowing down. The cost is that a two-stroke is optimised around roughly 70-80% MCR, and running far below that puts every balance - air to fuel, thermal and lubrication - outside its design condition.

2. Combustion and thermal consequences

  • Reduced scavenge air pressure. The turbocharger is sized for high load, so at low load the air-fuel ratio falls and combustion is incomplete.
  • Low compression and maximum pressure, giving late, slow burning and poor thermal efficiency.
  • Poorer atomisation where injection pressure depends on speed - large droplets, afterburning, and cool nozzles prone to dribbling and trumpet deposits.
  • Soot and unburnt fuel through the whole gas path - piston crown, exhaust valve, receiver, turbine nozzle ring and economiser.

3. Turbocharger and scavenge-side dangers

  • Surging, from running close to the compressor surge line and aggravated by a fouled turbine side, with mechanical damage to rotor and bearings.
  • Auxiliary blowers running continuously, because scavenge pressure never rises enough to cut them out.
  • Scavenge space and port fouling by oil-soaked carbon - the classic precondition for a scavenge fire.
  • Fouled nozzle rings and blades, reducing air supply further: a self-reinforcing loop.

4. Cylinder condition - cold corrosion and lubrication

This is the most damaging effect. Lower heat release lets the liner wall fall below the acid dew point, so sulphuric acid formed from fuel sulphur and water vapour condenses on it. The result is cold corrosion - rapid corrosive wear concentrated near the top ring reversal point. It is aggravated when cylinder oil BN is not matched to the duty, or when feed rate is cut for economy; the opposite error, persistent over-lubrication, gives deposits, bore polishing and eventually scuffing. Low-sulphur fuel does not remove the problem - it changes the correct oil BN.

5. Consequences beyond the main engine

  • Exhaust gas economiser. Low exhaust temperature and flow cut steam production, so the auxiliary boiler must run - eroding the saving - while soot builds on the tubes. A later load increase can ignite it as a soot fire.
  • Exhaust valves running cool, with deposits, poor rotation and seat blow-by.
  • Manoeuvring: reduced propeller inflow gives less rudder authority, and prolonged running near a barred speed range must be avoided.
  • Hull and propeller fouling increases at low speed and during warm-water idling, degrading the very efficiency sought.

6. Engineering measures to resolve the problems

  • Low-load or part-load tuning, so injection and exhaust valve timing suit the new band. On electronically controlled engines this is a control change, not mechanical rework.
  • Turbocharger cut-out on multi-turbocharger engines, or variable turbine area or an exhaust bypass, to restore scavenge pressure and move away from surge.
  • Cylinder lubrication set from evidence: match BN to fuel sulphur and liner condition, and set feed rate from scavenge drain analysis (iron content, residual BN) and scrape-down inspection.
  • Raise liner wall temperature where the design allows, by jacket water control or liner insulation, to keep the surface above the acid dew point.
  • Where slowing down is used for EEXI compliance, implement it as an approved engine or shaft power limitation with a sealed, recorded override, documented in the technical file and accepted by class.

7. Operational and maintenance measures

  • Planned periodic load-up runs to burn off deposits, taken in the passage rather than left to a manoeuvring situation.
  • More frequent scavenge space inspection and economiser soot blowing and water washing, treating the boiler as a fire risk before any load increase.
  • Condition monitoring - PMI pressure analysis, exhaust deviation, turbocharger speed against scavenge pressure, and liner wear at shorter intervals than the maker's baseline.
  • Update the SEEMP and the maintenance plan to the new profile: the engine is running in a condition those intervals were not written for.

8. Conclusion

Speed reduction is the cheapest short-term carbon measure, but not a free one: it moves the engine outside its design envelope and transfers cost from fuel to wear, fouling and fire risk. Retuned, correctly lubricated and monitored, those risks are controllable; unmanaged, cold corrosion and scavenge or economiser fires are the predictable results.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

The question has two explicit halves - drawbacks and dangers, then how they are resolved. A common failure is to write a long list of problems and then two lines of remedies. Give the second half real weight: roughly nine or ten marks of problems and six or seven of solutions. Grouping the problems by where they occur (combustion, turbocharger and scavenge, cylinder, everything else) rather than as an undifferentiated list also shows you understand the mechanism rather than having memorised a list.

The central mechanism - get this right and the rest follows

Almost every drawback traces back to one fact: the turbocharger is sized for high load and cannot deliver design air at low load. Less air gives incomplete combustion; incomplete combustion gives soot; soot fouls the turbine and economiser; a fouled turbine gives still less air. Separately, less heat release lowers liner wall temperature, and below the acid dew point that produces cold corrosion. If you can state those two chains, you can derive the whole answer in the exam without recalling a list.

Common mistakes

  • Treating cold corrosion as a low-sulphur-fuel problem. It is a liner temperature problem. Low-sulphur fuel changes the correct cylinder oil BN; it does not remove condensation.
  • Claiming a specific percentage of MCR as the universal definition of 'low load'. The band depends on engine type and tuning - say 'well below the design point of roughly 70-80% MCR' instead.
  • Forgetting the exhaust gas economiser entirely. It is where a low-load soot fire actually starts, and it is worth marks.
  • Answering only about the main engine when the question says 'risks associated with low speed operation' - manoeuvrability, hull fouling and auxiliary boiler load are part of the picture.
  • Presenting engine power limitation as something a Chief Engineer simply sets. It is an approved arrangement recorded in the EEXI technical file, with a controlled override.

Examiner traps

"So slow steaming saves fuel - what is the problem?" The trap is to agree too readily. The correct engineering answer is that the fuel saving is real but partly offset by auxiliary boiler firing, increased maintenance and hull fouling, and it carries fire and wear risks that must be actively managed.

"Which regulation makes you slow down?" None does. EEXI (regulation 25) is a technical index and CII (regulation 28) is an operational rating; speed reduction is a common means of compliance, not a requirement. Saying "MARPOL requires slow steaming" is wrong.

Currency - what was true in January 2026

At the date of this paper, EEXI and CII were in force and had been since 1 January 2023. The IMO Net-Zero Framework was approved at MEPC 83 in April 2025 but its adoption was adjourned at the extraordinary MEPC session of 14 to 17 October 2025, to reconvene twelve months later. So in January 2026 the Net-Zero Framework was not adopted and not in force. Do not write that a global GHG fuel standard or carbon price is in force. If you mention the Framework, say it is approved and awaiting adoption.

Likely oral follow-up

  • Why does cold corrosion concentrate at the top of the liner? That is where the wall is coolest relative to the gas and where the top ring reverses, so condensed acid is mechanically worked into the surface.
  • How would you know cold corrosion is happening before you open up? Rising iron content in scavenge drain oil analysis with falling residual BN, and accelerating liner wear measurements.
  • Why must you be careful increasing load after a long slow-steaming passage? Accumulated soot in the economiser can ignite. Soot blow first, and raise load in stages.
  • What is turbocharger cut-out and when is it used? Isolating one turbocharger on a multi-turbocharger engine at low load to restore scavenge pressure and move away from surge.
  • Does low-sulphur fuel mean you can use a low-BN cylinder oil? It means you should match BN to sulphur - but if the liner is running cold you may still need alkalinity, so decide on drain analysis, not on fuel grade alone.

Memory framework

Work outward from the cylinder: AIR - BURN - WALL - REST. Air: turbocharger cannot supply design air (steps 2 and 3). Burn: incomplete combustion, soot, fouling. Wall: liner too cold, cold corrosion, lubrication mismatch (step 4). Rest: economiser, exhaust valves, manoeuvring, hull (step 5). Then the two remedy steps are simply fix the machine (step 6) and fix the operation (step 7).

Regulation and source map

  • MARPOL Annex VI regulation 25 - Required EEXI, which existing ships must meet (attained EEXI is regulation 23); engine or shaft power limitation is an accepted means of achieving it
  • MARPOL Annex VI regulation 26 - SEEMP, with Part III for the CII plan
  • MARPOL Annex VI regulation 28 - operational carbon intensity indicator and A to E rating
  • IMO Net-Zero Framework - approved MEPC 83 (April 2025); adoption ADJOURNED at the extraordinary MEPC session, 14 to 17 October 2025, to reconvene twelve months later; not adopted, not in force
  • Engine-designer service guidance on part-load / low-load tuning, turbocharger cut-out and cylinder oil feed rate - industry guidance, not regulation
  • SOLAS II-1 and class requirements for scavenge and economiser fire precautions - the safety context for the fire risks described

Uncertainty and applicability

The regulatory statements in this answer are verified against IMO primary sources. The engineering content is established marine-engineering principle supported by engine-designer service guidance, and it is written to apply generally to large slow-speed two-stroke engines. It is deliberately not tied to one designer's figures: load thresholds, tuning options, permissible jacket water temperatures and cylinder oil recommendations differ between engine types and between tuning packages, and the ship's own maker documentation and class approval govern in every case. Where this answer says 'roughly 70-80% MCR' it is describing the usual optimisation region, not a specification. Whether a specific ship may apply engine power limitation, and on what conditions, depends on its own EEXI technical file and class acceptance.

Model written answer

1. Why tank coatings are a statutory matter

Corrosion is the principal structural degradation mechanism in ballast and cargo tanks, and the response is regulated rather than left to maintenance policy. SOLAS regulation II-1/3-2 requires protective coatings for dedicated seawater ballast tanks in all ship types and for double-side skin spaces of bulk carriers, complying with the Performance Standard for Protective Coatings, IMO resolution MSC.215(82). The PSPC is written to a target useful coating life of 15 years, which section 4.1 defines as the period over which the system is intended to remain in "GOOD" condition. The coating, not the steel, is therefore the thing being managed.

2. Influence of temperature on corrosion rate

  • Corrosion in a tank is an electrochemical process, and its rate rises with temperature - as a rough kinetic approximation the rate roughly doubles for each 10°C rise over the normal range.
  • That is offset by a competing effect: the solubility of dissolved oxygen falls as temperature rises. In a vented tank where oxygen can escape, the rate therefore peaks and then falls at higher temperatures; in a closed or continuously replenished system it keeps climbing.
  • Heated boundaries dominate in practice. Ballast tanks adjacent to heated cargo tanks, heated fuel tanks or the machinery space run warmer than the sea and corrode faster than identical tanks elsewhere in the ship.
  • Temperature cycling is worse than steady heat. It drives condensation on the underdeck and upper structure, so those surfaces undergo repeated wet and dry cycling - the most aggressive condition, because the surface is continually re-oxygenated.
  • In cargo oil tanks the effect is chemical too: heated crude raises the temperature while inert gas contributes carbon dioxide and sulphur oxides, which dissolve in the condensate to form acids - hence severe underdeck corrosion and pitting on the tank bottom beneath water and sludge.

3. Causes of coating deterioration

  • Defective surface preparation - mill scale left in place, residual soluble salts, inadequate blast profile or cleanliness. This is the single largest cause, and it is built in at construction.
  • Application faults - dry film thickness below specification, missed stripe coats at edges and welds, dry spray, and application outside the coating's temperature, humidity or dew-point limits.
  • Edges, welds and cut-outs - sharp edges and weld spatter hold too little paint, so breakdown starts there and spreads.
  • Mechanical damage from ballasting and cargo operations, staging, tools, hose handling and crew traffic.
  • Ageing and chemical attack - chalking, embrittlement, loss of adhesion, osmotic blistering, cathodic disbondment, and attack by heated or chemical cargoes and by tank cleaning.
  • Design and access - poor drainage, pockets that never dry, and areas that cannot be reached for inspection or repair.
  • Deferred maintenance. Breakdown is self-accelerating: once rust creeps under the film, adjacent coating is undermined.

4. How coating condition assessment reveals corrosion status

Coating condition is assessed visually and reported in three grades used throughout the survey regime: GOOD - only minor spot rusting; FAIR - local breakdown at edges of stiffeners and weld connections, and/or light rusting over 20% or more of the areas under consideration, but less than POOR; and POOR - general breakdown of coating over 20% or more of the areas, or hard scale over 10% or more of the areas under consideration.

  • It is a leading indicator. Coating breakdown shows where corrosion will occur before any measurable steel is lost, whereas thickness measurement is a lagging indicator that reports damage already done.
  • It drives the survey regime. Under the Enhanced Survey Programme a rating below GOOD must be recorded and re-examined at subsequent annual surveys, and a POOR rating brings extended examination and thickness measurement.
  • It targets effort - telling the Chief Engineer and the surveyor which tanks and which specific areas need close-up survey, re-coating or steel renewal.
  • It is judged alongside anode condition, so coating and cathodic protection are assessed as one system.

5. Information obtained from the Coating Technical File

The CTF is required by PSPC section 3.4, is reviewed by the Administration, and by section 3.4.5 must be kept on board and maintained throughout the life of the ship. It carries the specification of the coating system, the record of the shipyard's and owner's coating work, and the criteria for coating selection, job specification, inspection, maintenance and repair. At new construction (section 3.4.2) it must contain:

  • the Statement of Compliance or Type Approval Certificate for the coating system;
  • the Technical Data Sheet - product name and identification, materials, components, composition and colours, minimum and maximum dry film thickness, application methods and tools, the required condition of the surface to be coated (de-rusting grade, cleanliness and profile), and environmental limitations of temperature and humidity;
  • shipyard work records - the actual space and area coated in square metres, the coating system applied, time of coating, thickness and number of layers, ambient conditions during coating, and the method of surface preparation;
  • procedures for inspection and repair of the coating during construction;
  • the coating log issued by the coating inspector, confirming application in accordance with the specification and recording any deviations, with the daily log and non-conformity reports;
  • the shipyard's verified inspection report - completion date, result, remarks and inspector's signature; and
  • procedures for in-service maintenance and repair of the coating system.

Thereafter in-service maintenance, repair and partial re-coating are recorded in it (3.4.3), and a full re-coating requires the 3.4.2 items again (3.4.4). The file is the ship's coating history: what was specified, what was actually applied and under what conditions, and everything done since.

Study guide

Why this structure scores

Four printed sub-items, no printed marks, so treat them as four equal quarters and give each its own heading. The verbs differ and you should follow them: discuss for the first two, describe for the third, and state for the fourth. Sub-item (iv) is a list question - the marks are in naming the CTF contents, not in writing an essay about coatings, so give it as a list and make it complete.

The temperature answer needs both effects

Most candidates write only "hotter means faster" and stop. The complete answer has two competing effects: reaction kinetics rise with temperature, but dissolved oxygen solubility falls. In a vented tank where oxygen escapes the rate peaks and then declines; in a closed system it keeps rising. Then add the point that actually matters on a ship - cycling beats steady heat, because wet and dry cycling on the underdeck continually re-oxygenates the surface. Say that the corrosion rate roughly doubles per 10°C as an approximation, not as a standard; it is general kinetics, not a class rule.

Coating condition is a leading indicator - this is the point of (iii)

Sub-item (iii) asks how coating assessment makes you aware of the corrosion status, and the answer is about timing. Coating breakdown tells you where corrosion is going to happen; thickness measurement tells you where it already has. That is why the survey regime is keyed to the coating grade rather than to steel loss - by the time the steel is measurably thin, the opportunity to intervene cheaply has gone. If you write one sentence in this sub-item, write that one.

Common mistakes

  • Answering (iv) with generalities about coatings. It says state the information obtained - it wants the CTF's actual contents.
  • Confusing the Coating Technical File with the Ship Construction File or the Survey Report File. The CTF is coatings only, required by the PSPC, kept on board for the life of the ship.
  • Giving the 10°C doubling rule as though it were a regulatory figure, and omitting the oxygen-solubility counter-effect.
  • Quoting the GOOD / FAIR / POOR percentages loosely. FAIR is light rusting over 20% or more but less than POOR; POOR is general breakdown over 20% or more, or hard scale over 10% or more. The hard-scale limb is the one people forget.
  • Treating coating breakdown as a painting problem. It is a survey-driver: the grade changes what class requires of the ship.

Examiner traps

"What is the target useful life of a PSPC coating?" Fifteen years - but say what that means. Section 4.1 defines it as the period over which the system is intended to remain in GOOD condition, and it adds that actual life varies with conditions in service. It is a design target, not a guarantee or a replacement interval.

"Where does GOOD condition come from?" PSPC section 2.6 defines it as the condition with minor spot rusting, referring across to the ESP regime - so the coating standard and the survey standard use the same vocabulary deliberately.

"Which tanks does the PSPC cover?" Dedicated seawater ballast tanks in all ship types and double-side skin spaces of bulk carriers. Cargo oil tanks of crude oil tankers are covered by a separate, later performance standard - do not merge them.

Likely oral follow-up

  • What is the 90/10 rule? PSPC section 2.8 - 90% of all thickness measurements shall be at or above the nominal dry film thickness, and none of the remaining 10% below 0.9 times NDFT.
  • What is a stripe coat and why does it matter? Extra painting of edges, welds and hard-to-reach areas, because those are where film thickness is naturally lowest and breakdown begins.
  • How long is the CTF kept? On board and maintained throughout the life of the ship - PSPC section 3.4.5.
  • What happens if a ballast tank is rated POOR? It is recorded, re-examined at subsequent annual surveys and brings extended examination and thickness measurement.
  • Why do ballast tanks next to heated cargo corrode faster? Higher steel temperature raises the electrochemical rate, and the temperature difference promotes condensation and wet-dry cycling.
  • What is recorded in the CTF after a partial re-coat? The maintenance, repair and partial re-coating activities, under section 3.4.3; a full re-coating requires the 3.4.2 items again.

Memory framework

The four sub-items are a life cycle of the coating: WHY IT CORRODES (temperature), WHY THE PAINT FAILS (deterioration), HOW YOU KNOW (condition assessment), WHERE IT IS WRITTEN DOWN (the CTF). For the CTF contents themselves, work in build order: certificate, data sheet, what the yard actually did, how it was inspected, how it is repaired - that ordering reproduces PSPC 3.4.2 almost item for item.

Regulation and source map

  • SOLAS chapter II-1 regulation 3-2 - protective coatings of dedicated seawater ballast tanks in all types of ships and double-side skin spaces of bulk carriers
  • SOLAS chapter XII regulation 6 - the bulk carrier limb of the same requirement
  • IMO resolution MSC.215(82) - the Performance Standard for Protective Coatings (PSPC)
  • MSC.215(82) section 2.6 - GOOD condition is the condition with minor spot rusting as defined in resolution A.744(18)
  • MSC.215(82) section 2.8 - NDFT and the 90/10 practice

Uncertainty and source status

The Coating Technical File contents, the 15-year target useful life and the GOOD-condition and NDFT definitions are quoted from the PSPC text itself and are primary-verified. Two items are weaker and are marked as such. First, MIW holds no licensed copy of the ESP Code, so the full FAIR and POOR wording and the survey consequences that follow from a rating are taken from consistent classification-society and industry restatements - the percentages are given because they are quoted identically across those sources, but they sit at authoritative-secondary status, not primary, and a candidate quoting them should be prepared to attribute them to the ESP regime rather than to the PSPC. Second, the doubling of corrosion rate per 10°C is a general kinetic approximation, not a maritime standard, and is qualified in the answer by the opposing oxygen-solubility effect. Acquiring a licensed ESP Code would move the survey-consequence material to primary and is worth recording as a corpus need.

UNCLOS makes a trade. Understand this question first

A marine safety investigation is not a trial, and the Code exists to keep it from becoming one.
